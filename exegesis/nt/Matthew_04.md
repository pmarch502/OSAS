# Matthew 4 -- First-Century Reading

## Context Within the Author's Argument

**What came before (Matthew 3):** The author introduced John the Baptist as the herald of the kingdom, established that covenant membership requires fruit and not merely Abrahamic descent, and culminated in Jesus's baptism -- where heaven itself declared him "my beloved Son" by fusing Psalm 2 (royal enthronement) with Isaiah 42 (the servant of YHWH). The Spirit descended upon Jesus, and his first words were about fulfilling "all righteousness." The chapter ended with a publicly confirmed identity and an anointing of the Spirit.

**What comes after (Matthew 5):** Jesus ascends a mountain and begins teaching with authority. The Sermon on the Mount opens with blessings pronounced on those who embody kingdom character -- the poor in spirit, those who hunger for righteousness, the merciful, the persecuted. He declares that his followers are salt and light, and that he has come not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. The pivotal statement is that entrance into the kingdom requires a righteousness exceeding that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20). He then demonstrates what this surpassing righteousness looks like through a series of intensified commands.

**How chapter 4 connects them:** Chapter 3 gave Jesus his identity (Son of God, Spirit-anointed servant-king) and his program (fulfilling all righteousness). Chapter 4 validates that identity through testing, then launches the public work that identity demands. The testing in the wilderness proves Jesus to be the faithful Son that Israel was not. The geographical move to Galilee, framed by an Isaiah quotation, positions the kingdom's light as dawning on those in darkness. The calling of disciples creates the nucleus of a renewed people. The healing and teaching tour demonstrates in action what the kingdom looks like. Only then, with identity proven, geography established, a community formed, and the kingdom demonstrated, does the author position Jesus to sit down on the mountain in chapter 5 and articulate the kingdom's demands. The sequence is not arbitrary: tested identity must precede authoritative teaching.

## The Spirit Leads Jesus Into Testing (vv. 1-2)

"Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tested by the devil." The word "then" (tote) is the author's characteristic transition marker, used dozens of times throughout the document. Here it signals that what follows is a direct consequence of what just happened. The Spirit that descended on Jesus in 3:16 now drives the action. The testing is not something that happens to Jesus despite the Spirit's presence -- it happens because of the Spirit's initiative.

The word "tested" (peirasthenai, from peirazo) requires care. In the LXX, this word group is used both for God testing his people and for people testing God. In Exodus 15:25 and 16:4, God "tested" (epeirasen) Israel in the wilderness to see whether they would walk in his instructions. In Deuteronomy 6:16, Israel is commanded not to "test" (ekpeiraseis) the Lord their God "as you tested him at Massah." The same word operates from both directions -- God testing people to prove their faithfulness, and people testing God by demanding proof of his. This double meaning becomes critical in the third temptation.

The location -- the wilderness (eremon) -- is loaded with scriptural memory. Israel spent forty years in the wilderness after passing through water (the Red Sea). Jesus enters the wilderness after passing through water (baptism in the Jordan). Israel was called God's son: "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1, already quoted by the author in 2:15). Jesus has just been declared God's Son (3:17). The parallel is unmistakable and deliberate: the author is presenting Jesus as recapitulating Israel's story. The question is whether this Son will prove faithful where that son did not.

The period of "forty days and forty nights" reinforces the parallel. Israel wandered forty years (Deuteronomy 8:2). Moses fasted forty days and forty nights on Sinai (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 9:9). Elijah traveled forty days and forty nights to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). The number forty in Israel's tradition consistently marks a period of divine testing or preparation. By fasting for this period, Jesus enters into the full pattern of Israel's wilderness experience compressed into his own body.

The statement "he was hungry" (husteron epeinasen) after the fast is understated, but its placement is deliberate. It sets up the first temptation, and it echoes Deuteronomy 8:3 directly: God humbled Israel and "let you be hungry" (eleimagchonasen se kai epeinases) and fed them manna, "that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone." The author has set every element in place before the tempter speaks.

## First Test: Bread From Stones (vv. 3-4)

"And the tempter came and said to him, 'If you are the Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread.'"

The address "If you are the Son of God" (ei huios ei tou theou) hooks directly into the declaration of 3:17. The tempter does not deny the identity; he leverages it. The conditional "if" (ei with the indicative) does not express doubt -- it means something closer to "since you are." The logic is: since this is who you are, exercise the power that comes with it. Use your divine sonship to meet your own physical need.

The suggestion itself -- turning stones to bread -- is not inherently evil. Jesus will later multiply loaves for crowds (14:13-21, 15:32-39). The issue is not the act but the source of the command. The tempter asks Jesus to act on the tempter's initiative rather than the Father's. The testing pattern from Deuteronomy clarifies: in the wilderness, Israel's hunger was part of God's pedagogy. To short-circuit the hunger by acting outside God's provision would be to fail the lesson.

Jesus answers: "It is written, 'Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God'" (Deuteronomy 8:3). The phrase "it is written" (gegraptai) is the author's standard formula for scriptural citation throughout the document. Jesus does not argue, reason, or negotiate. He answers with scripture, and specifically with scripture from Deuteronomy -- the book that narrates and interprets Israel's wilderness testing.

The Deuteronomy passage in its full context reads: "He humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna, which you did not know, nor did your fathers know, that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 8:3). The point of Israel's hunger was to teach dependence on God's word -- his instruction, his timing, his provision. Jesus's answer demonstrates that he has learned what Israel did not: the Father's word sustains more fundamentally than bread. He will wait for the Father's provision rather than act on the tempter's suggestion.

## Second Test: The Temple Pinnacle (vv. 5-7)

"Then the devil took him to the holy city and set him on the pinnacle of the temple and said to him, 'If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is written: He will command his angels concerning you, and on their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone.'"

The location shifts to the temple in Jerusalem -- "the holy city" (ten hagian polin), a designation the author uses again at 27:53. The "pinnacle" (pterygion, literally "little wing") of the temple was its highest point, traditionally understood as the southeast corner overlooking the Kidron Valley, a sheer drop of considerable height.

The tempter now quotes scripture himself -- Psalm 91:11-12. This is significant. The tempter adopts Jesus's own method (answering with scripture) and turns it back on him. Psalm 91 is a psalm of divine protection for the one who "dwells in the shelter of the Most High" (91:1). The tempter's quotation is accurate but his application is distorted. Psalm 91 promises divine protection for the faithful one going about God's purposes -- it does not invite the faithful one to manufacture a crisis in order to force God to act. There is a difference between trusting God in danger and engineering danger to test whether God will respond.

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy again: "Again it is written, 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test'" (Deuteronomy 6:16). The word "test" here is ekpeiraseis -- the same word group used in verse 1, but now in the opposite direction. In verse 1, God tests Jesus; here, the temptation is for Jesus to test God. Deuteronomy 6:16 continues: "as you tested him at Massah." The Massah incident (Exodus 17:1-7) was Israel's paradigmatic failure of trust: lacking water, they demanded that God prove himself ("Is the LORD among us or not?" -- Exodus 17:7). They turned a moment of need into a demand for proof, converting trust into a transaction. The tempter is inviting Jesus to do the same: turn your sonship into a test of whether the Father will really protect you.

Jesus's "again it is written" (palin gegraptai) is also instructive. He does not reject the tempter's scripture; he adds a second scripture that qualifies it. Scripture interprets scripture. Psalm 91's promise of protection is genuine, but Deuteronomy 6:16's prohibition against testing God governs how that promise is received. The faithful one trusts the promise without manufacturing scenarios to verify it. This hermeneutical move -- using one text to interpret and limit another -- is itself a model the author places on Jesus's lips at the very beginning of his public career.

## Third Test: All the Kingdoms (vv. 8-10)

"Again, the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. And he said to him, 'All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me.'"

The scene moves to "a very high mountain" (oros hypselos lian). Mountains carry enormous weight in this author's narrative. Moses received the Torah on a mountain. Jesus will deliver his great teaching from a mountain (5:1). He will be transfigured on a mountain (17:1). And at the end of the document, the risen Jesus will commission his disciples on a mountain in Galilee (28:16). The author consistently uses mountains as sites of revelation and authority. Here, however, the mountain is the site of a counterfeit offer of authority.

The offer -- "all the kingdoms of the world and their glory" -- is precisely what Psalm 2 promises to the Son of God. Psalm 2:7-8: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession." The tempter is offering what the Father has already promised, but through a different means. Instead of the path the Father has appointed -- which, as the author's narrative will reveal, runs through suffering, rejection, and the cross -- the tempter offers a shortcut. The price is worship (proskyneo) directed to the wrong object.

The first-century hearer steeped in Israel's scriptures would recognize this as the fundamental sin that pervades the narrative of Israel's history. The very first commandment -- "You shall have no other gods before me" (Exodus 20:3) -- and the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4-5) define Israel's identity as the people who worship YHWH alone. Every catastrophe in Israel's recorded history -- the golden calf, the Baal worship under Ahab, the syncretism that led to exile -- stemmed from directing worship to something other than God. The tempter is proposing the primal covenant violation.

Jesus's answer is his sharpest: "Be gone, Satan! For it is written, 'You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve'" (Deuteronomy 6:13). This is the first time Jesus uses the name "Satan" (Satana -- from the Hebrew satan, "adversary"). The response is a direct command followed by the third Deuteronomy quotation. The progression through Deuteronomy is notable: 8:3, then 6:16, then 6:13. All three come from the same section of Deuteronomy (chapters 6-8) that recounts and interprets Israel's wilderness testing. Jesus is living inside the text of Deuteronomy, answering each test from the very passages that explained Israel's failures.

The verb "serve" (latreuseis) in the Deuteronomy quotation carries the sense of worship-service -- the devotion of one's whole life to God. The author's Jesus does not merely refuse the shortcut; he reaffirms the exclusive claim of God on worship and service. The kingdoms will come to him, but through the Father's appointed path, not through compromise with the adversary.

## The Testing's Significance in the Author's Argument (vv. 11)

"Then the devil left him, and behold, angels came and were ministering to him."

The promise of Psalm 91 -- angelic protection and service -- is fulfilled, but on God's timing, not as the result of a manufactured crisis. The angels come after the testing is endured, not because Jesus threw himself off the temple. The author's point is precise: divine provision follows faithful endurance, not presumptuous demand.

The three tests as a sequence recapitulate the three central failures of Israel in the wilderness: demanding bread when God was teaching dependence (Exodus 16), testing God at Massah by demanding proof of his presence (Exodus 17), and turning to idolatry at the golden calf (Exodus 32). In each case, Israel as God's "son" (Hosea 11:1) failed. In each case, Jesus as God's declared Son (3:17) succeeds. The author's argument is not merely that Jesus resists temptation in a generic moral sense. It is that Jesus accomplishes what Israel was called to do and failed to do. He is the faithful Son, the true Israel, who passes through the wilderness testing and comes out having worshiped God alone, trusted his word alone, and refused to put him to the test.

This has direct consequences for the narrative ahead. When Jesus begins to teach, he teaches with the authority of one who has done what he commands. When he demands a righteousness exceeding that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20), he speaks as one who has already demonstrated that surpassing righteousness -- not by being untested, but by being tested and remaining faithful.

## The Move to Galilee and the Isaiah Quotation (vv. 12-16)

"Now when he heard that John had been arrested, he withdrew to Galilee." The verb "withdrew" (anechoresen) is the same word used for Joseph's retreat from Judea to Galilee in 2:22, and for Jesus's later withdrawals from confrontation (12:15, 14:13, 15:21). The author uses this word to describe strategic relocations that are simultaneously retreats from threat and advances toward purpose. The arrest of John is not just background information -- it signals the closure of the herald's phase and the opening of the one the herald announced.

"And leaving Nazareth he went and lived in Capernaum by the sea, in the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali." The geographical specificity is deliberate and sets up the author's next scriptural quotation. Capernaum is on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, in the ancient tribal territories of Zebulun and Naphtali. These were the northernmost tribes, the first to fall to Assyrian conquest in 732 BCE under Tiglath-Pileser III (2 Kings 15:29). They were the first to experience the darkness of foreign domination and exile.

The author then quotes Isaiah 9:1-2: "The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, the way of the sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles -- the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light, and for those dwelling in the region and shadow of death, on them a light has dawned."

In its original context, Isaiah 9 follows the darkness of the Assyrian crisis described in Isaiah 8. The promise is that the very region first plunged into darkness -- the northern territories -- will be the first to see light. The light in Isaiah 9 is connected to the birth of the royal child in 9:6-7: "For to us a child is born, to us a son is given, and the government shall be upon his shoulder." This child will sit on David's throne and establish it with justice and righteousness forever.

The author's quotation accomplishes two things. First, it explains Jesus's apparently unremarkable relocation to a fishing village as prophetic fulfillment -- the same interpretive move the author made with the flight to Egypt (2:15), the slaughter in Ramah (2:17-18), and settlement in Nazareth (2:23). Second, it frames the entire Galilean ministry that follows as the dawning of the eschatological light Isaiah promised. Galilee is not a detour or a retreat from Jerusalem's significance. It is the scripturally appointed place where the light first breaks.

The phrase "Galilee of the Gentiles" (Galilaia ton ethnon) is worth noting. This region's mixed population -- Jews and Gentiles in proximity -- foreshadows the scope of the kingdom that will eventually extend to all nations (28:19). The light that dawns here does not respect the boundaries that the Pharisees and Sadducees of chapter 3 might have expected.

## Jesus Begins Proclaiming the Kingdom (v. 17)

"From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near.'"

The phrase "from that time" (apo tote) marks a major structural transition in the author's narrative. It appears only one other time in this document -- at 16:21, "From that time Jesus began to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer." These two "from that time" markers divide the narrative: 4:17 launches the public proclamation of the kingdom; 16:21 pivots toward the suffering through which the kingdom will be established.

Jesus's message is verbatim identical to John's in 3:2: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near" (metanoeite, engiken gar he basileia ton ouranon). This is not coincidence or shorthand. The author presents Jesus as taking up and continuing the herald's message. But the speaker has changed. John was the voice preparing the way; Jesus is the Lord whose way was prepared. The same words now carry the weight of the one heaven itself identified as the Son.

"Repent" (metanoeite) is a command for a total reorientation of life -- not merely feeling sorry, but turning around. "The kingdom of heaven has drawn near" (engiken) uses the perfect tense: it has drawn near and remains near. The kingdom is not a distant future event being predicted. It is a present reality pressing in, demanding response now.

## The Calling of the First Disciples (vv. 18-22)

The author narrates two parallel calling scenes. Both occur beside the Sea of Galilee, both involve pairs of brothers, and both result in the immediate abandonment of livelihood to follow Jesus.

First, Jesus sees Simon (called Peter) and Andrew his brother "casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen." He says: "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men" (deute opiso mou, kai poieso hymas haleis anthropon). The metaphor repurposes their trade: what they did with fish, they will now do with people. The verb "make" (poieso) indicates a process -- Jesus will form them into something they are not yet. The response is total and immediate: "Immediately they left their nets and followed him" (eutheos aphentes ta diktya ekolouthyesan auto).

Going on from there, he sees James the son of Zebedee and John his brother "in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets." He calls them, and "immediately they left the boat and their father and followed him." The escalation is notable. Simon and Andrew left their nets -- their tools. James and John left their boat and their father -- their livelihood and their family. The cost of following increases.

The author offers no backstory, no deliberation, no negotiation. The call is bare, the response is total, and the break is clean. This literary pattern is not accidental. It mirrors the call of Elisha by Elijah in 1 Kings 19:19-21, where Elijah throws his mantle on Elisha, who then leaves his oxen (his livelihood) and follows. But where Elisha asked to kiss his father and mother goodbye, these disciples simply leave. The authority of the caller is greater, and the urgency is greater.

The pronoun tracking is significant. "Follow me" (opiso mou) -- the "me" is Jesus. Throughout the chapter, Jesus has been obedient to the Spirit (v. 1), obedient to the Father's word (vv. 4, 7, 10), and responsive to the Father's purposes. Now he issues commands of his own, and others follow him. The one who followed the Spirit into the wilderness now calls others to follow him. The authority displayed here flows from the tested and proven identity established in vv. 1-11.

## The Galilean Ministry Summary (vv. 23-25)

"And he went throughout all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction among the people."

This summary verse contains the three activities that will characterize Jesus's entire Galilean ministry: teaching (didaskon), proclaiming (keryssonx), and healing (therapeuon). The phrase "the gospel of the kingdom" (to euangelion tes basileias) appears here for the first time and will recur at 9:35 and 24:14. The "good news" is not about the kingdom as a detached subject -- it is the kingdom's own announcement, the news that the kingdom itself generates by its arrival.

The healing is described comprehensively: "every disease and every affliction" (pasan noson kai pasan malakian). The word "every" (pasan) appears twice, emphasizing the completeness of his restorative power. In the context of Isaiah 9's "great light" dawning on those in darkness and the shadow of death, these healings are not merely compassionate acts. They are evidence that the darkness is being pushed back. The kingdom of heaven, having drawn near, manifests itself in the defeat of disease and suffering.

"So his fame spread throughout all Syria." Syria, to the north, was predominantly Gentile territory. The light that dawned in "Galilee of the Gentiles" is already spilling beyond Israel's boundaries.

"And great crowds followed him from Galilee and the Decapolis and Jerusalem and Judea and from beyond the Jordan." The geographical scope of these crowds is remarkable. The Decapolis was a league of Hellenistic (Gentile) cities east of the Jordan. "Beyond the Jordan" (peran tou Iordanou) is Transjordan, another largely non-Jewish area. Jerusalem and Judea represent the Jewish heartland. The author is describing a movement that draws from every compass point -- Jewish and Gentile territories alike. This geographical breadth, paired with the Isaiah quotation about "Galilee of the Gentiles," signals that the kingdom's scope exceeds ethnic and territorial boundaries from its very inception.

The verb "followed" (ekolouthyesan) is the same verb used when the four disciples followed Jesus in vv. 20 and 22. The crowds follow, but their following is different. They come for healing and spectacle. The four disciples were called, and they left everything. The author will develop this distinction throughout the document -- the difference between the crowds who follow for benefit and the disciples who follow at cost.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **It validates Jesus's sonship through testing, not merely through declaration.** The heavenly voice of 3:17 declared Jesus the Son of God. The wilderness testing proves it. The author's argument requires both: identity must be demonstrated, not merely announced. This sets a pattern that governs the rest of the document -- claims about identity (whether Jesus's or anyone else's) must be validated by conduct. The parallel with John's demand for "fruit worthy of repentance" (3:8) is direct: even the Son of God shows the fruit of his identity.

2. **It establishes Jesus as the faithful Israel.** By structuring the testing around Israel's three wilderness failures and answering each with the Deuteronomy passages that narrated those failures, the author argues that Jesus is the true and faithful Son who accomplishes what Israel as a whole did not. This Israel-recapitulation theme has been building since chapter 2, where Jesus went down to Egypt and was called back out (2:15, quoting Hosea 11:1). Jesus's personal history repeats Israel's national history, but with a different outcome.

3. **It positions Galilee as the scripturally appointed starting point for the kingdom's light.** The Isaiah 9 quotation transforms what might look like a peripheral beginning into a prophetically significant one. The first to enter darkness are the first to see light. The author's geography is theology.

4. **It marks the structural beginning of Jesus's public proclamation.** The "from that time" formula of 4:17 signals a major narrative shift. Everything before this -- genealogy, birth, flight, return, herald, baptism, testing -- was preparation. The public work begins here, with the same message the herald proclaimed, but now spoken by the one heaven identified and the wilderness validated.

5. **It introduces the call to discipleship as total and immediate.** The four fishermen who leave everything model the response the kingdom demands. Their call foreshadows the radical demands of chapters 5-7 (the Sermon on the Mount) and anticipates the cost-of-discipleship teaching that will become explicit later (10:37-39, 16:24-26). The kingdom does not request partial allegiance.

6. **It demonstrates the kingdom in action before defining it in words.** Jesus heals every disease and every affliction before he preaches the Sermon on the Mount. The author shows the kingdom at work in bodies before articulating the kingdom's demands in speech. This sequence matters: the authority of Jesus's teaching in chapters 5-7 rests on the demonstrated authority of chapters 3-4.
