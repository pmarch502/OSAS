# John 6 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (John 5):** Chapter 5 narrates the healing of a man who had been paralyzed for thirty-eight years at the pool of Bethesda in Jerusalem, on the Sabbath. When the Jewish authorities confront Jesus for this Sabbath violation, Jesus responds with a statement that escalates the conflict far beyond a dispute about rest days: "My Father is working until now, and I am working" (5:17). The author notes that "the Jews" sought to kill him because he was not only breaking the Sabbath but also "calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God" (5:18). What follows is an extended discourse on the Father-Son relationship: the Son does nothing on his own but only what he sees the Father doing (5:19); the Father shows the Son all that he himself does (5:20); the Father raises the dead and gives life, and the Son likewise gives life to whom he wills (5:21); the Father has given all judgment to the Son (5:22); whoever hears the Son's word and believes the one who sent him has eternal life and does not come into judgment but has passed from death to life (5:24). The discourse then appeals to a series of witnesses who confirm Jesus's identity: John the Baptist (5:33-35), the works Jesus performs (5:36), the Father himself (5:37), and the scriptures (5:39). The chapter's climax directly names Moses: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me. But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe my words?" (5:46-47).

**What the author argues immediately after (John 7):** Chapter 7 opens with a geographical and temporal note: "After these things Jesus was walking in Galilee, for he was unwilling to walk in Judea because the Jews were seeking to kill him" (7:1). The Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot) is near. Jesus's brothers urge him to go to Judea and display his works publicly, but the author notes that "not even his brothers were believing in him" (7:5). Jesus eventually goes to the feast in secret and teaches in the temple, provoking a division among the people -- some say he is "the Prophet," others say he is the Christ, others object that the Christ does not come from Galilee (7:40-43). The chapter's climactic moment comes when Jesus stands and cries out: "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink. He who believes in me, as the scripture said, 'From his innermost being will flow rivers of living water'" (7:37-38). The author glosses this: "But this he spoke of the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were going to receive; for the Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified" (7:39). The chapter ends with the officers sent to arrest Jesus returning empty-handed, confessing "Never has a man spoken the way this man speaks" (7:46), and Nicodemus briefly reappearing to ask whether their law judges a man without hearing him (7:50-51).

**How chapter 6 connects the surrounding argument:** The end of chapter 5 left the reader with a challenge about Moses: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me" (5:46). Chapter 6 immediately takes up the Moses question -- not through further argument about the scriptures but through enacted demonstration. Jesus performs two signs that directly parallel the two great acts of God through Moses in the exodus: he provides bread for the multitude in the wilderness (echoing the manna of Exodus 16) and walks on the sea (echoing the crossing of the Red Sea in Exodus 14). The chapter then unfolds a discourse in which the crowd explicitly invokes Moses and the manna, and Jesus responds by claiming that he himself is what the manna pointed to -- "the bread of life" that came down from heaven. The question at the end of chapter 5 -- do you believe Moses? -- is tested in chapter 6 by whether the crowd can recognize in Jesus the fulfillment of what Moses mediated.

The chapter also continues the escalation of conflict and the winnowing of followers that runs through this section of the document. In chapter 5, "the Jews" in Jerusalem sought to kill Jesus. In chapter 6, the opposition spreads: first "the Jews" grumble (6:41, 52), then "many of his disciples" find his teaching intolerable and withdraw (6:60, 66). The circle of those who reject Jesus narrows from the authorities to the general crowd to his own followers. By the chapter's end, only the Twelve remain, and even among them, one is identified as "a devil" (6:70). The departure of many disciples in chapter 6 sets the stage for chapter 7, where Jesus avoids Judea because of the death threat and even his brothers do not believe. The winnowing that began with official opposition in Jerusalem has reached the ranks of Jesus's own disciples in Galilee.

The geographical movement reinforces the argument. Chapter 5 took place in Jerusalem; chapter 6 moves entirely to Galilee, on and around the Sea of Tiberias. Chapter 7 will return to Jerusalem for the Feast of Tabernacles. The placement of the bread-of-life discourse in Galilee rather than Jerusalem means that the Moses-and-manna argument is conducted not before the Pharisees and rulers of chapter 5 but before a Galilean crowd that had followed Jesus because of his healing signs (6:2). The audience shifts, but the response is the same: grumbling, rejection, withdrawal. The author demonstrates that the failure to receive Jesus is not confined to the Jerusalem establishment. It extends to the crowds and even to the disciples.

The Passover setting (6:4) is crucial to the chapter's internal logic. The author has now mentioned Passover twice (2:13, 6:4), and will mention it a third time (11:55, 12:1, 13:1). Each Passover marks a major section of the narrative. At the first Passover, Jesus cleansed the temple and spoke of destroying and raising the temple of his body. At this second Passover, Jesus provides bread and declares himself the bread of life whose flesh must be eaten and whose blood must be drunk. At the third Passover, Jesus will be the Passover lamb, slain. The progression is deliberate: temple, bread, sacrifice. Each Passover moves closer to the event the Passover itself commemorates -- the lamb slain, the blood applied, the deliverance accomplished.

## Verses 1-4: Setting -- The Sea, the Mountain, the Crowd, the Passover

"After these things Jesus went away to the other side of the Sea of Galilee (or Tiberias). A large crowd was following him, because they saw the signs which he was performing on those who were sick. Then Jesus went up on the mountain, and there he sat down with his disciples. Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was near."

**"After these things" (meta tauta).** This transitional phrase (also used at 2:12, 3:22, 5:1, 5:14, 7:1, 19:28, 21:1) signals a movement to a new scene without specifying how much time has elapsed. The scene shifts from Jerusalem (chapter 5) to Galilee without a connecting journey narrative. The author is not interested in travelogue; he is interested in argument.

**"The Sea of Galilee (or Tiberias)" (tes thalasses tes Galilaias tes Tiberiados).** The double name is characteristic of this author, who often provides alternative identifications for his readers (cf. 1:38, "Rabbi, which translated means Teacher"; 1:41, "Messiah, which translated is Christ"; 4:25, "Messiah is coming, he who is called Christ"). The city of Tiberias was built by Herod Antipas around 20 CE and named in honor of the emperor Tiberius. For a readership outside Palestine, "Tiberias" would be the more recognizable name. The sea functions as the boundary that Jesus crosses, creating the spatial dynamics of the chapter: Jesus crosses over (v. 1), goes up on the mountain (v. 3), comes back across the sea (vv. 16-21), and the crowd pursues him across (vv. 22-25).

**"A large crowd was following him, because they saw the signs."** The reason for the crowd's pursuit is explicitly stated: they have seen the signs (ta semeia) he was performing on the sick. This is precisely the category of belief that the author flagged as inadequate in 2:23-25: many believed because of signs, but Jesus did not entrust himself to them. The crowd follows because of what they have seen, not because of what they have understood. The distinction between sign-based following and genuine believing will become the central issue of the discourse that follows.

**"Jesus went up on the mountain" (anelthen eis to oros ho Iesous).** The definite article -- "the mountain" (to oros), not merely "a mountain" -- may carry typological significance. In Israel's story, "the mountain" is supremely Sinai/Horeb, where Moses received the Torah and God fed Israel with manna (the manna provision began after the exodus and the Sinai covenant). Jesus ascending the mountain with his disciples before feeding the multitude evokes Moses on Sinai. The author does not develop the comparison explicitly here -- the crowd will make the connection themselves in verse 14 -- but the staging is unmistakable: a leader ascends a mountain, sits with his followers, and is about to provide bread for the people in the wilderness.

**"Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was near" (en de engys to pascha, he heorte ton Ioudaion).** This is not a casual time-marker. The author's inclusion of Passover timing is always theologically purposeful (2:13, 6:4, 11:55, 12:1, 13:1). Passover commemorated the exodus -- the night God struck down the firstborn of Egypt while Israel, protected by the blood of the lamb on their doorposts, was spared (Exodus 12). The feast celebrated God's deliverance of his people from slavery through the sacrifice of a lamb, the application of its blood, and the eating of its flesh with unleavened bread. Every element of this chapter -- bread from heaven, flesh to be eaten, blood referenced, the crowd's invocation of Moses, the exodus patterns -- will resonate with the Passover background. The author is staging the bread-of-life discourse within the Passover framework so that the reader hears Jesus's claims about his flesh and blood against the sound of the Passover liturgy.

## Verses 5-13: The Feeding of the Multitude

"Therefore Jesus, lifting up his eyes and seeing that a large crowd was coming to him, said to Philip, 'Where are we to buy bread, so that these may eat?' This he was saying to test him, for he himself knew what he was intending to do. Philip answered him, 'Two hundred denarii worth of bread is not sufficient for them, for everyone to receive a little.' One of his disciples, Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, said to him, 'There is a lad here who has five barley loaves and two fish, but what are these for so many people?' Jesus said, 'Have the people sit down.' Now there was much grass in the place. So the men sat down, in number about five thousand. Jesus then took the loaves, and having given thanks, he distributed to those who were seated; likewise also of the fish, as much as they wanted. When they were filled, he said to his disciples, 'Gather up the leftover fragments so that nothing will be lost.' So they gathered them up, and filled twelve baskets with fragments from the five barley loaves which were left over by those who had eaten."

**"He was saying this to test him" (touto de elegen peirazon auton).** The author's parenthetical reveals that Jesus's question is not a request for information but a probe. The verb peirazo ("to test, try, prove") is used in the LXX for God's testing of his people. Exodus 16:4: before giving the manna, God tells Moses, "Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them (hina peirasō autous), whether they will walk in my law or not." Exodus 15:25: at Marah, after providing drinkable water, "there he tested them (epeirasen autous)." Deuteronomy 8:2: "You shall remember all the way which the LORD your God has led you in the wilderness these forty years, that he might humble you, testing you (ekpeirasei se), to know what was in your heart." The parallel is exact: God tested Israel by providing bread in the wilderness to reveal what was in their hearts; Jesus tests Philip by asking about provision for the crowd to reveal what is in his understanding. The echo of Deuteronomy 8:2 is reinforced by the echo of 2:25 -- Jesus "knew what was in man." Now he tests to expose what is in a specific disciple.

**Philip and Andrew.** Philip answers from the perspective of calculation: two hundred denarii (roughly eight months' wages for a laborer) would not suffice. Andrew points to a boy with five barley loaves and two fish but recognizes their absurd inadequacy. Both disciples respond in terms of human resources. The test reveals that they have not yet grasped who is asking the question. The one through whom "all things came into being" (1:3) does not face a resource problem.

**"Five barley loaves" (pente artous krithinous).** The specification of barley bread is unique to this author among the four accounts. Barley was the grain of the poor -- wheat bread was more desirable and expensive. The detail recalls the barley loaves in the Elisha narrative: 2 Kings 4:42-44 narrates that a man brought Elisha "twenty loaves of barley" and some fresh grain, and Elisha commanded, "Give them to the people that they may eat." His servant protested: "What, shall I set this before a hundred men?" Elisha repeated: "Give them to the people that they may eat, for thus says the LORD, 'They shall eat and have some left over.'" They ate and had some left over. The parallel is striking: barley loaves, insufficient quantity, a protest from the servant/disciple, a command to distribute, and leftovers. But the scale is vastly different: Elisha fed a hundred with twenty loaves; Jesus feeds five thousand with five. The one greater than Elisha is here -- and the discourse will argue that the one greater than Moses is here as well.

**"Have the people sit down" (poiesate tous anthropous anapesein).** The verb anapesein ("to recline, sit down") is the posture of eating at a meal. The note that "there was much grass in the place" suggests spring -- consistent with the Passover timing (Nisan, roughly March-April). The detail may also echo Psalm 23:2 (LXX 22:2): "He makes me lie down in green pastures" -- the shepherd providing for his flock in a grassy place.

**"Jesus then took the loaves, and having given thanks, he distributed" (elaben oun tous artous ho Iesous kai eucharistesas diedoken).** The action sequence -- taking, giving thanks, distributing -- uses vocabulary that would resonate with any first-century community accustomed to communal meals. The verb eucharisteo ("to give thanks") appears again in this chapter at verse 23 ("the place where they ate the bread after the Lord had given thanks"), connecting the feeding to the language of thanksgiving that characterized early communal meals. The author presents Jesus as the host of the meal, the one who provides and distributes. Unlike the Synoptic accounts, where Jesus gives the bread to the disciples to distribute, this author states that Jesus himself distributed (diedoken) to those seated. The emphasis is on Jesus as the direct provider.

**"As much as they wanted" (hoson ethelon).** The provision is not merely adequate but abundant. This echoes the manna provision: "They gathered it morning by morning, every man as much as he should eat" (Exodus 16:21). It also echoes the prophetic vision of messianic abundance: Ezekiel 34:29, "I will establish for them a renowned planting place, and they will not again be victims of famine in the land." The phrase establishes that no one in the multitude went unsatisfied.

**"Gather up the leftover fragments so that nothing will be lost" (synagagete ta perisseusanta klasmata, hina me ti apoletai).** Jesus's instruction uses language that resonates beyond the immediate scene. The verb apollymi ("to be lost, to perish") is the same word used in 3:16 ("shall not perish") and will appear throughout this chapter in connection with eternal life and its opposite. In the immediate context, it refers to bread fragments. But the author's ear for double meaning is well established. The concern that "nothing be lost" (me ti apoletai) will reappear in verse 39: "This is the will of him who sent me, that of all that he has given me I lose nothing" (hina pan ho dedoken moi me apolesoautou). The same verb, the same concern. The gathering of fragments so that nothing is wasted foreshadows Jesus's stated mission to lose nothing of what the Father has given him.

**"Twelve baskets" (dodeka kophinous).** The number twelve corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel. Whether the author intends this symbolism explicitly, the number would carry this resonance for any first-century Jewish reader. The provision is sufficient for all Israel, with abundance remaining.

## Verses 14-15: The Crowd's Response -- The Prophet and the Attempt to Make Him King

"Therefore when the people saw the sign which he had performed, they said, 'This is truly the Prophet who is to come into the world.' So Jesus, perceiving that they were intending to come and take him by force to make him king, withdrew again to the mountain by himself alone."

**"The Prophet who is to come into the world" (ho prophetes ho erchomenos eis ton kosmon).** The crowd's identification draws on Deuteronomy 18:15-18, where Moses tells Israel: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers. You shall listen to him." This was the same text that shaped Samaritan messianic expectation (the Taheb, discussed in the analysis of 4:25). For the Galilean crowd at Passover time, the connection is straightforward: Moses provided bread from heaven; this man has provided bread in the wilderness; therefore he must be the prophet like Moses. The logic is valid as far as it goes -- Jesus is indeed the prophet like Moses -- but the crowd's understanding of what that means is fatally incomplete, as the next verse reveals.

**"Take him by force to make him king" (harpazein auton hina poiesosin basilea).** The verb harpazein ("to seize, snatch, take by force") indicates that this was not an invitation but an intended compulsion. The crowd wants to impose kingship on Jesus according to their own understanding of what the prophet-like-Moses should do: lead Israel as a political-military ruler, as Moses led them out of Egypt. The Passover context intensifies this: Passover commemorated liberation from foreign rule. A prophet who can feed multitudes in the wilderness -- as Moses did -- can surely lead a revolt against Rome. The crowd sees in the sign a confirmation of their nationalistic hopes.

Jesus's withdrawal is immediate and total. He goes "to the mountain by himself alone" (eis to oros autos monos). The author emphasizes his solitude: autos monos ("he himself alone"). Jesus refuses a kingship defined by human political expectation. He has not come to repeat the exodus as a political liberation. The irony deepens as the document progresses: Jesus is indeed a king (18:36-37), but his kingdom is "not of this world." The crowd wants the right person for the wrong office. They recognize the sign but misread what it signifies.

## Verses 16-21: Jesus on the Sea

"Now when evening came, his disciples went down to the sea, and after getting into a boat, they started to cross the sea to Capernaum. It had already become dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. The sea began to be stirred up because a strong wind was blowing. Then, when they had rowed about three or four miles, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and drawing near to the boat; and they were frightened. But he said to them, 'I am; do not be afraid.' So they were willing to receive him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going."

**The exodus pattern continues.** The feeding of the multitude corresponded to the manna in the wilderness. Now the crossing of the sea corresponds to Israel's crossing of the Red Sea. The elements align: darkness, water, wind, fear, divine intervention, safe arrival. The entire first half of chapter 6 recapitulates the exodus in compressed form: bread in the wilderness, then passage through the sea, with the divine presence revealed in both.

**"It had already become dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them" (skotia ede egegonei kai oupo elelythei pros autous ho Iesous).** The author notes the darkness before noting Jesus's absence. In this document, darkness is never merely atmospheric. It is the condition of being without the light (1:5, 3:19, 8:12, 12:35, 12:46). The disciples are in darkness because the light has not yet come to them. The contrast with the next scene -- Jesus appearing on the water and identifying himself -- follows the pattern of Genesis 1: darkness, then the word, then light.

**"They saw Jesus walking on the sea" (theorousin ton Iesoun peripatounta epi tes thalasses).** The LXX background for walking on the sea belongs to God alone. Job 9:8 (LXX): God "alone stretched out the heavens and walks on the sea (peripaton epi thalasses) as on firm ground." Psalm 77:19 (LXX 76:20): "Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters, yet your footprints were unseen." Isaiah 43:16: "Thus says the LORD, who makes a way through the sea and a path through the mighty waters." Habakkuk 3:15: "You trampled the sea with your horses." These texts describe God's sovereign dominion over the chaotic waters -- the sea being, in Israel's cosmology, the realm of chaos and danger over which only God exercises mastery. A first-century reader familiar with these texts would hear the scene as a theophany: the one walking on the water is doing what only God does.

**"I am; do not be afraid" (ego eimi; me phobeisthe).** The phrase ego eimi ("I am") in this context functions as more than simple self-identification ("It's me"). The combination of ego eimi with "do not be afraid" echoes the divine reassurance pattern in Isaiah. Isaiah 41:10: "Do not fear, for I am with you (ego eimi meta sou)." Isaiah 43:1-2: "Do not fear, for I have redeemed you... when you pass through the waters, I will be with you." Isaiah 43:5: "Do not fear, for I am with you (ego eimi meta sou)." Isaiah 44:2: "Do not fear, O Jacob my servant." The pattern is consistent: God identifies himself ("I am"), addresses the fear ("do not be afraid"), and the context is deliverance through threatening waters. Jesus walks on the sea, speaks the divine self-identification, and commands the cessation of fear. The author presents this as a theophany on water, consistent with the prologue's declaration that the Word was God (1:1).

**"Immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going."** The sudden arrival at the destination suggests a miraculous compression of the remaining journey. The author does not elaborate or explain. The effect is to complete the exodus sequence: the people have been fed, the sea has been crossed, and the divine presence has been revealed. What follows in the discourse is the interpretation of these signs -- what they mean and what they demand.

## Verses 22-27: The Crowd Seeks Jesus -- Perishable and Imperishable Food

"The next day the crowd that stood on the other side of the sea saw that there was no other small boat there, except one, and that Jesus had not entered with his disciples into the boat, but that his disciples had gone away alone. There came other small boats from Tiberias near to the place where they ate the bread after the Lord had given thanks. So when the crowd saw that Jesus was not there, nor his disciples, they themselves got into the small boats, and came to Capernaum seeking Jesus. When they found him on the other side of the sea, they said to him, 'Rabbi, when did you get here?' Jesus answered them and said, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, you seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled. Do not work for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you, for on him the Father, God, has set his seal.'"

**"You seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled."** Jesus's diagnosis of the crowd's motive is sharp. He draws a distinction between seeing signs (semeia) and eating bread. A sign, in this author's vocabulary, is not merely a miracle but a visible act that points beyond itself to Jesus's identity and mission (2:11, 4:54, 20:30-31). The crowd saw the miracle but did not see the sign -- they registered the food but not what the food signified. They are pursuing Jesus as a bread-provider, not as the one the bread reveals. This echoes the category of inadequate sign-faith established in 2:23-25 and addressed in 4:48 ("Unless you people see signs and wonders, you will not believe"). The crowd is even further from understanding than those passages described: they did not even see the sign as a sign; they saw it only as a meal.

**"Do not work for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to eternal life" (me ergazesthe ten brosin ten apollymenen alla ten brosin ten menousan eis zoen aionion).** Two foods are contrasted. The first "perishes" (apollymenen -- the same root as apollymi, "to be lost, to perish," used in v. 12 for the fragments and in 3:16 for those who do not believe). The second "endures" (menousan -- from meno, "to remain, abide," the verb that pervades this document: the Spirit remaining on Jesus in 1:33, the wrath remaining on the disobedient in 3:36, the mutual abiding of Jesus and the believer in 15:4-10). The contrast between perishing food and enduring food echoes the manna itself: the manna spoiled if kept overnight (Exodus 16:19-20) -- it was, by design, food that perished. Jesus points to food that does not perish, that endures into the age to come.

**"Which the Son of Man will give to you."** The provider of this imperishable food is "the Son of Man" -- the title that in this document connects to Daniel 7:13-14 (the figure who receives dominion from the Ancient of Days) and to the one who descended from heaven (3:13). The Son of Man gives this food; it is not earned or produced by human effort, despite the verb "work for" (ergazesthe). The tension between "working for" food and "receiving" it as a gift from the Son of Man will be explored in the next exchange.

**"For on him the Father, God, has set his seal" (touton gar ho pater esphragisen ho theos).** The verb sphragizo ("to seal") indicates authentication, certification, and commissioning. In the ancient world, a seal marked ownership, guaranteed authenticity, and authorized the bearer to act on behalf of the one who applied the seal. God has sealed the Son of Man -- marked him, authorized him, certified him as the genuine provider of the food that endures to eternal life. The crowd should recognize this seal in the signs they have witnessed, but they have looked at the signs and seen only bread.

## Verses 28-34: The Work of God, the Bread from Heaven

"Therefore they said to him, 'What shall we do, so that we may work the works of God?' Jesus answered and said to them, 'This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.' So they said to him, 'What then do you do for a sign, so that we may see, and believe you? What work do you perform? Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, "He gave them bread out of heaven to eat."' Jesus then said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, it is not Moses who has given you the bread out of heaven, but it is my Father who gives you the true bread out of heaven. For the bread of God is that which comes down out of heaven, and gives life to the world.'"

**"What shall we do, so that we may work the works of God?" (ti poiomen hina ergazometha ta erga tou theou).** The crowd takes Jesus's instruction to "work for" imperishable food (v. 27) as a call to action. Their question assumes that the "food which endures" is obtained through human effort -- through performing the right works. This is the natural framework for a first-century Jew: faithfulness to God meant keeping the commandments, performing the prescribed works. The crowd's question is sincere.

**"This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent" (touto estin to ergon tou theou, hina pisteuete eis hon apesteilen ekeinos).** Jesus's answer collapses the plural "works" into a single "work" and redefines what that work is. The "work of God" is not a series of human performances but a single, continuous response: believing (pisteuete, present tense subjunctive -- ongoing belief) in the one God has sent. The phrase "the work of God" (to ergon tou theou) is ambiguous: it could mean "the work that God requires" or "the work that God himself accomplishes." Both meanings may be active: believing is what God requires, and genuine believing is itself something God enables (as verses 44-45 will argue). The present tense of pisteuete indicates that this is not a one-time act but an ongoing orientation.

**"What then do you do for a sign, so that we may see, and believe you?"** The audacity of this question is staggering. They have just eaten bread that Jesus multiplied for five thousand people. Now they ask for a sign. The question reveals that the feeding, for all its miraculous scale, has already faded as evidence. They want something more, something ongoing, something to match the precedent they are about to cite.

**"Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, 'He gave them bread out of heaven to eat.'"** The crowd raises the standard by which they will measure Jesus: Moses. Their fathers received manna -- bread from heaven -- not once but for forty years. One meal for five thousand is impressive, but Moses (so they argue) provided bread from heaven for the entire nation, every day, for a generation. The scripture they quote is a composite from Psalm 78:24 (LXX 77:24: "He rained down manna upon them to eat, and gave them the bread of heaven [arton ouranou]"), with echoes of Exodus 16:4 ("Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you"), Exodus 16:15 ("This is the bread which the LORD has given you to eat"), Nehemiah 9:15 ("You gave them bread from heaven for their hunger"), and Wisdom of Solomon 16:20 ("You gave your people the food of angels, and without their toil you supplied them from heaven with bread ready to eat").

The crowd's argument has a logic: if Jesus is the prophet like Moses, he should do what Moses did, and what Moses did was provide bread from heaven continuously. One miraculous meal is not enough. The demand reveals their transactional understanding: they want an ongoing, reliable food supply from a political-messianic leader. This is the bread-king they tried to make him in verse 15.

**"It is not Moses who has given you the bread out of heaven, but it is my Father who gives you the true bread out of heaven."** Jesus corrects three errors in their citation:

1. **The agent.** It was not Moses who gave the bread. Moses was the mediator, not the source. God gave the manna. The crowd attributed to Moses what belonged to God -- precisely the mistake that will lead to the grumbling later in the discourse. The correction recalls the end of chapter 5: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me" -- Moses himself pointed beyond himself to God's action.

2. **The tense.** Jesus shifts from the past ("has given," dedoken) to the present ("gives," didosin). The true bread from heaven is not a past event in the wilderness but a present reality: "my Father gives you the true bread out of heaven." The giving is happening now.

3. **The nature.** The manna was not "the true bread out of heaven." The adjective alethinos ("true, genuine, real") -- the same word used in 1:9 for "the true light" and in 4:23 for "the true worshipers" -- identifies the reality to which the manna pointed. The manna was a type, a shadow, a preliminary provision. The true bread is now being offered. The pattern is the same as 1:17: "The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ." Moses mediated what was preparatory; Jesus brings what is final.

**"The bread of God is that which comes down out of heaven, and gives life to the world" (ho artos tou theou estin ho katabainon ek tou ouranou kai zoen didous to kosmo).** The bread of God is described with two participles: "the one coming down" (ho katabainon, present tense -- continually descending) and "giving life" (zoen didous) to the world. The present tense of katabainon indicates not a single past descent but an ongoing coming-down. The scope is "the world" (to kosmo) -- not Israel alone, not the crowd alone, but the entire created human order. This echoes 3:16-17: God loved the world and sent the Son so that the world might be saved. The manna fed Israel in the wilderness; the bread of God gives life to the world.

## Verses 35-40: "I Am the Bread of Life" -- The First Declaration

"Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; he who comes to me will not hunger, and he who believes in me will never thirst. But I said to you that you have seen me, and yet do not believe. All that the Father gives me will come to me, and the one who comes to me I will certainly not cast out. For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me. This is the will of him who sent me, that of all that he has given me I lose nothing, but raise it up on the last day. For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who beholds the Son and believes in him will have eternal life, and I myself will raise him up on the last day.'"

**"I am the bread of life" (ego eimi ho artos tes zoes).** This is the first of the seven "I am" statements with predicates in this document (6:35, 8:12, 10:7, 10:11, 11:25, 14:6, 15:1). The ego eimi formula, as noted in the analysis of 4:26, carries the weight of the divine self-identification from Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 41:4, 43:10. But the predicate -- "the bread of life" -- identifies Jesus not as a provider of bread (which the crowd wanted) but as the bread itself. The crowd asked for bread like the manna; Jesus offers himself. The shift is total: from what Jesus does to who Jesus is. The bread of life is not a product Jesus distributes but the person Jesus is. To receive this bread is to receive him.

**"He who comes to me will not hunger, and he who believes in me will never thirst."** The two clauses are parallel: coming to Jesus and believing in Jesus are equivalent actions described through different metaphors. Coming = believing. The promise attached is absolute: the person who comes/believes will not hunger, will never thirst. The language echoes Isaiah 49:10 ("They will not hunger or thirst, neither will the scorching heat or sun strike them; for he who has compassion on them will lead them") and Isaiah 55:1-2 ("Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come, buy and eat... Why do you spend money for what is not bread, and your wages for what does not satisfy?"). The promise is not merely about physical sustenance but about the satisfaction of the deepest human need -- the need that no earthly provision can permanently address. The manna satisfied hunger but only until the next morning. Jesus satisfies permanently.

**"You have seen me, and yet do not believe."** The diagnosis repeats what was said in verses 26-27: the crowd has seen but has not believed. Seeing, in this document, is not sufficient for believing. The crowd has seen the feeding, seen Jesus himself, and still does not believe. The problem is not lack of evidence but lack of response to the evidence.

**"All that the Father gives me will come to me" (pan ho didosin moi ho pater pros eme hexei).** This statement requires careful attention to its grammar and its scriptural background.

The subject is pan ho -- "all that," neuter singular. The neuter singular pan ("all, everything") treats the group as a collective whole, a single entity. The Father gives a collective to Jesus, and this collective will come to him. The word didosin ("gives") is present tense -- the giving is ongoing, not a single past act.

The language of God "giving" a people draws on Israel's covenant history. God "gave" Israel the land (Deuteronomy 1:8, 1:25, 26:9). God "gave" Israel the Torah (Exodus 24:12, Deuteronomy 5:22). God "gave" the firstborn of Israel to the Levites (Numbers 3:9, 8:19). The concept of God assigning or entrusting a people to a leader is deeply rooted: God gave Israel into Moses's charge, gave the land to Joshua's care, gave the flock to the shepherds (Ezekiel 34). What the Father "gives" Jesus is a people -- those whom the Father is drawing to the Son through the means described in verse 45.

**"And the one who comes to me I will certainly not cast out" (kai ton erchomenon pros eme ou me ekbalo exo).** The pronoun shifts from neuter singular (pan ho, "all that") to masculine singular (ton erchomenon, "the one who comes"). The author moves from the collective to the individual. The collective that the Father gives will come; the individual who comes will not be cast out. The double negative ou me ("certainly not, by no means") is the strongest form of negation in Greek. The verb ekbalo exo ("cast out, throw outside") implies expulsion -- being driven from the inside to the outside. Jesus guarantees that no individual who comes to him will be expelled by him. The guarantee concerns Jesus's own action: he will not be the one to cast out. The statement addresses the fear that the one who comes might be rejected upon arrival. Jesus assures: I will not reject you.

**"For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me."** This grounds the non-rejection promise in Jesus's mission. He has not come to exercise independent judgment about who to accept and who to refuse. He has come to do the will of the one who sent him. The sending-and-obedience framework (established in 5:19, 5:30, and running through the entire document) means that Jesus's reception of those who come is not arbitrary but mandated by the Father.

**"This is the will of him who sent me, that of all that he has given me I lose nothing, but raise it up on the last day" (hina pan ho dedoken moi me apoleso ex autou alla anasteso auto te eschate hemera).** The Father's will is now stated with precision. The pronoun pan ho ("all that") is again neuter singular -- the collective. The verb apoleso ("I should lose") is from apollymi, the same word used for the bread fragments in verse 12 ("that nothing will be lost") and for perishing in 3:16. The concern that "nothing be lost" from the fragments now becomes the concern that "nothing be lost" from the people the Father has given. The verb anasteso ("I will raise up") and the phrase "on the last day" (te eschate hemera) introduce the eschatological promise of bodily resurrection. The neuter auto ("it") refers to the collective. The will of the Father is that the entire collective given to Jesus be preserved through resurrection.

**"For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who beholds the Son and believes in him will have eternal life, and I myself will raise him up on the last day" (hina pas ho theoron ton huion kai pisteuon eis auton eche zoen aionion, kai anasteso auton ego te eschate hemera).** Verse 40 restates the Father's will, but now the pronouns shift to the individual level. The neuter collective (pan ho, "all that") of verse 39 becomes the masculine individual (pas ho, "everyone who") of verse 40. The condition is stated: "beholding the Son and believing in him." Both participles -- theoron ("beholding, contemplating") and pisteuon ("believing") -- are present tense, indicating continuous action. The promise is parallel to verse 39: eternal life now, resurrection at the last day. But where verse 39 spoke of the collective given by the Father, verse 40 speaks of the individual who sees and believes.

The relationship between these two verses is significant. Verse 39 describes the Father's gift of a collective to Jesus. Verse 40 describes the individual's response -- beholding and believing. The two are not in tension but are two perspectives on the same reality. The Father's giving and the individual's believing are not alternatives; they are the divine and human dimensions of the same event. The Father gives; the individual beholds and believes. The collective is constituted by individuals who behold and believe. The "all that the Father gives" is not a fixed group determined apart from human response; it is the company of those whom the Father draws (v. 44) and who respond by beholding and believing (v. 40). The Father's initiative and the human response are both real, both necessary, and neither eliminates the other.

## Verses 41-51: The Grumbling, the Father's Drawing, and the Living Bread

"Therefore the Jews were grumbling about him, because he said, 'I am the bread that came down out of heaven.' They were saying, 'Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How does he now say, "I have come down out of heaven"?' Jesus answered and said to them, 'Do not grumble among yourselves. No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him; and I will raise him up on the last day. It is written in the prophets, "And they shall all be taught of God." Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father, comes to me. Not that anyone has seen the Father, except the one who is from God; he has seen the Father. Truly, truly, I say to you, he who believes has eternal life. I am the bread of life. Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died. This is the bread which comes down out of heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die. I am the living bread that came down out of heaven; if anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever; and the bread also which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.'"

**"The Jews were grumbling" (egongyzon oun hoi Ioudaioi).** The verb goggyzo ("to grumble, murmur") is deliberately chosen. In the LXX, this is the characteristic verb for Israel's grumbling against Moses in the wilderness. Exodus 16:2 (LXX): "The whole congregation of the sons of Israel grumbled (diegongyzon) against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness." Exodus 16:7: "He has heard your grumblings (ton goggysmon hymon) which you grumble (goggyzete) against the LORD." Exodus 16:8: "Your grumblings are not against us but against the LORD." Exodus 16:9: "He has heard your grumblings (goggysmon)." Exodus 16:12: "I have heard the grumblings (ton goggysmon) of the sons of Israel." Numbers 14:27: "How long shall this evil congregation grumble (goggyzousin) against me? I have heard the complaints (goggysmon) of the sons of Israel." Numbers 14:29: "In this wilderness your corpses shall fall, all your numbered ones... who have grumbled (diegongysan) against me."

The parallel is exact and devastating. In the wilderness, Israel grumbled against Moses when God provided manna -- bread from heaven. Now, "the Jews" grumble against Jesus when he claims to be the true bread from heaven. The wilderness generation's grumbling was a paradigmatic act of unbelief that resulted in their death in the wilderness (Numbers 14:29, Psalm 95:10-11). The author, by using this verb, places the crowd's response in the same category: they are re-enacting the wilderness rebellion. They are grumbling against the very provision God has sent.

**"Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?"** The objection is based on familiarity. They know his family, his origins, his earthly parentage. How can someone whose parents they know claim to have "come down from heaven"? The objection parallels that of 1:46 ("Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?") and anticipates 7:27, 41-42, where knowledge of Jesus's Galilean origin becomes a stumbling block. The irony, for the reader who has heard the prologue, is that the crowd's knowledge of Jesus's earthly family is precisely the veil that prevents them from seeing his heavenly origin. They know "the son of Joseph" but cannot perceive the one who was "in the beginning with God" (1:1-2).

**"No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (ean me ho pater ho pempsas me helkyse auton).** The verb helkyo (or helko) means "to draw, drag, pull." Its uses in this document and in the LXX require careful examination:

In this document, the verb appears again in 12:32: "And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (pantes helkyso pros emauton). The author's own usage in 12:32 defines the scope of the drawing: when Jesus is lifted up, he will draw all people (pantas) -- not a limited group but all. The mechanism of the drawing in 12:32 is the crucifixion itself: being "lifted up" is the means by which the drawing occurs, as the author's gloss in 12:33 makes clear ("He was saying this to indicate the kind of death by which he was about to die"). The verb also appears in 18:10 (Peter drew a sword) and 21:6, 11 (drawing a net full of fish). In the net passages, the drawing requires effort -- the net is heavy -- but the fish are not compelled individually; the net gathers them.

In the LXX, the critical use is Jeremiah 38:3 (31:3 in Hebrew): "The LORD appeared to him from afar: 'I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore I have drawn you with lovingkindness (heilkysa se)." God's drawing of Israel is through lovingkindness -- through the persistent, faithful love that characterized God's covenant relationship with his people. The drawing is not mechanical compulsion but the attractive power of covenant love.

The next verse (v. 45) defines the mechanism of the Father's drawing: "It is written in the prophets, 'And they shall all be taught of God.' Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father, comes to me." The Father draws by teaching. The drawing is not an irresistible force applied to passive objects but the instructive, revelatory action of God that creates the possibility of coming to Jesus. Those who hear and learn from the Father's teaching come. Those who do not hear and learn -- because they resist, because they grumble, because they prefer their own understanding -- do not come. The drawing is real and necessary (no one can come without it), but it operates through teaching, not through coercion.

**"It is written in the prophets, 'And they shall all be taught of God'" (esontai pantes didaktoi theou).** The quotation is from Isaiah 54:13 (LXX): "And I will make all your sons taught of God (didaktous theou), and your children shall be in great peace." The context in Isaiah 54 is the restoration of Israel after exile. God promises to rebuild Jerusalem with precious stones, to establish her in righteousness, and to make her children "taught of God." The promise is part of the new covenant vision -- the same trajectory as Jeremiah 31:33-34 ("I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts... they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest"). The "all" (pantes) in both Isaiah 54:13 and Jesus's quotation indicates that the teaching is not restricted to an elite but is offered broadly. All will be taught; those who respond to the teaching come to Jesus.

**"Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father, comes to me" (pas ho akousas para tou patros kai mathon erchetai pros eme).** The two participles -- akousas ("having heard") and mathon ("having learned") -- are aorist, indicating completed action. The person who has heard and learned from the Father comes (present tense, erchetai) to Jesus. The hearing and learning precede the coming. This sequence clarifies the "drawing" of verse 44: the Father draws by teaching, and those who have genuinely heard and learned from that teaching come to Jesus. The Father's initiative is the teaching; the human response is the hearing, learning, and coming.

The phrase "from the Father" (para tou patros) indicates that the teaching originates with the Father, not that people see the Father directly. Jesus immediately clarifies in verse 46: "Not that anyone has seen the Father, except the one who is from God; he has seen the Father." No one has direct access to the Father's person; the teaching comes through the means the Father provides -- the scriptures (5:39), the works (5:36), the testimony of the prophets (Isaiah 54:13), and supremely through the Son who alone has seen the Father (1:18).

**"Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died" (hoi pateres hymon ephagon to manna en te eremo kai apethanon).** Jesus returns to the crowd's own argument from verse 31. They cited the manna as the standard by which to judge Jesus. He now turns their standard against them: the manna-eaters died. The manna sustained physical life temporarily but did not prevent death. The wilderness generation that ate the manna died in the wilderness -- many of them under God's judgment for their grumbling and unbelief (Numbers 14:29, 26:64-65). The manna could not give what Jesus offers: life that overcomes death.

**"I am the living bread that came down out of heaven; if anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever" (ego eimi ho artos ho zon ho ek tou ouranou katabas; ean tis phage ek toutou tou artou zesei eis ton aiona).** Jesus adds a new element: this bread is "living" (ho zon) -- it carries life within itself, just as the Word carried life within himself (1:4, "In him was life"). The conditional "if anyone eats" (ean tis phage) opens the promise to every person without restriction, while making clear that eating is required. The promise -- "will live forever" (zesei eis ton aiona) -- goes beyond what the manna could accomplish. The manna sustained life for a day; this bread sustains life for the age.

**"The bread also which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (kai ho artos de hon ego doso he sarx mou estin hyper tes tou kosmou zoes).** This is the pivotal statement of the discourse. The bread Jesus will give is identified as his flesh (sarx). The verb "will give" (doso) is future tense -- the giving has not yet occurred. The preposition hyper ("for, on behalf of") indicates substitutionary or representative action: the flesh is given for the benefit of, for the sake of, the world's life. The word sarx ("flesh") is the same word used in the prologue: "the Word became flesh" (1:14). The flesh that the Word took on in the incarnation is the flesh that will be given -- given in death -- for the life of the world.

The Passover background now comes into sharp focus. At Passover, a lamb was slaughtered and its flesh was eaten. The lamb's blood was applied to the doorposts as protection from death (Exodus 12:7, 13). The lamb's flesh was roasted and consumed as part of the deliverance meal (Exodus 12:8-11). Jesus, identified as "the Lamb of God" (1:29), now declares that his flesh is the bread that must be eaten for the life of the world. The Passover lamb's flesh sustained the Israelites on the night of deliverance; Jesus's flesh -- given in death -- gives life to the world.

## Verses 52-59: Eating Flesh and Drinking Blood

"Then the Jews began to argue with one another, saying, 'How can this man give us his flesh to eat?' So Jesus said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in yourselves. He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day. For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink. He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him. As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so he who eats me, he also will live because of me. This is the bread which came down out of heaven; not as the fathers ate and died; he who eats this bread will live forever.' These things he said in the synagogue, as he taught in Capernaum."

**"How can this man give us his flesh to eat?"** The crowd's response follows the same pattern of literal misunderstanding seen with Nicodemus ("How can a man be born when he is old?" 3:4), the Samaritan woman ("Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty nor come all the way here to draw," 4:15), and the disciples about food ("No one brought him anything to eat, did he?" 4:33). The pattern is consistent: Jesus speaks on a level the hearers cannot immediately access, and their literalism creates the occasion for deeper teaching. But here, unlike the previous instances, Jesus does not soften or explain away the language. He intensifies it.

**"Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in yourselves" (ean me phagete ten sarka tou huiou tou anthropou kai piete autou to haima, ouk echete zoen en heautois).** The language is deliberately shocking, particularly to a Jewish audience. The Torah explicitly and repeatedly prohibits the consumption of blood. Leviticus 17:10-14: "If anyone of the house of Israel or of the strangers who sojourn among them eats any blood, I will set my face against that person who eats blood and will cut him off from among his people. For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your souls; for it is the blood by reason of the life that makes atonement... You shall not eat the blood of any flesh." Genesis 9:4: "Only you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood." Deuteronomy 12:23: "Only be sure not to eat the blood, for the blood is the life, and you shall not eat the life with the flesh." The prohibition against consuming blood was one of the most fundamental dietary laws, observed universally by Jews. Jesus's command to "drink his blood" would be heard as a command to violate one of the Torah's most basic prohibitions.

Yet the very reason the Torah gives for the blood prohibition illuminates what Jesus is claiming. Leviticus 17:11: "The life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your souls." Blood carries life. Blood makes atonement. The blood is given by God for atonement. Jesus is claiming that his blood -- his life poured out in death -- is given by God for the life of the world, just as the sacrificial blood was given on the altar for the atonement of Israel. To "drink his blood" is to receive the life that his death makes available. The language is not about literal consumption but about participation in the life released through his death.

The Passover framework confirms this. On the night of the original Passover, the lamb's blood was the instrument of deliverance: those who applied the blood were spared; those who did not were struck (Exodus 12:12-13). The lamb's flesh was eaten as the deliverance meal. Now Jesus presents his flesh and blood as the ultimate Passover provision: his flesh is eaten, his blood is received, and the result is not one night's deliverance from Egypt but eternal life and resurrection.

**"He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day."** The verb "eats" here is trogo (v. 54), a different verb from the earlier phago (v. 53). Trogo is more vivid -- it means "to gnaw, chew, munch" -- and it intensifies the concreteness of the language rather than spiritualizing it. The author refuses to let the hearer escape into abstraction. The promise is the same as in verses 39-40: eternal life as present possession, resurrection on the last day as future promise. The condition is the same in form: ongoing participation. The present-tense participle ho trogon ("the one eating") describes a continuous activity, not a single past event.

**"For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink" (he gar sarx mou alethes estin brosis, kai to haima mou alethes estin posis).** The adjective alethes ("true, genuine") or alethinos (in some manuscripts) is the same language used to distinguish the true bread from the manna (v. 32), the true light from all other lights (1:9), the true worshipers from all other worshipers (4:23). Jesus's flesh is the genuine food -- the food that all other food pointed toward. His blood is the genuine drink. The manna was real food that sustained real bodies, but it was not the true food: it could not prevent death. Jesus's flesh and blood are the true food and drink because they accomplish what food and drink are ultimately for: sustaining life, permanently.

**"He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him" (ho trogon mou ten sarka kai pinon mou to haima en emoi menei kago en auto).** The verb meno ("to abide, remain, dwell") now describes the result of eating and drinking: mutual indwelling. The one who eats and drinks abides in Jesus, and Jesus abides in that person. This is the language that will reach its fullest development in chapter 15 (the vine and branches discourse): "Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it abides in the vine, neither can you unless you abide in me" (15:4). The eating and drinking that this discourse describes is the means by which the mutual abiding is established and sustained. The abiding is not automatic or irreversible in chapter 15 -- branches that do not abide are removed and wither (15:2, 6). Here in chapter 6, the abiding is the condition of the one who is currently eating and drinking -- the present-tense participles describe what is happening now, not a guaranteed future state.

**"As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so he who eats me, he also will live because of me."** Jesus traces the chain of life: the Father is the source of life ("the living Father," ho zon pater -- God is the living God throughout Israel's scriptures: Joshua 3:10, 1 Samuel 17:26, Psalm 42:2, Daniel 6:20, 26); the Father sent the Son, and the Son lives because of the Father; the one who eats Jesus lives because of Jesus. The life flows from Father to Son to the one who participates in the Son. This is not independent life but derived, dependent, relational life -- the same structure described in the prologue ("In him was life," 1:4) and in the chapter 5 discourse ("As the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself," 5:26).

**"These things he said in the synagogue, as he taught in Capernaum."** The author locates the discourse in a specific setting. This is not a private conversation but public teaching in a synagogue. The synagogue setting means the audience includes not only the crowd that followed from the feeding but also the local Jewish community of Capernaum. The teaching is on record, in a public place, before witnesses.

## Verses 60-66: Many Disciples Withdraw

"Therefore many of his disciples, when they heard this, said, 'This is a difficult statement; who can listen to it?' But Jesus, conscious that his disciples grumbled at this, said to them, 'Does this cause you to stumble? What then if you see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before? It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh profits nothing; the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life. But there are some of you who do not believe.' For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were who did not believe, and who it was that would betray him. And he was saying, 'For this reason I have said to you, that no one can come to me unless it has been granted him from the Father.' As a result of this many of his disciples withdrew and were not walking with him anymore."

**"Many of his disciples" (polloi oun ek ton matheton autou).** The word "disciples" (mathetai) here does not refer to the Twelve alone. The term is broader -- it includes the wider circle of those who had been following Jesus and learning from him. The shock of the flesh-and-blood language has produced offense among Jesus's own followers, not merely among hostile outsiders.

**"This is a difficult statement; who can listen to it?" (skleros estin ho logos houtos; tis dynatai autou akouein).** The word skleros means "hard, harsh, rough" -- not intellectually difficult but offensive, repellent, hard to accept. The same word appears in the LXX for Pharaoh's "hard heart" (Exodus 7:3, skleruno, related) and for the "stiff-necked" people (Exodus 33:3, 5, sklerotrachelon). The disciples' objection is not that they cannot understand the teaching but that they find it intolerable.

**"Does this cause you to stumble?" (touto hymas skandalizei).** The verb skandalizo means "to cause to stumble, to give offense, to trap." A skandalon in the LXX is a snare or trap (Psalm 140:5 LXX 139:6, Psalm 141:9 LXX 140:9, Joshua 23:13). Jesus asks whether his teaching has become a trap that catches them, a stumbling stone over which they fall. The verb will appear again in 16:1: "These things I have spoken to you so that you may not be caused to stumble (skandalisthete)."

**"What then if you see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?"** Jesus does not resolve the scandal but deepens it. If the flesh-and-blood language offends them now, what will they make of the Son of Man ascending -- returning to heaven, to the place he occupied before the incarnation (1:1-2, 3:13)? The statement could be read two ways: (1) The ascension will resolve the scandal by demonstrating that the flesh-and-blood language was never about literal cannibalism but about the self-offering of the one who came from heaven and returns to heaven; or (2) The ascension will compound the scandal by adding another impossible claim to the one they already cannot accept. Both readings may be operative simultaneously: the ascension clarifies and escalates at the same time.

**"It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh profits nothing; the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life" (to pneuma estin to zoopoioun, he sarx ouk ophelei ouden; ta rhemata ha ego lelaleka hymin pneuma estin kai zoe estin).** This verse is the interpretive key to the entire discourse. Jesus is not retracting the flesh-and-blood language of the preceding verses but explaining how it functions. The Spirit is the life-giver (zoopoioun -- "the one who makes alive," from zoopoieo, used in the LXX for God's life-giving action: Nehemiah 9:6, "You give life [zoopoieis] to all of them"; 2 Kings 5:7, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive [zoopoiesai]?"). The flesh by itself -- taken in isolation from the Spirit -- accomplishes nothing. Flesh without Spirit is the category Jesus described in 3:6: "That which is born of the flesh is flesh." The words Jesus has spoken are "spirit and life" -- they participate in the Spirit's life-giving power.

This means the eating and drinking of flesh and blood described in verses 53-56 is accomplished through the Spirit by means of Jesus's words. The words are the vehicle of the Spirit, and the Spirit is the giver of life. To eat Jesus's flesh and drink his blood is to receive his words -- the words he has spoken in this discourse and throughout the document -- by the Spirit's power, and through them to participate in the life released by his death. The flesh-and-blood language points to Jesus's death (the giving of his flesh "for the life of the world," v. 51); the Spirit gives access to the life that death releases; the words are the means by which the Spirit communicates that life to the hearer.

**"There are some of you who do not believe" (eisin ex hymon tines hoi ou pisteuousin).** Jesus identifies the real problem among the offended disciples: not confusion but unbelief. The discourse has been testing the crowd from the beginning -- the same function as the test of Philip in verse 6. The flesh-and-blood language has operated as a filter: those who believe press through the offense to receive the life; those who do not believe withdraw.

**"For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were who did not believe, and who it was that would betray him."** The author's parenthetical links the unbelieving disciples to the specific disciple who will betray Jesus -- Judas. The phrase "from the beginning" (ex arches) indicates that Jesus's knowledge of their unbelief is not retrospective but has been present throughout. This connects to the author's earlier statement that Jesus "knew what was in man" (2:25). The word "betray" (paradidomi, "to hand over") anticipates the passion narrative (13:2, 11, 21; 18:2, 5; 19:11).

**"No one can come to me unless it has been granted him from the Father" (ean me e dedomenon auto ek tou patros).** This restates verse 44 ("unless the Father draws him") in different language. The verb "granted" (dedomenon, perfect passive participle of didomi, "to give") describes something that has been given -- a permission, an enabling, an opening. The perfect tense indicates a completed action with ongoing effect: the granting has occurred and its result persists. The Father's granting is the same as the Father's drawing, and the mechanism is the same as verse 45: hearing and learning from the Father. Those who hear the Father's teaching and learn from it are granted the ability to come; those who stop their ears and refuse to learn are not.

**"As a result of this many of his disciples withdrew and were not walking with him anymore" (ek toutou polloi ek ton matheton autou apelthon eis ta opiso kai ouketi met' autou periepatoun).** The phrase "withdrew" (apelthon eis ta opiso, literally "went away to the things behind, turned back") echoes the language of Israel's apostasy. In the LXX, "turning back" describes Israel's repeated abandonment of God. Isaiah 1:4: "They have turned away backward (apestrepsan eis ta opiso)." Jeremiah 7:24: "They went backward and not forward." Psalm 78:57 (LXX 77:57): "They turned back and acted treacherously like their fathers; they twisted like a deceitful bow." The phrase "were not walking with him anymore" (ouketi met' autou periepatoun) uses the verb peripateo ("to walk"), which in Jewish idiom describes one's way of life and religious commitment (Deuteronomy 5:33, "You shall walk in all the way which the LORD your God has commanded you"; Psalm 1:1, "Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked"). To walk with someone was to follow their teaching and share their life. These disciples ceased walking with Jesus. They had been following; now they stopped. The departure is real, not hypothetical. The author records it as a historical fact.

This is the most direct demonstration in the document that following Jesus can be abandoned. These were mathetai -- disciples, learners, followers. They had heard his teaching, seen his signs, accompanied him. They chose to leave. The text does not describe them as having been false disciples all along, nor does it say they never truly followed. It says they "were not walking with him anymore" -- the imperfect tense (periepatoun) implies that they had been walking with him previously and now stopped.

## Verses 67-71: Peter's Confession and the Identification of Judas

"So Jesus said to the twelve, 'You do not want to go away also, do you?' Simon Peter answered him, 'Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have believed and have come to know that you are the Holy One of God.' Jesus answered them, 'Did I myself not choose you, the twelve, and yet one of you is a devil?' Now he meant Judas the son of Simon Iscariot, for he, one of the twelve, was going to betray him."

**"You do not want to go away also, do you?" (me kai hymeis thelete hypagein).** The particle me introduces a question expecting a negative answer, but the question is genuine: Jesus does not coerce the Twelve into staying. They are free to leave, as the other disciples have just done. The question acknowledges the reality of the departure and extends the choice to the inner circle. There is no compulsion in the invitation to remain.

**"Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (kyrie, pros tina apeleusometha; rhemata zoes aioniou echeis).** Peter's response is not a triumphant confession of theological understanding but a confession of dependency and trust. He does not claim to understand the flesh-and-blood language. He does not explain the discourse. He simply identifies the alternative: there is no one else to go to. The "words of eternal life" (rhemata zoes aioniou) echo verse 63: "the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life." Peter has heard the same words that drove others away, and he has found in them life rather than offense. His response is the model of what the discourse has demanded: continuing to come to Jesus rather than turning back, even when the teaching is hard.

**"We have believed and have come to know" (hemeis pepisteukamen kai egnokamen).** Both verbs are in the perfect tense: pisteuo ("to believe") and ginosko ("to know"). The perfect tense indicates completed action with ongoing result: "We have come to believe and stand in that belief; we have come to know and continue in that knowledge." The order -- believing, then knowing -- is the progression the author advocates: faith leads to understanding, not the reverse. The Samaritans in 4:42 followed the same sequence: they believed because of the woman's word, then heard Jesus for themselves and "knew" (oidamen).

**"You are the Holy One of God" (sy ei ho hagios tou theou).** The title "the Holy One of God" (ho hagios tou theou) is distinctive. It does not appear frequently as a messianic title, but it connects to the language of consecration and setting apart. In the LXX, "holy" (hagios) is applied to God himself as his essential characteristic (Isaiah 6:3, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts"; Psalm 99:3, 5, 9, "He is holy"; Habakkuk 3:3). When applied to persons, it describes those set apart by God for a specific purpose: Aaron is called "the holy one of the LORD" (Psalm 106:16 LXX 105:16). Elisha is called "a holy man of God" (2 Kings 4:9). In the LXX of Judges 13:7, Samson is described as "a Nazirite of God" (hagion theou in some versions). Peter's confession identifies Jesus as the one uniquely consecrated by God, set apart from all others, belonging to God in a way no other person does. The title aligns with the sealing of the Son of Man by the Father (v. 27) and with Jesus's later prayer: "I sanctify myself" (hagiazō emauton, 17:19).

**"Did I myself not choose you, the twelve, and yet one of you is a devil?"** Jesus responds to Peter's confession not with affirmation but with a sobering disclosure. Three elements require attention:

1. **"I chose you" (ego hymas exelexamen).** The verb eklegomai ("to choose, select, elect") is used for Jesus's choosing of the Twelve. The initiative was Jesus's, not theirs. He chose them (cf. 15:16: "You did not choose me, but I chose you"). In the LXX, eklegomai is the verb used for God's election of Israel: Deuteronomy 7:6, "The LORD your God has chosen you (exelexato se) to be a people for his own possession." Deuteronomy 14:2, "The LORD has chosen you (exelexato se) to be a people for his own possession." Isaiah 44:1, "Israel, whom I have chosen (exelexamen)." The same verb that describes God's election of Israel describes Jesus's election of the Twelve. Yet election, in Israel's history, did not guarantee the faithfulness of every individual within the elected group. Israel was chosen, but many within Israel rebelled. The Twelve are chosen, and one of them is "a devil."

2. **"One of you is a devil" (heis ex hymon diabolos estin).** The word diabolos ("slanderer, accuser, devil") is the LXX translation of the Hebrew satan ("adversary"). In 1 Chronicles 21:1 (LXX), satan stands against Israel. In Job 1-2, the satan appears before God as an accuser. In Zechariah 3:1-2, the satan stands at the right hand of the high priest Joshua to accuse him. Jesus does not say Judas is possessed by a devil but that he is a devil -- an adversary, one who will work against Jesus's mission from within the chosen circle. The statement is present tense (estin, "is") -- Judas's adversarial nature is already operative, even before the betrayal occurs.

3. **The juxtaposition of election and betrayal.** Jesus chose the Twelve, and one of them will betray him. The author does not present this as a contradiction or a failure of Jesus's judgment but as something Jesus knew "from the beginning" (v. 64). The choice of Judas was not a mistake. Yet the betrayal is real, and Judas's moral responsibility is not diminished by Jesus's foreknowledge. The text holds both realities without resolving the tension: Jesus chose; Judas will betray. Election does not override the agency of the one elected. This mirrors the pattern of Israel's election throughout the Hebrew scriptures: God chose Israel; Israel repeatedly turned away. The election was real; the turning away was also real.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. **The chapter is structured as a new exodus narrative.** The feeding of the multitude corresponds to the manna (Exodus 16), the walking on the sea corresponds to the crossing of the Red Sea (Exodus 14), and the discourse that follows interprets these signs by claiming that Jesus is the reality to which the exodus provisions pointed. The end of chapter 5 challenged the crowd: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me" (5:46). Chapter 6 tests that claim by presenting Jesus as the one who does what God did through Moses -- providing bread, mastering the sea -- and then declaring himself to be what the manna pointed to: the true bread from heaven. The Moses question runs from start to finish.

2. **The Passover setting controls the discourse's logic.** The nearness of Passover (v. 4) provides the interpretive framework for the flesh-and-blood language. Passover involved the slaughter and eating of the lamb, the application of blood for deliverance from death, and the eating of unleavened bread during the journey to freedom. Jesus's declaration that his flesh must be eaten and his blood must be drunk recasts himself as the Passover provision. The lamb's flesh was eaten; Jesus's flesh is eaten. The lamb's blood protected from death; Jesus's blood gives eternal life. The Passover delivered from slavery in Egypt; Jesus delivers from death itself. Each Passover in this document (2:13, 6:4, 11:55-13:1) moves closer to the event the Passover anticipated: the death of the Lamb of God (1:29).

3. **The discourse systematically distinguishes the manna from the true bread.** The manna came through Moses, but the true bread comes from the Father (v. 32). The manna fed Israel in the wilderness; the true bread gives life to the world (v. 33). Those who ate the manna died (v. 49, 58); those who eat the true bread will live forever (v. 51, 58). The manna was bread about which one could ask "what is it?" (the Hebrew man hu, from which "manna" derives, Exodus 16:15); the true bread identifies itself: "I am the bread of life" (v. 35). The pattern is the same as throughout this document: what came through Moses was real but preparatory; what comes through Jesus is the reality that the preparation anticipated (1:17, 2:6-10, 4:12-14, 5:46-47).

4. **The "drawing" and "giving" language describes God's initiative operating through teaching, not through compulsion.** The Father draws (v. 44), gives (v. 37, 39), and grants (v. 65). The mechanism is defined in verse 45: "They shall all be taught of God. Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father, comes to me." The Father's initiative is the teaching; the human response is the hearing, learning, and coming. Those who hear and learn come; those who resist the teaching do not. The drawing is real and necessary -- "no one can come to me unless the Father draws him" -- but it operates through the attractive, revelatory power of God's self-disclosure, consistent with the pattern described in Jeremiah 31:3 ("I have drawn you with lovingkindness"). The scope of the drawing, as defined by the author's own usage in 12:32, is universal: "I will draw all people to myself."

5. **The relationship between the Father's gift and the individual's response is complementary, not competitive.** Verse 37 describes the Father giving a collective (neuter singular, pan ho) to Jesus: all of this collective will come. Verse 40 describes the individual (masculine singular, pas ho theoron ... kai pisteuon) who beholds and believes: this individual will have eternal life and be raised on the last day. The collective is constituted by the individuals who behold and believe. The Father's giving and the individual's believing are two perspectives on the same event -- the divine side and the human side. Neither cancels the other. The "all that the Father gives" are those who respond to the Father's teaching (v. 45); the individuals who believe are those whom the Father has drawn. The two descriptions are not a paradox to be resolved but a reality with two faces.

6. **The present-tense grammar of believing, eating, and drinking is consistent and deliberate.** Throughout the discourse, the conditions for receiving eternal life are stated in present-tense participles: ho pisteuon ("the one believing," vv. 35, 40, 47), ho trogon ("the one eating," vv. 54, 56, 57, 58), ho erchomenos ("the one coming," v. 35). These describe ongoing, continuous activities, not completed one-time acts. The promises attached -- eternal life, never hungering, never thirsting, being raised on the last day -- are for the person in the state of currently believing, currently eating, currently coming. The author does not address the question abstractly, but the departure of disciples in verse 66 demonstrates concretely that the "walking with Jesus" can cease. Those who depart are no longer in the condition the present-tense participles describe.

7. **The departure of many disciples is the chapter's most striking narrative event.** These are not hostile outsiders or religious authorities. They are mathetai -- people who had been following Jesus, learning from him, walking with him. The flesh-and-blood teaching offended them, and they "went back and no longer walked with him" (v. 66). The author presents this as a real departure from real discipleship, not as the unmasking of people who were never actually following. The verb peripateo ("to walk") in the imperfect tense (periepatoun) implies they had been walking with Jesus and now stopped. Their departure proves that proximity to Jesus, exposure to his teaching, and initial following do not constitute a permanent, irreversible state. The teaching demands ongoing trust, and many chose not to continue.

8. **Peter's confession models the response the discourse demands.** Peter does not claim to understand everything Jesus has said. He does not resolve the scandal of the flesh-and-blood language. He simply identifies the absence of alternatives -- "Lord, to whom shall we go?" -- and affirms that Jesus has "the words of eternal life." His perfect-tense verbs ("we have believed and have come to know") describe a state of ongoing commitment that has been established and persists. The confession is not triumphalistic but dependent: it rests not on Peter's strength of understanding but on the recognition that there is nowhere else to go.

9. **The identification of Judas as "a devil" within the chosen Twelve demonstrates that election does not guarantee faithfulness.** Jesus chose the Twelve (v. 70), and one of them is an adversary. This mirrors the pattern of Israel's history: God chose Israel, yet many within Israel proved faithless. The election of the people did not override the agency of individuals within that people. Judas was chosen, was among the Twelve, had access to everything the other disciples had -- and he will betray. The chapter ends not with the comfort of election but with the warning that even among the chosen, betrayal is possible. The author holds election and human agency together without collapsing either one.

10. **The chapter's argument spirals through repetition and intensification.** The phrase "I will raise him up on the last day" appears four times (vv. 39, 40, 44, 54), each time in a slightly different context but always paired with a condition -- being given by the Father (v. 39), beholding and believing (v. 40), being drawn by the Father (v. 44), eating flesh and drinking blood (v. 54). The repetition drives the promise into the hearer's memory while the varying conditions prevent any single formulation from being isolated and treated as the whole picture. The resurrection promise is for those who believe, who eat, who come, who are drawn -- all descriptions of the same reality from different angles. The author builds a cumulative portrait rather than a single propositional statement.

11. **Verse 63 provides the hermeneutical key to the entire discourse.** "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh profits nothing; the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life." The flesh-and-blood language of the discourse is not about literal consumption. The Spirit is the life-giver; the words Jesus has spoken are the vehicles of that Spirit and that life. To eat Jesus's flesh and drink his blood is to receive, by the Spirit's power, the life made available through Jesus's death. The words of the discourse are themselves "spirit and life" -- they carry the reality they describe. This connects the bread-of-life discourse to the prologue's theology of the Word: the Word who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, in whom was life -- that Word has spoken, and his words are spirit and life. To receive his words by faith is to eat the bread of life.

12. **The chapter as a whole presents faith as ongoing, costly, and subject to abandonment.** The discourse begins with a crowd that follows Jesus because of signs (v. 2) and ends with most of them gone (v. 66). The feeding attracted thousands; the discourse repelled the majority. The bread-of-life claim sorted the crowd, the flesh-and-blood language sorted the disciples, and the identification of Judas sorted even the Twelve. At each stage, the question is the same: will you continue to come to Jesus, or will you turn back? The chapter does not present believing as a single moment that secures an unchangeable status. It presents believing as a continuous response to an increasingly demanding revelation, a response that many begin but not all sustain.
