# Revelation 11 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 10):** Between the sixth and seventh trumpets, just as between the sixth and seventh seals (chapter 7), the author inserted an extended interlude. The pattern is now recognizable: six judgments, then an interpretive pause, then the climactic seventh. In 10:1-11, a "strong angel" descended from heaven wrapped in a cloud, with a rainbow on his head, his face like the sun, and his feet like pillars of fire -- imagery combining the theophany traditions with the description of the risen figure in chapter 1. This angel held an open small scroll (biblaridion, 10:2) and planted one foot on the sea and one on the land, signifying comprehensive authority. He cried out with a loud voice, and seven thunders spoke, but John was told to seal up what the thunders said and not write it (10:4) -- a reversal of the normal prophetic pattern in which God commands the prophet to speak, and an echo of Daniel 12:4, where Daniel was told to "seal up the book until the time of the end." But here the sealing is selective: only the thunders are sealed; the rest of the revelation continues.

The angel then made a solemn oath, swearing by the one "who created the heaven and the things in it, and the earth and the things in it, and the sea and the things in it" (10:6) -- the Creator language of Exodus 20:11 and Psalm 146:6 -- that "there will be no more delay" (chronos ouketi estai, 10:6). The word chronos here means "time" in the sense of elapsed duration, waiting period, or delay. The declaration "no more delay" responds to the martyrs' cry of 6:10 ("How long?") and to the instruction in 6:11 that they should wait "a little while longer" (eti chronon mikron). The delay is expiring. When the seventh angel sounds his trumpet, "the mystery of God is finished" (etelesthē to mystērion tou theou, 10:7), "as he announced to his servants the prophets" (hos euēngelisen tous heautou doulous tous prophētas). The word mystērion in Jewish usage denotes a divine purpose previously hidden but now disclosed; the verb euēngelisen ("announced good news to") indicates that the completion of this mystery was the content of prophetic hope.

John was then commanded to take the small scroll and eat it (10:8-10), replicating Ezekiel's experience in Ezekiel 2:8-3:3, where the prophet ate a scroll of lamentations, mourning, and woe. The scroll was sweet in John's mouth but bitter in his stomach -- the prophetic word is a gift to receive but a burden to deliver. Finally, John was recommissioned: "You must prophesy again concerning many peoples and nations and tongues and kings" (10:11). This recommissioning sets the stage for what immediately follows. The content John was given to prophesy -- the small scroll he ingested -- is now delivered. Chapter 11:1-13 is the content of that scroll, the first installment of John's renewed prophetic commission.

**What the author argues immediately after (Revelation 12):** After the seventh trumpet sounds and the heavenly declaration is made that "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (11:15), and after the temple in heaven is opened revealing the ark of the covenant (11:19), the vision shifts dramatically. In 12:1, "a great sign" (sēmeion mega) appears in heaven: a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and a crown of twelve stars on her head. She is pregnant and crying out in labor pain (12:2). A second sign appears: a great red dragon with seven heads and ten horns and seven diadems on his heads (12:3). His tail sweeps a third of the stars of heaven and hurls them to earth (12:4, echoing Daniel 8:10). The dragon stands before the woman to devour her child as soon as it is born (12:4). She gives birth to a male child "who is to rule all the nations with a rod of iron" (12:5) -- language drawn directly from Psalm 2:9, the royal psalm applied to the Davidic king and, in this document, to the risen Christ (2:27; 19:15). The child is caught up to God and to his throne (12:5). The woman flees to the wilderness, where God has prepared a place for her, and she is nourished there for 1,260 days (12:6) -- the same period assigned to the two witnesses in 11:3 and equivalent to the forty-two months of 11:2.

War erupts in heaven: Michael and his angels fight against the dragon and his angels (12:7-8). The dragon is defeated and "thrown down" (eblēthē, 12:9), and he is identified with four names: "the great dragon, the serpent of old who is called the devil and Satan, who deceives the whole world" (12:9). A loud voice in heaven declares: "Now the salvation and the power and the kingdom of our God and the authority of his Christ have come" (12:10) -- echoing the seventh trumpet's declaration in 11:15. The brothers "conquered" (enikēsan) the accuser "by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, and they did not love their life even when faced with death" (12:11). But the earth and the sea are warned: "Woe to the earth and the sea, because the devil has come down to you, having great wrath, knowing that he has only a short time" (12:12). The dragon pursues the woman, who is given eagle's wings and nourished in the wilderness for "a time and times and half a time" (12:14) -- Daniel's formula from Daniel 7:25 and 12:7, the same period as the 1,260 days and forty-two months. The dragon, unable to reach the woman, goes off "to make war with the rest of her children, who keep the commandments of God and hold to the testimony of Jesus" (12:17).

**How chapter 11 connects the surrounding argument:** Chapter 11 occupies a pivotal position in the document's structure. It completes the interlude between the sixth and seventh trumpets (begun in chapter 10), delivers the content of the small scroll John was told to eat, sounds the seventh and final trumpet, and opens the temple in heaven to reveal the ark of the covenant. It is simultaneously the conclusion of the trumpet sequence (begun in 8:6) and the gateway to the second half of the document, where the conflict between God's people and the beast/dragon will be described in full.

The chapter's internal structure has two distinct parts. The first part (11:1-13) continues the interlude begun in chapter 10. John, having been recommissioned as a prophet, now acts out a prophetic sign (measuring the temple) and delivers the prophetic content of the small scroll (the account of the two witnesses). The second part (11:14-19) concludes the trumpet sequence: the second woe is declared past (11:14), the seventh trumpet sounds (11:15), and the heavenly response includes the declaration that God's kingdom has arrived, the judgment of the dead, and the opening of the heavenly temple.

The time periods in this chapter -- forty-two months (11:2) and 1,260 days (11:3) -- are mathematically equivalent (42 months of 30 days each = 1,260 days) and correspond to Daniel's "time, times, and half a time" (Daniel 7:25; 12:7), which is three and a half years. This same period recurs in 12:6 (1,260 days) and 12:14 ("time, times, and half a time") and 13:5 (forty-two months). The author uses this period as a single, consistent duration that governs the entire period of testimony, persecution, and trampling. It represents the bounded season during which hostile forces are permitted to exercise power over God's people and God's holy space, while God's witnesses continue their testimony under duress.

The chapter also introduces, for the first time in the document, "the beast that comes up out of the abyss" (to thērion to anabainon ek tēs abyssou, 11:7). The beast has been foreshadowed -- the abyss was opened in 9:1-2, and Abaddon/Apollyon was identified as its angelic ruler in 9:11 -- but 11:7 is the first appearance of to thērion as a named agent of opposition to God's witnesses. This figure will be developed extensively in chapter 13, where a beast rises from the sea (13:1) and another from the earth (13:11), and in 17:8, where the beast "was, and is not, and is about to come up out of the abyss." The introduction in 11:7 is brief and unelaborated, as if the author assumes the audience will learn more later; the point here is simply that the agent who kills the witnesses comes from the abyss -- the same realm of confined hostile powers that produced the locust-army of chapter 9.

The connection backward to the prayers of the saints is maintained. The trumpet sequence began with the incense angel offering the prayers of the saints on the golden altar (8:3-5), and fire from that altar was hurled to earth. The sixth trumpet's command came from the golden altar (9:13). The seventh trumpet brings the declaration that the time has come to reward God's servants the prophets (11:18) -- the same servants whose prayers initiated the trumpet judgments. The arc from prayer to judgment to vindication reaches its structural completion in the seventh trumpet.

The connection forward to chapter 12 is equally precise. The time periods match (1,260 days in 11:3 and 12:6; forty-two months in 11:2 and 13:5). The declaration of God's kingdom in the seventh trumpet (11:15) is echoed in 12:10. The temple opened in heaven in 11:19 reveals the ark of the covenant, the symbol of God's covenant faithfulness, which grounds the vision of the woman, the child, and the dragon that immediately follows. The theophany formula in 11:19 -- "flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder and an earthquake and a great hailstorm" -- serves as both the climax of the trumpet sequence and the transition into the new visionary cycle of chapters 12-14.

## Verses 1-2: The Measuring of the Temple

"Then there was given me a measuring rod like a staff, and someone said, 'Get up and measure the temple of God and the altar, and those who worship in it. Leave out the court which is outside the temple and do not measure it, for it has been given to the nations; and they will tread under foot the holy city for forty-two months.'"

**"There was given me a measuring rod like a staff" (edothē moi kalamos homoios rhabdō).** The divine passive edothē ("was given") continues the pattern that has governed the entire document: every instrument and every commission originates from God. John does not acquire the measuring rod on his own initiative; it is given to him. The word kalamos ("reed, cane") refers to a measuring reed, a tool used in construction and surveying. The word rhabdos ("rod, staff") connotes authority and function -- a shepherd's staff, a ruler's scepter, a surveyor's instrument. The measuring rod is both a practical tool and a symbol of prophetic authority to delimit and define.

The action of measuring in Israel's prophetic tradition carries specific significance. The most extensive precedent is Ezekiel 40-43, where the prophet is transported in a vision to a high mountain and sees "a man whose appearance was like the appearance of bronze, with a line of flax and a measuring reed in his hand" (Ezekiel 40:3). This figure proceeds to measure every dimension of the temple -- its gates, courts, chambers, walls, and the altar -- in exhaustive detail across four chapters. The measuring in Ezekiel serves to delineate the restored temple that God's glory will inhabit: it defines sacred space. What is measured is preserved, sanctified, and claimed by God.

In Zechariah 2:1-5 (LXX 2:5-9), the prophet sees "a man with a measuring line in his hand" (2:1) going to measure Jerusalem "to see how wide and how long it is" (2:2). An angel then declares that Jerusalem will be "inhabited as villages without walls" because of its multitude of inhabitants, and that God himself "will be a wall of fire around her" (2:5). In Zechariah, the measuring signifies divine protection and future restoration. The measured city is the city God intends to fill and defend.

The act John is commanded to perform -- measuring the temple -- draws on both precedents. As in Ezekiel, the measuring defines sacred space and marks what belongs to God. As in Zechariah, the measuring implies divine protection of what is measured. The instruction to measure is simultaneously an act of prophetic definition (this space is God's) and an act of prophetic assurance (what God claims, God protects).

**"Get up and measure the temple of God and the altar, and those who worship in it" (egeire kai metrēson ton naon tou theou kai to thysiastērion kai tous proskunountas en autō).** The command egeire ("get up, rise") is an imperative of energetic action -- John is not passively observing but actively participating in a prophetic sign-act. The word naos ("temple, sanctuary") is distinct from hieron, the broader term for the entire temple complex including its courtyards. The naos is the inner sanctuary -- the holy place and the holy of holies, the space where God's presence dwells. Throughout this document, naos consistently refers to the inner sanctum of God's dwelling: in 3:12, the conqueror at Philadelphia will become "a pillar in the temple (naos) of my God"; in 7:15, the great multitude serves God "day and night in his temple (naos)"; in 11:19, "the temple (naos) of God which is in heaven was opened."

The three objects of measurement are: the naos (the inner sanctuary), the altar (to thysiastērion), and those who worship in it (tous proskunountas en autō). The inclusion of worshippers as objects of measurement is striking. Buildings and altars can be measured in cubits; people cannot. The measuring of the worshippers signifies that God's claim and protection extend not merely to the physical structure but to the community that worships within it. The worshippers are counted, known, and defined by their location within the measured space. This echoes the sealing of the 144,000 in 7:3-8, where the servants of God were individually identified and marked before the judgments began. Measuring and sealing serve the same function: they delimit the community that belongs to God and is protected by God through the coming tribulation.

The altar (to thysiastērion) connects to the altar that has appeared repeatedly in the heavenly scenes. In 6:9, the souls of the slain were under the altar. In 8:3-5, the angel offered incense with the prayers of the saints on the golden altar. In 9:13, the command for the sixth trumpet came from the horns of the golden altar. The altar is the point of contact between God and his people -- the place where sacrifices are offered, prayers ascend, and divine commands issue. Its inclusion in the measuring affirms that the worship of the community -- its prayers, its sacrifices, its testimony -- is under God's protection.

**"Leave out the court which is outside the temple and do not measure it" (kai tēn aulēn tēn exōthen tou naou ekbale exōthen kai mē autēn metrēsēs).** The verb ekbale ("throw out, cast out, exclude") is stronger than merely "omit" -- it carries the force of deliberate exclusion. The outer court (hē aulē hē exōthen) is the court of the Gentiles in the temple complex, the area outside the inner sanctum where non-Jews were permitted. In the earthly temple, a barrier wall (the soreg) separated the court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, and inscriptions in Greek and Latin warned that any non-Jew who passed beyond it would be responsible for his own death. The distinction between the measured inner space and the excluded outer court maps onto the distinction between the protected community and the space given over to hostile forces.

The instruction not to measure the outer court means that it is not claimed, not protected, not defined as sacred in the way the inner sanctuary is. It is, in effect, surrendered -- not because God is powerless over it but because the divine purpose assigns it to a different function during this period.

**"For it has been given to the nations" (edothē tois ethnesin).** Again the divine passive edothē ("it has been given"). The outer court is given to the nations (ta ethnē) by God's own decision. The nations do not seize it by their own power; their access is permitted. The same verb governs the beast's authority in 13:5 ("authority was given to it") and 13:7 ("it was given to him to make war with the saints and to overcome them"). In every case, the hostile power operates within boundaries set by divine permission. The nations' trampling of the holy city is not evidence of God's absence but of God's deliberate allowance for a defined period.

**"They will tread under foot the holy city for forty-two months" (tēn polin tēn hagian patēsousin mēnas tessarakonta duo).** The verb pateo ("to tread, to trample") echoes two critical scriptural texts. First, Daniel 8:13, where a holy one asks "How long will the vision about the regular sacrifice apply, while the transgression causes horror, so as to allow both the holy place and the host to be trampled?" (LXX: to hagion kai hē dynamis dothēsetai patēthēnai). Daniel's vision of trampling concerned the desecration of the temple by Antiochus Epiphanes in 167 BCE, when the daily sacrifice was abolished and the abomination of desolation was set up (Daniel 11:31; 1 Maccabees 1:54). Second, Luke 21:24, where Jesus declared that "Jerusalem will be trampled under foot by the Gentiles (pathoumenē hypo ethnōn) until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled." The verbal and conceptual parallels are precise: the nations trample the holy city, and this trampling endures for a defined season.

"The holy city" (hē polis hē hagia) is Jerusalem's scriptural title. In Isaiah 52:1: "Put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city" (hē polis hē hagia, LXX). In Nehemiah 11:1: "the holy city Jerusalem." In Daniel 9:24: "your holy city" (tēn polin tēn hagian sou, LXX). The designation carries weight: this is not merely a political capital but the city God set apart, the city where God chose to place his name (Deuteronomy 12:5; 1 Kings 11:36). That the holy city is subjected to trampling by the nations is a renewal of the pattern already known from Israel's history: Babylon trampled Jerusalem in 586 BCE, Antiochus desecrated it in 167 BCE, and Rome destroyed it in 70 CE. The trampling of the holy city is not without precedent; it is the recurring pattern of what happens when God permits judgment on his own people's sacred space.

**"Forty-two months" (mēnas tessarakonta duo).** This period equals three and a half years, or 1,260 days (using the standard prophetic month of thirty days). The number derives from Daniel. In Daniel 7:25, the fourth beast's horn "will speak out against the Most High and wear down the saints of the Highest One, and he will intend to make alterations in times and in law; and they will be given into his hand for a time, times, and half a time" (kairon kai kairous kai hēmisy kairou, Theodotion). In Daniel 12:7, the angel swears "that it would be for a time, times, and half a time; and as soon as they finish shattering the power of the holy people, all these events will be completed." The expression "a time, times, and half a time" (one year + two years + half a year) equals three and a half years.

The number three and a half is half of seven -- the number of completeness in this document. A half-seven represents an incomplete, truncated, bounded period. It is long enough to be severe but divinely prevented from reaching completion. The same period is expressed in three different forms across the document: forty-two months (11:2; 13:5), 1,260 days (11:3; 12:6), and "a time, times, and half a time" (12:14). The variation in expression does not indicate different durations but different perspectives on the same bounded season. Forty-two months emphasizes the calendar measure; 1,260 days emphasizes the day-by-day endurance required; "a time, times, and half a time" connects directly to Daniel's language and the pattern of persecution under Antiochus Epiphanes that provided the original context for the number.

## Verses 3-6: The Two Witnesses

"And I will grant authority to my two witnesses, and they will prophesy for twelve hundred and sixty days, clothed in sackcloth.' These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth. And if anyone wants to harm them, fire flows out of their mouth and devours their enemies; so if anyone wants to harm them, he must be killed in this way. These have the power to shut up the sky, so that rain will not fall during the days of their prophesying; and they have power over the waters to turn them into blood, and to strike the earth with every plague, as often as they desire."

**"I will grant authority to my two witnesses" (dōsō tois dysin martyrsin mou).** The speaker of this sentence is not explicitly identified by the text. The most natural reading is that God speaks, since the expression "my two witnesses" (tois dysin martyrsin mou) uses the first person possessive -- "my witnesses." The word martyrsin ("witnesses") is from martys, the same word used of Jesus in 1:5 ("the faithful witness," ho martys ho pistos) and of Antipas in 2:13 ("Antipas, my witness, my faithful one, who was killed among you," Antipas ho martys mou ho pistos mou, hos apektanthē par' hymin). The martyres in this document are not neutral observers but those who bear testimony at the cost of their lives. The word will develop, across the document's usage, the connotation of one who testifies unto death -- a meaning already present in the description of Antipas.

The number two carries legal significance. In Deuteronomy 19:15: "A single witness shall not rise up against a man on account of any iniquity or any sin which he has committed; on the evidence of two or three witnesses a matter shall be confirmed" (epi stomatos duo martyrōn kai epi stomatos triōn martyrōn stathēsetai pan rhēma, LXX). Deuteronomy 17:6: "On the evidence of two witnesses or three witnesses, he who is to die shall be put to death; he shall not be put to death on the evidence of one witness." The requirement of two witnesses to establish testimony was foundational in Israelite jurisprudence. God's sending of two witnesses ensures that the testimony they bear is legally sufficient, meeting the standard God himself established. The testimony cannot be dismissed on procedural grounds.

**"They will prophesy for twelve hundred and sixty days, clothed in sackcloth" (prophēteusousin hēmeras chilias diakosias hexēkonta, peribeblēmenous sakkous).** The 1,260 days is the same period as the forty-two months of verse 2, viewed from the perspective of the witnesses rather than the nations. While the nations trample the holy city for forty-two months, the witnesses prophesy for 1,260 days. The trampling and the prophesying are contemporaneous. The witnesses' testimony continues through the entire period of the city's oppression. They are not removed from the scene of affliction; they prophesy within it.

The word prophēteusousin ("they will prophesy") defines their activity. In the prophetic tradition, prophesying is not primarily predicting the future but declaring God's word to the present generation -- pronouncing God's assessment, calling for repentance, announcing God's purposes. The prophets of Israel -- Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel -- spoke God's word into situations of national crisis, unfaithfulness, and oppression. The witnesses' prophesying during the period of trampling places them in this tradition: they are God's spokesmen during the season of greatest adversity.

Sackcloth (sakkos) is the garment of mourning, repentance, and prophetic lamentation. In 2 Kings 1:8, Elijah was described as "a hairy man with a leather belt around his waist" -- the rough clothing of the prophet. In Isaiah 20:2, God told Isaiah to "loose the sackcloth from your loins." In Jonah 3:5-8, the people of Nineveh "put on sackcloth" in response to Jonah's preaching. In Matthew 11:21, Jesus said Tyre and Sidon would have repented "in sackcloth and ashes." The witnesses' sackcloth signals that their prophesying is not triumphant proclamation but urgent, grieving testimony -- a call to repentance delivered in the garb of mourning. They mourn what the nations are doing to the holy city even as they bear witness against it.

**"These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" (houtoi eisin hai duo elaiai kai hai duo lychniai hai enōpion tou kyriou tēs gēs hestōtes).** This sentence directs the hearer to a specific scriptural source: Zechariah 4:1-14. In Zechariah's vision, the prophet saw a golden lampstand with a bowl on top and seven lamps, with two olive trees standing beside it, one on each side (Zechariah 4:2-3). The angel explained: "These seven are the eyes of the LORD which range to and fro throughout the earth" (Zechariah 4:10). When Zechariah asked about the two olive trees, the angel answered: "These are the two anointed ones (literally 'sons of oil,' huioi tēs piototētos, LXX) who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (Zechariah 4:14).

In Zechariah's context, the two "sons of oil" were identified as the two leaders of restored Israel: Joshua the high priest (Zechariah 3:1-10) and Zerubbabel the governor (Zechariah 4:6-10) -- the priestly and royal figures who together led the post-exilic community. The lampstand represented the temple, and the two olive trees supplied the oil that kept its lamps burning. The imagery conveyed that Israel's worship and witness were sustained by the twin streams of priestly and royal anointing.

The author of Revelation transforms Zechariah's imagery. Instead of one lampstand and two olive trees, there are two lampstands and two olive trees. In this document, lampstands represent the assemblies of God's people. In 1:20, the risen Christ explained: "The seven lampstands are the seven assemblies" (hai hepta lychniai hepta ekklēsiai eisin). A lampstand is a witness-bearing community, the entity that holds up the light. By identifying the two witnesses as both olive trees and lampstands, the author combines the Zecharian role (anointed ones who sustain God's worship) with the Revelation role (communities that bear witness). The witnesses are both the source of oil (anointing, the Spirit's empowerment) and the bearers of light (testimony before the world).

The phrase "the Lord of the earth" (tou kyriou tēs gēs) comes directly from Zechariah 4:14 (LXX: ho kyrios pasēs tēs gēs) and Zechariah 6:5. The title asserts God's sovereignty over the earth -- the same earth that the nations are trampling. The witnesses stand before the sovereign Lord of the very territory the nations presume to dominate. Their position "before the Lord" signifies both their commission (they serve in his presence) and their accountability (they report to him).

**"If anyone wants to harm them, fire flows out of their mouth and devours their enemies" (kai ei tis autous thelei adikēsai, pyr ekporeuetai ek tou stomatos autōn kai katesthiei tous echthrous autōn).** The fire proceeding from the witnesses' mouths has two scriptural backgrounds. First, 2 Kings 1:10-12, where Elijah called down fire from heaven that consumed two successive companies of fifty soldiers sent by King Ahaziah to arrest him. Second, and more precisely, Jeremiah 5:14, where God declared to Jeremiah: "Because you have spoken this word, behold, I am making my words in your mouth fire, and this people wood, and it will consume them" (LXX: idou egō didōmi tous logous mou eis to stoma sou pyr, kai ton laon touton xyla, kai kataphagetai autous). In Jeremiah, the fire is explicitly identified as God's word -- the prophetic word itself is the fire that consumes. The author of Revelation draws on both traditions: the Elijah tradition of fire from heaven against those who oppose God's prophet, and the Jeremiah tradition of the prophetic word as fire. The fire that proceeds from the witnesses' mouths is their testimony itself, which functions as a consuming judgment against those who oppose it.

The verb adikēsai ("to harm") is the same verb used throughout the trumpet sequence for the harm that the judgments and locusts inflict (7:2-3; 9:4, 10, 19). The word connects the threats against the witnesses to the larger pattern of harm that runs through the trumpet judgments. Those who attempt to harm God's witnesses encounter the same quality of destructive force that the trumpet judgments deploy against the unrepentant.

**"These have the power to shut up the sky, so that rain will not fall during the days of their prophesying" (houtoi echousin tēn exousian kleisai ton ouranon, hina mē hyetos brechē tas hēmeras tēs prophēteias autōn).** This power identifies the witnesses with Elijah. In 1 Kings 17:1, Elijah declared to King Ahab: "As the LORD, the God of Israel lives, before whom I stand, there shall be neither dew nor rain these years, except by my word." In 1 Kings 18:1, after "many days," God said to Elijah: "Go, show yourself to Ahab, and I will send rain on the face of the earth." In James 5:17, the tradition specifies the duration: "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed earnestly that it would not rain, and it did not rain on the earth for three years and six months" -- three and a half years, the same period as the 1,260 days. The connection is precise: the drought Elijah called lasted the same duration assigned to the witnesses' prophesying. The witnesses exercise the same authority over the heavens that Elijah exercised, for the same duration that Elijah's drought lasted.

The word exousia ("power, authority") in this document consistently denotes delegated authority -- power given by a higher source. The witnesses' power to shut the heavens is not inherent but granted, as verse 3 indicated ("I will grant authority").

**"They have power over the waters to turn them into blood, and to strike the earth with every plague, as often as they desire" (exousian echousin epi tōn hydatōn strephein auta eis haima kai pataxai tēn gēn en pasē plēgē hosakis ean thelēsōsin).** This power identifies the witnesses with Moses. In Exodus 7:17-20, Moses (through Aaron) struck the water of the Nile with his staff and turned it to blood -- the first of the ten plagues. In Exodus 7:19, God commanded: "Take your staff and stretch out your hand over the waters of Egypt" (LXX: ekteinon tēn cheira sou epi ta hydata Aigyptou). The witnesses' power to turn water to blood and to "strike the earth with every plague" (pataxai tēn gēn en pasē plēgē) recapitulates Moses's entire plague ministry. The word plēgē ("plague, blow, stroke") is the LXX word for the Egyptian plagues (Exodus 11:1: eti mian plēgēn epagō epi Pharaō).

The combination of Elijah's power (shutting the heavens) and Moses's power (turning water to blood and striking with plagues) is deliberate. Moses and Elijah are the two towering figures of Israel's prophetic tradition -- the lawgiver and the prophet, the one who brought Israel out of Egypt and the one who turned Israel back from Baal worship. In the Transfiguration narratives (Matthew 17:1-8; Mark 9:2-8; Luke 9:28-36), Moses and Elijah appeared with Jesus as the representatives of the Law and the Prophets. In Malachi 4:4-5 (LXX 3:22-23), the final verses of the prophetic corpus, both are invoked: "Remember the law of Moses my servant" (4:4) and "Behold, I am going to send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and terrible day of the LORD" (4:5). The two witnesses embody the full prophetic tradition of Israel -- the witness of the Torah and the witness of the Prophets -- continuing their testimony during the season of the nations' trampling.

The phrase "as often as they desire" (hosakis ean thelēsōsin) indicates that the witnesses' plague-authority is not a one-time event but an ongoing capacity exercised at their discretion throughout the 1,260 days. Their ministry is sustained and active, not a single dramatic act.

## Verses 7-10: The Beast Kills the Witnesses

"When they have finished their testimony, the beast that comes up out of the abyss will make war with them, and overcome them and kill them. And their dead bodies will lie in the street of the great city which mystically is called Sodom and Egypt, where also their Lord was crucified. Those from the peoples and tribes and tongues and nations will look at their dead bodies for three and a half days, and will not permit their dead bodies to be laid in a tomb. And those who dwell on the earth will rejoice over them and celebrate; and they will send gifts to one another, because these two prophets tormented those who dwell on the earth."

**"When they have finished their testimony" (hotan telesōsin tēn martyrian autōn).** The verb telesōsin ("they finish, they complete") is a subjunctive of teleō, the same verb used for the "finishing" of the mystery of God in 10:7 (etelesthē). The witnesses are not killed in the middle of their work; they are killed after they have completed it. Their testimony runs its full course -- 1,260 days -- before the beast is permitted to act. The beast's power to kill them is itself bounded by the prior requirement that the testimony be finished. God's witnesses are not vulnerable to destruction until God's purpose for their testimony has been accomplished.

This pattern has precedent in the document. In 6:11, the martyrs were told to wait "until the number of their fellow servants... who were to be killed as they themselves had been, was completed" (plērōthōsin). The deaths of God's servants occur within a divine timetable, not at the random discretion of hostile powers.

**"The beast that comes up out of the abyss" (to thērion to anabainon ek tēs abyssou).** This is the first occurrence of to thērion ("the beast") as a named figure in the document, though the abyss from which it comes has been introduced in 9:1-2, 11. The beast is not described here; no heads, horns, or other features are mentioned. The identification is entirely spatial: it comes "out of the abyss" -- the same abyss that was opened by the fallen star in 9:1, from which the locust-army emerged. The beast is a denizen of the abyss, the realm of confined hostile powers. Its origin marks it as belonging to the same order of opposition as Abaddon/Apollyon, the king of the locusts (9:11).

The fuller description will come in 13:1-10, where a beast rises from the sea with ten horns and seven heads and blasphemous names, and in 17:8, where the beast is described as the one that "was, and is not, and is about to come up out of the abyss and go to destruction" (ek tēs abyssou anabainein kai eis apōleian hypagei). The author introduces the beast here without elaboration because the narrative function in chapter 11 is specific: the beast is the agent that kills the witnesses. What it is, what it represents, and how it operates will be developed later. For now, the audience needs to know only that the witnesses are slain by a power that comes from the same abyss the locust torments came from.

**"Will make war with them, and overcome them and kill them" (poiēsei met' autōn polemon kai nikēsei autous kai apoktenei autous).** The language is military: "make war" (poiēsei polemon). The beast wages war against the witnesses -- not a private murder but a public conflict. The verb nikēsei ("will overcome, will conquer") is from nikaō, the same verb used throughout the seven messages for the "conquering" that the assemblies are called to (2:7, 11, 17, 26; 3:5, 12, 21) and that the Lamb has accomplished (5:5). The beast "conquers" the witnesses in the sense of defeating and killing them. But this document's definition of conquering has already been established: the Lamb "conquered" by being slain (5:5-6). The apparent defeat of the witnesses -- their being "conquered" by the beast -- will prove to follow the same pattern: conquest through suffering, vindication through apparent defeat. The word nikaō is used with deliberate irony: the beast thinks it has won.

In 13:7, the same language will recur: "It was given to him to make war with the saints and to overcome them" (edothē autō poiēsai polemon meta tōn hagiōn kai nikēsai autous). The parallel confirms that the beast's war against the two witnesses in chapter 11 is the same war it wages against the saints in chapter 13. The two witnesses are representative of the broader community of faithful testimony.

**"Their dead bodies will lie in the street of the great city" (to ptōma autōn epi tēs plateias tēs poleōs tēs megalēs).** The word ptōma ("corpse, dead body, fallen thing") emphasizes the physical reality of their death. Their bodies lie publicly displayed, unhonored, unburied. In the ancient world, denial of burial was the ultimate indignity. In the Iliad, the fate of Hector's body -- whether it would be honored with burial or left to the dogs -- was a central dramatic concern. In Israel's tradition, denial of burial was a sign of divine curse: Deuteronomy 28:26: "Your carcasses will be food for all birds of the sky and for the beasts of the earth, and there will be no one to frighten them away." Jeremiah 8:2: "They will not be gathered or buried; they will be as dung on the face of the ground." Psalm 79:2-3: "They have given the dead bodies of your servants for food to the birds of the heavens, the flesh of your godly ones to the beasts of the earth. They have poured out their blood like water round about Jerusalem; and there was no one to bury them." The public exposure of the witnesses' bodies is an act of contempt and desecration.

**"The great city which mystically is called Sodom and Egypt, where also their Lord was crucified" (hē polis hē megalē, hētis kaleitai pneumatikōs Sodoma kai Aigyptos, hopou kai ho kyrios autōn estaurōthē).** The identification of "the great city" requires careful attention to the text's own markers. The author provides three identifying features: (1) it is called "Sodom" spiritually; (2) it is called "Egypt" spiritually; and (3) it is where "their Lord was crucified."

The third marker is historically specific: Jesus was crucified in Jerusalem. The crucifixion is a fixed geographical fact in first-century tradition. The clause "where also their Lord was crucified" (hopou kai ho kyrios autōn estaurōthē) points unambiguously to Jerusalem. The word "also" (kai) adds the crucifixion as an additional identification on top of the spiritual names.

The adverb pneumatikōs ("spiritually, mystically, figuratively") signals that the names "Sodom" and "Egypt" are not geographical designations but characterizations. Jerusalem is called "Sodom" because it has become like Sodom -- the city God destroyed for its wickedness. This identification has deep prophetic roots. Isaiah 1:9-10: "Unless the LORD of hosts had left us a few survivors, we would be like Sodom, we would be like Gomorrah. Hear the word of the LORD, you rulers of Sodom (archontes Sodomōn, LXX); give ear to the instruction of our God, you people of Gomorrah!" Jeremiah 23:14: "In the prophets of Jerusalem I have seen a horrible thing: the committing of adultery and walking in falsehood... they have all become to me like Sodom, and her inhabitants like Gomorrah." Ezekiel 16:46-56: the extended comparison in which God tells Jerusalem that Sodom was her "sister" and that Jerusalem's sins exceeded Sodom's. The prophetic tradition of calling Jerusalem "Sodom" is well established and indicates not a place but a condition: Jerusalem has reached the moral degradation that warranted Sodom's destruction.

Jerusalem is called "Egypt" because it has become a place of bondage, oppression, and hostility to God's people. Egypt in Israel's memory is the house of slavery (Exodus 20:2: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery," ex oikou douleias), the place where Pharaoh oppressed Israel, killed the male children, and refused to let God's people go. By calling Jerusalem "Egypt," the author characterizes the holy city as a place that has become what Egypt was -- an oppressive power that enslaves and kills God's servants. The irony is devastating: the city that celebrated the exodus has become Egypt.

The phrase "the great city" (hē polis hē megalē) will recur in 16:19, 17:18, and 18:10-21, where it designates Babylon. This raises a question: is "the great city" Jerusalem or Babylon? The text in 11:8 identifies the great city specifically as the place where the Lord was crucified -- Jerusalem. But the same title will later be applied to Babylon/Rome. The author may be intentionally collapsing the two: Jerusalem, insofar as it oppresses God's witnesses and aligns with the beast, has become Babylon. The same spiritual condition that characterizes the world-dominating empire can characterize the holy city when it turns against God's purposes. The two are not identical geographically but are identical functionally -- both kill God's servants.

**"Those from the peoples and tribes and tongues and nations will look at their dead bodies for three and a half days" (blepousin ek tōn laōn kai phylōn kai glōssōn kai ethnōn to ptōma autōn hēmeras treis kai hēmisy).** The fourfold designation "peoples and tribes and tongues and nations" (laōn kai phylōn kai glōssōn kai ethnōn) is a recurring formula in this document representing the totality of humanity (5:9; 7:9; 10:11; 13:7; 14:6; 17:15). The global audience underscores the public nature of the witnesses' humiliation. Their defeat is not local or hidden; it is witnessed worldwide.

The "three and a half days" (hēmeras treis kai hēmisy) mirrors, in miniature, the three and a half years of the witnesses' prophesying. As they prophesied for 1,260 days (three and a half years), they lie dead for three and a half days. The proportional echo links the two periods: the time of their humiliation in death is a fraction of the time of their prophetic ministry. The three and a half days may also echo the three days of Jesus's burial before his resurrection, with the additional half-day extending the period to match the symbolic three-and-a-half pattern that governs the chapter.

**"Will not permit their dead bodies to be laid in a tomb" (ouk aphiousin ta ptōmata autōn tethēnai eis mnēma).** The denial of burial is the deliberate choice of the onlookers, not mere neglect. The verb aphiousin ("they allow, they permit") in the negative means active prevention. Burial was a sacred obligation in Israelite and ancient Near Eastern culture. To refuse burial was to deny the dead person's humanity and dignity. The onlookers' refusal to permit burial extends the contempt beyond death: the witnesses are not merely killed but dishonored.

**"Those who dwell on the earth will rejoice over them and celebrate; and they will send gifts to one another" (hoi katoikountes epi tēs gēs chairousin ep' autois kai euphrainontai, kai dōra pempsousin allēlois).** The phrase "those who dwell on the earth" (hoi katoikountes epi tēs gēs) is the document's standard designation for humanity in its God-opposing posture (3:10; 6:10; 8:13; 13:8, 14; 17:2, 8). This group has appeared consistently as the target of the judgments and the ones who refuse to repent (9:20-21). Their celebration at the witnesses' death is a grim festival -- a holiday declared over the silencing of God's spokesmen. The sending of gifts to one another recalls the celebration of a national festival. In Esther 9:19, 22, the feast of Purim was marked by "sending portions of food to one another" (apostellontes meridas allēlois). The earth-dwellers create their own anti-festival, celebrating the apparent defeat of divine testimony.

**"Because these two prophets tormented those who dwell on the earth" (hoti houtoi hoi duo prophētai ebasanisan tous katoikountas epi tēs gēs).** The word ebasanisan ("tormented") is from basanizō, the same word used for the locusts' torment in 9:5. The prophets' testimony is experienced as torment by the earth-dwellers -- not because the prophets inflict physical pain but because the prophetic word, declaring God's truth against human wickedness, is unbearable to those who refuse to hear it. The prophetic tradition consistently depicts God's word as a painful reality for those who resist it. In Amos 7:10, the priest Amaziah told Jeroboam that "Amos has conspired against you in the midst of the house of Israel; the land is unable to endure all his words." In 1 Kings 18:17, Ahab called Elijah "the troubler of Israel." In 1 Kings 22:8, Ahab said of Micaiah: "I hate him, because he does not prophesy good concerning me, but evil." The prophetic word is torment to those who are committed to the lies it exposes.

## Verses 11-13: Resurrection, Ascension, and Earthquake

"But after the three and a half days, the breath of life from God came into them, and they stood on their feet; and great fear fell on those who were watching them. And they heard a loud voice from heaven saying to them, 'Come up here.' Then they went up into heaven in the cloud, and their enemies watched them. And in that hour there was a great earthquake, and a tenth of the city fell; seven thousand people were killed in the earthquake, and the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven."

**"After the three and a half days, the breath of life from God came into them" (meta tas treis hēmeras kai hēmisy pneuma zōēs ek tou theou eisēlthen en autois).** The phrase "breath of life from God" (pneuma zōēs ek tou theou) draws directly from Ezekiel 37:5, 10, the valley of dry bones. In Ezekiel 37:5, God declared to the dry bones: "Behold, I will cause breath (pneuma, LXX) to enter you that you may come to life." In 37:10, "the breath (to pneuma) came into them, and they came to life and stood on their feet, an exceedingly great army" (estēsan epi tōn podōn autōn). The verbal parallels are precise: breath/spirit (pneuma) enters, and they stand on their feet (estēsan epi tous podas autōn, 11:11; estēsan epi tōn podōn autōn, Ezekiel 37:10).

In Ezekiel 37, the vision of the dry bones was an enacted prophecy of Israel's national restoration. The bones represented "the whole house of Israel" (37:11), and their revivification represented God's promise: "I will open your graves and cause you to come up out of your graves, my people; and I will bring you into the land of Israel" (37:12). The resurrection of the two witnesses draws on this national-restoration imagery. As Ezekiel's dry bones represented Israel raised from the death of exile, the witnesses' resurrection represents the vindication of God's prophetic testimony after its apparent defeat by the beast.

The phrase "from God" (ek tou theou) specifies the source of the life-giving breath. The resurrection is not a natural recovery but a divine act. The same God who commissioned the witnesses, empowered their testimony, and permitted their death now reverses their death by his own breath.

**"They stood on their feet; and great fear fell on those who were watching them" (estēsan epi tous podas autōn, kai phobos megas epepesen epi tous theōrountas autous).** The standing on their feet echoes Ezekiel 37:10 precisely. The phrase "great fear fell" (phobos megas epepesen) recalls the response to divine action throughout the scriptures. In Exodus 15:16, after the crossing of the sea: "Terror and dread fall upon them" (epepesen ep' autous phobos kai tromos, LXX). In Joshua 2:9, Rahab told the spies: "The terror of you has fallen on us" (epepesen ho phobos hymōn eph' hēmas). In Luke 1:12, when the angel appeared to Zechariah: "Zechariah was troubled, and fear fell upon him" (phobos epepesen ep' auton). The great fear that falls on the onlookers is the recognition that they are witnessing God's direct intervention. The celebration of verse 10 is abruptly terminated by the terror of verse 11.

**"They heard a loud voice from heaven saying to them, 'Come up here'" (ēkousan phōnēn megalēn ek tou ouranou legousan autois, Anabate hōde).** The command "Come up here" (Anabate hōde) is the same command given to John in 4:1 (anaba hōde), now in the plural (anabate, addressing both witnesses). In 4:1, the command summoned John into the heavenly throne room to receive the vision. Here, the command summons the witnesses into heaven after their vindication. The verbal echo links the two scenes: as John was taken up to see God's purposes, the witnesses are taken up after fulfilling God's purposes.

The voice is "from heaven" (ek tou ouranou), unambiguously divine in origin. The witnesses' ascension is not their own achievement but a divine summons -- the same authority that sent them now recalls them.

**"They went up into heaven in the cloud, and their enemies watched them" (anebēsan eis ton ouranon en tē nephelē, kai etheōrēsan autous hoi echthroi autōn).** The ascension "in the cloud" (en tē nephelē) echoes multiple scriptural precedents. In 2 Kings 2:11, Elijah "went up by a whirlwind to heaven" as Elisha watched. In Acts 1:9, Jesus "was lifted up while they were looking on, and a cloud received him out of their sight." In Daniel 7:13, "one like a son of man was coming with the clouds of heaven." The cloud is the vehicle of divine presence and divine transport throughout Israel's tradition -- the pillar of cloud in the exodus (Exodus 13:21-22), the cloud that covered Sinai (Exodus 24:15-18), the cloud that filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34-35).

The detail that "their enemies watched them" (etheōrēsan autous hoi echthroi autōn) is pointed. The enemies who refused to bury the witnesses, who celebrated their death, who sent gifts to one another in triumph, now watch helplessly as the witnesses ascend. The reversal is complete: the ones who were publicly displayed in death are now publicly vindicated in ascension. The enemies are the audience for a spectacle they cannot prevent and cannot deny.

**"In that hour there was a great earthquake" (en ekeinē tē hōra egeneto seismos megas).** The earthquake accompanies divine action throughout the document. In 6:12, a great earthquake accompanied the sixth seal. In 8:5, an earthquake followed the hurling of fire from the altar. In 16:18, a "great earthquake, such as there had not been since man came to be upon the earth" accompanies the seventh bowl. Earthquakes in this document are theophanic markers -- indicators of God's active intervention in the world. The earthquake in 11:13 is God's immediate response to the witnesses' vindication: the earth itself reacts to the divine act.

**"A tenth of the city fell; seven thousand people were killed in the earthquake" (to dekaton tēs poleōs epesen, kai apektanthēsan en tō seismō onomata anthrōpōn chiliades hepta).** The fraction "a tenth" (to dekaton) is notably restrained compared to the fractions in the trumpet and seal judgments, where "a fourth" (6:8) and "a third" (8:7-12; 9:15, 18) governed the scope of destruction. A tenth is the smallest fraction in the document's judgment vocabulary. The restraint suggests that this judgment, while real and severe, is proportionally limited -- a warning, not a comprehensive destruction.

The phrase "seven thousand people" (onomata anthrōpōn chiliades hepta -- literally "names of men, seven thousands") is notable for its use of onomata ("names"). Each death is an identified person, not an anonymous statistic. The use of "names" individualizes the dead: they are known, counted, named. In 3:4, the faithful in Sardis were "a few names" (oliga onomata) who had not soiled their garments. The author's use of "names" treats individuals as specifically accountable before God.

The number 7,000 may recall 1 Kings 19:18, where God told Elijah: "I will leave seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to Baal." In Elijah's context, the 7,000 were the faithful remnant who had not apostasized. Here the 7,000 are those killed in the earthquake -- an inversion of the Elijah precedent. Where God preserved 7,000 from judgment in Elijah's day, here 7,000 are struck by judgment. The inversion may signal that the situation has reversed: the city that kills God's prophets (as Jezebel killed the prophets in Elijah's day, 1 Kings 18:4) now experiences the judgment that fell on Baal-worshippers.

**"The rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (hoi loipoi emphoboi egenonto kai edōkan doxan tō theō tou ouranou).** This response is the most theologically significant sentence in this section of the chapter, and it stands in deliberate contrast to 9:20-21. After the fifth and sixth trumpets -- after the locust torments and the killing of a third of humanity -- the survivors "did not repent" (ou metenoesan, 9:20-21). The refusal to repent was the tragic climax of chapter 9, the demonstration that escalating judgment alone does not produce human acknowledgment of God.

Here, after the earthquake, "the rest" (hoi loipoi) respond differently: they "gave glory to the God of heaven" (edōkan doxan tō theō tou ouranou). The phrase "gave glory to God" is not merely an expression of emotional awe. In Joshua 7:19, Joshua told Achan: "Give glory to the LORD God of Israel and give praise to him" -- a demand for confession and acknowledgment of God's justice. In Jeremiah 13:16: "Give glory to the LORD your God before he brings darkness." In this document, 14:7 will issue the command: "Fear God and give him glory, for the hour of his judgment has come." To "give glory to God" is to acknowledge God's sovereignty, to recognize his action, and to submit to his assessment. It is the response that the trumpet judgments were designed to produce but that 9:20-21 showed the earth-dwellers refusing.

The title "the God of heaven" (tō theō tou ouranou) appears in Daniel 2:18, 19, 28, 37, 44, where it designates the God of Israel as sovereign over all earthly kingdoms. Nebuchadnezzar used the title after Daniel interpreted his dream (Daniel 2:47: "Your God is indeed a God of gods and a Lord of kings"). The title places God above all earthly powers -- above the beast, above the nations who trample the holy city, above the city that kills God's witnesses.

Whether the response of "the rest" represents genuine repentance or merely terrified acknowledgment, the text does not specify with certainty. The verb edōkan ("they gave") is a simple act of attribution: they assigned glory to God. The contrast with 9:20-21 is clear -- they did respond, whereas the survivors of the sixth trumpet did not. Whether this response leads to lasting transformation or is merely momentary remains open. What is textually certain is that the combined witness of the prophets' testimony, their death, their resurrection, and the earthquake produced a response that the trumpet plagues alone could not. The pattern suggests that it is the witness of faithful testimony -- including testimony unto death and divine vindication of that testimony -- that has the power to break through human resistance in a way that raw judgment does not.

## Verse 14: The Transition to the Third Woe

"The second woe is past; behold, the third woe is coming quickly."

**"The second woe is past" (hē ouai hē deutera apēlthen).** This editorial transition connects to the structural markers established in 8:13, where the eagle announced three woes corresponding to the final three trumpets. The first woe (the fifth trumpet, the locust-army) was declared past in 9:12. The second woe encompasses the sixth trumpet (9:13-21) and the interlude that followed (10:1-11:13). The interlude between the sixth and seventh trumpets -- the angel with the small scroll, the measuring of the temple, and the two witnesses -- is thus included within the second woe, not set apart from it. The witnesses' prophesying, death, resurrection, and the earthquake are part of the second woe's content.

**"The third woe is coming quickly" (hē ouai hē tritē erchetai tachy).** The word tachy ("quickly, without delay, soon") echoes the document's opening: "the things which must soon take place" (ha dei genesthai en tachei, 1:1). The third woe corresponds to the seventh trumpet, which is about to sound in the next verse. The acceleration of the narrative is palpable: the announcement of imminence creates urgency. What follows is the climax toward which the entire trumpet sequence has been building.

## Verses 15-18: The Seventh Trumpet

"Then the seventh angel sounded; and there were loud voices in heaven, saying, 'The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ; and he will reign forever and ever.' And the twenty-four elders, who sit on their thrones before God, fell on their faces and worshiped God, saying, 'We give you thanks, O Lord God, the Almighty, who are and who were, because you have taken your great power and have begun to reign. And the nations were enraged, and your wrath came, and the time came for the dead to be judged, and the time to reward your bond-servants the prophets and the saints and those who fear your name, the small and the great, and to destroy those who destroy the earth.'"

**"The seventh angel sounded" (ho hebdomos angelos esalpisen).** The seventh trumpet is the climactic element in the series that began in 8:6. As the seventh seal contained the seven trumpets (8:1-2), the seventh trumpet opens onto the final revelation of God's purposes. The number seven marks structural completeness. The trumpet sequence that began with the four judgments on the natural world (8:7-12), escalated through the woe-trumpets (9:1-21), and paused for the interlude of the witnesses (10:1-11:13) now reaches its consummation.

**"Loud voices in heaven" (phōnai megalai en tō ouranō).** The voices are plural (phōnai) but their content is unified. The heavenly realm responds in chorus to the seventh trumpet's sounding. The phrase recalls 4:5, where "flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder" proceed from the throne, and 5:11-12, where myriads of angels sing in a single voice. The heavenly response to the trumpet is not a single speaker but a collective declaration.

**"The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ; and he will reign forever and ever" (egeneto hē basileia tou kosmou tou kyriou hēmōn kai tou Christou autou, kai basileusei eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn).** This declaration is the theological climax of the trumpet sequence and one of the pivotal statements in the entire document. The verb egeneto ("has become") is aorist -- it states the transition as accomplished fact. The kingdom of the world (hē basileia tou kosmou) is not gradually becoming God's; it has become God's. The declaration is proleptic from the perspective of the earthly narrative (the beast has not yet been fully revealed, Babylon has not yet fallen) but absolutely certain from the perspective of heaven, where God's purposes are already accomplished.

The phrase "our Lord and his Christ" (tou kyriou hēmōn kai tou Christou autou) draws directly from Psalm 2:2 (LXX): "The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the LORD and against his anointed (kata tou kyriou kai kata tou christou autou)." Psalm 2 is the royal psalm of the Davidic king's enthronement, in which God declares: "I have installed my King upon Zion, my holy mountain" (2:6) and "You are my Son, today I have begotten you" (2:7). The nations rage (2:1) and the rulers conspire (2:2), but God laughs at their conspiracy (2:4) and establishes his anointed despite their opposition. The seventh trumpet's declaration announces the fulfillment of Psalm 2: the nations' rage (which the elders will reference in verse 18) has not prevented God from establishing his kingdom. The anointed king reigns.

The phrase "he will reign forever and ever" (basileusei eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn) echoes the declaration about God in Exodus 15:18: "The LORD shall reign forever and ever" (kyrios basileuōn ton aiōna kai ep' aiōna kai eti, LXX). The song of Moses at the sea celebrated God's kingship demonstrated through the deliverance of Israel. The same kingship is now declared universally -- not over Israel alone but over the kingdom of the world. The reign is not temporary but eternal: eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn ("unto the ages of the ages"), the strongest expression of endlessness available in Greek.

**"The twenty-four elders, who sit on their thrones before God, fell on their faces and worshiped God" (hoi eikosi tessares presbyteroi hoi enōpion tou theou kathēmenoi epi tous thronous autōn epesan epi ta prosōpa autōn kai prosekunēsan tō theō).** The twenty-four elders were introduced in 4:4, seated on twenty-four thrones around the central throne, clothed in white garments and wearing golden crowns. They have appeared at every major moment of heavenly worship: in 4:10, they cast their crowns before the throne; in 5:8, they fell before the Lamb with harps and golden bowls of incense; in 5:14, they fell down and worshipped. Here, their response to the seventh trumpet is to fall on their faces (epesan epi ta prosōpa autōn) -- the posture of full prostration, the most complete physical expression of worship and submission.

**"We give you thanks, O Lord God, the Almighty, who are and who were" (eucharistoumen soi, kyrie ho theos ho pantokratōr, ho ōn kai ho ēn).** The elders' worship takes the form of thanksgiving (eucharistoumen, "we give thanks"). This is not merely praise for God's attributes but gratitude for God's action. The thanksgiving is prompted by something God has done, not merely by what God is.

The title "Lord God, the Almighty" (kyrie ho theos ho pantokratōr) is the document's standard divine title (1:8; 4:8; 15:3; 16:7; 19:6; 21:22). The word pantokratōr in the LXX translates the Hebrew Shaddai or Sabaoth ("Almighty" or "of hosts") and designates God as the one who holds power over all things. The title asserts comprehensive sovereignty -- there is no domain, no kingdom, no power outside God's control.

The temporal formula "who are and who were" (ho ōn kai ho ēn) is notable for what it omits. In 1:4, 8 and 4:8, the formula was threefold: "who is and who was and who is to come" (ho ōn kai ho ēn kai ho erchomenos). Here, the third element -- "who is to come" -- is dropped. The omission is theologically deliberate. The coming has happened. God is no longer "the one who is to come" because God has come. His kingdom has been established, his reign has begun. The third element of the formula has been fulfilled by the event the seventh trumpet announces. The two-element formula marks the transition from anticipation to realization.

**"Because you have taken your great power and have begun to reign" (hoti eilēphas tēn dynamin sou tēn megalēn kai ebasileusas).** The verb eilēphas ("you have taken") is a perfect tense of lambanō -- a completed action with present results. God has taken up his power. The word dynamin ("power") here is not a new acquisition -- God has always possessed all power. The "taking" is the active exercise of power that had been, in the narrative, held in reserve. Throughout the seal and trumpet sequences, God's power was exercised through intermediaries -- the horsemen, the angels, the natural calamities -- and partially, in fractions of a fourth and a third. Now God has taken his full power (tēn dynamin sou tēn megalēn, "your great power") and has begun to reign directly and completely.

The verb ebasileusas ("you have begun to reign" or "you have reigned") is an ingressive aorist -- it marks the beginning of God's reign in its full, unmediated form. This does not mean God was not sovereign before; the entire document has insisted on God's sovereignty from the first verse. It means that the exercise of God's reign over "the kingdom of the world" -- the realm that has been under the usurped dominion of the beast and the dragon -- has now been fully asserted.

**"The nations were enraged" (ta ethnē ōrgisthēsan).** The verb ōrgisthēsan ("were enraged, became angry") echoes Psalm 2:1: "Why do the nations rage?" (hina ti ephryaxan ethnē, LXX). The nations' rage against God and his anointed is the foundational conflict of Psalm 2, and the elders' song places the trumpet sequence within that psalm's framework. The nations' rage is not a surprise or a setback; it is the expected response of hostile powers to God's assertion of sovereignty. Psalm 2 declared that God laughs at the nations' conspiracy (2:4) and establishes his king despite their opposition (2:6). The seventh trumpet announces the same outcome.

**"Your wrath came" (ēlthen hē orgē sou).** The elders respond to the nations' rage with the declaration that God's wrath has arrived. The word orgē ("wrath") connects to 6:16-17, where the terrified cry at the sixth seal was: "The great day of their wrath has come" (ēlthen hē hēmera hē megalē tēs orgēs autōn). What was anticipated at the sixth seal is now declared accomplished at the seventh trumpet. God's wrath is not arbitrary anger but the settled, just response of the holy God to persistent human rebellion -- the same wrath the prophets announced (Isaiah 13:9, 13; Zephaniah 1:15, 18; Nahum 1:6).

**"The time came for the dead to be judged" (ho kairos tōn nekrōn krithēnai).** The word kairos ("appointed time, decisive season") -- the same word used in 1:3 ("the time is near") -- specifies that this is the divinely appointed moment for judgment of the dead. The judgment of the dead encompasses both the vindication of the righteous dead (the souls under the altar in 6:9-11, who cried "How long?") and the condemnation of the unrighteous dead. The details of this judgment will be developed in 20:11-15, the vision of the great white throne.

**"The time to reward your bond-servants the prophets and the saints and those who fear your name, the small and the great" (dounai ton misthon tois doulois sou tois prophētais kai tois hagiois kai tois phoboumenois to onoma sou, tous mikrous kai tous megalous).** The reward (misthos) has been promised throughout the seven messages: the tree of life (2:7), the crown of life (2:10), hidden manna (2:17), the morning star (2:28), white garments (3:5), a pillar in God's temple (3:12), a seat on the throne (3:21). The time for these promises to be fulfilled has arrived. The recipients are described in three overlapping categories: "bond-servants the prophets" (tois doulois sou tois prophētais), "the saints" (tois hagiois), and "those who fear your name" (tois phoboumenois to onoma sou). The breadth of designation -- prophets, saints, God-fearers, small and great -- encompasses the entire faithful community. No one is excluded by rank or status; the small and the great receive the same reward.

The phrase "your bond-servants the prophets" (tois doulois sou tois prophētais) echoes the language used of the Old Testament prophets throughout Israel's scriptures. In 2 Kings 17:13: "The LORD warned Israel and Judah through all his prophets and every seer" (literally, "through all his servants the prophets"). In Amos 3:7: "Surely the Lord GOD does nothing unless he reveals his secret counsel to his servants the prophets." In Jeremiah 7:25: "I sent you all my servants the prophets." The connection is deliberate: the prophets who have borne testimony throughout Israel's history, including the two witnesses of this chapter, are now rewarded.

**"To destroy those who destroy the earth" (diaphtheirai tous diaphtheirontas tēn gēn).** The verb diaphtheirai ("to destroy, to corrupt, to ruin") is applied first to God's action ("to destroy") and then to the earth-destroyers' activity ("those who destroy"). The wordplay is precise: the destroyers are destroyed. The principle is retributive justice -- the punishment matches the crime. Those who corrupted and ruined God's creation will be ruined by God's judgment.

The identity of "those who destroy the earth" encompasses both the human powers that oppress God's creation and the spiritual powers behind them. The beast from the abyss, the dragon, the nations that rage -- all are agents of earth-destruction. In 19:2, the declaration will come that God "has judged the great harlot who was corrupting the earth with her immorality" (ephtheiren tēn gēn en tē porneia autēs -- the same root phtheirō). God's creative purpose, established in Genesis 1-2 and celebrated by the elders in 4:11 ("you created all things"), is defended against those who would undo it. The Creator destroys the destroyers.

## Verse 19: The Opened Temple and the Ark of the Covenant

"And the temple of God which is in heaven was opened; and the ark of his covenant appeared in his temple, and there were flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder and an earthquake and a great hailstorm."

**"The temple of God which is in heaven was opened" (ēnoigē ho naos tou theou ho en tō ouranō).** The verb ēnoigē ("was opened") is a divine passive -- God opens his own temple. The naos is the inner sanctuary, the holy of holies -- the space that in the earthly temple was accessible only to the high priest, and only once a year on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:2, 15-17; Hebrews 9:7). The opening of the heavenly naos signifies unrestricted access to the divine presence and the revelation of what was hidden within. The veil that separated the holy of holies from view is, in effect, removed. What the earthly temple concealed is now revealed.

The heavenly temple has been the setting for the entire vision since chapter 4. The throne, the altar, the incense, the golden altar, the living creatures -- all belong to the heavenly temple's furnishings. But the naos itself, the innermost chamber, has not been opened until now. Its opening at the seventh trumpet marks a new level of disclosure. What was behind the veil is now visible.

In 15:5, the heavenly temple will be opened again: "the temple (naos) of the tabernacle of testimony in heaven was opened." In 15:8, "the temple was filled with smoke from the glory of God and from his power; and no one was able to enter the temple until the seven plagues of the seven angels were finished." The openings of the heavenly temple mark structural transitions: the first opening (11:19) transitions from the trumpet sequence to the vision cycles of chapters 12-14; the second opening (15:5) introduces the bowl sequence. Each opening reveals something and initiates a new phase of the document's vision.

**"The ark of his covenant appeared in his temple" (ōphthē hē kibōtos tēs diathēkēs autou en tō naō autou).** The verb ōphthē ("appeared, was seen, became visible") indicates that the ark was present all along but is now disclosed to sight. The ark of the covenant (hē kibōtos tēs diathēkēs) was the most sacred object in Israel's worship. In Exodus 25:10-22, God commanded Moses to construct the ark of acacia wood overlaid with gold, with the mercy seat (hilastērion) on top, flanked by two golden cherubim. The ark contained the two tablets of the covenant (Deuteronomy 10:5), and God declared: "There I will meet with you; and from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim which are upon the ark of the testimony, I will speak to you about all that I will give you in commandment for the sons of Israel" (Exodus 25:22). The ark was the throne of God's presence among his people, the place where God met with Israel, and the physical symbol of the covenant relationship.

The earthly ark disappeared from Israel's history at the time of the Babylonian destruction of the temple in 586 BCE. In 2 Maccabees 2:4-8, a tradition recorded that Jeremiah hid the ark in a cave on Mount Nebo, and that "the place shall be unknown until God gathers his people together again and shows his mercy" (2 Maccabees 2:7). The author of Revelation does not follow this tradition of a hidden earthly ark. Instead, the ark appears in the heavenly temple. The heavenly reality is the source; the earthly ark was a copy. The letter to the Hebrews makes a similar argument: Moses was instructed to construct the tabernacle "according to the pattern which was shown you on the mountain" (Hebrews 8:5, citing Exodus 25:40). The earthly furnishings were shadows of heavenly realities. The heavenly ark -- the original, the reality -- is now revealed.

The appearance of the ark at this moment carries profound covenantal significance. The seventh trumpet has just announced that "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ." The elders have declared that the time has come to reward God's servants and destroy the destroyers. At this moment of consummation, the ark is revealed -- the visible sign that God's covenant with his people remains in force. The covenant that was established at Sinai (Exodus 19-24), symbolized by the ark that held the covenant tablets, renewed through the prophets, and contested by the nations throughout Israel's history, is not forgotten. The God who made the covenant is faithful to it. The revelation of the ark is the visual assurance that the judgments and the kingdom announced by the seventh trumpet are the outworking of covenant faithfulness, not arbitrary power.

**"There were flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder and an earthquake and a great hailstorm" (egenonto astrapai kai phōnai kai brontai kai seismos kai chalaza megalē).** This theophany formula has appeared three times previously in the document, with escalating intensity:

First, 4:5: "Out of the throne come flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder" (astrapai kai phōnai kai brontai). Three elements.

Second, 8:5: "There followed peals of thunder and sounds and flashes of lightning and an earthquake" (egenonto brontai kai phōnai kai astrapai kai seismos). Four elements -- the earthquake is added.

Third, 11:19: "Flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder and an earthquake and a great hailstorm" (astrapai kai phōnai kai brontai kai seismos kai chalaza megalē). Five elements -- the great hailstorm is added.

The formula will reach its maximum intensity at 16:18-21, the seventh bowl: "flashes of lightning and sounds and peals of thunder; and there was a great earthquake... and huge hailstones" -- with the addition of "such as had not occurred since man came to be upon the earth."

The escalation across the document is deliberate and structural. Each appearance of the theophany formula marks a climactic moment (the throne room, the transition from seals to trumpets, the seventh trumpet, the seventh bowl), and each adds an element of intensity. The formula originates in the Sinai theophany: Exodus 19:16-18 describes "thunder and lightning flashes and a thick cloud upon the mountain and a very loud trumpet sound... Mount Sinai was all in smoke because the LORD descended upon it in fire... the whole mountain quaked violently" (LXX: brontai kai astrapai kai nephelē gnophōdēs... eseisen de pan to oros sphodra). The theophany formula in Revelation is the Sinai event recurring at cosmic scale. Each recurrence signals that God is acting as he acted at Sinai -- revealing himself, establishing his covenant, and judging with sovereign power -- but with increasing intensity as the document moves toward its consummation.

The hailstorm (chalaza megalē) connects to the seventh Egyptian plague (Exodus 9:23-25: "The LORD sent thunder and hail, and fire ran down to the earth") and to the first trumpet (8:7: "hail and fire, mixed with blood"). The hailstorm at the seventh trumpet combines the Sinai theophany tradition with the exodus plague tradition, marking the seventh trumpet as both a revelation of God's presence and a judgment on the unrepentant world.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. **The measuring of the temple (11:1-2) is a prophetic sign-act that defines and protects the inner community of God's worship while conceding the outer space to the nations for a bounded period.** The distinction between the measured naos and the excluded outer court draws on Ezekiel 40-43 (where measuring defines sacred space) and Zechariah 2 (where measuring signifies divine protection). What God measures, God claims and protects. The worshippers within the measured space are numbered and known. The outer court's surrender to the nations is not a failure of God's sovereignty but an exercise of it: the nations are given (edothē, divine passive) their limited season of domination.

2. **The forty-two months and 1,260 days are the same period expressed from two perspectives: the nations' trampling and the witnesses' prophesying.** Both durations derive from Daniel's "time, times, and half a time" (Daniel 7:25; 12:7) and represent a bounded season of persecution that is half of seven -- severe but divinely prevented from reaching completeness. The same period recurs in 12:6, 12:14, and 13:5, governing the entire arc of opposition to God's people in the second half of the document.

3. **The two witnesses embody the full prophetic tradition of Israel, combining the powers and characteristics of Moses and Elijah.** Their power to shut the heavens from rain replicates Elijah's ministry (1 Kings 17:1); their power to turn water to blood and strike with plagues replicates Moses's ministry (Exodus 7-12). Their identification as "two olive trees and two lampstands" draws on Zechariah 4, where the two anointed ones represented the priestly and royal leadership of God's people. The number two satisfies the Deuteronomic requirement for valid legal testimony (Deuteronomy 19:15). The witnesses represent not individual figures but the entire witness-bearing function of God's people during the season of tribulation.

4. **The beast from the abyss is introduced in 11:7 as the agent that kills the witnesses, but only after they have completed their testimony.** The beast's power to kill them is bounded by the prior requirement that their prophetic commission be fulfilled. The abyss from which the beast comes is the same realm that produced the locust-army of chapter 9 and to which the dragon will be confined in 20:1-3. The beast's introduction here is brief and will be fully developed in chapters 13 and 17.

5. **"The great city" is identified by three markers -- Sodom, Egypt, and the place of the crucifixion -- that point to Jerusalem, understood not geographically but characterologically.** The prophetic tradition of calling Jerusalem "Sodom" (Isaiah 1:9-10; Jeremiah 23:14; Ezekiel 16:46-56) characterizes the city's moral condition, not its location. The designation "Egypt" characterizes it as a place of bondage and oppression of God's people. The collapsing of Jerusalem, Sodom, Egypt, and (later) Babylon into a single characterological identity reflects the prophetic insight that any city -- even the holy city -- becomes the enemy of God when it kills God's prophets.

6. **The three-and-a-half-day period of the witnesses' death mirrors in miniature their three-and-a-half-year ministry.** The denial of burial, the public gloating, and the sending of gifts represent the world's celebration at the silencing of prophetic testimony. The earth-dwellers' identification of the prophets' words as "torment" (ebasanisan, 11:10) reveals the true nature of their objection: the prophetic word exposes what they wish to remain hidden.

7. **The resurrection of the witnesses draws directly on Ezekiel 37 (the valley of dry bones) and represents the vindication of prophetic testimony after its apparent defeat.** The breath of life from God enters them, they stand on their feet, and they ascend to heaven in the cloud while their enemies watch. The pattern follows the Lamb's own conquest: apparent defeat through death, followed by divine vindication through resurrection. The witnesses' vindication is the corporate enactment of the pattern established by the "faithful witness" of 1:5.

8. **The response of "the rest" in 11:13 -- "they were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" -- stands in deliberate contrast to the refusal to repent in 9:20-21.** The combined impact of prophetic testimony, martyrdom, resurrection, and earthquake produces a response that the trumpet plagues alone could not. This suggests that the document assigns to faithful witness-bearing a redemptive function that raw judgment does not possess. The witnesses' testimony, sealed by their death and vindicated by their resurrection, accomplishes what escalating destruction could not: the acknowledgment of God by those who had previously refused it.

9. **The seventh trumpet sounds the declaration that is the theological center of the entire trumpet sequence: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (11:15).** The declaration draws on Psalm 2, the royal enthronement psalm, and Exodus 15:18, the song of Moses celebrating God's eternal reign. The kingdom transfer is stated as accomplished fact. The elders' song declares that God has "taken his great power and begun to reign" -- the ingressive aorist marking the transition from restrained sovereignty to fully exercised dominion.

10. **The omission of "who is to come" from the divine title in 11:17 is theologically deliberate.** In 1:4, 8 and 4:8, the formula was "who is and who was and who is to come." At the seventh trumpet, the formula becomes "who is and who was" -- the "coming" element is dropped because it has been fulfilled. God has come. The anticipated future has arrived.

11. **The opening of the heavenly temple to reveal the ark of the covenant is the climactic image of the chapter and a statement of covenant faithfulness.** The ark -- lost to Israel since 586 BCE, containing the covenant tablets, representing God's presence among his people -- is revealed in the heavenly temple at the moment the kingdom is declared transferred. The revelation assures the audience that God's judgments, God's kingdom, and God's vindication of his servants are all grounded in the same covenant faithfulness that was established at Sinai and symbolized by the ark. God has not forgotten his covenant. The kingdom that has arrived is the covenant's consummation.

12. **The escalating theophany formula (11:19) -- now five elements: lightning, sounds, thunder, earthquake, and hailstorm -- marks the seventh trumpet as the most intense divine self-manifestation in the trumpet sequence.** The formula originated at Sinai and has been building across the document (4:5; 8:5; 11:19; 16:18-21). Each recurrence adds an element, signifying that God's action is intensifying as the document progresses toward the consummation. The hailstorm connects to both the exodus plagues and the first trumpet, unifying the judgment imagery in a single climactic moment.

13. **The chapter's position in the document is structurally pivotal.** It concludes the trumpet sequence that began in 8:6 and transitions into the second half of the document (chapters 12-22), where the conflict between God's people and the dragon/beast will be described in full. The time periods introduced here (forty-two months, 1,260 days) will govern the narrative through chapters 12-13. The beast introduced here will dominate chapters 13 and 17. The kingdom declared here will be fully established in chapters 19-22. The ark revealed here grounds everything that follows in covenant faithfulness. Chapter 11 is the hinge: it closes the first half's sequence of sealed scroll, seven seals, and seven trumpets, and opens the second half's drama of the woman, the dragon, the beast, and the Lamb's final victory.
