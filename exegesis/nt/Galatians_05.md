# Galatians 5 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**Where this stands in the letter:** The Hagar allegory ended with a declaration -- "we are not children of the slave woman but of the free" (4:31). Chapter 5 opens with the imperative that follows from it and then changes what the letter is doing.

Verses 1-6 state, in the plainest language anywhere in the letter, what accepting circumcision would cost. Verses 7-12 attack the other teachers directly and end in the letter's most bitter sentence. And from verse 13 the letter turns from what the Galatians must not do to what they should.

**5:13 is the hinge of the whole letter**, and the two halves are joined by a single word. Eleutheria, "freedom," appears at 5:1 as a gift Christ secured and at 5:13 as a responsibility the Galatians were called to. Everything before it argues that they must not take on the conversion programme; everything after it addresses what they should be doing instead.

**A question this chapter raises and does not obviously answer.** Having argued for four chapters that the Galatians must not place themselves under the law, Paul now tells them that "the whole law is fulfilled in one word" (5:14), quotes Leviticus as that word, and says that against the fruit of the Spirit "there is no law" (5:23). How those statements stand together with 5:2-4 is the chapter's central interpretive problem, and it should be felt rather than smoothed over. Any reading of Galatians that cannot hold both 5:4 and 5:14 without straining is incomplete.

**What the author argues immediately after (Galatians 6):** Chapter 6 continues the ethical instruction into particular cases -- restoring someone who has fallen, bearing one another's burdens, the reciprocity of sowing and reaping -- and then closes with a postscript in Paul's own handwriting (6:11-18) that returns to the other teachers, their motives, and the cross.

**How chapter 5 connects to what follows:** "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (6:2) restates 5:14 as an instruction. "The one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life" (6:8) restates the flesh-Spirit contrast of 5:16-26 in agricultural terms. And 6:15's "neither circumcision nor uncircumcision, but a new creation" repeats 5:6 with a different second half.

## Verses 1-6: Freedom, and What Circumcision Would Cost

> For freedom Christ set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery. Look: I, Paul, say to you that if you accept circumcision, Christ will be of no benefit to you. I testify again to every man who accepts circumcision that he is a debtor to do the whole law. You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by law; you have fallen from grace. For through the Spirit, by faith, we eagerly await the hope of righteousness. For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but faith working through love.

**Verse 1: "For freedom Christ set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery."**

**Tē eleutheria hēmas Christos ēleutherōsen** places a noun beside its own cognate verb -- freedom, freed -- which in Greek is emphatic rather than clumsy. The dative may express purpose ("for freedom") or advantage ("for the sake of freedom"). Either way, freedom was not a by-product of what Christ did; it was its object.

**Stēkete** ("stand firm") is a military image -- to hold a position under pressure, to refuse to give ground. It is a later Greek form built on histēmi with that added sense. Israel's scriptures use the same picture at the Red Sea, in the moment before the deliverance: "Do not be afraid. **Stand firm**, and see the salvation of the LORD" (Exodus 14:13).

**Mē palin zygō douleias enechesthe** ("do not again be held in a yoke of slavery"). Enechō is "to be held fast in, to be entangled, to be caught in something." The passive suggests being caught rather than choosing.

**The yoke, and why it is not a straightforwardly negative image.** This needs setting out at some length, because a modern reader will hear "yoke" as oppression, and a first-century Jewish reader would not have.

A yoke is a shaped wooden frame laid across the necks of draught animals, usually a pair, so that they pull together. It is not an instrument of punishment; it is what makes an animal's strength useful, and it couples two animals so that neither has to pull alone. A well-fitted yoke is a kindness to the ox; a badly fitted one galls.

In Jewish usage the image was applied to accepting God's rule and God's instruction, and it was **positive**. The Mishnah describes the recitation of the Shema as taking on oneself "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" and then "the yoke of the commandments" (m. Berakhot 2:2) -- and the order is significant: one accepts God's reign first, and the commandments follow from it rather than earning it. Elsewhere: "Whoever takes upon himself the yoke of Torah, they remove from him the yoke of government and the yoke of worldly care" (m. Avot 3:5), where the yoke of Torah is precisely what *relieves* other burdens.

Jesus uses the image the same way: "Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:29-30). A yoke is offered, and it is offered as relief from a heavier one.

So Paul is not stating a truism when he calls something a yoke of slavery. He is **inverting** a positive image, and the inversion is carried entirely by the genitive douleias ("of slavery"). The question the verse poses is not whether to be yoked but to what.

**The hardest comparison, and it should be faced directly rather than avoided.** At the Jerusalem meeting in Acts 15:10, Peter asks: "Why are you putting God to the test by placing a **yoke** on the neck of the disciples that neither our fathers nor we have been able to **bear**?"

Read plainly, that says the yoke was unbearable and that Israel never managed it -- which is the premise a great deal of Western reading of Galatians has assumed.

There is a lexical observation that bears on it and should be given its weight without being overstated. The verb for "bear" in Acts 15:10 is **bastasai**, and the verb for placing the yoke on is **epitheinai**. Both reappear in Matthew 23:4, of the scribes and Pharisees: "they bind heavy burdens, **hard to bear** (dysbastakta), and **lay them on** (epitithéasin) men's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger." Same verb for laying on; same root for bearing.

And in Matthew 23 the burden is specified. It is the accumulated ruling and the practice built up around the commandments -- not the written Torah, which Jesus in the same chapter tells his hearers to observe: "practise and observe whatever they tell you, but not what they do" (23:2-3). That is the same distinction the vocabulary at Galatians 1:14 draws, where Paul names his own former zeal as zeal for **paradoseis**, handed-down traditions, rather than for nomos.

Whether Peter's yoke should be understood that way is a decision the reader must make. The lexical link is real; the inference from it is not compelled by the link alone.

**One further difficulty in the verse, and it is a real one.** Palin ("again") raises the same problem as at 4:9: Gentiles cannot return to something they never carried, and 4:8 has established that these readers were pagans and not synagogue adherents. The word therefore attaches most naturally to the *condition* -- being enslaved -- rather than to any particular content of it. What would be repeated is the posture, not the yoke.

**Verse 2: "Look: I, Paul, say to you that if you accept circumcision, Christ will be of no benefit to you."**

**Ide egō Paulos legō hymin.** Three features make this the most emphatic sentence in the letter, and they compound.

The interjection **ide** ("look, see here") demands attention and interrupts the flow.

The pronoun **egō** is grammatically unnecessary -- Greek verb endings already carry the person -- so its presence is emphasis.

And the **name** is stated outright: *I, Paul*. Compare 6:11's "see with what large letters I am writing with my own hand." At the two points where Paul most wants to be unmistakable, he puts himself in the sentence.

**Ean peritemnēsthe** ("if you get circumcised") is present subjunctive: a live possibility, contemplated but not yet acted on. The whole letter is written into that window. Nobody addressed has taken the step.

**Ophelēsei** is from ōpheleō, a commercial verb: to profit, to benefit, to be of advantage. Christos hymas ouden ōphelēsei -- Christ will profit you nothing. The negative ouden is total.

**Verse 3: "I testify again to every man who accepts circumcision that he is a debtor to do the whole law."**

**Martyromai** ("I testify, I bear witness, I solemnly declare") carries a formal, quasi-legal ring -- the language of giving evidence rather than of offering an opinion. **Palin** ("again") indicates Paul has said this before, whether in person or in earlier communication, which means the Galatians are proceeding against information they already have.

**Panti anthrōpō peritemnomenō** ("to every man who receives circumcision") universalizes: there is no case to which this does not apply.

**Opheiletēs estin holon ton nomon poiēsai** -- "he is a debtor to do the whole law." Opheiletēs is one who owes: a debtor, someone under an obligation that can be called in.

**What proselyte conversion actually involved, and why circumcision was the decisive step.** A reader needs this to see what the sentence is doing, because on a modern hearing "circumcision" sounds like a ritual detail and the sentence sounds like a threat.

For a Gentile male to become a Jew in this period was a defined process with three components: circumcision; immersion in water (a ritual bath); and, while the temple stood, a sacrifice. The result was a change of legal and communal status. The man was no longer a Gentile. He was reckoned a member of the covenant people, and he took on the obligations that membership carried -- the whole of the Torah, not a selection from it.

**And the question of whether circumcision was strictly required is documented from precisely this period.** Josephus tells the story of Izates, king of Adiabene, in the first century (Antiquities 20.34-48). Izates came under Jewish influence through a merchant named Ananias and wished to be circumcised. Ananias **dissuaded him**, arguing that he could worship God without it and that God would pardon him, since the circumstances were exceptional. Later a Galilean named Eleazar, whom Josephus describes as extremely strict about the ancestral laws, found Izates reading the Torah and told him that reading it was not enough -- he must do what it commanded. Izates was circumcised.

The story is worth having in front of a reader of Galatians because it is the same dispute, among Jews, in the same period, over an actual Gentile: whether a God-fearing Gentile must complete the process or may stop short of it. Two Jewish teachers took the two positions. Paul's letter is written into an argument that was already live.

**Paul's statement is therefore a description of the transaction, not a warning that the law is unkeepable.** The other teachers appear to have been presenting circumcision as an addition -- one more thing, and then the Galatians' standing would be secure. Paul's answer is that it is not an addition to anything. It is a door, and what lies through it is an entire covenant with its full obligation. You cannot take the sign without taking what the sign signifies.

Note the verb: **poiēsai**, "to do." It will contrast sharply with the verb Paul uses of the whole law eleven verses later, and the contrast is deliberate.

**Verse 4: "You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by law; you have fallen from grace."**

Two images, and both describe a relation rather than a possession.

**Katērgēthēte apo Christou** ("you were severed from Christ"). Katargeō means "to render inoperative, to make ineffective, to nullify a connection, to put out of action." It is a favourite Pauline verb -- he uses it some twenty-five times -- and it appears three times in this letter: of what the law cannot do to the promise (3:17), of what would happen to the offence of the cross (5:11), and here.

The tense is aorist and the voice passive: a separation that has taken effect. The preposition apo ("from") names what it is a separation from -- a person, not a benefit.

**Tēs charitos exepesate** ("you fell out of grace"). Ekpiptō is "to fall out of, to drop from, to be driven off course." Outside the New Testament it is used of a ship falling away from its course under wind, and of a flower dropping from its stem -- the same verb appears at 1 Peter 1:24, quoting Isaiah 40:7, of the flower that falls.

**Charis** here names a sphere rather than an attitude: the Galatians were "called in the grace of Christ" (1:6), Paul was called "through his grace" (1:15), the pillars recognized "the grace given to me" (2:9), and Paul refuses to "set aside the grace of God" (2:21). It is where they were standing.

**Hoitines en nomō dikaiousthe** ("whoever are being justified by law") is present tense -- the process is under way, not completed. This matches the letter's consistent picture: at 1:6 they are "changing over"; at 5:2 circumcision is a live possibility; at 5:10 Paul is confident they will come round. The description is of people in the act, not of people who have arrived.

**A structural observation worth making, because it recurs throughout the letter.** Neither verb describes something being taken away from an owner. Katargeō breaks a connection; ekpiptō describes falling out of a position. What is lost, on this description, is lost by separation from Christ -- not by the confiscation of a possession, not by the reversal of a credit, not by the cancellation of a transfer.

The same shape appears at 5:21 (an inheritance not entered) and at 6:8-9 (a harvest reaped only by those who do not give out). Whatever account one gives of the letter's warnings, this is their consistent grammar, and it is worth noticing that a reader's prior theology will tend to supply a different grammar without the text asking for it.

**Verse 5: "For through the Spirit, by faith, we eagerly await the hope of righteousness."**

**Hēmeis gar pneumati ek pisteōs.** The pronoun "we" is emphatic and stands over against the "you" of verse 4 -- those seeking a standing by law, and those waiting for one by Spirit and faith.

**Elpida dikaiosynēs apekdechometha.** Apekdechomai is a doubly-prefixed verb (apo- and ek- attached to dechomai, "to receive") and it means to await eagerly, to expect with straining attention. Paul uses it for the creation's longing and the believer's at Romans 8:19, 23, 25, and for the awaited Saviour at Philippians 3:20.

Righteousness appears here as something **hoped for and awaited**, not only as something already held.

That sits alongside the aorist and perfect forms elsewhere in Paul -- "having been justified by faith, we have peace with God" (Romans 5:1) -- without being harmonized. The letter uses the vocabulary in both directions and does not resolve the tension. Readers should notice when an interpretation resolves it for them, and in which direction.

**Verse 6: "For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but faith working through love."**

**Oute peritomē ti ischyei oute akrobystia.** Ischyō means "to be strong, to have force, to avail, to count for something." The nouns peritomē and akrobystia are used here, as often in this period, as terms for the two bodily states and by extension for the two groups of humanity.

**The negation runs in both directions, and this is structurally important.** Uncircumcision counts no more than circumcision does.

A position that simply opposed Jewish practice would need only the first half of the sentence. Paul denies that *either* bodily state confers anything -- which means the target is not circumcision but the assignment of standing to a bodily condition. The identical two-way formula returns at 6:15, and it is worth noticing that a reading of Galatians which makes the letter anti-Jewish has to account for the half of the sentence that rules out the opposite error.

**Pistis di' agapēs energoumenē** ("faith working through love"). **Energeō** means "to be operative, to be at work, to produce an effect" -- it is the verb behind "energy," and it denotes actual functioning rather than latent capacity. Paul used it at 2:8 of God working in Peter's apostolate and in his own, and at 3:5 of God working miracles among the Galatians.

The participle here may be middle ("faith working itself out through love") or passive ("faith made effective by love"); the form is identical and the grammar allows both.

Either way, faith is described as **something that operates**. It is not an inert state set against action. The preposition dia ("through") names love as the channel through which the operation runs.

**This should be held together with everything the letter says about not being justified by works of law**, because the two are frequently set against each other in a way this verse forbids. Whatever "not by works of law" means, it cannot mean that faith is passive -- because the same author, in the same letter, four verses after saying that seeking justification by law severs a man from Christ, defines the faith that counts by what it does.

## Verses 7-12: The Ones Who Cut In

> You were running well. Who cut in on you, to keep you from obeying the truth? This persuasion is not from him who calls you. A little leaven leavens the whole lump. I have confidence in the Lord that you will take no other view; and the one who is troubling you will bear the judgment, whoever he is. But if I, brothers, still preach circumcision, why am I still persecuted? In that case the offense of the cross has been removed. I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves!

**Verse 7: "You were running well. Who cut in on you, to keep you from obeying the truth?"**

**Etrechete kalōs** ("you were running well") is imperfect -- an ongoing course, sustained over time, and going well throughout. The athletic image continues from 2:2 ("lest I should be running, or had run, in vain") and appears again at Philippians 2:16 and 1 Corinthians 9:24-26. Footraces were the most familiar competitive event in a Greek city, and the metaphor needed no explaining to this audience.

**Enekopsen** is from **enkoptō** -- literally "to cut into." Its two established uses both fit and may both be in play. In military contexts it describes breaking up a road to impede an advancing army. In athletic contexts it describes cutting across a runner's path so as to break his stride. Either way: something was going well and someone got in the way.

The interrogative tis ("who?") echoes 3:1 ("who has bewitched you?") and is likewise rhetorical. Paul knows, and the whole letter says so.

**Alētheia mē peithesthai** ("not to obey the truth"). Peithomai with the dative is "to be persuaded by, to obey, to yield to." **Alētheia** ("truth") echoes 2:5 and 2:14, where Paul twice speaks of "the truth of the gospel" -- once of a stand taken in Jerusalem and once of a line Peter failed to walk straight toward. The truth in view is not a general commitment to honesty but this specific message.

**Verse 8: "This persuasion is not from him who calls you."**

**Hē peismonē** ("the persuasion, the persuading") occurs nowhere else in the New Testament and is rare in Greek generally. It denotes the act or the effect of persuading -- the campaign of argument being conducted among them. The word choice may be a play on the peithesthai of verse 7: a persuasion that keeps you from being persuaded by the truth.

**Ek tou kalountos hymas** ("from the one who calls you") uses the present participle: God is *presently* calling, which is a small but real note of hope in a hostile paragraph. The same participle appeared at 1:6, where the Galatians are "deserting him who called you." The preposition ek denies divine origin.

Whatever arguments the other teachers are making, and whatever scriptures they are citing -- and they were surely citing scripture, since Genesis 17 is on their side of the question -- the campaign does not come from the God who called these congregations.

**Verse 9: "A little leaven leavens the whole lump."**

A proverb. Paul uses the identical sentence at 1 Corinthians 5:6, in a quite different context.

**Zymē** is leaven -- in practice a piece of fermented dough kept back from a previous batch and worked into a new one to start it rising. The point of the proverb is disproportion: a very small quantity transforms the entire mass, and once it is in, it cannot be confined to the part it started in.

In Israel's scriptures leaven carries a further association. All leaven was to be removed from the house at Passover (Exodus 12:15, 19; 13:7), and leaven was excluded from offerings burnt on the altar (Leviticus 2:11). From that it came to serve as an image of a permeating and corrupting influence -- as in "beware the leaven of the Pharisees" (Mark 8:15).

The application here is to containment. The Galatians may suppose that accepting one requirement leaves the rest of what they have intact. The proverb denies that such a thing is possible.

**Verse 10: "I have confidence in the Lord that you will take no other view; and the one who is troubling you will bear the judgment, whoever he is."**

**Egō pepoitha eis hymas en kyriō.** The perfect pepoitha ("I have been persuaded, and so am confident") describes a settled state rather than a momentary feeling. The basis is stated as **en kyriō**, "in the Lord": Paul's confidence is not in these congregations' resilience but is located outside them.

**This is the most hopeful sentence in the letter**, and it sits between the severance of verse 4 and the bitterness of verse 12. Both belong to the same man writing the same paragraph, and a reading that keeps only one of the two registers has lost the letter's actual temperature.

**Ouden allo phronēsete** ("you will think nothing otherwise") uses phroneō, "to have a mindset, to be disposed, to take a view." It is a verb about settled disposition rather than momentary opinion.

**Ho tarassōn hymas** ("the one troubling you") is singular, whereas 1:7 had a plural ("some who are troubling you"). Whether a single ringleader is in view or the singular is generalizing cannot be determined, though the closing phrase suggests the latter.

**Bastasei to krima** ("he will bear the judgment") uses bastazō -- the verb that appears at Acts 15:10 for bearing a yoke, and that will appear three times in the next chapter, of burdens (6:2), of a load (6:5), and of Paul's own scars (6:17).

**Hostis ean ē** ("whoever he may be") is striking and should not be passed over. Paul declines to make the man's identity relevant -- which is precisely the principle established at 1:8-9, where even Paul himself or an angel from heaven would fall under the curse for preaching another gospel. The messenger's credentials are not the test, and Paul applies the rule to his opponents exactly as he applied it to himself.

**Verse 11: "But if I, brothers, still preach circumcision, why am I still persecuted? In that case the offense of the cross has been removed."**

The verse presupposes an accusation: someone has said that Paul preaches circumcision. What lay behind such a claim the letter does not say, and speculation is not required. The word **eti** ("still") implies a contrast with an earlier period -- perhaps his life before the call, perhaps something else.

The argument is a proof by contradiction, and it runs on a fact Paul takes as public and undeniable: he is being persecuted. **Diōkomai** is present passive -- an ongoing condition, not a past episode. If he were preaching what the other teachers preach, that would stop, because their programme is precisely what removes the offence.

**Ara katērgētai to skandalon tou staurou** ("then the stumbling-block of the cross has been abolished"). Katargeō is the verb of 5:4, here in the perfect.

**Skandalon** originally denoted the trigger-stick of a trap -- the piece that springs when an animal touches it -- and by extension anything that causes a person to stumble and fall. The Greek scriptures use it for an obstacle placed in someone's path: "you shall not put a stumbling block (skandalon) before the blind" (Leviticus 19:14).

**What makes the cross offensive is worth stating, since the sentence assumes it and a modern reader does not feel it.**

Crucifixion was a Roman method of public execution reserved for slaves, rebels and provincials without citizenship. The condemned man was fixed to a beam and left in the open to die slowly, in view of passers-by, and often left unburied. It was designed to humiliate as much as to kill, and Roman writers of the period treat it as a subject barely fit for polite mention. Cicero calls the very word unfit for a Roman citizen's ears.

To claim that God's decisive act took that form was not merely surprising; it was disgraceful. Paul says as much elsewhere: the crucified Messiah is "a stumbling block (skandalon) to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23).

And the offence *increases* if the cross is claimed to be sufficient on its own, because that empties the recognized route of entry of its significance. Supplement the cross with circumcision and the offence subsides -- the message becomes a variant of something already intelligible -- and the persecution subsides with it.

This verse and 6:12 form a matched pair: Paul argues from his own persecution that he cannot be preaching circumcision, and argues that his opponents press it precisely in order to avoid what he suffers.

**Verse 12: "I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves!"**

**Ophelon kai apokopsontai hoi anastatountes hymas.**

**Ophelon** expresses an unattainable wish -- "would that," "if only." **Apokoptō** is "to cut off, to amputate," and in the middle voice with no stated object it was the standard term for castration.

**Two contexts sharpen the sentence, and both should be known before it is dismissed as mere abuse.**

*The Torah's own ruling.* Deuteronomy 23:1 in the Greek version excludes from the assembly of the LORD the man "whose testicles are crushed or whose male organ is **cut off**" -- apokekommenos, the same verb. So the wish carries a legal edge: men who are pressing an entrance requirement on others would, by taking their own logic one step further, disqualify themselves from the assembly under the very law they champion. Paul is not simply being crude; he is making an argument about where the logic of cutting flesh for standing terminates.

*The local religious landscape.* The region of Galatia was strongly associated with the cult of Cybele, the Great Mother, whose priests -- the galli -- were self-castrated, a practice notorious throughout the Mediterranean world. A congregation in that region would not need it explained. The wish therefore also says: follow this reasoning to its end and you arrive where the pagan priests already are -- which is the same move the letter made at 4:8-10, where taking on the programme is described as a return to what they came out of.

**Hoi anastatountes hymas** ("those who unsettle you") is the third designation for the other teachers, after "those troubling you" (1:7) and "the one troubling you" (5:10). Anastatoō means "to stir up, to turn upside down, to throw into disorder"; Acts uses it of those who "turned the world upside down" (17:6) and of an insurrectionist (21:38). The word is about public disturbance rather than private error.

## Verses 13-15: Freedom for Service

> For you were called to freedom, brothers. Only do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love serve one another. For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: "You shall love your neighbor as yourself." But if you bite and devour one another, watch out that you are not consumed by one another.

**Verse 13: "For you were called to freedom, brothers. Only do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love serve one another."**

The letter turns here. Everything to this point has argued that the Galatians must not do something; from here Paul says what they should do. It is worth noticing that the turn does not come with a new premise -- it comes with the same word the previous section ended on.

**Ep' eleutheria eklēthēte** ("you were called to freedom"). The passive verb has God as its implied agent, as at 1:6 and 5:8. Freedom is not something they achieved or claimed; it is what they were summoned into.

**Aphormēn tē sarki** ("an opportunity for the flesh"). **Aphormē** is a military term: the base of operations, the fortified position from which a campaign is launched, the starting-point of an expedition. Paul uses it several times for a pretext or an opening someone exploits (Romans 7:8, 11; 2 Corinthians 5:12; 11:12).

The warning is therefore not that freedom might be *misunderstood* but that it might be *used* -- that it could become the staging ground from which something else operates. A base of operations is where a campaign begins.

**Dia tēs agapēs douleuete allēlois** ("through love serve one another as slaves").

**The verb is the paradox, and it is unmistakable in Greek.** Douleuō is the verb of slavery -- from doulos, "slave." It is the same root as the douleia in "yoke of slavery" twelve verses earlier, the same root as the enslavement under the stoicheia at 4:3 and 4:8-9, and the same root as the katadoulōsousin ("that they might enslave us") of 2:4.

Paul has spent the letter arguing that his readers must not be enslaved. He now commands them to be slaves to each other.

The point is not verbal cleverness. **Freedom in this letter is not the absence of obligation; it is the transfer of it.** What Christ ended was a servitude imposed as the price of standing. What replaces it is a servitude freely given to people who are in no position to demand it. The Galatians are not being released from service into autonomy, and any account of the letter's "freedom" that omits verse 13 has taken half a sentence.

**Verse 14: "For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'"**

**Ho gar pas nomos en heni logō peplērōtai.** Two things in this clause require attention, and they are the chapter's central data.

***First, the verb, and its difference from 5:3.***

**Peplērōtai** is the perfect passive of plēroō -- "to fill, to fill up, to bring to completion, to fulfil." The perfect tense in Greek describes a completed action whose result stands: not "is being fulfilled," not "will be fulfilled," but "stands fulfilled."

Eleven verses earlier, Paul said the man who accepts circumcision is a debtor **holon ton nomon poiēsai** -- "to *do* the whole law." Here he says **ho pas nomos... peplērōtai** -- the whole law *stands fulfilled* in one word.

Two different verbs, two different constructions, both applied to "the whole law," within eleven verses of each other. Whatever Paul is doing, he is not saying the same thing twice, and the difference between doing and fulfilling is where the chapter's problem lives.

***Second, the move itself is a recognized Jewish one, and this is decisive for how it should be heard.***

Summarizing the Torah in a single principle was a familiar exercise, and in none of its known instances did it mean that the remainder could be discarded.

**Hillel**, a generation before Paul, asked by a Gentile to teach him the whole Torah while standing on one foot, answered: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary -- **go and learn**" (b. Shabbat 31a). The summary is immediately followed by an instruction to go and study everything it summarizes. Nobody hearing it would have taken it as permission to skip the rest.

**Jesus**, asked for the greatest commandment, names love of God and love of neighbour and adds: "On these two commandments **hang** all the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:37-40). The verb is kremannymi, "to hang," as a door hangs on its hinge. A hinge does not replace the door; it is what the door depends on and turns on, and a door with no hinge does not open.

**Micah 6:8** performs the same reduction from within the prophets: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?"

**Paul himself** does it again at Romans 13:8-10, and there he makes the relation explicit: he lists four commandments from the Decalogue, says they "are summed up in this word, 'You shall love your neighbour as yourself,'" and concludes "love is therefore the **plērōma** of the law" -- the fullness, that which fills it up.

The citation is **Leviticus 19:18**, quoted from the Greek version. It is worth noticing where it stands in its own book: in the middle of a chapter of Torah legislation, surrounded by commandments about leaving gleanings for the poor, paying wages on time, not cursing the deaf or tripping the blind, judging without partiality, and not bearing a grudge. Paul is quoting Torah, as Torah, as the summary of Torah.

**Verse 15: "But if you bite and devour one another, watch out that you are not consumed by one another."**

**Daknete kai katesthiete** ("you bite and eat up"). Both verbs belong to the animal world. Daknō is what a dog or a snake does. Katesthiō is devouring, eating something down -- the verb used of birds eating the seed in the parable of the sower.

**Analōthēte** ("you be consumed") is from analiskō, "to use up completely, to spend, to destroy." It is the word for consuming a resource until nothing remains.

**This verse changes how the rest of the chapter should be read**, and it is easy to skip past. It indicates that the crisis in Galatia had already produced conflict *inside* the congregations -- not merely a theological disagreement between Paul and some visitors, but people tearing at each other. The catalogue of vices at 5:19-21 and the closing warning at 5:26 should both be read with this in view.

## Verses 16-18: Spirit and Flesh

> But I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not carry out the desire of the flesh. For the flesh desires against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other, to keep you from doing what you want. But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under law.

**Verse 16: "But I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not carry out the desire of the flesh."**

**Pneumati peripateite** ("walk by the Spirit"). Peripateō is "to walk about," and its use for the conduct of life is a Jewish idiom rather than a Greek one. It renders the Hebrew halakh, "to walk," which is the standard scriptural way of speaking about how a person lives: "blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked" (Psalm 1:1); "teach me your way, O LORD, that I may walk in your truth" (Psalm 86:11); "he has told you... what the LORD requires of you: to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). The noun halakhah, the body of practical rulings on how to live, comes from the same root.

To speak of walking is therefore to speak of daily conduct, step by step, and not of a stance or a state.

**Epithymian sarkos ou mē telesēte** uses **ou mē** with the subjunctive -- the strongest form of negation available in Greek, a categorical denial about the future. Teleō is "to bring to completion, to carry out, to accomplish."

**The construction is a promise attached to a command rather than two commands.** Paul does not say "walk by the Spirit *and* resist the flesh," which would give the reader two things to do. He gives one thing to do and states what will follow from it.

**Verse 17: "For the flesh desires against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other, to keep you from doing what you want."**

**Hē gar sarx epithymei kata tou pneumatos, to de pneuma kata tēs sarkos.** The verb epithymeō ("to desire, to set the heart on") governs both clauses, which puts flesh and Spirit in the same grammatical position: both want something, and each wants against the other.

**Antikeitai allēlois** ("they lie opposed to one another") describes a standing structural opposition rather than an occasional friction. Antikeimai is used of adversaries in a lawsuit and of opposing armies drawn up against each other.

**The final clause is genuinely ambiguous and should be reported as such rather than resolved by translation.** Hina mē ha ean thelēte tauta poiēte -- "so that you may not do the things you want."

Whose wanting is being frustrated, and which things? Three readings:

- *The flesh prevents the good one wills.* This is the reading that assimilates the verse to Romans 7 ("I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate").
- *The Spirit prevents the evil one wills.* This fits the promise of verse 16, where the Spirit's leading is what makes the flesh's desire uncompleted.
- *The conflict as such means one cannot simply act on unexamined desire.* There is no neutral ground on which the will operates autonomously; a person is governed by one or the other.

The sentence does not specify, and its immediate context supports the second and third as much as the first: the paragraph is about being led by one power or the other, and it is framed by a promise rather than by a lament.

**Verse 18: "But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under law."**

**Ei de pneumati agesthe, ouk este hypo nomon.**

The phrase hypo nomon is the one fixed at 4:4-5, where Christ himself was "born under law" in order to redeem those under law. Since Christ observed Torah, the phrase cannot describe Torah practice; it denotes a standing under the law's jurisdiction and sanctions.

**Agesthe** ("you are being led") is present passive -- an ongoing condition in which the Spirit is the active party. The verb is used of leading an animal, of conducting a person somewhere, of a defendant being brought before a court.

**The verse's placement is what keeps it from being misread, and it should be noticed.** It stands immediately before a catalogue of behaviours whose practitioners "will not inherit the kingdom of God" (5:21), and three verses before a list of what the Spirit produces. Whatever "not under law" means here, it is stated in a context wholly concerned with conduct, and it is followed at once by a severe warning about conduct.

So the sentence is not a release from moral obligation. Any reading that makes it one has to explain why Paul immediately supplies a list of things that forfeit the kingdom, and why he says three verses later that there is no law against what the Spirit grows.

## Verses 19-21: The Works of the Flesh

> Now the works of the flesh are evident: sexual immorality, impurity, sensuality, idolatry, sorcery, enmities, strife, jealousy, fits of anger, rivalries, dissensions, divisions, envy, drunkenness, orgies, and things like these. I warn you, as I warned you before, that those who practise such things will not inherit the kingdom of God.

**Verse 19a: "Now the works of the flesh are evident."**

**Phanera** ("evident, manifest, out in the open") declares that these are not hidden, disputed, or a matter for expert discernment. Nobody needs to be told that they belong on the list, which is why Paul can simply name them without argument.

**Ta erga tēs sarkos** ("the works of the flesh") uses **erga**, the plural noun that has run through the letter in the phrase erga nomou, "works of law" (2:16; 3:2, 5, 10). The choice of noun is deliberate and will contrast sharply with verse 22.

**The list, and its shape.** Fifteen items are named, and they fall into recognizable groups:

*Sexual (three):* **porneia** (sexual immorality of any kind -- the general term), **akatharsia** (impurity, uncleanness), **aselgeia** (unrestrained indecency, behaviour that has stopped caring who sees).

*Religious (two):* **eidōlolatria** (idolatry -- literally image-service), **pharmakeia** (the word covers drugs, potions, poisons and magic together, since the practices were not separated).

*Social (eight):* **echthrai** (enmities, hostilities), **eris** (strife, quarrelling), **zēlos** (jealousy -- the same word that named Paul's own former zeal at 1:14, here in its destructive sense), **thymoi** (outbursts of rage, plural: not a temperament but episodes), **eritheiai** (rivalries, self-seeking, working for one's own advancement), **dichostasiai** (dissensions, standings-apart), **haireseis** (factions, parties -- the word from which "heresy" descends, but here meaning a division of the community into camps), **phthonoi** (envyings).

*Excess (two):* **methai** (drunkenness), **kōmoi** (carousing, revels).

**The distribution is worth counting, and it is the key to reading the list correctly.** Eight of the fifteen -- more than half -- concern the breakdown of relations within a group. Given that verse 15 has just described the Galatians biting and devouring one another, and that verse 26 will close the chapter on provoking and envying one another, the weighting is not accidental.

This is not a general catalogue of vice dropped into the letter from a stock of such lists. It is aimed. A congregation that has fallen out with itself over a theological crisis is being shown what its own behaviour belongs to.

The closing phrase **kai ta homoia toutois** ("and things like these") marks the list as representative rather than complete.

**Verse 21b: "I warn you, as I warned you before, that those who practise such things will not inherit the kingdom of God."**

**Prolegō hymin kathōs proeipon** ("I tell you in advance, as I told you before"). Both verbs carry the pro- prefix. This was part of Paul's original teaching among them, not a new threat produced by the present crisis -- which means the Galatians have heard it and are behaving this way anyway.

**Hoi ta toiauta prassontes** ("those who practise such things") uses the present participle of prassō.

**The tense is load-bearing and should not be flattened in translation.** The present participle denotes continued or habitual action -- a practice, a settled way of going on -- rather than a single act. The sentence is not about a person who does one of these things once; it is about a person whose life consists in them. English "practise" catches this; "do" does not.

**Basileian theou ou klēronomēsousin** ("will not inherit the kingdom of God"). The verb is klēronomeō, from the inheritance word-group that has run through the letter: the inheritance of 3:18, the heirs of 3:29 and 4:7, the son who is an heir at 4:7. The warning is expressed as an **inheritance not entered** -- the same grammar as 5:4 (a connection severed) and 6:8-9 (a harvest not reaped).

And the addressees are the same people addressed throughout: the Galatian believers, who received the Spirit (3:2), who are sons of God (3:26), who are Abraham's offspring and heirs according to promise (3:29), in whose hearts the Spirit cries Abba (4:6).

## Verses 22-26: The Fruit of the Spirit

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control; against such things there is no law. And those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires. If we live by the Spirit, let us also walk in line by the Spirit. Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another.

**Verses 22-23a: "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control."**

**Ho karpos tou pneumatos** -- and **karpos is singular**, against the plural erga of verse 19.

**The contrast was available in the vocabulary and Paul took it.** One word would have served for both lists; he used two, and the difference between them is not decorative.

Erga are things **made** -- produced by effort, various, each one a separate accomplishment that can be counted and displayed. It is the noun that has appeared throughout the letter in "works of law," and it will appear again at 6:13 in the boasting the other teachers want to do.

Karpos is what **grows** -- borne rather than manufactured, arising from what the plant is rather than from what it decides, and singular even when it hangs in many pieces on the same tree. A farmer does not achieve fruit; he tends a tree and the fruit follows from its nature.

Nine qualities are named as one crop:

**Agapē** (love), which was the content of 5:13-14 and will be the content of 6:2. **Chara** (joy). **Eirēnē** (peace -- the word rendering shalom, wholeness rather than mere absence of conflict). **Makrothymia** (patience; literally "long-temperedness," the opposite of the thymoi, the short outbursts, in the vice list). **Chrēstotēs** (kindness, usefulness to others -- the quality of being good *for* something). **Agathōsynē** (goodness). **Pistis**. **Prautēs** (gentleness -- not weakness but strength under control; the word is used of a horse that has been broken, which has lost none of its power and gained a rider). **Enkrateia** (self-mastery, having power over oneself -- notable as the last item, since it is precisely what the flesh's works lack).

**Pistis** in this list may be rendered "faith" or "faithfulness" -- the same ambiguity that runs through 2:16 and 3:11, and a standing reminder that the Greek word does not divide the way the English pair does.

**Two of the nine are direct answers to items in the vice list**: makrothymia against thymoi, and enkrateia against the whole final group. And the first, agapē, is the answer to the eight social vices at once.

**Verse 23b: "Against such things there is no law."**

**Kata tōn toioutōn ouk estin nomos** is grammatically ambiguous: tōn toioutōn may be neuter ("such things," the qualities just listed) or masculine ("such people," those who bear them). The Greek forms are identical and the sentence works either way.

Either way the substance is the same: **the law does not stand against what the Spirit produces.**

This is the clearest indication in the letter that the Spirit's work and the law's direction are not opposed to one another. There is no commandment that forbids love, or patience, or self-control; nothing in the Torah is transgressed by a person who is gentle. What the Spirit grows is not what the law prohibits.

**Read together with 5:18** -- "if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under law" -- the two verses give a coherent position that neither gives alone. A person led by the Spirit is not in the law's crosshairs, because what the Spirit produces is what the law was pointing toward. Neither verse says the law's content has been withdrawn, and the second explicitly says the opposite.

**Verse 24: "And those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires."**

**Tēn sarka estaurōsan** ("they crucified the flesh"). Two features are easy to miss and both matter.

*The subject is the believers.* Paul does not write "the flesh was crucified" or "God crucified the flesh." **Hoi tou Christou Iēsou... estaurōsan** -- those who belong to Christ Jesus did it. The verb is active and they are its subject.

*The tense is aorist* -- a completed action, viewed as a single whole rather than as a process.

**Syn tois pathēmasin kai tais epithymiais** ("with its passions and desires") specifies what went to the cross with it. Epithymia is the noun from the verb of 5:16-17, so the sentence closes what that passage opened.

The verb connects backward to 2:19-20 ("I have been crucified with Christ") and forward to 6:14 ("the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world"). Crucifixion is the letter's recurring image for a decisive and irreversible severance, and in every instance it involves participation in Christ's own death rather than a separate act performed alongside it.

**Verse 25: "If we live by the Spirit, let us also walk in line by the Spirit."**

**Stoichōmen** ("let us walk in line, let us keep in step") is from stoicheō, whose root sense is standing in a row or rank -- soldiers in formation, items in a column, elements in a series. It is the same root as **stoicheia** (4:3, 9), as **systoichei** ("corresponds to," 4:25), and as **stoichēsousin** ("as many as walk by this rule," 6:16). Four appearances of one root across three chapters, covering the elements that enslave, the column Hagar stands in, and the line believers are to keep.

The conditional **ei** ("if") is not doubtful but assumed: since we live by the Spirit -- and 3:2-5 and 4:6 have established that they do -- let our conduct fall into line with it.

**The relation between the two halves is the section's whole logic.** The indicative comes first and the imperative follows from it: they are told to become what they already are. That is the same structure as 5:1 (Christ set you free -- therefore stand firm) and 5:13 (you were called to freedom -- therefore serve one another).

**Verse 26: "Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another."**

**Kenodoxoi** is a compound of kenos ("empty") and doxa ("glory, reputation") -- seeking a glory with nothing inside it. The word appears only here in the New Testament, though the related noun kenodoxia appears at Philippians 2:3, also in a context about not putting oneself above others.

The two participles that follow are both reciprocal. **Allēlous prokaloumenoi** ("provoking one another") uses a verb meaning to call someone out, to challenge to a contest. **Allēlois phthonountes** ("envying one another") uses the verb behind phthonoi, one of the eight social vices at 5:21.

**The section therefore ends where it began.** It opened with the Galatians biting and devouring each other (5:15) and closes with them provoking and envying each other, using a word taken directly from the vice list in between. The theological crisis has produced a social one, and the instruction is aimed at that rather than at conduct in the abstract.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. **The chapter states the cost of the conversion programme in the most direct language in the letter, and 5:3 explains why the stakes are so high.** "I, Paul, say to you"; "Christ will be of no benefit"; "severed from Christ"; "fallen from grace." Nothing is hedged. And the reason is not that the law is bad but that circumcision is not an addition -- it is enrolment in a covenant with its whole obligation, which is what proselyte conversion meant and what the other teachers appear to have obscured. The Izates story in Josephus shows the same argument being conducted among Jews, over an actual Gentile, in the same period.

2. **The yoke image is not straightforwardly negative, and reading it as though it were flattens the verse.** Jewish usage speaks of gladly taking up the yoke of the kingdom and of the commandments; Jesus offers a yoke that is "easy." Paul is inverting a positive image, and the inversion is carried by the genitive "of slavery." On the hardest comparison -- Peter's unbearable yoke at Acts 15:10 -- there is a real lexical link to Matthew 23:4, where the burden that is "hard to bear" is accumulated ruling rather than written Torah, and where the same verb is used for laying it on. That link is evidence; it is not proof, and it should not be presented as more than it is.

3. **5:6 negates in both directions, and that is structurally decisive for reading the letter.** "Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything." A position that simply opposed Jewish practice would need only half the sentence. What is denied is that either bodily state confers standing -- and 6:15 repeats the formula in the same two-way form.

4. **"Faith working through love" (5:6) rules out any reading in which faith is set against action as such.** Energeō denotes actual operation and effect. The faith that counts is defined by what it does -- by the same author, in the same letter, four verses after saying that seeking justification by law severs a man from Christ. An interpretation must hold both sentences without straining either.

5. **The letter's hinge is 5:13, and it converts freedom into obligation.** "Called to freedom... through love serve one another as slaves" -- and the verb is douleuō, from the same root as the slavery Christ freed them from and as the enslavement of 2:4 and 4:3. Freedom here is not release from all service but release into a service that is given rather than exacted.

6. **5:3 and 5:14 use different verbs of "the whole law" eleven verses apart, and the difference is the chapter's central datum.** The proselyte is a debtor *to do* (poiēsai) the whole law; the whole law *stands fulfilled* (peplērōtai, perfect) in one word. And the summarizing move belongs to a recognized Jewish practice -- Hillel, Jesus, Micah, and Paul himself at Romans 13:10 -- in which the summary never means the remainder is discarded. Hillel's version ends "go and learn."

7. **The vocabulary of 5:19 and 5:22 is deliberately unmatched, and the difference is doing work.** Erga (plural) of the flesh, karpos (singular) of the Spirit. Works are produced by effort, are many, and can be counted and displayed -- which is exactly what 6:13 says the other teachers want to do with the Galatians' bodies. Fruit is grown from a single source and is one thing with many aspects.

8. **All of the chapter's warnings share one grammar, and it is worth noticing before a theology is imported.** Severed from Christ and fallen out of grace (5:4); an inheritance not entered (5:21); and, in the next chapter, a harvest reaped only by those who do not give out (6:8-9). None describes a possession confiscated or a credit reversed. All describe a relation departed from or a destination not reached.

9. **5:18 and 5:23 must be read together, and together they resist a simple law-versus-Spirit opposition.** "If led by the Spirit, you are not under law" stands three verses before a list of behaviours that forfeit the kingdom, so it is not a release from obligation. And "against such things there is no law" says that what the Spirit produces is not what the law opposes. The letter is not setting the Spirit against the law's *content*; hypo nomon names a standing, and 4:4 is the control on the phrase.

10. **The ethical material is aimed at a specific situation: these congregations are fighting each other.** "Biting and devouring" (5:15); eight of the fifteen works of the flesh concerning communal breakdown; a closing warning about provoking and envying that reuses a word from the list (5:26). The theological crisis has become a social one, and the chapter's instruction addresses people who have started tearing at one another over it.
