# Matthew 1 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

This is the opening chapter of the document. There is no preceding argument within the text. What follows immediately (chapter 2) continues the birth narrative with foreigners arriving to honor the child, a hostile king attempting to destroy him, and additional citations from Israel's scriptures presented as fulfilled. The author's opening chapter therefore functions as the foundation for everything that follows: it establishes who this person is by placing him within Israel's story, and it frames the entire narrative that will unfold.

---

## Verses 1-17: A Record of Origin Connecting Abraham, David, and the Anointed One

### The Opening Declaration (v.1)

The author opens with "biblos geneseos" -- "book of the genesis/origin" -- of Jesus Christ, son of David, son of Abraham. This phrase directly echoes the LXX language of Genesis 2:4 ("these are the generations of the heavens and the earth") and Genesis 5:1 ("this is the book of the generations of Adam"). A first-century Jewish reader steeped in the LXX would hear this as a deliberate literary signal: the author is announcing a new origin story that stands in the tradition of the creation accounts themselves.

The two identifiers -- "son of David" and "son of Abraham" -- are not arbitrary. Each corresponds to a specific covenant:

- **Son of Abraham** invokes Genesis 22:18: "In your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed." This is a promise about what will come through Abraham's line.
- **Son of David** invokes 2 Samuel 7:12-16, the promise that David's descendant would have an everlasting kingdom. This is a promise about rule and a throne.

By naming both, the author is claiming that this person is the convergence point of two foundational promises in Israel's story. The order is significant: David is named first, then Abraham. The genealogy itself, however, begins with Abraham and ends at Jesus. The author foregrounds the Davidic identity (the more immediately relevant claim for a "Christ/Anointed One") while structuring the actual record to show the entire sweep from Abraham forward.

### The Genealogy Itself (vv.2-16)

The genealogy follows a rigid "A begat B" pattern through the patriarchs, the kings of Judah, and the post-exilic period. Several features demand attention:

**The Four Women.** In a patrilineal genealogy, the inclusion of any women is unusual. The author names four: Tamar (v.3), Rahab (v.5), Ruth (v.5), and the wife of Uriah (v.6 -- notably, Bathsheba is never named; she is identified only by her connection to Uriah the Hittite). Each of these women has an irregular or remarkable story in Israel's scriptures:

- Tamar (Genesis 38) posed as a prostitute to conceive by her father-in-law Judah, after he failed to fulfill his levirate obligation. The text of Genesis vindicates her: "She is more righteous than I" (Genesis 38:26).
- Rahab (Joshua 2, 6) was a Canaanite prostitute in Jericho who sheltered Israel's spies and was incorporated into Israel.
- Ruth (the book of Ruth) was a Moabite -- from a people excluded from the assembly of YHWH (Deuteronomy 23:3) -- who attached herself to Israel through loyalty to Naomi and was grafted into the Davidic line.
- The wife of Uriah (2 Samuel 11-12): by refusing to name Bathsheba and instead identifying her through Uriah, the author keeps the reader's attention on the irregularity -- David's adultery and the murder of a loyal soldier. Uriah was a Hittite, a foreigner serving in Israel's army.

The common thread is not simply "women" or "sinners." Each case involves an irregular or boundary-crossing union that nonetheless advances God's purposes and produces the next link in the covenant line. At least three of the four are Gentiles or connected to Gentiles. The author is establishing from the very first page that the line leading to the Anointed One has always included the unexpected, the irregular, and the nations -- and that God's purposes have consistently worked through such situations rather than despite them.

**The Exile as Structural Marker (v.11).** The author marks the deportation to Babylon as a hinge point. This is not merely chronological notation. In Israel's self-understanding, the exile was the great catastrophe -- the loss of land, temple, and king. The prophets (Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Isaiah 40-55) interpreted it as consequence of Israel's covenant unfaithfulness and simultaneously looked beyond it to restoration. By structuring the genealogy around the exile, the author places the birth of the Anointed One within a narrative of exile-and-restoration.

**The Grammatical Break at Verse 16.** Throughout the genealogy, the pattern is uniform: "A begat B." But at verse 16, the pattern breaks: "Jacob begat Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ." The Greek pronoun "of whom" (ex hes) is feminine singular -- it refers to Mary, not Joseph. The author has constructed an entire genealogy tracing Joseph's Davidic lineage but then, at the critical moment, states that Jesus was born of Mary, not begotten by Joseph. This grammatical shift is precise and deliberate. It creates a tension: the legal Davidic claim runs through Joseph, but Jesus' origin is through Mary. The narrative section (vv.18-25) exists to resolve this tension.

### The Numerical Structure (v.17)

The author explicitly organizes the genealogy into three sets of fourteen generations: Abraham to David, David to the Babylonian exile, exile to the Anointed One. This is an imposed structure -- the author has compressed the actual historical record (omitting several known kings between Joram and Uzziah, for instance) to achieve this pattern.

The number fourteen is significant. In Hebrew, David's name (dalet-vav-dalet) has a numerical value of 4+6+4 = 14. The author appears to be encoding David's name into the very architecture of the history. The message to a first-century reader familiar with such numerological conventions would be: the entire trajectory of Israel's history from Abraham onward is a Davidic story -- patterned around and oriented toward the fulfillment of the Davidic promise.

The three eras also map to a narrative arc: rise (Abraham to David -- from promise to kingdom), fall (David to Babylon -- from kingdom to exile), and restoration (Babylon to the Anointed One -- from exile to fulfillment). The author is arguing that the Anointed One's arrival is the culmination that completes the pattern and resolves the story.

## Verses 18-25: The Origin of Jesus Explained

### The Situation (vv.18-19)

Having created the genealogical tension in v.16 (legal Davidic line through Joseph, birth through Mary), the author now resolves it. Mary is betrothed (espoused) to Joseph. In first-century Jewish practice, betrothal (erusin/kiddushin) was a legally binding arrangement -- the woman was legally the man's wife, though they had not yet begun cohabitation or conjugal life. During this period, Mary is found to be pregnant. The author states the cause plainly: "of the Holy Spirit." This is stated as narrative fact, not argued or defended.

Joseph is described as "dikaios" -- just, righteous. In Jewish usage rooted in the LXX and Torah, this means Torah-observant, one who lives in conformity with God's commandments. His dilemma is real: Torah provisions (Deuteronomy 22:20-21, 24:1) address situations of apparent sexual unfaithfulness. A "just" man in a strict sense would be expected to act on these provisions. Yet the author says Joseph was "not willing to make her a public example" and intended to divorce her quietly. The author presents Joseph's righteousness as encompassing both fidelity to Torah and mercy -- he seeks a path that upholds the law without destroying Mary. This characterization matters because Joseph is about to become the vehicle through which Jesus receives his legal Davidic identity.

### The Angel's Message (vv.20-21)

An angel of the Lord appears to Joseph in a dream. The address is critical: "Joseph, son of David." This is the only time in the narrative that Joseph is directly called "son of David." The angel is not making casual conversation; the address reinforces why Joseph matters -- he is the Davidic link.

The angel's instructions resolve the tension:

1. **"Do not fear to take Mary your wife"** -- Joseph is told to complete the marriage, not to divorce her.
2. **"That which is conceived in her is of the Holy Spirit"** -- the pregnancy is from God, not from unfaithfulness. Joseph's dikaios status is preserved; there is no violation of Torah to respond to.
3. **"She shall bring forth a son, and you shall call his name Jesus (Yeshua)"** -- the act of naming is critical. In Jewish practice, the father names the child. By commanding Joseph to name the child, the angel is directing Joseph to perform the act that constitutes legal paternity. Through this naming, Jesus is formally incorporated into Joseph's line -- the line of David. The genealogical tension is resolved: Jesus is legally David's descendant through Joseph's acceptance and naming, while his actual origin is from the Holy Spirit through Mary.

**"For he shall save his people from their sins."** The name Yeshua means "YHWH saves" or "YHWH is salvation." The angel gives the reason for the name: "he shall save his people from their sins." Two elements require attention:

- **"His people"**: Given the context -- the genealogy has just traced the line of Israel from Abraham through David to this child -- "his people" would be understood as Israel. This person is being presented as Israel's deliverer.
- **"From their sins"**: This is a striking specification. First-century Jewish hopes for deliverance included liberation from Rome, restoration of sovereignty, rebuilding of national glory. The author, through the angel's words, defines the deliverance differently: it is salvation from sins. This connects directly to the prophetic interpretation of the exile. The prophets (Daniel 9:24, Jeremiah 31:31-34, Ezekiel 36:25-27, Isaiah 40:1-2) understood Israel's fundamental problem as covenant unfaithfulness -- sin -- and the exile as its consequence. If the exile was caused by sin, then genuine restoration requires dealing with sin itself. The author is presenting this child as the one who will accomplish what the return from Babylon did not fully achieve.

### The Fulfillment Citation (vv.22-23)

The author introduces a formula that will recur throughout the document: "Now all this was done, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet." This is the first of a series of such citations that structure the early chapters.

The quotation is from Isaiah 7:14 (following the LXX, which uses "parthenos" -- virgin -- to render the Hebrew "almah"). In Isaiah's original context, this was a sign given to King Ahaz during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis (735-732 BCE): before a child conceived now would be old enough to know right from wrong, the two threatening kings would be removed. The author reads this text as having a deeper or fuller realization in the birth he is narrating.

The name "Emmanuel" -- "God with us" -- is introduced here. Notably, the child is not actually called Emmanuel in the narrative; he is called Jesus. The author is not claiming the child's name is Emmanuel but rather that the meaning of Emmanuel -- "God with us" -- is what this birth signifies. This is an interpretive claim about the significance of the event, not a naming.

This phrase -- God being present with his people -- has deep roots in Israel's story: the tabernacle, the temple, the pillar of cloud and fire, the promise of presence throughout the wilderness. It also connects to a theme that bookends the entire document: in the final verse of this author's work (Matthew 28:20), the risen Jesus says "I am with you always, to the end of the age." The opening chapter introduces "God with us"; the closing chapter fulfills it. The author has constructed an inclusio -- a literary bracket -- around the entire narrative.

### Joseph's Obedient Response (vv.24-25)

Joseph does exactly what the angel commands. He takes Mary as his wife, does not have sexual relations with her until after the birth, and names the child Jesus. Every element of the angel's instruction is fulfilled. The author emphasizes Joseph's obedience -- he is the model of the righteous person who hears God's word and acts on it.

The act of naming is stated last and emphatically: "he called his name Jesus." Through this act, the legal adoption is complete. The child of Mary, conceived by the Holy Spirit, is now legally the son of Joseph, son of David, son of Abraham.

## Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 1

The author constructs a careful two-part opening:

1. **The genealogy** (vv.1-17) establishes that the entire history of Israel from Abraham through David through the exile has been moving toward this moment. The Davidic and Abrahamic covenants converge in this child. The inclusion of Gentile and irregular women signals that God's purposes have always worked through the unexpected. The numerical structure encodes David's name into the pattern of history itself.

2. **The birth narrative** (vv.18-25) resolves the genealogy's central tension (Davidic line through Joseph, birth through Mary) by showing how Joseph's obedient act of naming legally incorporates Jesus into the Davidic line. It defines the child's mission not as political liberation but as salvation from sins -- connecting to the prophetic understanding of exile as the consequence of covenant unfaithfulness. And it frames the birth as the arrival of "God with us," the presence of God dwelling among his people in a new way.

The chapter as a whole argues that this birth is not an isolated event but the culmination of Israel's entire story -- the resolution of promises made to Abraham and David, the answer to the exile, and the arrival of God's presence among his people.
