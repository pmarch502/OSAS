# Matthew 3 -- First-Century Reading

## Context Within the Author's Argument

**What came before (Matthew 2):** The author established that Jesus is the legitimate king of Israel whose birth and early movements fulfilled prophetic scripture. Magi recognized his kingship, Herod perceived him as a rival to the throne, and the family's movements through Egypt and back to Nazareth were each tied to prophetic fulfillment. The chapter ended with the family settled in Nazareth, and the narrative paused there.

**What comes after (Matthew 4):** The Spirit that descended on Jesus at the end of chapter 3 immediately drives him into the wilderness for testing. After the testing, Jesus begins his public activity in Galilee, calling disciples and proclaiming the same message John proclaimed: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near."

**How chapter 3 connects them:** Chapters 1-2 established who Jesus is by genealogy, birth, and prophetic geography. Chapter 3 transitions from private identity to public confirmation. A herald announces the arrival, and then heaven itself publicly identifies Jesus as God's Son. Chapter 4 then tests that identity and launches the public work. The author's sequence is deliberate: lineage and birth (1-2), herald and heavenly confirmation (3), testing and commencement (4). Without the herald and the divine declaration in chapter 3, the transition from hidden child in Nazareth to public figure in Galilee would have no narrative bridge.

## The Herald's Appearance and the Isaiah Quotation (vv. 1-6)

The chapter opens with "In those days" -- a loose time marker that jumps forward from Jesus's childhood to the appearance of John in the wilderness of Judea. The author gives John a single proclamation: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near" (metanoeite, engiken gar he basileia ton ouranon).

The author then immediately identifies John as the figure spoken of by Isaiah the prophet, quoting from Isaiah 40:3 (LXX): "A voice of one crying in the wilderness: 'Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.'"

This quotation demands close attention to its original context. In Isaiah 40, this cry opens the great declaration of Israel's return from Babylonian exile. The "way of the Lord" in Isaiah is the way YHWH himself would travel as he led his people home -- a new exodus. The voice commands the wilderness to be leveled, the rough terrain smoothed, because YHWH is coming through. The first-century hearer familiar with Isaiah would understand this: the author is claiming that what is about to happen with Jesus is the arrival of YHWH to redeem his people -- the long-awaited return from exile, the restoration that the physical return from Babylon had never fully accomplished.

By applying this passage to John, the author makes John the voice and makes Jesus the "Lord" whose way is being prepared. This is not a casual proof-text. The author is positioning the entire narrative within Isaiah's framework of divine visitation and national restoration.

John's appearance is then described: camel's hair clothing and a leather belt around his waist. This is a deliberate echo of Elijah's description in 2 Kings 1:8 (LXX: "a hairy man with a leather belt around his waist"). A first-century hearer steeped in Israel's scriptures would immediately connect this figure to Elijah. Malachi 4:5 (3:23 LXX) had promised that God would send "Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the LORD." John's appearance signals that the eschatological moment Malachi announced has arrived.

The response to John is sweeping: "Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region around the Jordan" went out to him, being baptized in the Jordan, confessing their sins. The scope of the response matters -- the author presents this as a broad national movement, not a fringe sect. The Jordan itself carries weight. Israel's entry into the promised land under Joshua came through the Jordan. A baptism in the Jordan symbolically reenacts that crossing -- a people passing through water to enter into God's purposes anew.

## John's Confrontation With the Pharisees and Sadducees (vv. 7-10)

When John sees "many of the Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism," he does not welcome them. He addresses them as "brood of vipers" (gennemata echidnon) and asks: "Who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?"

The phrase "coming wrath" (mellouses orges) indicates that John understands his own moment as the last window before judgment falls. The agricultural metaphor he deploys reinforces this: "Already the axe is laid to the root of the trees" -- not poised above, not swinging, but already positioned at the root. The imminence is total.

John's demand is specific: "Bear fruit worthy of repentance" (karpon axion tes metanoias). The word "fruit" (karpos) appears here for the first time in Matthew, but the author will use it repeatedly and consistently throughout the document. In 7:16-20, fruit identifies true versus false prophets. In 12:33, the tree is known by its fruit. In 13:8 and 13:23, the good soil produces fruit. In 21:19, the fig tree without fruit is cursed. In 21:43, the kingdom will be given to a people producing its fruits. The author's usage is consistent: "fruit" always means the visible, tangible evidence of genuine response to God. It is never abstract or invisible. John's demand here sets the pattern for the entire document.

John then strikes at the deepest assumption of his audience: "Do not presume to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father.'" This is an extraordinary claim. Abrahamic descent was the foundational marker of covenant identity. John declares it insufficient. God is able "from these stones" (ek ton lithon touton) to raise up children to Abraham. The wordplay between "children" (tekna) and "stones" (lithon) may echo the Hebrew/Aramaic banim/abanim, but the theological point is clear regardless: covenant identity is not secured by genealogy alone. What matters is the fruit -- the evidence of genuine repentance.

This connects directly to the author's own genealogy in chapter 1. That genealogy carefully traced Jesus's Abrahamic and Davidic descent. But now through John, the author signals that physical descent from Abraham, while it matters for establishing Jesus's credentials, does not automatically secure standing for everyone who shares that descent. The axe is at the root. The criterion is fruit.

The judgment is binary: "Every tree not bearing good fruit is cut down and thrown into fire." There is no third category -- no fruitless tree that remains standing. The author presents this as a total sorting.

## The Coming One and the Two Baptisms (vv. 11-12)

John now distinguishes his own role from the one coming after him. "I baptize you with water for repentance" -- his baptism is preparatory, connected to the turning (metanoia) he has been demanding. "But he who comes after me is mightier than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry."

The sandal reference is significant in a first-century Jewish context. Carrying or loosening sandals was considered a task beneath even a Hebrew slave -- it was work assigned to Gentile slaves. John is placing himself below the lowest servant in relation to the coming one.

The coming one "will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (en pneumati hagio kai pyri). The pronoun "you" (hymas) here is addressed broadly -- not only to the Pharisees and Sadducees but to all present. The question is whether "Holy Spirit and fire" describe one baptism or two. The next verse provides the interpretive key.

Verse 12 explains with a threshing floor image: "His winnowing fork is in his hand, and he will clear his threshing floor, gathering the wheat into the barn but burning the chaff with unquenchable fire." This is a separation image. The threshing floor does not improve the grain -- it separates what is already wheat from what is already chaff. The wheat goes to the barn (gathering, safety); the chaff goes to fire (destruction). The "unquenchable" (asbesto) character of the fire means it cannot be stopped or put out until it has consumed what is thrown into it.

Read in light of verse 12, the "Holy Spirit and fire" of verse 11 appear to describe two outcomes for two groups, not one experience for one group. The wheat receives the Spirit; the chaff receives the fire. This reading is consistent with John's entire speech, which has been about sorting: fruit-bearing trees versus fruitless ones, wheat versus chaff, those who repent versus those who presume on Abrahamic descent.

The threshing floor image has deep roots in Israel's scriptures. In Isaiah 41:15-16, God makes Israel into a threshing sledge to thresh the mountains (hostile nations), and the wind carries them away. In Jeremiah 15:7, God winnows his own people with a winnowing fork "in the gates of the land" because they did not turn from their ways. The image can apply to nations being judged or to Israel itself being sorted. John's usage falls into the latter category: Israel itself is on the threshing floor.

## Jesus Comes to John for Baptism (vv. 13-15)

Jesus arrives from Galilee at the Jordan "to be baptized by him" -- the pronoun "him" referring to John. John resists: "I need to be baptized by you, and you come to me?" John recognizes the incongruity. He has just declared himself unworthy to carry this one's sandals, and now this one stands before him requesting the baptism of repentance.

Jesus's answer is precise: "Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness" (plerосаi pasan dikaiosynen). Two key words require attention.

"Fulfill" (plerosai) is a programmatic word for this author. It appeared in 1:22 ("all this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken"), 2:15, 2:17, and 2:23. Each time, it describes the completion or filling-up of what God declared through the prophets. Jesus uses the same word here, but applies it not to a specific prophetic text but to "all righteousness." The fulfillment is comprehensive -- it is about completing the full scope of what God requires.

"Righteousness" (dikaiosyne) is equally important in Matthew's larger argument. The author will use it at 5:6 (those who hunger and thirst for righteousness), 5:10 (those persecuted for righteousness' sake), 5:20 (your righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees), 6:1 (practicing righteousness before people), 6:33 (seek first the kingdom and his righteousness), and 21:32 (John came "in the way of righteousness"). Across these uses, righteousness means conformity to God's will and purposes -- doing what God requires. Jesus submits to John's baptism not because he has sins to confess but because doing so is part of completing the full pattern of what God's purposes demand. The "us" in "it is fitting for us" includes both Jesus and John -- both are playing their roles in the divine plan.

The word "now" (arti) is also notable. It implies that this arrangement is temporary -- appropriate for this moment in the unfolding plan, even if the normal hierarchy (John as lesser, Jesus as greater) would suggest otherwise.

## The Heavens Open and the Divine Declaration (vv. 16-17)

After Jesus is baptized, "the heavens were opened to him" (eneochthesan auto hoi ouranoi). The pronoun "him" refers to Jesus. The opening of the heavens recalls Ezekiel 1:1 ("the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God") and Isaiah 64:1 ("Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down"). In Isaiah's prayer, the opened heavens meant God's direct intervention in the world. The author presents this as that kind of moment.

"He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming upon him." The Spirit's descent onto Jesus echoes several prophetic texts. Isaiah 11:2: "The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" -- describing the shoot from the stump of Jesse, the ideal Davidic king. Isaiah 42:1: "I have put my Spirit upon him" -- describing the Servant of YHWH. Isaiah 61:1: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me, because he has anointed me" -- the anointed herald of good news. The Spirit's descent is an anointing, identifying Jesus as both the Davidic king and the Servant figure from Isaiah.

The voice from heaven declares: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (houtos estin ho huios mou ho agapetos, en ho eudokesa). This sentence combines language from at least two texts:

- **Psalm 2:7**: "You are my Son" -- a royal enthronement psalm, originally spoken at the coronation of the Davidic king. In its original setting, God declares the king to be his son, establishing the king's authority over the nations.

- **Isaiah 42:1**: "My chosen one in whom my soul delights" -- describing the Servant of YHWH, whose mission is to bring justice to the nations, not by force but by faithfulness.

The voice fuses the royal identity (Psalm 2) with the servant identity (Isaiah 42). The first-century hearer would hear both: this is the king, and this is the servant. The author has been building toward this. The genealogy established Davidic royal lineage. The birth narrative showed prophetic fulfillment. Now heaven itself confirms the identity -- and it is an identity that carries both royal authority and the servant's mission.

The use of "this is" (houtos estin) rather than "you are" (su ei, as in Psalm 2:7 directly) is significant. The declaration is made to onlookers, not privately to Jesus. It is a public announcement: this one, here, is the Son.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

Matthew 3 accomplishes several things within the author's developing case:

1. **It introduces the kingdom of heaven as an imminent reality.** This phrase -- "the kingdom of heaven has drawn near" -- appears here for the first time. Jesus will repeat it verbatim in 4:17, and it will pervade the entire document. The author introduces it through John and then transfers it to Jesus, showing continuity between herald and king.

2. **It establishes that covenant identity requires more than genealogy.** John's declaration that God can raise children of Abraham from stones, and his demand for fruit worthy of repentance, sets up a theme the author will pursue through the entire narrative: belonging to God's people is demonstrated by conduct, not guaranteed by descent.

3. **It introduces the judgment-as-sorting motif.** The axe at the root, the threshing floor, the wheat and chaff -- these images of total, binary separation will recur throughout Matthew (13:24-30, 13:47-50, 25:31-46). The author builds a consistent picture: the coming of the kingdom involves the sorting of people based on their response.

4. **It publicly identifies Jesus through heaven's own voice.** After two chapters of indirect identification (genealogy, prophetic geography, magi's recognition), the author now provides direct, divine identification. The identity announced -- Son of God combining Davidic royalty and Isaianic servanthood -- will govern how every subsequent action and teaching of Jesus should be understood.

5. **It introduces "righteousness" as a governing concept.** Jesus's first spoken words in this document are about fulfilling "all righteousness." This word will become one of the author's most important, defining what it means to live under God's reign.
