# 1 Timothy 5 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**The document's genre and occasion:** This is a mandate letter from Paul to Timothy, his delegate in Ephesus, authorizing and instructing him to manage a community in crisis. The crisis, identified in chapter 1, is false teaching -- certain persons promote myths, genealogies, and speculative elaborations of the Torah rather than the stewardship from God that operates through faith (1:3-7). The letter functions simultaneously as personal correspondence and public authorization: it will be read before the Ephesian assembly, and its instructions carry apostolic authority. The letter's stated purpose appears in 3:14-15: "I am writing these things to you so that, if I delay, you may know how one ought to behave in the household of God, which is the church of the living God, a pillar and buttress of the truth." The phrase "household of God" (oikos theou) is critical for chapter 5, which is entirely concerned with the management of relationships and responsibilities within that household.

**What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Timothy 4):** Chapter 4 opens with an explicit warning: "The Spirit expressly says that in later times some will depart from the faith by devoting themselves to deceitful spirits and teachings of demons, through the insincerity of liars whose consciences are seared" (4:1-2). Two specific prohibitions of the false teachers are identified: "they forbid marriage and require abstinence from foods that God created to be received with thanksgiving" (4:3). Paul counters: everything God created is good, and nothing is to be rejected if received with thanksgiving, for it is made holy by the word of God and prayer (4:4-5). The chapter then turns to Timothy personally. He is to be "a good servant of Christ Jesus, being trained in the words of the faith and of the good teaching that you have followed" (4:6). He is to "have nothing to do with irreverent, silly myths" (4:7) -- the same myths identified in 1:4. He is to "train himself for godliness" (4:7), which "holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come" (4:8). He is to "command and teach these things" (4:11), to let no one despise his youth (4:12), to devote himself to public reading, exhortation, and teaching (4:13), and not to neglect the gift given to him through prophecy with the laying on of hands by the council of elders (4:14). The chapter closes with a comprehensive charge: "Practice these things, immerse yourself in them, so that all may see your progress. Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching. Persist in this, for by so doing you will save both yourself and your hearers" (4:15-16).

The final verse of chapter 4 is the immediate bridge to chapter 5. Paul has just told Timothy that his conduct and teaching will save both himself and his hearers. Chapter 5 specifies what that conduct looks like when applied to the various groups who compose the assembly: older and younger men and women, widows, and elders. The transition from "keep a close watch on yourself" (4:16) to "do not rebuke an older man but encourage him as you would a father" (5:1) is seamless: Paul moves from the general principle (watch yourself and your teaching) to the specific application (here is how you handle each group).

**What the author argues immediately after (1 Timothy 6):** Chapter 6 opens with instructions about slaves under the yoke: they are to regard their masters as worthy of all honor, "so that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled" (6:1). Those who have believing masters are not to be disrespectful on account of the brotherhood but are to serve them even better, "since those who benefit by their good service are believers and beloved" (6:2a). Paul then returns to the false teaching crisis: "If anyone teaches a different doctrine and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that accords with godliness, he is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing" (6:3-4a). Such teaching produces "envy, dissension, slander, evil suspicions, and constant friction among people who are depraved in mind and deprived of the truth, imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (6:4b-5). Paul contrasts this with the statement that "godliness with contentment is great gain" (6:6), proceeds to warn about the love of money (6:9-10), and then delivers a final personal charge to Timothy (6:11-16), instructions for the rich (6:17-19), and a closing exhortation to "guard the deposit" (6:20-21).

**How chapter 5 connects the two:** Chapter 4 concluded with Timothy's personal responsibility -- his self-watch, his teaching, his persistence. Chapter 6 will address economic relationships (slaves and masters, love of money, instructions to the rich) and return to the false teaching problem. Chapter 5 stands between them as the detailed working-out of what it means for Timothy to manage the household of God. The chapter's three major sections -- conduct toward different age groups (5:1-2), the care of widows (5:3-16), and the treatment of elders (5:17-25) -- together constitute a practical manual for household management within the community. The word oikos ("house, household") and its cognates pervade the chapter: widows are to "manage their households" (oikodespotein, 5:14), families are to care for their own (5:4, 8), and the entire chapter assumes the framework announced in 3:15 -- the community is the household of God, and Timothy must know how to conduct himself within it. The chapter is not a miscellaneous collection of rulings; it is the practical content of the oikonomia theou ("stewardship of God," 1:4) that the false teachers have replaced with speculation.

## Verses 1-2: The Household Framework -- How Timothy Should Address Each Group

"Do not rebuke an older man but encourage him as you would a father, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters, in all purity."

**"Do not rebuke an older man"** (presbytēro mē epiplēxēs). The noun presbyteros can mean either "older man" (in terms of age) or "elder" (a recognized leader of the community). The word will appear again in verses 17 and 19 with the technical sense of community leader ("elders who rule well," 5:17; "an accusation against an elder," 5:19). Here in verse 1, the context strongly suggests the age meaning rather than the office meaning. The reason is the fourfold structure that follows: older men, younger men, older women, younger women. Paul is classifying the community by age and gender, establishing familial analogies for each group. This is the household model at work -- the community is to be treated as a family, and Timothy's conduct toward each group should mirror the behavior appropriate within a family.

The verb epiplēssein ("to strike at, to rebuke sharply, to censure") means to attack with words -- a harsh, cutting reprimand. The word appears only here in the New Testament. In classical Greek, it describes physical striking, but by the first century it had acquired the metaphorical sense of verbal assault. Paul does not tell Timothy never to correct an older man; he tells him not to do it with verbal violence. The alternative is positive: parakalein ("to encourage, to exhort, to appeal to, to come alongside") -- the same verb Paul used in 1:3 ("as I urged you") and that appears throughout the Pauline letters as the standard word for brotherly exhortation (Romans 12:1; 1 Corinthians 1:10; 2 Corinthians 5:20). Timothy is to encourage an older man hos patera ("as a father") -- as one would appeal to one's own father, with respect, deference, and affection rather than with harshness and confrontation.

**"Younger men as brothers"** (neōterous hōs adelphous). The adjective neōteros ("younger") describes men closer to Timothy's own age. The relational analogy is adelphos ("brother") -- the equality of peers within the same family. Timothy should treat younger men not as subordinates to be ordered around but as brothers to be engaged with mutual respect.

**"Older women as mothers"** (presbyteras hōs mēteras). The same familial model: an older woman is to be treated with the honor and tenderness one gives a mother. The use of mētēr ("mother") is not decorative but functional -- it prescribes a specific quality of relationship: reverence, care, and deference.

**"Younger women as sisters, in all purity"** (neōteras hōs adelphas en pasē hagneia). The relational analogy for younger women is adelphē ("sister"). The addition of en pasē hagneia ("in all purity") is significant. The noun hagneia ("purity, chastity, moral cleanness") is related to hagnos ("pure, chaste, holy") and describes freedom from sexual impropriety or even the appearance of it. The qualification is specific to Timothy's relationship with younger women and reflects practical wisdom: a young man working closely with young women in a community setting must conduct himself with conspicuous propriety. The emphasis on "all" (pasē) -- complete, thoroughgoing, every kind of purity -- underscores the seriousness of this boundary. Timothy's authority must never be compromised by even a hint of sexual irregularity.

The fourfold classification establishes the governing principle for everything that follows in the chapter: the community is a household, its members are family, and Timothy's authority must be exercised with the relational qualities appropriate to family rather than with the impersonal harshness of a bureaucratic administrator. This principle does not eliminate authority -- Timothy still has a mandate to charge, correct, and command -- but it defines the manner in which that authority is to be exercised.

## Verses 3-8: True Widows and Family Responsibility

"Honor widows who are truly widows. But if a widow has children or grandchildren, let them first learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents, for this is pleasing in the sight of God. She who is truly a widow, left all alone, has set her hope on God and continues in supplications and prayers night and day, but she who is self-indulgent is dead even while she lives. Command these things as well, so that they may be without reproach. But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."

**"Honor widows who are truly widows"** (chēras tima tas ontōs chēras). The verb timan ("to honor, to value, to show respect") carries both relational and material dimensions. In the LXX and in Jewish usage, to "honor" one's parents (Exodus 20:12, tima ton patera sou kai tēn mētera sou) includes practical care, not merely verbal respect. The fifth commandment's "honor" was understood to include economic support, shelter, and provision. The same verb will appear in verse 17 for elders who "rule well" and are to receive "double honor" (diplēs timēs) -- where the material dimension (compensation) is made explicit. When Paul says "honor widows," he means provide for them, support them, give them the community's care. But he immediately qualifies: tas ontōs chēras -- "the truly widows," "the ones who are really widows." The adverb ontōs ("truly, really, in fact") distinguishes a category. Not every woman who has lost a husband qualifies for community support. The rest of the paragraph defines who does and who does not.

The social context is essential. In the first-century Mediterranean world, a woman without a husband or male protector was economically vulnerable in ways that have no modern equivalent. Women generally could not own property independently (with limited exceptions in Roman law), could not conduct business in their own names, and depended on the household structure -- father, then husband, then sons -- for economic survival. A widow without children, without family, without independent resources was among the most vulnerable persons in society. The Torah specifically commanded care for widows alongside orphans and sojourners -- the three categories of the defenseless. Deuteronomy 10:18: God "executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing." Deuteronomy 14:29: the tithe of the third year is for "the Levite, the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow." Deuteronomy 24:17, 19-21: the legal protections for widows include the right to glean from the harvest. Exodus 22:22-24: "You shall not mistreat any widow or fatherless child. If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry, and my wrath will burn." Isaiah 1:17: "Learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause." Psalm 68:5 (LXX 67:6): God is "father of the fatherless and protector of widows." James 1:27: "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction."

The community's care for widows was not optional generosity but a fundamental obligation rooted in the Torah and the prophets. Paul's concern in this passage is not whether widows should be cared for -- that is settled -- but how to administer the care wisely, distinguishing those who genuinely need community support from those who have other resources.

**"But if a widow has children or grandchildren, let them first learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents"** (ei de tis chēra tekna ē ekgona echei, manthanetōsan prōton ton idion oikon eusebein kai amoibas apodidonai tois progonois). The pronoun structure requires careful tracking. "If a widow has children or grandchildren" identifies the widow's situation. "Let them learn" (manthanetōsan, third person plural imperative) -- the "them" refers to the children or grandchildren, not to the widow. It is the descendants who are commanded to learn something. What they must learn is two things:

(1) **"To show godliness to their own household"** (ton idion oikon eusebein). The verb eusebein ("to show reverence, to act with piety, to be devout toward") is the verbal form of eusebeia ("godliness, piety"), one of the letter's key terms (2:2; 3:16; 4:7, 8; 6:3, 5, 6, 11). Eusebeia in this letter consistently denotes proper reverence and duty -- right conduct that flows from right relationship to God. Here the object of eusebeia is not God directly but ton idion oikon ("their own household") -- their own family. The instruction is pointed: the first expression of godliness is not in the temple or the assembly but in the home. A person who claims eusebeia toward God but neglects their own family has contradicted their profession. The word prōton ("first") establishes priority: before anything else, before community involvement or public display, the children and grandchildren must show godliness at home.

(2) **"To make some return to their parents"** (amoibas apodidonai tois progonois). The noun amoibē ("return, recompense, repayment") appears only here in the New Testament. It denotes a reciprocal payment -- giving back something in exchange for what was received. The verb apodidonai ("to give back, to repay, to render what is owed") reinforces the reciprocal nature: this is not charity but debt. The parents or grandparents (progonoi, "ancestors, forebears, those who came before") raised the children; the children now owe them care in return. The concept is identical to the fifth commandment's logic: children owe their parents honor, and that honor includes material support.

**"For this is pleasing in the sight of God"** (touto gar estin apodekton enōpion tou theou). The phrase apodekton enōpion tou theou ("acceptable/pleasing before God") appeared previously in 2:3, where prayer for all people was declared "good and pleasing in the sight of God our Savior." The same formula here applies to family care: providing for one's parents and grandparents is not merely a cultural obligation but something that God finds acceptable -- it accords with God's own character as the one who defends widows and orphans.

**"She who is truly a widow, left all alone, has set her hope on God and continues in supplications and prayers night and day"** (hē de ontōs chēra kai memonōmenē ēlpiken epi ton theon kai prosmenei tais deēsesin kai tais proseuchais nyktos kai hēmeras). Now Paul defines the "truly widow." She is characterized by three things:

(1) She is memonōmenē ("left alone, isolated, bereft") -- the perfect passive participle of monoun ("to leave alone, to isolate"), from monos ("alone"). She has no family network to support her. She is genuinely alone.

(2) She has "set her hope on God" (ēlpiken epi ton theon). The verb elpizein ("to hope, to trust, to set one's expectation") in the perfect tense (ēlpiken) indicates a settled state: she has placed her hope on God and continues in that posture. Her hope is not on human resources (she has none) but on God. The preposition epi with the accusative ("upon") indicates the object on which her hope rests. This echoes the widow of Luke 18:1-8, who persistently brings her cause to the judge and will not relent, and whom Jesus uses as a model of faith. It also echoes the portrait of the widow Anna in Luke 2:36-37, who "did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day."

(3) She "continues in supplications and prayers night and day" (prosmenei tais deēsesin kai tais proseuchais nyktos kai hēmeras). The verb prosmenein ("to remain in, to continue in, to persist in") describes ongoing, habitual activity. The phrase nyktos kai hēmeras ("night and day") is a Hebraic expression for continuous, unceasing practice. This widow's life is defined by prayer -- she has nothing else and no one else, and she has turned her entire existence toward God. She is the community's intercessor, its living embodiment of dependence on God.

**"But she who is self-indulgent is dead even while she lives"** (hē de spatalousa zōsa tethnēken). The verb spatalan ("to live luxuriously, to live in pleasure, to be self-indulgent, to give oneself to pleasure") describes a life oriented toward comfort and consumption rather than toward God. The same verb appears in James 5:5: "You have lived on the earth in luxury (etryphēsate) and in self-indulgence (espatalēsate). You have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter." A widow who uses her situation not for prayer and dependence on God but for self-indulgent living has contradicted the very condition that would make her eligible for community support. The judgment is severe: zōsa tethnēken -- "living, she has died." The participle zōsa ("living, being alive") describes her physical state; the perfect tense tethnēken ("she has died") describes her spiritual state. She is physically alive but spiritually dead. The perfect tense indicates a completed action with ongoing results: her death is an accomplished reality, not a threatened possibility.

The language of being "dead while alive" has precedent in Israel's prophetic tradition. Ezekiel 37:1-14 describes Israel as dead bones -- the people exist physically but are spiritually dead. The prodigal son in Jesus' parable was described by his father as having been "dead" and now being "alive again" (Luke 15:24, 32), where "dead" described his state of alienation from the household, not his physical condition. The widow who lives for pleasure has removed herself from the life that comes through hope in God and has entered a state of spiritual death.

**"Command these things as well, so that they may be without reproach"** (kai tauta parangelle, hina anepilēmptoi ōsin). The verb parangellein ("to command, to charge, to give orders") -- the same military language from 1:3 and 1:18 -- indicates that these are not suggestions. Timothy is to issue authoritative instructions. The purpose is stated: hina anepilēmptoi ōsin -- "so that they may be without reproach." The adjective anepilēmptos ("not to be laid hold of, beyond reproach, irreproachable") is composed of the alpha-privative + epilambanesthai ("to seize, to lay hold of, to take hold of"). A person who is anepilēmptos cannot be seized by accusers -- there is nothing in their conduct that provides a handle for criticism. The same word appears in 3:2 as a qualification for the overseer ("above reproach") and in 6:14 for Timothy himself ("keep the commandment unstained, above reproach"). The "they" (the subject of ōsin) refers most naturally to the families and widows together -- all parties involved should conduct themselves so that outsiders and insiders alike can find no legitimate criticism.

**"But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever"** (ei de tis tōn idiōn kai malista oikeiōn ou pronoei, tēn pistin ērnētai kai estin apistou cheirōn). This verse delivers one of the letter's most severe judgments. The verb pronoein ("to take thought for, to provide for, to care for in advance") means active provision -- not merely feeling concern but taking concrete steps to supply what is needed. The scope is twofold: tōn idiōn ("one's own," i.e., relatives in the broader sense) and kai malista oikeiōn ("and especially members of one's household" -- those in the immediate domestic circle). The phrase kai malista ("and especially, and above all") creates a narrowing focus: provide for your relatives generally, but above all for those in your own household.

The judgment is devastating: tēn pistin ērnētai -- "he has denied the faith." The verb arneisthai ("to deny, to reject, to refuse to acknowledge") is the word used for Peter's denial of Jesus (Matthew 26:70, 72; Mark 14:68, 70; Luke 22:57). In 2 Timothy 2:12, Paul writes: "if we deny him, he also will deny us." In Titus 1:16, the false teachers "profess to know God, but they deny him by their works." The denial here is not verbal -- the person may still profess the faith with their mouth -- but practical. By failing to provide for their own family, they have enacted a denial of everything the faith teaches about love, responsibility, and the character of God. The faith that produces love from a pure heart, good conscience, and sincere faith (1:5) cannot coexist with the neglect of one's own dependents. The profession is emptied of its content when the practice contradicts it.

The comparison is equally severe: estin apistou cheirōn -- "he is worse than an unbeliever." The apistos ("unbeliever, one without faith") is someone outside the community of faith. Even unbelievers -- pagans, those who do not know God -- provide for their families. The natural law written in the human conscience, the basic human instinct of care for one's kin, operates even among those who have no knowledge of the gospel. A believer who fails to do what even an unbeliever would do has fallen below the moral floor of common humanity. They are not merely equivalent to unbelievers; they are worse (cheirōn, the comparative of kakos, "bad"). This is the same logic Paul employed in 1 Corinthians 5:1, where sexual immorality "of a kind not even tolerated among pagans" was found in the Corinthian community. The community of faith should exceed the world's standard, not fall beneath it.

This verse has direct implications for the letter's overall argument. The false teachers promote speculations and myths instead of the oikonomia theou ("stewardship of God," 1:4). Paul has been describing the actual content of that stewardship -- how the household of God operates in practice. The most basic element of that stewardship is the provision for one's own household. Anyone who fails at this most elemental level has denied the very faith they claim to profess.

## Verses 9-10: Enrollment of Widows -- The Qualifications

"Let a widow be enrolled if she is not less than sixty years of age, having been the wife of one husband, and having a reputation for good works: if she has brought up children, has shown hospitality, has washed the feet of the saints, has cared for the afflicted, and has devoted herself to every good work."

**"Let a widow be enrolled"** (chēra katalegesthō). The verb katalegein ("to enroll, to register, to place on a list") appears only here in the New Testament. In classical and Hellenistic Greek, it had a technical sense: to register for military service, to place on an official roll. The term indicates that the community maintained a formal list or register of widows who received ongoing support. This was not ad hoc charity but an organized system of care -- a recognized institution within the community. The widow who was "enrolled" received regular provision and may also have taken on specific responsibilities within the community's life (the description that follows suggests women of active service, not merely passive recipients).

**"If she is not less than sixty years of age"** (mē elatton etōn hexēkonta gegonuia). The age requirement -- sixty years -- was significant in the first-century world. Life expectancy was considerably lower than in modern societies, and a woman who had reached sixty was considered elderly. The age threshold served multiple purposes. First, it ensured that the community's resources went to those genuinely beyond the ability to support themselves through remarriage or labor. Second, it corresponded to the point at which remarriage was unlikely and the woman's status as a permanent widow was settled. Third, it may have reflected a cultural understanding of the age at which a woman passed from active household management to a role defined primarily by prayer and service.

**"Having been the wife of one husband"** (henos andros gynē). The phrase is the exact mirror of the qualification for overseers (3:2, mias gynaikos andra, "husband of one wife") and for deacons (3:12, "husband of one wife"). The meaning of this phrase has been debated, but the text itself provides its own interpretive framework through the parallel: whatever "husband of one wife" means for the overseer, "wife of one husband" means the same for the enrolled widow. The phrase most likely describes marital fidelity -- a woman who was faithful to her husband during his lifetime, a one-man woman. Whether it excludes women who remarried after a husband's death or who were divorced is not addressed in the text; the emphasis falls on the quality of marital devotion rather than on a legal count.

**"And having a reputation for good works"** (en ergois kalois martyroumenē). The verb martyreisthai ("to be witnessed, to be testified about, to have a reputation") in the passive indicates that others testify about her -- her good works are publicly known and attested. The adjective kalos ("good, noble, beautiful, excellent") characterizes the works as worthy. This parallels 2:10, where women who profess godliness are to adorn themselves "with good works" (di' ergōn agathōn). The enrolled widow is one whose good works are already a matter of community record.

Paul then provides five specific examples of good works that constitute this reputation:

(1) **"If she has brought up children"** (ei eteknotrophēsen). The verb teknotrophein ("to bring up children, to rear children, to nourish children") appears only here in the New Testament. It combines teknon ("child") with trophein ("to nourish, to feed, to rear"). The word describes not merely bearing children but the active, sustained work of raising them -- feeding, teaching, nurturing, forming. This may include not only her own biological children but also orphans or other children she took into her care.

(2) **"Has shown hospitality"** (ei exenodochēsen). The verb xenodochein ("to receive strangers, to show hospitality, to take in guests") appears only here in the New Testament. It combines xenos ("stranger, guest, foreigner") with dechesthai ("to receive, to welcome"). Hospitality was one of the most highly valued virtues in the ancient Mediterranean world and in Jewish tradition specifically. Abraham's reception of the three visitors at Mamre (Genesis 18:1-8) was the paradigmatic act of hospitality. The Torah commanded: "You shall not oppress a sojourner. You know the heart of a sojourner, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 23:9). In Hebrews 13:2: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" -- a direct reference to the Abraham story. Hospitality in the early communities was not merely a social nicety; it was essential to the movement's survival. Traveling missionaries, messengers, and displaced believers depended on the hospitality of local believers for shelter and sustenance (3 John 5-8; Romans 12:13; Romans 16:1-2). A woman who had shown hospitality had demonstrated the character of the God who welcomes the outcast.

(3) **"Has washed the feet of the saints"** (ei hagiōn podas enipsen). The verb niptein ("to wash") with podas ("feet") describes a specific act of menial service. In the first-century world, where people walked on dusty roads in sandals, foot-washing was a basic act of hospitality performed by the lowest-ranking member of a household -- typically a slave. Abraham offered water for foot-washing to his guests (Genesis 18:4). Abigail offered to wash the feet of David's servants (1 Samuel 25:41), presenting herself as a servant. Jesus washed his disciples' feet (John 13:1-17) and explicitly drew the moral: "If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you" (John 13:14-15). The phrase "saints" (hagiōn, "holy ones, set-apart ones") is Paul's standard designation for members of the believing community (Romans 1:7; 1 Corinthians 1:2; 2 Corinthians 1:1; Philippians 1:1). The enrolled widow has demonstrated willingness to perform the lowliest service for fellow believers -- she has not stood on her dignity but has stooped to serve.

(4) **"Has cared for the afflicted"** (ei thlibomenois epērkesen). The verb eparkein ("to provide assistance, to help, to relieve, to be sufficient for") appears only here and in verse 16 in the New Testament. The dative thlibomenois (present passive participle of thlibein, "to press, to afflict, to oppress, to distress") describes those who are under pressure, suffering, experiencing difficulty. The enrolled widow has demonstrated active care for people in distress -- not merely sympathy but practical assistance.

(5) **"Has devoted herself to every good work"** (ei panti ergō agathō epēkolouthēsen). The verb epakolouthein ("to follow after, to pursue, to devote oneself to") means to follow closely, to track, to pursue with dedication. The phrase panti ergō agathō ("every good work") is comprehensive -- she has not been selective in her service but has pursued every kind of good work available to her. The word "every" (panti) underscores the comprehensiveness of her character: good works are not occasional acts but the pattern of her life.

The five qualifications together paint a portrait of a woman whose life has been defined by self-giving service: raising children, welcoming strangers, performing humble tasks, caring for the suffering, and pursuing every kind of good. These are the credentials for enrollment -- not wealth, not social status, not theological sophistication, but a demonstrated lifetime of practical godliness.

## Verses 11-15: Younger Widows -- The Problem and the Solution

"But refuse to enroll younger widows, for when their passions draw them away from Christ, they desire to marry and so incur condemnation for having abandoned their first pledge. Besides that, they learn to be idlers, going about from house to house, and not only idlers but also gossips and busybodies, saying what they should not. So I would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage their households, and give the adversary no occasion for slander. For some have already strayed after Satan."

**"But refuse to enroll younger widows"** (neōteras de chēras paraitou). The verb paraiteisthai ("to refuse, to decline, to reject, to beg off, to excuse oneself from") means to turn aside a request -- to say no. Timothy is to refuse to place younger widows on the community's roll. The reason is immediately given, and it is practical, not principled: the enrollment apparently involved a commitment to remain unmarried, a pledge to devote oneself to the community's service in widowhood. Younger widows, Paul argues, are unlikely to sustain this commitment.

**"For when their passions draw them away from Christ, they desire to marry"** (hotan gar katastrēniasōsin tou Christou, gamein thelousin). The verb katastrēnian ("to feel strong desires against, to become wanton against") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of kata ("against") + strēnian ("to live in luxury, to be wanton, to feel strong desires"). The genitive tou Christou ("against Christ") identifies the object against which these desires pull: the young widow's natural desire for companionship and marriage works against her commitment to Christ -- specifically, against the pledge she made when she was enrolled. The verb does not describe sinful desire in the abstract; it describes the natural pull of youth that conflicts with a specific commitment made during a period of grief. The desire to marry (gamein thelousin) is not itself condemned -- Paul will explicitly commend remarriage for younger widows in verse 14. The problem is not the desire but the breaking of the pledge.

**"And so incur condemnation for having abandoned their first pledge"** (echousai krima hoti tēn prōtēn pistin ēthetēsan). The noun krima ("judgment, condemnation, verdict") indicates a negative evaluation -- they come under judgment. The reason is stated: hoti tēn prōtēn pistin ēthetēsan -- "because they have set aside their first faith/pledge." The noun pistis here carries the sense of "pledge, commitment, fidelity" rather than "faith" in the theological sense, though the two are not entirely separable. The adjective prōtē ("first") identifies this as the pledge they made at the time of enrollment. The verb athetein ("to set aside, to nullify, to reject, to break") means to treat as invalid something that was binding. In the LXX, athetein is used for the breaking of covenants: Ezekiel 17:15-19 describes Israel's king who "broke the covenant" (ēthetēsen tēn diathēkēn) with the king of Babylon, and God declares that this broken oath will be visited on the king's own head. To athetein a pledge is to act faithlessly, to treat one's own word as worthless.

The prōtē pistis -- "first pledge" or "first faith" -- most likely refers to the commitment made at the time of enrollment. The widow, at the time she was placed on the roll, pledged herself to a life of prayer, service, and singleness devoted to Christ. When younger widows later desire to marry, they break this pledge. The condemnation they incur is not for marrying (which Paul commends in v. 14) but for making a solemn commitment and then abandoning it.

**"Besides that, they learn to be idlers, going about from house to house"** (hama de kai argai manthanousin periechomenai tas oikias). The conjunction hama de kai ("besides that, at the same time also") introduces additional problems beyond the broken pledge. The adjective argos ("idle, inactive, unemployed, lazy") describes the condition of having nothing productive to do. The verb manthanein ("to learn") -- the same verb used in 2:11 for women who are commanded to learn -- here takes on a negative coloring: these younger widows learn the wrong things. Instead of learning sound teaching, they learn idleness. The participle periechomenai (from perierchesthai, "to go around, to visit from place to place") with tas oikias ("the houses") describes a pattern of going from house to house -- visiting homes in the community without productive purpose.

**"And not only idlers but also gossips and busybodies, saying what they should not"** (ou monon de argai alla kai phlyaroi kai periergoi, lalousai ta mē deonta). Three nouns characterize the behavior:

(1) Argai ("idle") -- repeated from the previous clause for emphasis.
(2) Phlyaroi ("gossips, babblers") -- from the verb phlyarein ("to talk nonsense, to gossip, to babble"). The word appears in 3 John 10, where Diotrephes is accused of "talking wicked nonsense" (logois ponērois phlyarōn) against the apostle. The idle house-to-house visiting has become a vehicle for spreading harmful talk.
(3) Periergoi ("busybodies, meddlers") -- from peri ("around") + ergon ("work"), literally "working around" in the sense of concerning oneself with what is not one's business. In Acts 19:19, the related noun perierga describes "magic arts" practiced in Ephesus. In 2 Thessalonians 3:11, Paul uses the same word family: "we hear that some among you walk in idleness (ataktōs), not busy (ergazomenous) but busybodies (periergazomenous)" -- a wordplay between "working" and "working around" (i.e., meddling). The busybody invests energy not in productive work but in managing other people's affairs.

The final phrase lalousai ta mē deonta ("saying the things that are not proper, speaking what they should not") adds a further dimension: these younger widows are not merely idle and nosy but are speaking inappropriately. In the context of a community plagued by false teaching, the possibility that these younger widows are spreading false doctrine as they go from house to house is very real. The description parallels 2 Timothy 3:6-7, where false teachers "creep into households and capture weak women, burdened with sins and led astray by various passions, always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth." The younger widows of 1 Timothy 5 may be the same women, or women in the same situation -- vulnerable to false teaching and then becoming vectors for its spread through their house-to-house visiting.

**"So I would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage their households, and give the adversary no occasion for slander"** (boulomai oun neōteras gamein, teknogonein, oikodespotein, mēdemian aphormēn didonai tō antikeimenō loidorias charin). The verb boulomai ("I desire, I wish, I will") -- the same verb used in 2:8 ("I desire then that in every place the men should pray") -- expresses Paul's considered judgment. His recommendation is fourfold:

(1) Gamein ("to marry") -- remarriage, not singleness, is Paul's recommendation for younger widows. This directly counters the false teachers who "forbid marriage" (4:3). The false teaching promoted an asceticism that Paul regards as dangerous and destructive. Marriage is good; it was created by God (4:3-4); and for younger widows it provides the structure and purpose that prevents the problems described in verses 11-13.

(2) Teknogonein ("to bear children") -- the same word family as teknogonia ("childbearing") in 2:15. Childbearing is affirmed as a worthy and godly activity, again in contrast to the ascetic false teaching.

(3) Oikodespotein ("to manage their households, to rule their houses") -- the verb is composed of oikos ("house") + despotein ("to rule, to govern, to be master of"). The woman is to be the oikodespotēs ("master of the house") of her own household. The word is strong -- it describes real authority and administrative responsibility within the domestic sphere. The same term appears in Mark 14:14 and Luke 22:11 for the "master of the house" who provides the upper room for the Passover. Paul envisions the remarried younger widow as having genuine authority within her household -- she is not a passive dependent but an active manager.

(4) Mēdemian aphormēn didonai tō antikeimenō loidorias charin ("to give the adversary no occasion for slander"). The noun aphormē ("occasion, opportunity, starting point, base of operations") is a military term -- the launching point from which an attack is made. The antikeimenō ("the one who opposes, the adversary") could refer to human opponents (those who would slander the community from outside) or to Satan (the cosmic adversary). The noun loidoria ("slander, reproach, reviling") describes verbal abuse. The young widow who is idle, gossiping, and meddling provides ammunition to those who wish to attack the community. By contrast, the young widow who marries, bears children, and manages her household gives no such opening.

**"For some have already strayed after Satan"** (ēdē gar tines exetrapēsan opisō tou Satana). The verb ektrepesthai ("to turn aside, to deviate, to stray") is the same word used in 1:6 for those who "swerved from" the aims of the charge and "wandered away into vain discussion." The adverb ēdē ("already") indicates that this is not a theoretical warning but a report on events that have occurred. The phrase opisō tou Satana ("after Satan, in the direction of Satan") identifies the destination of their straying. Some younger widows in the Ephesian community have already turned aside from the path of faith and have gone after Satan. Whether this means they have joined in the false teaching, abandoned the community, fallen into immoral behavior, or some combination of these is not specified. The point is that the danger Paul describes is not hypothetical -- it has already happened, and Timothy's policy must account for this reality.

This verse connects to the larger pattern in the letter. In 1:6, some have "swerved" from sound teaching. In 1:19-20, Hymenaeus and Alexander have "made shipwreck of their faith" and were "handed over to Satan." In 4:1, some will "depart from the faith." Now in 5:15, some have "already strayed after Satan." The letter consistently treats the departure from faith as a real and present danger, not a theoretical impossibility. People within the community -- named individuals, younger widows, unnamed false teachers -- have actually departed. The letter exists precisely because this is happening.

## Verse 16: The Household's Responsibility Restated

"If any believing woman has relatives who are widows, let her care for them. Let the church not be burdened, so that it may care for those who are truly widows."

**"If any believing woman has relatives who are widows"** (ei tis pistē echei chēras). The adjective pistē ("believing, faithful") identifies the woman as a member of the community. She "has" (echei) widows -- that is, she has widowed relatives in her family network: a mother, an aunt, a grandmother, other female relatives who have lost their husbands.

**"Let her care for them"** (eparkeito autais). The verb eparkein ("to provide assistance, to help, to be sufficient for") -- the same verb used in verse 10 for the enrolled widow who "cared for the afflicted" -- is now directed at the believing woman. She is to provide the same kind of practical help for her own widowed relatives that the enrolled widow was known for providing to others.

**"Let the church not be burdened"** (hina mē hē ekklēsia barēthē). The verb bareisthai ("to be burdened, to be weighed down") describes the strain on the community's limited resources. The ekklēsia ("assembly, congregation, church") has finite means. If families do not care for their own widows, the burden falls on the community, depleting the resources available for those who have no family at all. Paul's reasoning is economic and practical: family care frees community resources for genuine need.

**"So that it may care for those who are truly widows"** (hina tais ontōs chērais eparkesei). The phrase ontōs chērais ("truly widows") returns to the distinction established in verse 3. The "truly widows" are those who are genuinely alone (v. 5) -- no children, no grandchildren, no believing relatives to care for them. The community's resources should be directed toward these women, not diverted to support widows whose families are capable of providing for them.

The verse restates and reinforces the principle of verses 4 and 8: family care is the primary responsibility; community care is the safety net for those who have no family. The two systems work together, but only if families fulfill their obligation first.

## Verses 17-18: Elders Who Rule Well -- Double Honor

"Let the elders who rule well be considered worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching. For the Scripture says, 'You shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain,' and, 'The laborer deserves his wages.'"

**"Let the elders who rule well"** (hoi kalōs proestōtes presbyteroi). The word presbyteroi now carries its technical sense -- not "older men" (as in v. 1) but "elders," the recognized leaders of the community. The verb proistanai ("to stand before, to preside over, to lead, to manage, to direct") describes the function of these elders: they preside over the community, they provide direction and governance. The adverb kalōs ("well, rightly, nobly") introduces a qualitative distinction: not all elders govern equally well. Some stand out for the quality of their leadership.

**"Be considered worthy of double honor"** (diplēs timēs axiousthōsan). The verb axioun ("to consider worthy, to deem deserving") with the genitive diplēs timēs ("of double honor") is the central statement. The noun timē ("honor, value, price, compensation") appeared in verse 3 for the honoring of widows. There, timē included material provision. Here, the adjective diplēs ("double, twofold") specifies: the elders who rule well deserve double the honor given to others. The word timē in the first-century world could mean both "honor/respect" and "price/compensation" -- the two senses are not separate but intertwined. The "double honor" most likely includes both public recognition (respect for their work) and material compensation (financial support for their labor). The next verse will make the material dimension explicit.

**"Especially those who labor in preaching and teaching"** (malista hoi kopiōntes en logō kai didaskalia). The phrase kai malista ("and especially, above all") narrows the focus within the category of elders who rule well. Among those who govern the community effectively, a subset deserves particular recognition: those who kopiōntes ("labor, toil, work hard, exhaust themselves") in logos ("word, preaching, speaking") and didaskalia ("teaching, instruction"). The verb kopian ("to labor, to toil, to work to exhaustion") describes not casual effort but hard, draining work. Paul uses it for his own apostolic labor (1 Corinthians 15:10, "I worked harder than any of them"; Galatians 4:11, "I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain"; Colossians 1:29, "For this I toil"). The preaching and teaching ministry is work -- demanding, time-consuming, exhausting. Those who do it well deserve compensation that reflects the cost of their labor.

**"For the Scripture says, 'You shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain'"** (legei gar hē graphē, boun aloōnta ou phimōseis). The first scriptural citation is from Deuteronomy 25:4 (LXX). The law prohibited putting a muzzle on an ox while it was working the threshing floor -- the animal that labored to separate the grain was permitted to eat from the grain as it worked. Paul cited this same text in 1 Corinthians 9:9 and drew an explicit application: "Is it for oxen that God is concerned? Does he not certainly speak for our sake? It was written for our sake, because the plowman should plow in hope and the thresher thresh in hope of sharing in the crop" (1 Corinthians 9:9-10). The Torah's concern for the laboring animal establishes a principle: those who labor deserve to benefit from their labor. If even an animal is not denied sustenance while it works, how much more should human workers -- especially those who labor in the word of God -- receive material support?

**"And, 'The laborer deserves his wages'"** (kai, axios ho ergatēs tou misthou autou). The second citation -- "the laborer deserves his wages" -- appears in Luke 10:7, where Jesus sends out the seventy-two and instructs them: "Remain in the same house, eating and drinking what they provide, for the laborer deserves his wages." The identical saying appears in Matthew 10:10 as "the laborer deserves his food." Paul introduces this saying with the same formula used for the Deuteronomy citation -- legei gar hē graphē ("for the Scripture says") -- placing Jesus' words on the same level of authority as the Torah. Whether Paul is citing a written Gospel, an oral tradition of Jesus' sayings, or both, he treats the saying as graphē -- Scripture, authoritative text. The principle is clear: those who work in preaching and teaching perform labor that deserves compensation. The community owes its teachers material support, just as a laborer is owed wages for work done.

## Verses 19-21: Accusations Against Elders -- Due Process and Impartiality

"Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses. As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear. In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels I charge you to keep these rules without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality."

**"Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses"** (kata presbyterou katēgorian mē paradechou, ektos ei mē epi duo ē triōn martyrōn). The noun katēgoria ("accusation, charge, indictment") is a legal term -- the formal bringing of charges against someone. The verb paradechesthai ("to receive, to accept, to admit, to entertain") means to take in, to consider, to give a hearing to. Timothy is not to accept an accusation against an elder casually or on the word of a single person.

The evidentiary standard Paul invokes is drawn directly from the Torah. Deuteronomy 19:15: "A single witness shall not suffice against a person for any crime or for any wrong in connection with any offense that he has committed. Only on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses shall a charge be established." Deuteronomy 17:6: "On the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses the one who is to die shall be put to death; a person shall not be put to death on the evidence of one witness." Numbers 35:30: "If anyone kills a person, the murderer shall be put to death on the evidence of witnesses. But no person shall be put to death on the testimony of one witness." The two-or-three-witness rule is one of the Torah's most fundamental protections against false accusation. Jesus applied the same principle to disputes within the community: "If your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone... But if he does not listen, take one or two others along with you, that every charge may be established by the evidence of two or three witnesses" (Matthew 18:15-16).

The protection is particularly important for elders, who are public figures and therefore targets. A leader who makes decisions, corrects behavior, and enforces standards will inevitably generate opposition. Without the safeguard of multiple witnesses, any disgruntled individual could paralyze the community's leadership by making unfounded accusations.

**"As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear"** (tous hamartanontas enōpion pantōn elenche, hina kai hoi loipoi phobon echōsin). The present participle hamartanontas ("those who are sinning, those who continue to sin") describes ongoing, unrepentant behavior -- not a single lapse but a pattern of sin. The verb elenchein ("to expose, to convict, to rebuke, to correct") means to bring the wrongdoing into the open, to demonstrate that it is wrong, to call the person to account. The setting is enōpion pantōn ("in the presence of all, before everyone") -- the entire assembly witnesses the rebuke. This is public discipline, not private counsel. The shift from verse 19 (where accusations require careful verification) to verse 20 (where confirmed sinners receive public rebuke) is deliberate: the standard of evidence is high, but once the sin is established, the response is public and unambiguous.

The purpose is pedagogical: hina kai hoi loipoi phobon echōsin -- "so that the rest also may have fear." The noun phobos ("fear, reverence, awe, respect") here carries the sense of reverential seriousness -- the awareness that sin has consequences, that the community does not tolerate persistent wrongdoing among its leaders. The "rest" (hoi loipoi) may refer to the other elders or to the community as a whole. The public rebuke serves both corrective and preventive functions: it addresses the wrongdoer and warns the observers.

**"In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels I charge you to keep these rules without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality"** (diamartyromai enōpion tou theou kai Christou Iēsou kai tōn eklektōn angelōn, hina tauta phylaxēs chōris prokrimatos, mēden poiōn kata prosklisin). The verb diamartyresthai ("to solemnly charge, to testify earnestly, to adjure") is the most emphatic form of witness-bearing available -- it means to charge someone in the strongest possible terms, calling on witnesses to attest the seriousness of the obligation. The witnesses Paul invokes are not human: they are God, Christ Jesus, and the elect angels.

The phrase tōn eklektōn angelōn ("the elect angels, the chosen angels") is unusual. The adjective eklektos ("chosen, elect, selected") applied to angels implies a distinction among angelic beings -- these are the angels who remained faithful, who were chosen or approved, in contrast to the angels who fell (cf. 2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6). Their presence as witnesses to Paul's charge places Timothy's conduct under cosmic scrutiny: he is accountable not merely to Paul or to the community but to the heavenly court.

Two vices are prohibited:

(1) **Prokrima** ("prejudgment, prejudice, bias formed before hearing the evidence"). The alpha-privative form (chōris prokrimatos, "without prejudgment") requires Timothy to approach every case with an open mind, not with conclusions already formed. He must not decide before hearing; he must not favor an accusation because he dislikes the accused, or dismiss it because he likes the accused.

(2) **Prosklisis** ("partiality, inclination toward one side, favoritism"). The word is composed of pros ("toward") + klinein ("to lean, to incline"). It describes the act of leaning toward one party at the expense of the other. Timothy must not show favoritism -- not to friends, not to allies, not to the powerful, not to the influential. Every case must be judged on its merits.

The command to avoid partiality has deep roots in the Torah. Leviticus 19:15: "You shall do no injustice in court. You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor." Deuteronomy 1:17: "You shall not be partial in judgment. You shall hear the small and the great alike. You shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God's." Deuteronomy 16:19: "You shall not pervert justice. You shall not show partiality, and you shall not accept a bribe." The command to Timothy is the command given to judges in Israel: judge fairly, without bias, because God himself is impartial.

## Verses 22-25: Timothy's Personal Conduct -- Purity, Health, and the Visibility of Character

"Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands, nor take part in the sins of others. Keep yourself pure. (No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments.) The sins of some people are conspicuous, going before them to judgment, but the sins of others appear later. So also good works are conspicuous, and even those that are not cannot remain hidden."

**"Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands"** (cheiras tacheos mēdeni epitithei). The phrase "laying on of hands" (epitithesis tōn cheirōn) in the early communities signified the conferral of authority, blessing, or commission. In Acts 6:6, the apostles laid hands on the seven chosen to serve. In Acts 13:3, the community at Antioch laid hands on Barnabas and Saul before sending them out. In 1 Timothy 4:14, Timothy himself received his gift "through prophecy with the laying on of hands by the council of elders." In 2 Timothy 1:6, Paul refers to "the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands." The laying on of hands is the community's means of publicly recognizing, commissioning, and empowering someone for a role.

The adverb tacheōs ("quickly, hastily, without due consideration") warns against speed in this process. Timothy is not to rush to ordain or commission people. The context -- immediately following instructions about disciplining sinful elders -- suggests a causal connection: the problems with unworthy elders could be prevented by greater care in the selection process. If Timothy lays hands on someone too quickly, before their character has been adequately tested, and that person later falls into sin, Timothy bears a share of responsibility.

**"Nor take part in the sins of others"** (mēde koinōnei hamartiais allotriais). The verb koinōnein ("to share in, to participate in, to have fellowship with") with the dative hamartiais ("sins") means to become a participant in another person's wrongdoing. The adjective allotrios ("belonging to another, foreign, not one's own") distinguishes: these are not Timothy's own sins but others' sins that he becomes implicated in. The connection to the previous clause is direct: by hastily commissioning someone unfit for leadership, Timothy shares in whatever sins that person subsequently commits in the role Timothy gave them. The responsibility of the one who appoints extends to the conduct of the one appointed.

**"Keep yourself pure"** (seauton hagnon tērei). The verb tērein ("to keep, to guard, to preserve, to maintain") with the reflexive pronoun seauton ("yourself") makes the responsibility personal: Timothy must actively guard his own moral condition. The adjective hagnos ("pure, chaste, innocent, undefiled") echoes the hagneia ("purity") of verse 2, where Timothy was instructed to treat younger women "in all purity." The imperative to maintain purity is comprehensive -- it covers sexual conduct, financial integrity, impartial judgment, and careful use of authority. In a chapter that has addressed the complexities of managing widows, disciplining elders, and navigating accusations, the command to "keep yourself pure" reminds Timothy that his own integrity is the foundation on which all his other responsibilities rest. If he becomes compromised, his ability to judge, correct, and lead is destroyed.

**"No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments"** (mēketi hydropotei, alla oinō oligō chrō dia ton stomachon sou kai tas pyknas sou astheneias). This verse is striking for its specificity and its apparent interruption of the argument. The verb hydropotein ("to drink water, to be a water-drinker") appears only here in the New Testament. The instruction is medical: Timothy has been drinking only water, perhaps as a form of ascetic discipline or perhaps out of personal conviction, and he suffers from stomach problems and frequent illnesses. Paul advises him to use oinō oligō ("a little wine") -- not heavy drinking but moderate medicinal use -- for the sake of his stomach (stomachos, "stomach, opening of the stomach") and his pyknas astheneias ("frequent weaknesses, frequent ailments"). The adjective pyknos ("frequent, numerous, close together") indicates that Timothy's health problems are recurring, not occasional.

The verse's placement between the command to "keep yourself pure" and the concluding observations about the visibility of sins and good works is not accidental. Paul has just told Timothy to keep himself pure. The qualification about wine serves as a clarification: the purity Paul commands does not require ascetic self-deprivation to the point of harming one's health. This connects directly to the false teaching identified in 4:3-5, which "forbids marriage and requires abstinence from foods that God created to be received with thanksgiving." Timothy may have been influenced -- even unwittingly -- by the ascetic tendencies of the false teaching, abstaining from wine as a form of spiritual discipline. Paul corrects this: asceticism that damages health is not godliness. God's good creation, including wine, may be received with thanksgiving and used for legitimate purposes. The command to keep yourself pure (v. 22b) and the permission to use wine for health (v. 23) together define the balanced path: moral purity is essential; physical self-punishment is not.

**"The sins of some people are conspicuous, going before them to judgment, but the sins of others appear later"** (tinōn anthrōpōn hai hamartiai prodēloi eisin proagousai eis krisin, tisin de kai epakolouthousin). The adjective prodēlos ("evident, manifest, conspicuous, clear to all") describes sins that are visible -- obvious to the community, impossible to hide. The verb proagein ("to go before, to precede") pictures these sins as walking ahead of their owner, arriving at the place of judgment before the person does. The metaphor is vivid: some people's sins are their advance party -- everyone can see what they have done before any formal inquiry begins.

But other people's sins epakolouthousin ("follow after, come along behind"). The same verb was used in verse 10 for the enrolled widow who "followed after every good work." Here it describes sins that trail behind -- they are not immediately visible, they do not announce themselves, but they arrive eventually. No sin remains permanently hidden. The truth comes out in time.

**"So also good works are conspicuous, and even those that are not cannot remain hidden"** (hōsautōs kai ta erga ta kala prodēla, kai ta allōs echonta krybēnai ou dynantai). The same principle applies to good works: some are prodēla ("conspicuous, evident") -- visible to all, recognized immediately. Others are ta allōs echonta ("those that are otherwise" -- i.e., good works that are not immediately apparent, that are done quietly, that are hidden from public view). But even these krybēnai ou dynantai ("are not able to be hidden") -- they will eventually come to light. The verb kryptein ("to hide, to conceal") in the passive infinitive (krybēnai) with the negative (ou dynantai) states an impossibility: hidden good works cannot remain hidden forever.

The relevance to the chapter's concerns is direct. Timothy has been instructed to evaluate people: which widows are genuine, which elders rule well, which accusers are credible, which sins deserve public rebuke. Verses 24-25 assure him that character eventually reveals itself. He should not be hasty (v. 22, "do not be hasty in the laying on of hands") because time will disclose what is hidden. Some people's sins are immediately obvious; others take time. Some people's good works are immediately evident; others require patience to discover. The advice is: take your time, be thorough, and trust that the truth will emerge. Character cannot hide indefinitely.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1 Timothy 5 is the practical core of the mandate letter. If the letter's purpose is to tell Timothy "how one ought to behave in the household of God" (3:15), then chapter 5 is the most detailed working-out of that purpose. Every section describes how the community manages its members -- with familial care, with economic responsibility, with due process, and with impartial judgment.

(1) **Verses 1-2 establish the household model.** The community is a family. Timothy's authority is exercised not as a bureaucratic superior but as a family member. Older men are fathers, younger men are brothers, older women are mothers, younger women are sisters. This framework governs everything that follows: the care of widows is not institutional charity but family obligation; the discipline of elders is not corporate management but family accountability; the command to purity is not professional ethics but family integrity.

(2) **Verses 3-8 define the community's obligation to widows and the family's prior responsibility.** The "truly widow" is the woman who has no family, who has set her hope on God, and whose life is defined by prayer. She deserves the community's full support. But when a widow has children or grandchildren, those family members must provide for her first. This is not merely practical but theological: failure to provide for one's own family is a denial of the faith, worse than the conduct of unbelievers. The stewardship of God (1:4) begins at home.

(3) **Verses 9-10 describe the qualifications for enrollment on the community's list of supported widows.** The qualifications are entirely practical: age (sixty years), marital fidelity ("wife of one husband"), and a demonstrated lifetime of good works -- raising children, showing hospitality, washing feet, caring for the afflicted, pursuing every kind of good. The enrolled widow is not a passive recipient but a woman whose entire life has been one of active service.

(4) **Verses 11-15 address the problem of younger widows.** Enrollment is not appropriate for them because their natural desires will conflict with the pledge of devoted singleness, and the idleness that results from enrollment without genuine vocation produces gossip, meddling, and the spread of harmful speech. Paul's solution is the opposite of the false teachers' prescription: younger widows should marry, bear children, and manage their households -- the very activities the false teachers disparage (4:3). Some younger widows have already strayed after Satan -- the danger is real and present, not theoretical.

(5) **Verse 16 restates the principle of family responsibility**, ensuring that the community's resources are reserved for those who genuinely have no other support.

(6) **Verses 17-18 establish the principle of compensation for elders.** Those who rule well deserve double honor -- both respect and material support. Those who labor in preaching and teaching deserve particular recognition. The principle is grounded in two scriptural authorities: the Torah's command not to muzzle the laboring ox (Deuteronomy 25:4) and the saying of Jesus that the laborer deserves his wages (Luke 10:7). Both are cited as "Scripture," placing them on equal authority.

(7) **Verses 19-21 establish due process for accusations against elders.** The Torah's two-or-three-witness rule (Deuteronomy 19:15) protects leaders from unfounded charges. But when sin is established, the rebuke is public, serving both correction and deterrence. Timothy is charged with cosmic solemnity -- before God, Christ Jesus, and the elect angels -- to exercise these responsibilities without prejudgment or partiality. The Torah's command to judges (Leviticus 19:15; Deuteronomy 1:17) governs Timothy's conduct.

(8) **Verses 22-25 address Timothy's personal responsibility in the appointment process and his own conduct.** Hasty ordination leads to shared guilt; careful evaluation prevents it. Timothy must keep himself pure -- morally, sexually, judicially. The aside about wine corrects any ascetic distortion of purity: purity does not require self-harm, and God's good gifts may be received for legitimate purposes. Finally, the chapter closes with an assurance: character will reveal itself. Sins and good works alike come to light eventually. Timothy can afford to be patient and thorough, because time discloses what is hidden.

The chapter's relationship to the letter's controlling themes is direct. The false teachers promote speculation rather than the oikonomia theou -- God's stewardship (1:4). Chapter 5 is the content of that stewardship. The false teachers forbid marriage (4:3); Paul commands younger widows to marry (5:14). The false teachers promote asceticism about food (4:3); Paul tells Timothy to use a little wine for his health (5:23). The false teachers aspire to be teachers without understanding (1:7); Paul establishes qualifications for leaders based on demonstrated character (5:9-10, 22, 24-25). The false teachers have made shipwreck of their faith (1:19); Paul insists that those who fail to provide for their families have denied the faith (5:8). At every point, the practical instructions of chapter 5 stand as the concrete alternative to the false teaching that has destabilized the Ephesian community. Sound doctrine produces sound practice: families that care for their own, communities that honor their faithful widows and their hard-working elders, leaders who judge without partiality, and a delegate who keeps himself pure while managing the household of God.

The chapter also sustains the letter's consistent treatment of faith as a reality that can be denied, abandoned, or destroyed by behavior. The person who does not provide for family "has denied the faith" (5:8) -- not by verbal repudiation but by practical failure. Some younger widows have "already strayed after Satan" (5:15) -- they have departed from the community's faith and life. The possibility of real departure, real denial, and real failure runs through every section of the chapter. Timothy's task is precisely to prevent these outcomes where possible and to respond appropriately where they have already occurred. The community's health depends on his vigilance, his integrity, and his willingness to act -- neither too hastily nor too slowly, neither with favoritism nor with prejudice, but with the steady application of the stewardship of God that operates through faith.
