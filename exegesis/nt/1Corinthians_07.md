# 1 Corinthians 7 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 5-6):** After concluding his four-chapter treatment of the factionalism crisis (chapters 1-4), Paul turned to a series of concrete failures within the Corinthian community. Chapter 5 addressed a specific case of sexual immorality -- a man maintaining a sexual arrangement with his father's wife, a violation of Leviticus 18:8 and Deuteronomy 22:30 (LXX 23:1) -- which the community had not only tolerated but was "puffed up" about (5:2). Paul commanded the community to expel the man, "handing him over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (5:5). The chapter established the distinction between inside and outside: the community judges those within; God judges those without (5:12-13). It closed with the Deuteronomic formula: "Purge the evil person from among you" (5:13, citing Deuteronomy 17:7 et al.).

Chapter 6 addressed two further failures. First (6:1-11), members were taking disputes to pagan courts instead of resolving them internally -- the mirror image of chapter 5's failure. The community that should have judged the immoral man (chapter 5) was outsourcing its other disputes to outsiders (6:1-8). Paul asked: "Do you not know that the saints will judge the world?" (6:2). He then listed categories of people who "will not inherit the kingdom of God" -- pornoi, eidololatrai, moichoi, malakoi, arsenokoitai, kleptai, pleonektai, methysoi, loidoroi, harpages (6:9-10) -- and declared: "And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (6:11). The past tense is emphatic: those identities were left behind.

The second half of chapter 6 (6:12-20) addressed sexual immorality in general, specifically union with prostitutes. This section is the immediate precursor to chapter 7 and its arguments flow directly into what follows. Paul quoted and corrected a Corinthian slogan: "All things are lawful for me" (panta moi exestin, 6:12) -- apparently a phrase some Corinthians used to justify their conduct. Paul's response: "but not all things are beneficial" (all' ou panta sympherei) and "I will not be dominated by anything" (all' ouk ego exousiasthesomai hypo tinos, 6:12). He then argued that the body is not for porneia but "for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (6:13). God raised the Lord and will raise the believers by his power (6:14). The believers' bodies are "members of Christ" (mele Christou, 6:15). To unite Christ's members with a prostitute is to become one body with her, citing Genesis 2:24: "the two shall become one flesh" (6:16). "But the one who joins himself to the Lord is one spirit with him" (6:17). Paul commanded: "Flee porneia" (6:18). Every sin a person commits is "outside the body" (ektos tou somatos), but the sexually immoral person "sins against his own body" (eis to idion soma hamartanei, 6:18). The body is "a temple of the Holy Spirit within you" (naos tou en hymin hagiou pneumatos, 6:19). The final declaration: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (6:19-20).

**What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 8):** Paul introduces a new topic with the same formula he uses to open chapter 7: "Now concerning" (peri de). "Now concerning food offered to idols" (peri de ton eidolothyton, 8:1). This is the second major question the Corinthians raised in their letter. Paul addresses the tension between "knowledge" (gnosis) -- which "puffs up" (physioi, using the same verb that has diagnosed the Corinthian disease since 4:6) -- and "love" (agape), which "builds up" (oikodomei, 8:1). The issue is whether believers may eat meat that has been sacrificed in pagan temples. Paul acknowledges the knowledge that "an idol is nothing" (8:4) and that "there is no God but one" (8:4). But he warns that this knowledge, deployed without regard for the "weak" (astheneis) conscience of other believers, can destroy the brother "for whom Christ died" (8:11). Chapter 8 thus continues the pattern established in chapters 5-7: Paul addresses specific practical questions about how the community's identity in Christ governs its daily conduct -- sexual behavior (chapters 5-7) giving way to eating practices (chapters 8-10). The thread connecting all these sections is the relationship between the believer's body, the community's holiness, and the surrounding pagan culture.

**How chapter 7 connects to what precedes and follows:** Chapter 7 does not change subjects abruptly. It is the direct continuation of the body theology that Paul developed in 6:12-20. Having argued that the body belongs to the Lord, that the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, that "you were bought with a price," and having commanded the community to "flee porneia," Paul now addresses the positive side of the same question: what is the proper place of sexual union, of marriage, of celibacy? The Corinthians' letter apparently included questions about these matters, and Paul signals this with the formula "peri de" ("now concerning") in 7:1 -- the first use of a phrase that will structure much of the rest of the letter (7:1, 7:25, 8:1, 12:1, 16:1, 16:12).

The connection operates at multiple levels. At the level of vocabulary, the word porneia appears in 7:2 as the danger that proper marriage guards against, connecting directly to the porneia Paul condemned in 5:1, 6:13, and 6:18. The phrase "your own body" (to idion soma) from 6:18 reappears conceptually in 7:4, where Paul says neither spouse has authority over their own body. The language of "calling" (kaleo, klesis) that opened the letter (1:1-2, 9, 24, 26) reappears as a major structuring concept in 7:15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22, 24, where Paul establishes the principle that each person should remain in the condition in which God called them. The eschatological framework -- the community living between the "already" of Christ's work and the "not yet" of his return -- which has been present since 1:7-8 ("as you wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ") becomes explicit and urgent in 7:26-31, where Paul describes "the present distress" and the "shortened time" that shapes all his practical counsel.

At the level of argument, chapter 7 completes a logical sequence. Chapter 5 said: this sexual arrangement violates your identity; expel the offender. Chapter 6 said: your body belongs to the Lord; flee porneia. Chapter 7 now says: here is how sexual life properly functions within the community's identity, and here is the broader principle that governs all questions about changing one's status -- remain in the condition in which God called you, because the form of this world is passing away. Chapter 7 also transitions toward chapter 8 by establishing the pattern of responding to specific Corinthian questions with pastoral counsel that applies theological principles to concrete situations.

## Verse 1: The Corinthian Slogan and Paul's Response

"Now concerning the matters about which you wrote: 'It is good for a man not to touch a woman'" (7:1).

The phrase peri de hon egrapsate ("now concerning the things about which you wrote") signals a new section of the letter. Paul shifts from addressing problems he has heard about through reports (Chloe's people, 1:11; the general report, 5:1) to responding to questions the Corinthians themselves raised in a letter to him. The verb egrapsate ("you wrote," aorist of grapho) confirms what was implied in 5:9 -- there is ongoing correspondence between Paul and this community. Their letter contained questions, and Paul is now working through them.

The statement kalon anthropo gynaikos me haptesthai ("it is good for a man not to touch a woman") is almost certainly a quotation from the Corinthians' own letter or a slogan circulating in the community. Several features support this reading. First, Paul uses the same technique elsewhere in this letter, quoting Corinthian slogans and then qualifying or correcting them: "all things are lawful for me" (6:12), "food is for the stomach and the stomach for food" (6:13), "all of us possess knowledge" (8:1). Second, the immediately following verse (7:2) begins with a "but" (de) that introduces a qualification, suggesting that verse 1b states a position Paul is about to modify. Third, the content of the slogan -- an absolute prohibition on sexual contact -- is more extreme than anything Paul himself argues in this chapter; Paul's own position, as the chapter will make clear, is considerably more nuanced.

The verb haptesthai ("to touch, to take hold of, to cling to") is a euphemism for sexual contact. In the LXX, the verb hapto in its middle voice carries this sense: Genesis 20:6, where God tells Abimelech, "I did not let you touch her" (ouk apheka se hapsasthai autes), referring to Sarah; Proverbs 6:29, "so is he who goes in to his neighbor's wife; no one who touches her will go unpunished" (pas ho haptomenos autes ou kathariethesetai). The slogan is not about casual physical contact but about sexual union.

The word kalon ("good, beautiful, fitting, appropriate") asserts that sexual abstinence is a good thing. Some in the Corinthian community have apparently taken Paul's body theology -- the body is the Lord's temple, flee porneia -- and extended it into a general ascetic principle: all sexual contact, even within marriage, should be avoided. This may reflect the influence of certain Greco-Roman philosophical traditions. Stoic thinkers such as Musonius Rufus (a near-contemporary of Paul) debated whether sexual pleasure was permissible even within marriage, arguing that intercourse should be limited to procreation. Certain streams of Cynic philosophy idealized the renunciation of bodily desires altogether. Within Jewish tradition, temporary sexual abstinence for purposes of prayer and purity had precedent (Exodus 19:15, where Moses tells the people to "not go near a woman" before the theophany at Sinai; 1 Samuel 21:4-5, where David's men affirm their purity from women before eating the bread of the Presence). But the Corinthian slogan goes further than temporary abstinence; it asserts abstinence as an absolute good.

Paul's response throughout the chapter will neither fully endorse nor fully reject this slogan. He will affirm that celibacy has genuine value (7:7-8, 26, 32-35, 38, 40) while insisting that marriage is proper, that married couples have mutual obligations, and that the ascetic impulse must not be imposed on those who do not possess the capacity for it.

## Verses 2-5: Marriage, Mutual Obligation, and the Body

"But because of cases of sexual immorality, each man should have his own wife and each woman her own husband" (7:2).

The word de ("but") introduces Paul's qualification of the Corinthian slogan. The phrase dia de tas porneias ("but because of the cases of sexual immorality") identifies the practical danger that unqualified abstinence creates. The plural porneias ("sexual immoralities, instances of fornication") does not refer to one specific case (as in chapter 5) but to the general threat of sexual transgression -- the same porneia Paul commanded them to flee in 6:18. If sexual abstinence is imposed as an absolute rule, including on those who are unable to sustain it, the result will not be holiness but porneia. The ascetic program, taken to its extreme, will produce the very thing it seeks to eliminate.

Paul's counsel is concrete: hekastos ten heautou gynaika echeto kai hekaste ton idion andra echeto -- "each man should have his own wife, and each woman should have her own husband." The verb echo ("to have") in the present imperative indicates an ongoing arrangement: each person should maintain the marital relationship. The reciprocity is notable. Paul does not say "each man should have a wife" (which would leave the woman passive). He says each man should have his own wife and each woman should have her own husband. The two clauses are parallel and symmetrical. Marriage is presented not as a male prerogative but as a mutual arrangement in which both partners have legitimate claims.

The word idion ("one's own") and heautou ("his own") emphasize the exclusivity and particularity of the marital bond. This is not a general permission for sexual activity but a direction toward the specific, committed relationship of marriage. The language echoes the singular "his own body" (to idion soma) from 6:18 and anticipates the body-authority language that follows immediately.

"The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband" (7:3).

The phrase ten opheilomenen eunoian ("the owed/obligated goodwill" or, in many manuscripts, ten opheilen, "the debt, the obligation") identifies the sexual dimension of marriage as a matter of obligation, not merely permission. The verb opheilo ("to owe, to be obligated, to be indebted") treats the sexual relationship as something owed -- a debt that each partner carries toward the other. The concept is not primarily about desire or pleasure but about mutual obligation within the covenant of marriage.

The word eunoia ("goodwill, favor, affection") in some manuscripts, or the simpler opheile ("debt, what is owed") in others, describes the conjugal dimension of the marriage relationship. Paul is not innovating here. Jewish tradition, particularly as reflected in Exodus 21:10, recognized the husband's obligation to provide his wife with food, clothing, and conjugal rights (onah). The Mishnah (Ketubot 5:6, though codified later, reflecting earlier practice) specifies the frequency of conjugal obligation based on the husband's occupation. Paul extends the obligation in both directions: not only does the husband owe the wife, but the wife also owes the husband. The reciprocity is remarkable in the ancient context, where sexual obligation was typically framed as a one-directional male right.

"For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does" (7:4).

This verse is one of the most striking statements about marriage in any first-century text. The verb exousiazo ("to have authority over, to exercise power over") comes from exousia ("authority, right, power"). In 6:12, Paul used the related form exousiasthesomai ("I will not be brought under the authority of anything") to describe his refusal to be dominated. Now the same root describes the authority that each spouse holds over the other's body.

The structure is perfectly symmetrical:

He gyne tou idiou somatos ouk exousiazei alla ho aner -- "the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does."

Homoios de kai ho aner tou idiou somatos ouk exousiazei alla he gyne -- "likewise also the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does."

The word homoios ("likewise, similarly, in the same way") forces the second clause to mirror the first exactly. What is true of the wife's body with respect to the husband is equally true of the husband's body with respect to the wife. Neither spouse is autonomous. Each has yielded bodily authority to the other. The concept of mutual authority over the other's body is without parallel in surviving first-century literature at this level of explicit reciprocity. Roman marriage law gave the paterfamilias authority over the household, including his wife; Jewish marriage law placed certain obligations on the husband but did not describe the wife as having authority over the husband's body. Paul's formulation is distinctively equalizing.

The theological ground for this mutual surrender of bodily autonomy was laid in 6:19-20: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price." If the individual believer does not have autonomous authority over his or her body because that body belongs to the Lord, then within marriage, the body also belongs to the spouse. The hierarchy of belonging runs: the body belongs to the Lord (6:19-20); within the marriage relationship, it also belongs to the spouse (7:4). Neither claim cancels the other. The Lord's ownership of the body includes the marriage relationship as a legitimate expression of bodily life; the spouse's claim on the body operates within the Lord's prior claim.

"Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control" (7:5).

The verb apostereo ("to deprive, to defraud, to rob, to withhold what is owed") is a strong word. In 6:7-8, Paul used the same verb: "Why not rather be defrauded?" (ti ouchi mallon apostereisthe, 6:7). In that context, it described the financial injustice that believers were suffering from other believers. Here it describes sexual withholding within marriage. The use of the same verb connects the two situations: withholding conjugal rights from a spouse is a form of defrauding them -- taking what belongs to them. The present imperative with me (me apostereite) is a prohibition of ongoing action: stop depriving one another, or do not make a practice of depriving one another.

Paul allows one exception: ei meti an ek symphonou pros kairon -- "unless perhaps by agreement for a time." Three conditions limit the exception. First, ek symphonou ("by mutual agreement, by consent") -- the abstinence must be jointly decided, not unilaterally imposed by one partner on the other. The word symphonos (from syn, "together," and phone, "voice") means to be in agreement, to sound together. Neither spouse may impose celibacy on the other. Second, pros kairon ("for a time, for a season") -- the abstinence is temporary, not permanent. The word kairos ("time, season, appointed time") identifies a bounded period. Third, the purpose is specified: hina scholasete te proseuche -- "so that you may devote yourselves to prayer." The verb scholazo ("to be free, to have leisure, to devote oneself") means to make oneself available, to clear space for something. The purpose of temporary abstinence is not ascetic self-denial for its own sake but a positive dedication to prayer. This echoes the Old Testament precedent of temporary sexual abstinence for sacred purposes (Exodus 19:15).

The instruction to "come together again" (kai palin epi to auto ete, or in variant readings, synerchesthe) after the period of prayer requires the resumption of the sexual relationship. The reason: hina me peiraze hymas ho Satanas dia ten akrasian hymon -- "so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control." The word Satanas ("Satan, the adversary") appeared in 5:5 in connection with the expelled man; here the adversary operates differently -- not as the agent of external punishment but as the exploiter of internal weakness. The word akrasia ("lack of self-control, incontinence") describes the inability to restrain one's desires. Paul is realistic about human capacity. Not everyone possesses the same ability to sustain celibacy. Prolonged abstinence within marriage, when one or both partners lack the capacity for it, creates vulnerability to temptation. The ascetic program that the Corinthian slogan advocates may sound spiritually impressive, but if it exceeds the actual capacity of the people involved, it opens the door to exactly the porneia it claims to prevent.

The logic is deeply practical. Paul is not theorizing about ideal spiritual states; he is assessing real human beings with varying capacities and directing them toward arrangements that will actually sustain their faithfulness, not arrangements that look impressive but collapse under the pressure of reality.

## Verses 6-7: Concession and Gift

"Now I say this by way of concession, not of command" (7:6).

The word syngnome ("concession, pardon, allowance, indulgence") means that what Paul has just said in verses 2-5 is a concession -- an allowance for human weakness -- not a command (epitage). Paul is not commanding marriage. He is not commanding sexual activity within marriage. He is granting permission, recognizing that for most people, marriage and its sexual dimension are the appropriate and necessary arrangement. The distinction between command and concession reveals Paul's own preference, which the next verse states explicitly.

"I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another" (7:7).

Paul's personal preference is clear: thelo de pantas anthropous einai hos kai emauton -- "I wish all people were as I myself am." The verb thelo ("to wish, to desire, to want") expresses a genuine desire, not a command. Paul's own condition appears to be celibacy. Whether Paul was never married, widowed, or divorced is debated and the text does not specify. What is clear is that at the time of writing, Paul lives without a wife and considers this condition advantageous.

But Paul immediately qualifies his preference with a theological principle: alla hekastos idion echei charisma ek theou, ho men houtos, ho de houtos -- "but each has his own gift from God, one in this way, one in that way." The word charisma ("gift, grace-gift") is the same word used in 1:7 for the spiritual gifts the Corinthians possess in abundance. Paul places both celibacy and marriage under the category of charisma -- divine gifts. Neither is earned, neither is a moral achievement, and neither is universally given. Some receive the charisma of celibacy; others receive the charisma of marriage. The phrase ek theou ("from God") identifies God as the source of both gifts. A person who lacks the gift of celibacy is not spiritually inferior; they have received a different gift. A person who possesses the gift of celibacy is not morally superior; they have received one gift rather than another.

This theological framing is crucial for the chapter's argument. The Corinthian slogan ("it is good for a man not to touch a woman") implies that celibacy is the higher spiritual state. Paul responds that both are charismata from God. The hierarchy that the slogan assumes is flattened. The question is not which state is higher but which gift each person has received.

## Verses 8-9: To the Unmarried and Widows

"To the unmarried and the widows I say that it is good for them to remain single, as I am" (7:8).

Paul now addresses two specific categories: tois agamois ("the unmarried") and tais cherais ("the widows"). The word agamos (from the alpha privative a- and gamos, "marriage") simply means "unmarried." It could include those who have never married, the divorced, or the widowed. The separate mention of widows (cherai) alongside the unmarried suggests that agamois here refers primarily to the never-married or the divorced, with widows named as an additional group.

Paul reaffirms his personal judgment: kalon autois ean meinosin hos kago -- "it is good for them if they remain as I am." The word kalon ("good") is the same word from the Corinthian slogan in verse 1. Paul agrees that celibacy is kalon -- a genuine good. But his agreement is qualified: it is good for those who can sustain it. The verb meno ("to remain, to stay, to abide") introduces a concept that will become the chapter's governing principle. Paul will develop the idea of "remaining" extensively in verses 17-24, where it becomes the foundational instruction: remain in the condition in which God called you.

"But if they cannot exercise self-control, they should marry. For it is better to marry than to burn with passion" (7:9).

The conditional ei de ouk enkrateuontai ("but if they are not exercising self-control") uses the present indicative, describing an actual ongoing condition rather than a hypothetical possibility. The verb enkrateuomai ("to exercise self-control, to have power over oneself, to be continent") describes the capacity to restrain one's desires. Paul treats this capacity as something that some people possess and others do not -- consistent with the charisma framework of verse 7. If a person does not have the gift of self-control sufficient for celibacy, marriage is the proper course.

The imperative gamesatosan ("let them marry") is an aorist imperative -- decisive action. The rationale: kreisson gar estin gamesai e pyrousthai -- "for it is better to marry than to burn." The verb pyrousthai ("to burn, to be on fire") is a present passive infinitive indicating an ongoing state of burning. In context, this burning refers to the consuming fire of unfulfilled sexual desire. The metaphor of fire for desire appears in Jewish wisdom literature: Sirach 9:8, "Turn away your eyes from a shapely woman... many have been misled by a woman's beauty, and by it passion is kindled like a fire" (hos pyr ekkainetai). Paul's judgment is practical: an ongoing state of burning desire is destructive and dangerous, creating exactly the vulnerability to porneia that verse 2 identified. Marriage resolves this burning by providing a legitimate context for sexual expression.

The comparison "better to marry than to burn" (kreisson gamesai e pyrousthai) is a comparative, not an absolute. Paul is not saying marriage is bad and celibacy good; he is saying that for those who burn, marriage is the better path. The assumption is that both options are open and both are legitimate, but the person's actual capacity determines which is appropriate.

## Verses 10-11: The Lord's Command on Divorce

"To the married I give this charge (not I, but the Lord): the wife should not separate from her husband" (7:10).

Paul now addresses tous gegamekotas ("those who have married") -- believers married to believers, as the context and the contrast with verses 12-16 (mixed marriages) make clear. The parenthetical ouk ego alla ho kyrios ("not I but the Lord") is striking. Paul distinguishes between his own pastoral counsel and a direct command from the Lord. Throughout this chapter, Paul has been careful to identify the source and authority of his instructions: verse 6 was a concession, not a command; verses 8-9 reflected his own judgment; now he cites a command that originates not from himself but from the Lord.

The "Lord's" command is a reference to the teaching of Jesus on divorce, which is preserved in the synoptic tradition: Mark 10:2-12 (Jesus declares that what God has joined, no human being should separate, and that anyone who divorces and remarries commits adultery); Matthew 5:31-32 (the exception clause for porneia); Matthew 19:3-9 (the fuller version of the same teaching); Luke 16:18 (the compressed version). Paul has access to this tradition -- whether through oral transmission from those who knew Jesus' teaching, or through early collections of Jesus' sayings -- and he treats it as carrying the Lord's direct authority, distinct from his own apostolic counsel.

The instruction: gynaika apo andros me choristhenai -- "a wife should not be separated from her husband." The verb chorizo ("to separate, to divide, to put apart") in the passive (choristhenai) may be either passive ("be separated") or middle ("separate herself"). In the context of divorce proceedings, it functions as a technical term for the dissolution of a marriage. Paul addresses the wife first, which may reflect the specific situation in Corinth -- some women, perhaps influenced by the ascetic slogan of verse 1, may have been leaving their husbands as a spiritual act.

"(But if she does separate, let her remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband), and the husband should not divorce his wife" (7:11).

The parenthetical ean de kai choristhe ("but if she does separate") acknowledges the reality that some separations may occur despite the Lord's command. Paul does not say the separation is legitimate; he addresses the situation that results from it. Two options are available to the separated wife: meneto agamos ("let her remain unmarried") or to andri katallageto ("let her be reconciled to her husband"). The verb katallasso ("to reconcile, to restore to right relationship, to exchange hostility for friendship") describes the repair of a broken relationship. Paul uses this word in 2 Corinthians 5:18-20 for God's reconciliation of the world to himself through Christ. The same word applied to the separated wife and husband indicates that restoration is the desired outcome. Remarriage to a different person is not among the options.

The second instruction -- kai andra gynaika me aphienai ("and a husband should not divorce his wife") -- uses the verb aphiemi ("to send away, to let go, to release, to divorce"). In Greek legal usage, aphienai described the husband's act of formally dismissing his wife. The instruction mirrors the one given to the wife: the husband should not divorce. The Lord's command applies symmetrically to both partners.

## Verses 12-16: Mixed Marriages -- Believers Married to Unbelievers

"To the rest I say (I, not the Lord): if any brother has a wife who is an unbeliever, and she consents to live with him, he should not divorce her" (7:12).

The phrase tois de loipois ("to the rest") addresses a category not covered by the Lord's command in verses 10-11: marriages in which one partner is a believer and the other is not. The parenthetical lego ego, ouch ho kyrios ("I say, not the Lord") does not mean Paul's counsel lacks authority; it means that the historical Jesus did not address this specific situation in his teaching on divorce (which dealt with Jewish marriages under the Torah). Paul is making clear that for this case, the apostolic pastoral counsel is his own, exercised under the authority of his apostleship but distinct from a dominical command that he can cite directly.

The term apistos ("unbeliever, faithless one, one who does not believe") identifies the non-believing spouse. In Paul's usage in this letter, the word describes someone outside the community of faith (cf. 6:6, where adelphos meta adelphou krinetai kai touto epi apiston refers to believers going before unbelievers in court; 14:22-24, where apistoi enter the assembly and hear prophecy). The situation Paul addresses is one that arose naturally in the early communities: a person came to faith after marriage, but the spouse did not. The conversion of one partner within an existing marriage created the question of whether the marriage could or should continue.

Paul's instruction: me aphieto auton ("he should not divorce her," or more precisely, "let him not send her away"). If the unbelieving wife syneudokei oikein met' autou ("consents to live with him"), the believing husband should maintain the marriage. The verb syneudokeo ("to consent, to agree, to approve together") indicates that the unbeliever's willingness is a necessary condition. Paul does not instruct the believer to force the continuation of the marriage; the arrangement depends on the unbeliever's consent.

"If any woman has a husband who is an unbeliever, and he consents to live with her, she should not divorce him" (7:13).

The instruction is perfectly reciprocal. What applies to the believing husband with an unbelieving wife applies identically to the believing wife with an unbelieving husband. The verb aphieto ("let her not send away, let her not divorce") is the same. The principle is the same. Paul's counsel does not differentiate between the genders in mixed-marriage situations.

"For the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife, and the unbelieving wife is made holy because of her husband. Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy" (7:14).

This is one of the most remarkable statements in the chapter. The verb hegiastai ("has been sanctified, has been made holy") is the perfect passive of hagiazo -- the same verb used in 1:2 for the community's own sanctification ("to those sanctified in Christ Jesus," hegiasmenois en Christo Iesou). The unbelieving spouse is "sanctified" through the believing spouse. The preposition en ("in, by, through") identifies the believing partner as the means or sphere of this sanctification.

What does this sanctification mean? It cannot mean that the unbelieving spouse has become a believer, since Paul continues to call them apistos and addresses the possibility of their departure in verse 15. The sanctification is relational, not personal conversion. The believing spouse's connection to the holy community extends a sphere of holiness that encompasses the marriage and the household. This concept has deep roots in Israel's understanding of holiness. In the Old Testament, holiness is not merely an abstract quality but a contagious reality that extends through relationships and spaces. The tabernacle consecrated the items within it; the priest's holiness affected the offerings he handled; the holy land set apart the people who dwelled in it. Haggai 2:12 illustrates the principle, though there the prophet argues that holiness does not transfer by second-order contact in the same way impurity does. Paul's application inverts the expected direction: instead of the unbeliever's impurity contaminating the believer (which might have been the Corinthians' fear, given their concern with purity in chapters 5-6), the believer's holiness sanctifies the unbeliever.

The argument from children confirms this: epei ara ta tekna hymon akatharta estin, nyn de hagia estin -- "since otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy." The word akatharta ("unclean, impure") is the opposite of hagia ("holy, consecrated, set apart"). Paul appeals to something the Corinthians apparently already accept: their children, born of mixed marriages, are regarded as "holy" within the community. If the children are holy despite having one unbelieving parent, then the same principle of holiness-through-relationship applies to the unbelieving spouse. Paul argues from the accepted case (the children) to the disputed case (the spouse). The logic is: you already treat your children as holy; the same principle that makes your children holy also makes your unbelieving spouse holy; therefore, do not divorce.

"But if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace" (7:15).

The conditional ei de ho apistos chorizetai, chorizestho -- "but if the unbeliever separates, let them separate." The present indicative chorizetai ("is separating, separates") describes an action already underway or a real condition. The imperative chorizestho ("let them separate") grants permission: the believer is not required to prevent the departure by force or compromise. If the unbelieving spouse decides to leave, the believing partner is released.

The declaration ou dedoulotai ho adelphos e he adelphe en tois toioutois ("the brother or sister is not enslaved in such cases") uses the perfect passive of douloo ("to enslave, to make a slave of, to bring into bondage"). The perfect tense indicates an ongoing state: the believing spouse has not been and is not now enslaved. The word douloo is strong -- it describes the condition of a slave (doulos), bound to serve without freedom to leave. Paul says the believing partner is not in bondage. The implication is that the marriage bond, when dissolved by the unbeliever's departure, does not continue to bind the believer. The believer is free.

The theological ground: en de eirene kekleken hymas ho theos -- "but God has called you in peace" (or "to peace"). The verb kaleo ("to call") reintroduces the calling language that has been foundational since the letter's opening (1:1-2, 9, 24, 26). God's calling of the believer was a calling into eirene ("peace, wholeness, well-being"). The Hebrew concept of shalom, which lies behind eirene, denotes not merely the absence of conflict but the presence of right order and wholeness. A marriage in which the unbeliever's departure has created an unbridgeable rupture is not a context of peace. Forcing the believer to remain bound to a dissolved relationship would violate the character of God's calling.

"For how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife?" (7:16).

The question ti gar oidas, gynai, ei ton andra soseis -- "for how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband?" -- is a genuine question about an uncertain future, not a statement of hope. The verb oida ("to know, to perceive") asks about the spouse's knowledge of an outcome that is inherently unknown. The verb sozo ("to save, to rescue, to deliver") carries its full weight: the question is whether continuing in the marriage will result in the unbelieving spouse's salvation -- their incorporation into the community of faith and the deliverance that belongs to it.

The question functions as a reason for accepting the unbeliever's departure rather than as a reason for holding on. Paul's logic runs: if you do not know whether staying will save your spouse, then you should not destroy your own peace on the uncertain possibility. The calling "to peace" (v. 15b) is certain; the salvation of the departing spouse is uncertain. Paul does not forbid the hope of the spouse's conversion, but he does not allow that hope to become a chain that binds the believing partner to an untenable situation.

## Verses 17-24: The Principle of Remaining -- Called, Circumcised, Enslaved

"Only let each person lead the life that the Lord has assigned to him, and to which God has called him. This is my rule in all the churches" (7:17).

This verse introduces the organizing principle that governs the entire chapter and that Paul draws from the specific cases already discussed. The phrase hekasto hos emerisen ho kyrios, hekaston hos kekleken ho theos, houtos peripateto -- "each one as the Lord has distributed, each one as God has called, thus let them walk" -- establishes a twofold ground for the instruction to remain: the Lord's distribution (emerisen, from merizo, "to divide, to distribute, to assign a portion") and God's calling (kekleken, perfect of kaleo, "to call"). Both are divine actions. The condition in which a person finds themselves when they are called by God is itself part of God's assignment. The person's task is to "walk" (peripateo, "to walk, to live, to conduct one's life") within that assignment.

The scope of the instruction is universal: houto en tais ekklesiais pasais diatassomai -- "this is what I direct in all the assemblies." The verb diatasso ("to arrange, to order, to direct, to prescribe") is a word of authoritative instruction. Paul's counsel here is not peculiar to Corinth; it is his standard teaching for every community. The Corinthians are not a special case receiving customized advice. They are being brought into conformity with the practice Paul establishes everywhere.

"Was anyone at the time of his call already circumcised? Let him not seek to remove the marks of circumcision. Was anyone at the time of his call uncircumcised? Let him not seek circumcision" (7:18).

The first illustration of the "remain" principle is circumcision -- the covenant sign of Israel. The word peritetmenos ("circumcised") identifies a Jew; akrobystia ("uncircumcision, foreskin") identifies a Gentile. Paul addresses both: the circumcised man should not seek epispasmo ("to draw over, to pull forward" -- the verb epispaomai refers to the surgical procedure, practiced by some Hellenized Jews, of restoring the appearance of a foreskin to reverse circumcision). This practice is attested in 1 Maccabees 1:15, where Jews under Antiochus Epiphanes "made themselves uncircumcised, and abandoned the holy covenant." Conversely, the uncircumcised man should not seek circumcision. Neither physical status -- circumcised or uncircumcised -- needs to be altered after the call.

"Circumcision is nothing, and uncircumcision is nothing. Keeping the commandments of God is what matters" (7:19).

The statement he peritome ouden estin kai he akrobystia ouden estin -- "circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing" -- is radical in a Jewish context. Circumcision was the sign of the Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 17:10-14), the mark that identified a male as belonging to the covenant community. For Paul to declare it "nothing" is not to deny its historical significance but to assert that in the new situation created by Christ, the physical marker no longer determines one's standing before God. This is consistent with Paul's argument in Galatians 5:6: "in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision means anything, but faith working through love" (en gar Christo Iesou oute peritome ti ischyei oute akrobystia alla pistis di' agapes energoumene); and in Galatians 6:15: "neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation" (oute gar peritome ti estin oute akrobystia alla kaine ktisis).

What does matter is teresis entolon theou -- "keeping the commandments of God." The word entole ("commandment, instruction, injunction") in the LXX refers to God's commands throughout the Torah and the prophets (Deuteronomy 4:2, 6:1, 8:1). Paul does not specify which commandments; the phrase functions as a summary of the obedient life that God requires. The logic is striking: circumcision, which was itself one of God's commandments to Abraham, is now declared "nothing" -- while "keeping the commandments of God" is everything. Paul is operating within the prophetic tradition that distinguished between the external sign and the internal reality the sign was meant to represent. Jeremiah 4:4: "Circumcise yourselves to the LORD; remove the foreskin of your hearts, O men of Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem" (peritemethetete to theo hymon kai peritelesthe ten akrobystian tes kardias hymon). Deuteronomy 10:16: "Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no longer stubborn" (peritemesthe ten sklerokardian hymon). The prophets already distinguished between the physical act and the heart-obedience it was supposed to signify. Paul brings that distinction to its culmination.

"Each one should remain in the condition in which he was called" (7:20).

The verse hekastos en te klesei he eklethee, en taute meneto -- "each person in the calling in which they were called, in this let them remain" -- is the chapter's thesis statement in compressed form. The word klesis ("calling, vocation, summons") is the noun form of kaleo ("to call"). It refers to the circumstances in which a person was situated when God's call reached them. The verb meno ("to remain, to stay, to abide, to continue") is the chapter's controlling verb. It appeared in verse 8 (the unmarried should "remain" as Paul is), verse 11 (the separated wife should "remain" unmarried), and will appear again in verses 24, 26, and 40.

The principle is not passivity or fatalism. Paul is not saying that social circumstances are irrelevant or that no change is ever appropriate (as verse 21 will immediately demonstrate). He is saying that the call of God does not require a change in external status. The Corinthians apparently assumed that their new identity in Christ demanded a corresponding change in their social arrangements -- that the circumcised should become uncircumcised, that the married should become celibate, that slaves should seek freedom (or the reverse). Paul says the call transforms the person within their existing circumstances; it does not mandate the alteration of those circumstances.

"Were you a slave when called? Do not be concerned about it. But if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity" (7:21).

The second illustration is slavery. The word doulos ("slave, servant, bondservant") describes a person in legal servitude. Slavery in the first-century Roman world was pervasive and diverse, ranging from the brutal conditions of agricultural and mining slaves to the relatively privileged positions of educated household slaves who managed estates, practiced medicine, or taught children. Corinth, as a major commercial city and Roman colony, would have had a significant enslaved population.

Paul's first instruction -- me soi meleto ("do not be concerned about it, do not let it trouble you") -- addresses the slave's anxiety about their status. The call of God has reached them in their enslaved condition; that condition does not impede the reality of their calling.

The second clause, all' ei kai dynasai eleutheros genesthai, mallon chresai, is among the most debated in the chapter. The verb chraomai ("to use, to make use of, to avail oneself of") requires an object, and the question is whether the implied object is "freedom" (if you can gain your freedom, use the opportunity) or "slavery" (even if you can gain your freedom, rather make use of your slavery). The grammar and the context favor the first reading: mallon chresai most naturally means "rather make use of it" -- i.e., take the opportunity for freedom. This reading is consistent with Paul's argument: remain in your condition as a general rule, but when circumstances change and freedom becomes available, take it. The principle of "remaining" is not absolute; it is a default posture that does not preclude taking advantage of legitimate opportunities when they arise.

"For he who was called in the Lord as a slave is a freedman of the Lord. Likewise he who was free when called is a slave of Christ" (7:22).

The wordplay is deliberate and subversive. The doulos ("slave") who is called "in the Lord" (en kyrio) becomes apeleutheros kyriou ("a freedman of the Lord"). The word apeleutheros is a technical term in Roman society: a former slave who has been formally manumitted, freed by their master and now bearing the patron-client relationship to the former owner. In Roman law, the freedman owed continuing obligations and loyalty to the patron who freed them. Paul applies this to the believing slave: though still a doulos in social terms, they are the Lord's freedman -- liberated by Christ, owing loyalty and service to Christ as patron. Their true master is the Lord, regardless of their legal status.

Conversely, the eleutheros ("free person") who is called becomes doulos Christou ("a slave of Christ"). The free person's social liberty does not exempt them from total obligation to Christ. The slave is raised; the free person is bound. Both are redefined by their relationship to Christ. The social categories of slave and free are not abolished (Paul does not tell slaves to rebel or free persons to sell themselves into slavery), but they are relativized. Neither status determines one's fundamental identity. Both are encompassed within the more fundamental relationship of belonging to Christ.

"You were bought with a price; do not become slaves of human beings" (7:23).

The phrase times egoresthete ("you were bought with a price") repeats exactly the statement from 6:20. The verb agorazo ("to buy, to purchase, to acquire in the marketplace") comes from agora ("marketplace"). The metaphor is commercial: the Corinthians were purchased. The "price" (time) is not specified in this passage but the context of the letter identifies it as Christ's death -- the sacrifice of "our Passover" (5:7). In 6:20, the purchasing language grounded the command to "glorify God in your body." Here it grounds a different command: me ginesthe douloi anthropon -- "do not become slaves of human beings."

This instruction operates at multiple levels. For the literally enslaved person, it means that their ultimate loyalty is to Christ, not to any human master, and that they should not voluntarily enter into bondage. For all believers, it means that their identity as Christ's purchased possession precludes ultimate allegiance to any human authority, faction, or teacher. The connection to the factionalism of chapters 1-4 is unmistakable: the Corinthians who declared "I am of Paul" or "I am of Apollos" were making themselves douloi anthropon -- slaves of human leaders. The connection to the ascetic program of verse 1 is also present: those who impose ascetic requirements on others are attempting to bring them under human authority rather than leaving them in the freedom that belongs to Christ's purchased people.

"So, brothers, in whatever condition each was called, there let him remain with God" (7:24).

The verse summarizes and closes the "remain" section. The phrase para theo ("with God" or "before God") adds a crucial dimension. The remaining is not mere social inertia; it is remaining "with God" -- in God's presence, under God's authority, within the relationship that God's calling established. The condition -- married or unmarried, circumcised or uncircumcised, slave or free -- is the context in which one lives coram deo ("before God"). The call of God sanctifies the circumstances of one's life without necessarily changing them.

## Verses 25-28: Concerning the Unmarried and the Present Distress

"Now concerning the betrothed, I have no command from the Lord, but I give my judgment as one who by the Lord's mercy is trustworthy" (7:25).

A new peri de ("now concerning") section opens, indicating another question from the Corinthians' letter. The word parthenoi (literally "virgins") has been variously understood. In its basic sense, it refers to unmarried young women (or, less commonly, young men) who have not had sexual relations. The context will suggest that Paul is addressing a specific situation: fathers (or guardians) who must decide whether to give their virgin daughters in marriage, and/or betrothed couples who must decide whether to proceed with marriage.

Paul again distinguishes his authority: epitagen kyriou ouk echo ("I have no command from the Lord") -- Jesus did not address this specific question in his teaching. Paul offers his own gnome ("judgment, opinion, counsel") as one who has received mercy from the Lord and is therefore pistos ("trustworthy, faithful"). The word pistos echoes 4:2, where faithfulness was the essential quality of a steward, and 7:17, where Paul's instructions carried the weight of what he directs "in all the assemblies." Paul is not diminishing his authority; he is being precise about its source. He is a trustworthy counselor, even when he cannot cite a direct word from the Lord.

"I think that in view of the present distress it is good for a person to remain as he is" (7:26).

The phrase dia ten enestoson anagken ("because of the present distress/necessity") introduces the eschatological factor that shapes all of Paul's practical counsel in this section. The word enestosan (perfect active participle of enistemi, "to be present, to be at hand, to impend") describes something that is currently present or imminent. The word anagke ("necessity, distress, compulsion, constraint") in the LXX frequently describes the pressure of calamity, the constraint of crisis, the hardship of difficult times. In Luke 21:23, Jesus used anagke for the distress that would come upon the land: "great distress shall be upon the earth" (anagke megale epi tes ges). In 2 Corinthians 6:4, Paul listed anagkai ("hardships, necessities") among the afflictions he endured as a servant of God.

What is the "present distress"? Paul does not specify. It may refer to persecution that the early communities were already experiencing, to the general tribulations expected before the Lord's return, to famine or social upheaval in Corinth, or to the combined pressures of living as a counter-cultural community in a hostile environment. Whatever its specific referent, the "present distress" creates a context in which Paul's practical counsel tilts toward minimizing new obligations and entanglements. Marriage is good, but it brings additional responsibilities and vulnerabilities; in a time of distress, those without such obligations are freer to navigate the crisis.

Paul's judgment: kalon anthropo to houtos einai -- "it is good for a person to remain as they are." Again, kalon ("good") and the verb "to be" (einai) as they are (houtos). The principle of remaining is applied to the current crisis: given the distress that is present, changing one's situation is inadvisable.

"Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek to be free. Are you free from a wife? Do not seek a wife" (7:27).

Two parallel instructions: dedesai gynaiki? me zetei lysin -- "are you bound to a wife? Do not seek release." The verb deo ("to bind, to tie") in the perfect passive (dedesai, "you have been bound and remain bound") describes the marriage bond. The word lysis ("loosing, release, dissolution") is the noun from lyo ("to loose, to unbind, to divorce"). The married man should not seek dissolution of his marriage.

Lelusai apo gynaikos? me zetei gynaika -- "have you been loosed from a wife? Do not seek a wife." The perfect passive lelusai ("you have been loosed") describes someone whose marriage has already been dissolved -- by death, divorce, or separation. This person should not seek to enter a new marriage. Both instructions follow the "remain as you are" principle.

"But if you do marry, you have not sinned, and if a betrothed woman marries, she has not sinned. Yet those who marry will have worldly troubles, and I would spare you that" (7:28).

Paul immediately qualifies the preceding instructions: ean de kai games, ouch hemartes -- "but if you do marry, you have not sinned." The verb hamartano ("to sin, to miss the mark, to transgress") is denied: marriage is not a sin. If the virgin (he parthenos) marries, she has not sinned either. Paul's counsel against marriage in the present circumstances is pragmatic, not moral. There is no sin in marrying. The charisma framework of verse 7 remains operative: marriage is a gift from God.

The practical concern: thlipsin de te sarki hexousin hoi toioutoi -- "but such people will have tribulation in the flesh." The word thlipsis ("tribulation, affliction, pressure, distress") describes the external pressures and difficulties of life. Those who marry will face additional thlipsis -- the practical difficulties that marriage brings, compounded by the "present distress" of verse 26. In a time of persecution or social upheaval, family obligations multiply the burdens a person must bear. Paul's desire: ego de hymon pheidomai -- "I am sparing you." The verb pheidomai ("to spare, to refrain from harming, to treat leniently") expresses pastoral concern. Paul's counsel toward celibacy in the present crisis is motivated by care for the Corinthians' well-being, not by an abstract preference for sexual renunciation.

## Verses 29-31: The Shortened Time and the Passing Form of the World

"This is what I mean, brothers: the appointed time has grown short. From now on, let those who have wives live as though they had none" (7:29).

The phrase touto de phemi, adelphoi, ho kairos synestalmenos estin -- "this I say, brothers: the time has been shortened" -- states the eschatological reality that grounds all of Paul's practical counsel. The word kairos ("time, season, appointed time, critical moment") is not chronos ("time in general, duration") but the specific, decisive time that determines the character of the present moment. The verb systello ("to shorten, to compress, to draw together, to wrap up") in the perfect passive participle (synestalmenos) describes a completed compression: the time has already been shortened and remains in its shortened state. The image is of a sail being furled, a rope being gathered in, a space being constricted. There is less time than there used to be. The interval between Christ's death and resurrection and his return is finite and is drawing to its close.

Paul draws a radical practical consequence: to loipon, hina kai hoi echontes gynaikas hos me echontes osin -- "from now on, let even those who have wives be as though they had none." This is not a command to abandon marriages or cease conjugal relations (which would contradict verses 3-5). It is a command to hold all earthly arrangements with a loose grip -- to live within them without being defined by them, to maintain them without being absorbed by them. The marriage relationship continues, but it no longer constitutes the believer's ultimate horizon.

"And those who mourn as though they were not mourning, and those who rejoice as though they were not rejoicing, and those who buy as though they had no possessions" (7:30).

Paul extends the "as though not" (hos me) formula across four domains of life:

Hoi klaiontes hos me klaiontes -- "those who weep as though not weeping." The verb klaio ("to weep, to cry, to lament") describes grief. The sorrow of present circumstances is real but not ultimate.

Hoi chairontes hos me chairontes -- "those who rejoice as though not rejoicing." The verb chairo ("to rejoice, to be glad") describes happiness. Present joy, like present sorrow, is relativized by the shortened time.

Hoi agorazontes hos me katechontes -- "those who buy as though not possessing." The verb agorazo ("to buy, to purchase") and katecho ("to hold fast, to possess, to retain") describe economic transactions and the ownership they produce. Even property ownership is to be held lightly.

The hos me ("as though not") formula does not negate the activities themselves. Paul does not command believers to stop marrying, mourning, rejoicing, or buying. He commands them to engage in these activities without ultimate attachment -- to participate in the present order while recognizing that it is not the final order. The formula produces a posture of engaged detachment: fully present in the world but not anchored to it.

"For the present form of this world is passing away" (7:31).

The ground of the hos me posture: paragei gar to schema tou kosmou toutou -- "for the form of this world is passing away." The word schema ("form, outward appearance, figure, shape") describes the external configuration of something -- its visible arrangement, its present structure. The word was used in 4:6, where Paul said he had "applied these things figuratively" (meteschematisa) to himself and Apollos. The schema is the outward form, not the underlying substance. The world's present form -- its social arrangements, its power structures, its economies, its institutions, including marriage -- is paragei ("passing away, going by, coming to an end"). The verb parago ("to pass by, to go past, to pass away") describes a procession moving along and leaving the observer behind, or a scene changing. The present arrangement of things is in transit. It is not permanent. It is giving way to something else.

This is the theological foundation that supports the entire chapter's practical counsel. The reason to hold marriage loosely, to avoid unnecessary entanglements, to remain in one's present condition, to treat circumcision and slavery as matters of indifference -- all of this flows from the recognition that the schema of this world is temporary. The question of whether to marry or not, whether to change one's social status or not, is set against a larger backdrop: the present order is passing. Decisions should be made in light of what is coming, not in light of what is leaving.

## Verses 32-35: Undivided Devotion

"I want you to be free from anxieties. The unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord" (7:32).

Paul now explains why he prefers celibacy for those who have the gift for it. The word amerimnous ("free from anxiety, without care, without worry") comes from the alpha privative a- and merimna ("care, anxiety, concern"). The verb merimnao ("to be anxious, to be concerned, to worry") will appear four times in verses 32-34. Paul's concern is about the division of attention and energy that marriage inevitably creates.

The unmarried man merimna ta tou kyriou, pos arese to kyrio -- "is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord." The verb aresko ("to please, to satisfy, to be pleasing to") describes the desire to satisfy someone whose approval matters. The unmarried person can direct this desire entirely toward the Lord.

"But the married man is anxious about worldly things, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided" (7:33-34a).

The married man merimna ta tou kosmou, pos arese te gynaiki -- "is anxious about the things of the world, how to please his wife." The phrase ta tou kosmou ("the things of the world") does not describe sinful things but the legitimate concerns of maintaining a household -- provision, protection, the practical demands of family life. The married man's attention is kai memeristai ("and divided" or "and he is divided"). The verb merizo ("to divide, to distribute, to separate into parts") is the same verb from verse 17, where God "distributed" to each person their circumstances. Here the division is internal: the married man's devotion is split between the Lord and his wife. This is not presented as sin but as the structural reality of marriage.

"And the unmarried or betrothed woman is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to be holy in body and spirit. But the married woman is anxious about worldly things, how to please her husband" (7:34b).

The same analysis applies to women, with perfect symmetry. The unmarried woman and the parthenos ("virgin, betrothed woman") can devote themselves to being hagia kai to somati kai to pneumati -- "holy both in body and in spirit." The phrase "holy in body and spirit" connects to the body theology of 6:19-20: the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit. The unmarried woman can direct the totality of her being -- body and spirit -- toward holiness before the Lord. The married woman, by contrast, must attend to ta tou kosmou, pos arese to andri -- "the things of the world, how to please her husband."

Paul's description is observational, not judgmental. He is not condemning marriage or declaring the married person's attention to their spouse sinful. He is describing the structural reality that marriage divides attention. For those living in "the present distress" (v. 26), with "the time shortened" (v. 29), this division has practical consequences. The person whose devotion is undivided is better positioned to navigate the crisis.

"I say this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and to secure your undivided devotion to the Lord" (7:35).

Paul clarifies his motive: touto de pros to hymon auton sympheron lego -- "this I say for your own advantage." The word sympheron ("advantageous, beneficial, profitable") echoes 6:12, where Paul countered the Corinthian slogan with "but not all things are beneficial" (all' ou panta sympherei). Paul is seeking what is genuinely beneficial for the Corinthians, not imposing arbitrary restrictions.

He is not throwing a brochos ("noose, restraint, halter") on them -- ouch hina brochon hymin epibalo. The word brochos means a rope or snare, something that restricts freedom. Paul is not trying to trap them into celibacy. His purpose is euschemon kai euparedron to kyrio aperispastos -- "for seemly devotion and faithful attendance upon the Lord without distraction." The word euschemon ("seemly, proper, becoming") describes what is fitting and honorable. The word euparedron ("sitting well beside, constantly attending") describes the posture of someone who sits attentively beside another, devoted and present. The word aperispastos ("without distraction, undiverted") is the goal: undivided attention to the Lord.

## Verses 36-38: The Case of the Betrothed

"If anyone thinks that he is not behaving properly toward his betrothed, if his passions are strong, and it has to be, let him do as he wishes: let them marry -- it is no sin" (7:36).

This passage addresses a specific situation. The phrase ei de tis aschemonein epi ten parthenon autou nomizei -- "if anyone thinks he is behaving improperly toward his virgin" -- describes a man who is betrothed to a virgin and who faces a practical crisis. The verb aschemoneo ("to behave improperly, to act unseemly, to disgrace") from the root aschemon ("unseemly, shapeless, dishonoring") describes behavior that violates what is fitting. In 12:23, Paul will use the same root for the "unpresentable" (aschemon) body parts. The man considers it unseemly to leave his betrothed in a state of permanent engagement without consummating the marriage.

The condition ean e hyperakmos ("if she/he is past the bloom of youth" or "if his passions are strong") has been debated. The word hyperakmos could describe the virgin who has passed the ideal age for marriage (making delay increasingly inappropriate) or the man whose sexual desire has become too strong to sustain the engagement without consummation. In either reading, the situation has reached a point where continued delay is creating problems rather than solving them.

The resolution: kai houtos opheilei ginesthai, ho thelei poieito, ouch hamartanei, gameitosan -- "and if it must happen, let him do what he wishes, he does not sin; let them marry." The verb opheilei ("it is necessary, it must be") indicates that circumstances have made marriage the necessary course. Once again, Paul affirms: ouch hamartanei -- "he does not sin." Marriage is not sin. The repeated assurance (also in v. 28) suggests that some in Corinth were teaching or implying that marriage was sinful -- a position Paul explicitly rejects every time the question arises.

"But whoever is firmly established in his heart, being under no necessity but having his desire under control, and has determined this in his heart, to keep her as his betrothed, he will do well" (7:37).

The alternative case: the man who is hedraios en te kardia ("firmly established in his heart"), who has no anagke ("necessity, compulsion") -- his passions do not overwhelm him -- and who has exousian peri tou idiou thelematos ("authority over his own will"), meaning he has the capacity to sustain the engagement without consummation. If he has kekriken en te idia kardia ("decided in his own heart") to keep his virgin (terein ten heautou parthenon), he kalos poiesei ("will do well"). The word kalos ("well, finely, nobly") affirms his decision positively.

"So then he who marries his betrothed does well, and he who refrains from marriage will do even better" (7:38).

The comparative judgment: hoste kai ho gamizoin ten heautou parthenon kalos poiei, kai ho me gamizoin kreisson poiesei -- "so then both the one who marries his virgin does well, and the one who does not marry does better." The word kreisson ("better") establishes a relative preference, not an absolute moral hierarchy. Both courses are kalos ("well, good"); one is kreisson ("better"). Paul's preference for celibacy, stated repeatedly throughout the chapter (vv. 7-8, 26, 32-35), is here formulated as a comparative: better, not exclusively right. The person who marries does well; the person who refrains, given the conditions Paul has described (present distress, shortened time, undivided devotion), does even better. But "better" is not "righteous versus sinful"; it is "good versus more advantageous in the present circumstances."

## Verses 39-40: Widows

"A wife is bound to her husband as long as he lives. But if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord" (7:39).

Paul returns to the category of widows (introduced in v. 8) and states the duration of the marriage bond: gyne dedetai eph' hoson chronon ze ho aner autes -- "a wife is bound for as long a time as her husband lives." The verb deo ("to bind") in the perfect passive (dedetai, "has been and remains bound") describes the marriage bond as a binding that persists throughout the husband's life. The word chronos ("time, duration") here is used in its straightforward sense: the binding lasts as long as the husband's life lasts.

If the husband dies (ean de koimethe ho aner, "but if the husband falls asleep" -- using the euphemism koimaomai, "to sleep," for death, which Paul also uses in 11:30 and 15:6, 18, 20, 51), the wife is eleuthera estin ho thelei gametheenai -- "free to be married to whom she wishes." The word eleuthera ("free") contrasts with the dedetai ("bound") of the first clause. Death dissolves the bond. The widow's freedom to remarry is genuine: ho thelei ("to whom she wishes") leaves the choice entirely to her.

The qualification: monon en kyrio -- "only in the Lord." The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") has been the letter's defining sphere since 1:2, where the community was described as "sanctified in Christ Jesus." The widow may marry whom she wishes, but the marriage must be "in the Lord" -- within the sphere of the community's identity, to a believer. This restriction is consistent with Paul's treatment of mixed marriages in verses 12-16: existing mixed marriages should be maintained if the unbeliever consents, but new marriages should be contracted within the community.

"Yet in my judgment she is happier if she remains as she is. And I think that I too have the Spirit of God" (7:40).

Paul states his own preference one final time: makarioterra de estin ean houtos meine -- "but she is more blessed if she remains as she is." The word makarioterra (comparative of makarios, "blessed, happy, fortunate") uses the language of beatitude -- the blessed condition. The widow who remains unmarried is, in Paul's judgment, in a more blessed condition. This is consistent with the entire chapter's trajectory: celibacy, for those who can sustain it, is preferred in light of the present distress and the shortened time.

The closing phrase -- doko de kago pneuma theou echein -- "and I think that I too have the Spirit of God" -- is delivered with understated irony. The verb dokeo ("to think, to suppose, to seem") combined with kago ("I too, I also") suggests that Paul is aware of competing claims of spiritual authority in Corinth. Some may be claiming the Spirit's authority for positions different from Paul's. Paul's response is quietly assertive: he too has the Spirit of God. The counsel he gives throughout this chapter is not merely human opinion; it comes from one who possesses the Spirit (2:10-16), who has been commissioned as an apostle (1:1), and who is a trustworthy steward of God's mysteries (4:1-2). The ironic understatement -- "I think I too have the Spirit" -- echoes the same tone Paul used in 4:8-13, where his ironic self-deprecation masked a serious claim to authority.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 7 addresses a cluster of practical questions about marriage, celibacy, divorce, and social status, but it is organized by a single theological principle and shaped by a single eschatological conviction.

The theological principle is "remain in the condition in which God called you" (vv. 17, 20, 24). This principle applies to marriage (vv. 2-16, 27), to circumcision (vv. 18-19), to slavery (vv. 21-23), and to betrothal and widowhood (vv. 25-40). The principle does not absolutize the status quo -- Paul allows for the gaining of freedom (v. 21), for marriage when burning with desire (v. 9), for remarriage of widows (v. 39). But the default posture is to remain. The call of God sanctifies the circumstances in which it finds the person. The believer does not need to change their external situation to serve God fully. The circumcised Jew does not need to become uncircumcised; the uncircumcised Gentile does not need to be circumcised; the married person does not need to become celibate; the celibate person does not need to marry; the slave does not need to become free (though they may if the opportunity arises). God's call reaches people where they are and transforms their relationship to their circumstances without necessarily transforming the circumstances themselves.

The eschatological conviction is that "the appointed time has grown short" and "the present form of this world is passing away" (vv. 29, 31). This conviction shapes all of Paul's practical counsel. He prefers celibacy not because marriage is sinful (he explicitly denies this at least three times: vv. 9, 28, 36) but because the present crisis and the approaching end make undivided devotion to the Lord the wisest course. The hos me ("as though not") formula of verses 29-31 defines the community's posture: engaged in the world's activities but not absorbed by them, holding all arrangements loosely because the form in which those arrangements exist is temporary.

The chapter also demonstrates Paul's pastoral method. He carefully distinguishes between the Lord's direct command (v. 10), his own apostolic counsel (vv. 12, 25), his concession (v. 6), and his personal preference (v. 7). He does not flatten all instruction into a single level of authority. He repeatedly affirms that marriage is not sin (vv. 9, 28, 36), countering what appears to be an ascetic faction in Corinth that was treating celibacy as morally superior. He addresses men and women with remarkable symmetry, granting each gender the same obligations (vv. 3-4), the same rights (v. 4), the same freedom (v. 15), the same spiritual capacity (v. 34), and the same options (vv. 27-28, 36-38, 39-40).

The chapter's connection to the broader argument of the letter is threefold. First, it continues the treatment of the body that Paul developed in 6:12-20. The body that belongs to the Lord and is a temple of the Holy Spirit is also the body that participates in marriage, sexual union, and the practical circumstances of daily life. Paul's body theology is not dualistic: the body is not rejected in favor of the spirit but integrated into the community's life with the Lord. Second, the "remain" principle addresses the same confusion about identity that produced the factionalism of chapters 1-4. The Corinthians have been changing allegiances, seeking new statuses, competing for position. Paul says: stop seeking change. Your identity is settled by God's calling. Remain in it. The frenetic pursuit of new spiritual positions -- new teachers, new statuses, new ascetic achievements -- is unnecessary and harmful. Third, the eschatological urgency of verses 29-31 provides the framework for the entire second half of the letter. The questions about food offered to idols (chapters 8-10), about worship practices (chapter 11), about spiritual gifts (chapters 12-14), and about the resurrection (chapter 15) all take place within the same temporal horizon: the time is short, the form of this world is passing, and the community must live accordingly.

The word that holds the chapter together is meno -- "remain." The believer remains in their marriage or their singleness, remains in their circumcision or their uncircumcision, remains in their slavery or their freedom, remains with God (v. 24) in whatever condition the call found them. The remaining is not passive resignation but active faithfulness within the circumstances of one's calling, lived in the knowledge that all such circumstances are temporary and that the ultimate reality -- the Lord's coming, the day of judgment, the fulfillment of God's purposes -- is drawing near.
