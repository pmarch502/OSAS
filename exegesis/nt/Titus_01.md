# Titus 1 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**The document's genre and occasion:** This is a letter from Paul to Titus, a co-worker who has been left behind on the island of Crete to complete organizational work that Paul began but could not finish before departing. The letter is, like 1 Timothy, a mandate letter -- a formal authorization from a superior to a delegate, empowering the delegate to act on the sender's behalf. The parallel with 1 Timothy is close in several respects: both letters are addressed to individuals left behind in a specific location to manage a specific problem; both contain instructions about appointing qualified leaders; both warn against false teachers; both deploy the "God our Savior" language and the medical metaphor of "sound teaching." But the situations differ in important ways. Timothy was left in Ephesus, a major city with an established community already experiencing a crisis of false teaching. Titus is on Crete, where the communities appear to be newer and less organized -- the work that remains is foundational: appointing elders "in every town" (1:5), which implies that no formal leadership structure yet exists. The crisis in Crete also has its own character. While the false teachers in Ephesus were described generally as promoters of myths and genealogies who desire to be law-teachers (1 Tim 1:4, 7), in Crete the false teachers are specifically identified as "those of the circumcision" (1:10, hoi ek tes peritomes), and their teaching involves "Jewish myths" and "commands of people who turn away from the truth" (1:14). The problem is more specifically defined: it is a Torah-observant faction imposing requirements on the communities that Paul considers contrary to the gospel.

The island of Crete held a particular place in the first-century Mediterranean world. It was one of the largest islands in the Mediterranean, with a long history stretching back to the Minoan civilization. By the Roman period, Crete was a province administered jointly with Cyrenaica (Libya). Its Jewish population is attested: Acts 2:11 lists "Cretans" among those present in Jerusalem at Pentecost who heard the apostles speak in their own languages. The existence of Jewish communities on the island provides the social context for the "circumcision party" that Titus must confront. The island also had a reputation in the Greco-Roman world -- a reputation Paul invokes through a quotation attributed to a Cretan prophet (1:12) -- for dishonesty and moral laxity. The word kretizein ("to Cretanize") had become a Greek verb meaning "to lie," and the island's association with deception was proverbial.

The letter is shorter and more compressed than 1 Timothy. Where 1 Timothy developed its themes at length -- the proper use of the law (1 Tim 1:8-11), Paul's personal testimony of mercy (1 Tim 1:12-17), detailed qualifications for overseers and deacons (1 Tim 3:1-13) -- Titus moves quickly. The elder qualifications in 1:5-9 are compressed into a few verses. The description of false teachers in 1:10-16 is pointed and specific rather than extended. The theological statements are dense summaries (1:1-4; 2:11-14; 3:3-7) rather than developed arguments. The letter reads as an efficient set of instructions for a trusted delegate who knows the situation and needs authorization more than explanation.

**What the author argues immediately after (Titus 2):** Chapter 2 opens with a direct contrast to the false teachers described at the end of chapter 1: "But as for you, teach what accords with sound doctrine" (sy de lalei ha prepei te hygiainouse didaskalia, 2:1). The conjunction de ("but") and the emphatic pronoun sy ("you") set Titus in opposition to the false teachers just described. Where they teach for shameful gain and upset whole households (1:11), Titus is to teach what befits sound doctrine. What follows is a series of instructions organized by social category -- older men (2:2), older women (2:3-4), younger women (2:4-5), younger men (2:6), and slaves (2:9-10) -- each describing the behavior appropriate to that group. Titus himself is to be a model of good works (2:7-8). The section culminates in a theological grounding statement: "For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age, waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works" (2:11-14). This theological statement picks up and develops the themes compressed in the greeting (1:1-4): the appearing of God's saving purpose, the hope of eternal life, the purification of a people, and the connection between grace and godly living. The whole of chapter 2 is the positive content of what sound teaching produces -- in contrast to the negative portrait of what false teaching produces in 1:10-16.

**How chapter 1 connects to what follows:** Chapter 1 establishes the foundation for the entire letter in three movements. First, the greeting (1:1-4) compresses the letter's theological framework into a dense statement: Paul's apostleship serves the faith and knowledge of God's elect, oriented toward the hope of eternal life promised before time and now manifested through preaching. Second, the appointment of elders (1:5-9) describes the structural response to the crisis: the communities need qualified leaders who can teach sound doctrine and refute opponents. Third, the description of false teachers (1:10-16) identifies the specific threat: insubordinate members of the circumcision party who teach Jewish myths and human commands for shameful gain, whose profession of knowledge is contradicted by their works. Every subsequent section of the letter responds to this foundation. Chapter 2's instructions about behavior by social category are the practical content of the "sound doctrine" that the elders must teach (1:9) and that Titus must model (2:1). Chapter 3's instructions about civic conduct ("be subject to rulers and authorities, obedient, ready for every good work," 3:1) and the theological statement about God's mercy and renewal (3:3-7) develop the themes of godliness (eusebeia) and good works (kala erga) that appear in the greeting (1:1) and the elder qualifications (1:8). And the closing warning to "avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law" (3:9) circles back to the false teachers of 1:10-16 and their Jewish myths and human commands (1:14), closing the ring the letter opened. Chapter 1 is the architectural blueprint for the building the rest of the letter constructs.

## Verses 1-4: The Greeting -- Servanthood, Apostleship, and the Promise Before Time

"Paul, a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, for the faith of God's elect and their knowledge of the truth that accords with godliness, in hope of eternal life, which God, who never lies, promised before the ages began, and at the proper time manifested in his word through the preaching with which I have been entrusted by the command of God our Savior; to Titus, my true child in a common faith: Grace and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Savior."

This greeting is the most theologically dense of any in the Pastoral Epistles. In 1 Timothy 1:1, the greeting was formal but compact: "Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus according to the command of God our Savior and of Christ Jesus our hope." In 2 Timothy 1:1, the greeting was warmer and more personally oriented: "Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God according to the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus." Here in Titus, the greeting expands into a compressed theological summary that encompasses Paul's identity, his mission's purpose, the temporal scope of God's plan, and the means of its disclosure. The greeting functions almost as a creed -- a summary of the entire gospel compressed into a single complex sentence.

**"Paul, a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ"** (Paulos doulos theou, apostolos de Iesou Christou). Two titles are stated, joined by de ("and, but, moreover"). The first is doulos theou -- "servant of God" or "slave of God." This title does not appear in the greetings of any other Pauline letter. In Romans 1:1, Paul calls himself doulos Christou Iesou ("servant of Christ Jesus"); in Philippians 1:1, Paul and Timothy are douloi Christou Iesou ("servants of Christ Jesus"). But here the servanthood is attributed specifically to God (theou), and the apostleship to Jesus Christ (Iesou Christou). The pairing creates a deliberate structure: Paul serves God and is sent by Christ. The two roles are complementary, not competitive: the one who serves God is the one whom Christ commissioned.

The noun doulos ("slave, servant, bondservant") in the LXX carries a distinctive weight when applied to those who serve God. Moses is called doulos theou or doulos kyriou repeatedly: Joshua 1:2 ("Moses my servant," Mouses ho doulos mou); Deuteronomy 34:5 ("Moses the servant of the LORD," Mouses doulos kyriou); 2 Kings 18:12 (LXX 4 Kingdoms 18:12); Nehemiah 1:7-8; Daniel 9:11; Malachi 4:4 (LXX 3:22). Joshua is called doulos kyriou at his death (Joshua 24:29; Judges 2:8). David is doulos throughout the Psalms and the historical books: "David my servant" (Psalm 89:3 LXX [88:4]; 2 Samuel 7:5; 1 Kings 11:13). The prophets collectively are "my servants the prophets" (2 Kings 17:13; Jeremiah 7:25; Amos 3:7). Abraham is doulos (Psalm 105:42 LXX [104:42]). The title doulos theou in the LXX is not a mark of degradation but of intimate commission -- it designates the person who stands in a special relationship to God, authorized to act on God's behalf, carrying God's purposes. By using this title, Paul places himself within the line of Israel's commissioned servants: Moses, Joshua, David, the prophets.

The second title, apostolos ("apostle, one sent, authorized representative"), functions as in the other letters. But the combination of the two titles -- doulos theou and apostolos Iesou Christou -- creates a dual identification that roots Paul's mission in both the God of Israel's history and the Christ who has now sent him. A first-century hearer would understand: this man serves the same God Moses served, and he has been dispatched by the Messiah.

**"For the faith of God's elect and their knowledge of the truth that accords with godliness"** (kata pistin eklekton theou kai epignosin aletheias tes kat' eusebeian). The preposition kata with the accusative here indicates purpose or direction: Paul's servanthood and apostleship operate "for" or "toward" these ends. Two purposes are stated:

(1) **The faith of God's elect** (pistin eklekton theou). Paul's apostleship serves the pistis ("faith, trust, believing") of the eklektoi ("chosen ones, elect, selected") of God. The noun eklektos in the LXX describes those whom God has selected for his purposes. Israel collectively is God's chosen: Isaiah 45:4, "For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my chosen one" (ton eklekton mou Israel); Isaiah 65:9, "my chosen shall inherit it" (hoi eklektoi mou); Isaiah 65:15, "my chosen" (tois eklektois mou); Isaiah 65:22, "my chosen shall long enjoy the work of their hands" (hoi eklektoi mou). The designation is corporate and national in the prophetic tradition -- Israel is God's elect people, chosen not because of their merit but because of God's purpose (Deuteronomy 7:7-8; 9:4-6). In Paul's usage, the eklektoi are those -- Jew and Gentile alike -- whom God has called into his people through Christ. Romans 8:33: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect?" (tis enkaleisei kata eklekton theou); Colossians 3:12: "Put on then, as God's chosen ones" (hōs eklektoi tou theou); Romans 11:7: "the elect obtained it" (he eklogē epetychèn). The term carries the full weight of Israel's identity as God's chosen people, now extended to encompass all who have responded to the gospel call.

Paul's apostleship exists to serve the faith of these chosen ones. He does not create the faith; he serves it. His preaching, teaching, and organizing are directed toward the establishment, strengthening, and preservation of the trust that God's people place in God.

(2) **Knowledge of the truth that accords with godliness** (epignosin aletheias tes kat' eusebeian). The noun epignosis ("knowledge, recognition, full knowledge, discernment") is an intensified form of gnosis ("knowledge"), with the prefix epi- adding a sense of directedness or thoroughness. In Paul's usage, epignosis often describes the knowledge of God or the gospel that is the goal of the community's growth. Colossians 1:9-10: "asking that you may be filled with the knowledge (epignosin) of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding, so as to walk in a manner worthy of the Lord." Colossians 2:2: "the knowledge (epignosin) of God's mystery, which is Christ." Ephesians 1:17: "that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ... may give you the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge (epignosei) of him." Epignosis is not mere intellectual awareness but participatory knowledge -- the kind of knowing that transforms the knower.

The truth (aletheia) that is known is qualified: it is truth kat' eusebeian -- "according to godliness" or "that accords with godliness." The preposition kata ("according to, in accord with, corresponding to") links truth and godliness as inseparable companions. The noun eusebeia ("godliness, piety, reverence, proper worship") is one of the most important terms in the Pastoral Epistles, appearing ten times in 1 Timothy and Titus (1 Tim 2:2; 3:16; 4:7, 8; 6:3, 5, 6, 11; 2 Tim 3:5; Titus 1:1). In Greek usage, eusebeia described the proper reverence owed to the gods, parents, and civic institutions -- a comprehensive orientation of life that honored what deserved honor. In the LXX and Jewish usage, it describes the piety that flows from the fear of God. The phrase kat' eusebeian establishes a criterion: the truth Paul serves is truth that produces godliness. This is not abstract or speculative knowledge; it is knowledge that changes how people live. The distinction is aimed at the false teachers, whose "knowledge" (they "profess to know God," 1:16) produces not godliness but disobedience and detestable works. True knowledge and godliness travel together; false knowledge and ungodliness travel together. The quality of a teaching's fruit reveals the nature of the teaching.

**"In hope of eternal life"** (ep' elpidi zoes aioniou). The preposition epi with the dative (ep' elpidi) expresses the basis or ground: Paul's apostleship rests upon, is grounded in, operates on the basis of the hope (elpis) of eternal life (zoes aioniou). The phrase zoe aionios ("life of the age, eternal life, life that belongs to the coming age") in Jewish usage describes the life of the age to come -- the quality of existence that belongs to God's future, when death and corruption are overcome. Daniel 12:2 (LXX): "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life (eis zoen aionion)." The phrase appears in the Gospels as what the faithful inherit or enter into (Matthew 19:16, 29; 25:46; Mark 10:17, 30; Luke 10:25; 18:18, 30; John 3:15-16, 36; 5:24; 6:40, 47, 54, 68; 17:2-3). In Paul's other letters: Romans 2:7 ("to those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality, he will give eternal life"); Romans 5:21 ("so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life"); Romans 6:22-23 ("the end is eternal life... the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord"); Galatians 6:8 ("the one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life"). Hope of eternal life is not a secondary benefit of faith but its foundational orientation: the entire mission is oriented toward the life of the coming age.

**"Which God, who never lies, promised before the ages began"** (hen epengeilato ho apseudes theos pro chronon aionion). The relative pronoun hen ("which") refers back to eternal life. This life was promised (epengeilato, from epangellesthai, "to promise, to pledge, to undertake") by God. The verb is the middle voice of epangellein, and in Paul's usage it consistently describes God's solemn pledges -- particularly the promise to Abraham (Romans 4:21; Galatians 3:19) and the promise of the Spirit (Galatians 3:14).

God is described with a striking adjective: apseudes -- "who never lies, truthful, incapable of falsehood." The word is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and pseudos ("lie, falsehood, deception"). It appears only here in the New Testament. In Greek literature, the word was used of oracles and divine speech -- utterances that are reliable because the speaker cannot deceive. The description has deep roots in Israel's testimony about God. Numbers 23:19: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind. Has he said, and will he not do it? Or has he spoken, and will he not fulfill it?" 1 Samuel 15:29: "The Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret." Psalm 89:35 (LXX 88:36): "Once for all I have sworn by my holiness; I will not lie to David." The attribute of divine truthfulness is not an abstract philosophical claim but a covenantal assertion: the God who made promises to Israel is the God who keeps them. He cannot lie because his character does not permit it.

The timing of the promise is specified as pro chronon aionion -- "before the ages of time" or "before eternal times." This is the same phrase that appeared in 2 Timothy 1:9, where God's purpose and grace were said to have been "given us in Christ Jesus before the ages began." The concept is the same: God's saving purpose precedes created time. Before history began, the promise of eternal life already existed in God's intention. This is not a late amendment to God's plan or a response to unforeseen circumstances. It is the original purpose, the plan from before the beginning.

The combination of the two elements -- God's inability to lie and the pre-temporal origin of the promise -- creates an extraordinarily strong basis for confidence. The promise is (a) made by one who cannot deceive, and (b) established before time itself. These two anchors -- the character of the promisor and the antiquity of the promise -- together form the ground on which Paul's hope and the community's hope rest. No subsequent event can destabilize a promise that predates all events and is guaranteed by one who is constitutionally incapable of falsehood.

**"And at the proper time manifested in his word through the preaching with which I have been entrusted by the command of God our Savior"** (ephanerdsen de kairois idiois ton logon autou en kerygmati ho episteuthen ego kat' epitagen tou soteros hemon theou). Several elements require attention:

The verb phaneroun ("to make visible, to reveal, to bring to light, to manifest") describes the transition from hidden to visible. What was promised before time has now been manifested -- brought into the open, made accessible to perception and understanding. The same verb appeared in 2 Timothy 1:10 for the manifestation of grace "through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus." The hidden-then-revealed pattern is fundamental to Paul's understanding of the gospel: God's purpose existed before time, was partially disclosed through Israel's history and scriptures, and has now been fully manifested through Christ and the proclamation about Christ.

The timing is described as kairois idiois -- "at the proper times" or "in his own appointed times." The noun kairos ("time, season, opportune moment, appointed time") differs from chronos ("time, duration, span"). While chronos describes time as duration (the passage of hours, days, years), kairos describes time as occasion -- the right moment, the appointed season, the fitting hour. The adjective idios ("one's own, proper, personal, appointed") indicates that these times belong to God: they are God's own appointed seasons. God determines when the hidden becomes visible. In Galatians 4:4, Paul writes: "But when the fullness of time had come (to pleroma tou chronou), God sent forth his Son." The same concept operates here: the manifestation occurred at the moment God had appointed.

What was manifested is ton logon autou -- "his word." The possessive autou ("his") identifies the word as God's. And the means of manifestation is en kerygmati -- "in preaching" or "through proclamation." The noun kerygma ("proclamation, preaching, what is proclaimed") is the public announcement of the gospel message. In 1 Corinthians 1:21: "it pleased God through the folly of what we preach (tou kerygmatos) to save those who believe." In 1 Corinthians 2:4: "my speech and my message (kerygma) were not in plausible words of wisdom but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power." In 1 Corinthians 15:14: "if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching (kerygma) is in vain." The kerygma is the public proclamation of the gospel -- the herald's announcement of what God has done in Christ.

Paul has been entrusted (episteuthen, aorist passive of pisteuein, "to be entrusted with") with this preaching. The passive voice indicates that the commission came from outside Paul -- he did not take it upon himself. And the authority behind the commission is specified: kat' epitagen tou soteros hemon theou -- "by the command of God our Savior." The noun epitage ("command, order, decree, injunction") is the same word that appeared in 1 Timothy 1:1 to describe the authority behind Paul's apostleship. It is the language of formal mandate, military commission, official dispatch. Paul preaches not because he chose to but because he was ordered to.

The title tou soteros hemon theou ("God our Savior") is a signature designation of the Pastoral Epistles. In 1 Timothy 1:1 it appeared in the greeting; in 1 Timothy 2:3, God our Savior "desires all people to be saved"; in 1 Timothy 4:10, "we have our hope set on the living God, who is the Savior of all people." In Titus, the title will appear again at 2:10 and 3:4. As noted in the analysis of 1 Timothy 1, the LXX consistently uses soter for God in the context of his demonstrated acts of deliverance -- the exodus, the return from exile, the rescue of individuals from death and danger. The title is not a philosophical predicate but a historical summary: this is the God who saves, as he has shown by doing so.

**"To Titus, my true child in a common faith"** (Tito gnesiō teknō kata koinen pistin). The addressee receives the same relational designation as Timothy: gnesios teknon -- "true/legitimate child." As in 1 Timothy 1:2, the adjective gnesios ("genuine, legitimate, born in wedlock") establishes Titus as Paul's authentic spiritual offspring, not a pretender. But where Timothy was Paul's true child en pistei ("in faith"), Titus is his true child kata koinen pistin -- "according to a common faith" or "in a shared faith." The adjective koinos ("common, shared, mutual") and the preposition kata ("according to") indicate that the faith binding Paul and Titus is one they hold in common. The faith is not private or exclusive to their personal relationship; it is the shared faith of the community, the common inheritance of all who believe. The word koinos here functions positively -- the faith is common in the sense of universally shared among believers, not common in the sense of ordinary or vulgar. In Acts 2:44, the early community "had all things in common" (eichon hapanta koina). The faith Paul and Titus share is the one faith (Ephesians 4:5) held by the one people of God.

Titus himself does not appear in the book of Acts, but he figures prominently in Paul's letters. In Galatians 2:1-3, Paul describes bringing Titus to Jerusalem: "I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me... But even Titus, who was with me, was not forced to be circumcised, though he was a Greek." Titus's uncircumcised status became a test case for the principle that Gentile believers need not submit to circumcision. In 2 Corinthians, Titus serves as Paul's emissary to Corinth during the painful conflict: Paul sent him with the "severe letter" (2 Cor 7:6-7, 13-15; 8:6, 16-17, 23; 12:18). Paul describes Titus as his "partner and fellow worker" (2 Cor 8:23) and expresses deep confidence in him. Titus is a Gentile believer, a tested co-worker, and a delegate Paul trusts with difficult assignments. His deployment to Crete follows the pattern established in Corinth: Paul sends Titus into a challenging situation, trusting his competence and character.

**"Grace and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Savior"** (charis kai eirene apo theou patros kai Christou Iesou tou soteros hemon). The greeting here is the standard twofold "grace and peace," without the third element "mercy" (eleos) that appeared in the greetings to Timothy (1 Tim 1:2; 2 Tim 1:2). The omission may reflect the different character of this letter: 1 and 2 Timothy were deeply personal, and the inclusion of mercy anticipated themes of God's compassion toward those who suffer or who have sinned. Titus is more administrative in register, and the standard greeting suffices.

The most notable feature of this greeting is the distribution of the title soter ("savior"). In verse 3, God is called "God our Savior" (tou soteros hemon theou). Here in verse 4, Christ Jesus is called "our Savior" (tou soteros hemon). The same title applied to both God and Christ creates a theological pairing that runs throughout the letter. In chapter 2, the pattern will continue: 2:10 speaks of "God our Savior," while 2:13 speaks of "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ." In chapter 3: 3:4 speaks of "God our Savior," while 3:6 speaks of "Jesus Christ our Savior." The alternation is deliberate and creates a theological unity: the saving work belongs to God and is executed through Christ. The God who saves and the Christ through whom he saves share the same title because they share the same purpose. For a first-century Jewish hearer, steeped in the conviction that salvation belongs to YHWH alone (Isaiah 43:11, "I, I am the LORD, and besides me there is no savior"), the application of soter to both God and Christ would be remarkable: it places Christ within the sphere of Israel's God's own saving activity.

## Verses 5-9: The Appointment of Elders -- Structure for the Communities

"This is why I left you in Crete, so that you might put in order what remained to be done, and appoint elders in every town as I directed you -- if anyone is above reproach, the husband of one wife, and his children are believers and not open to the charge of debauchery or insubordination. For an overseer, as God's steward, must be above reproach. He must not be arrogant or quick-tempered or a drunkard or violent or greedy for gain, but hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined. He must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught, so that he may be able to give instruction in sound doctrine and also to rebuke those who contradict it."

**What the author was arguing immediately before:** The greeting (1:1-4) established the theological framework -- Paul's apostleship serving the faith of God's elect, grounded in the hope of eternal life promised by the God who cannot lie, now manifested through preaching. **What the author argues immediately after:** The description of false teachers (1:10-16) identifies the specific threat that makes the appointment of qualified elders urgent. **How this section connects the two:** The theological framework of the greeting establishes what the communities need (faith, knowledge of truth, godliness, hope of eternal life), and the appointment of elders provides the structural means of delivering and protecting these things. The elder qualifications culminate in the ability to teach sound doctrine and refute opponents (1:9) -- which leads directly into the identification of those opponents in 1:10-16. The elder qualifications are not an abstract ideal but a direct response to a concrete threat: the communities need leaders who can withstand the false teachers described in the verses that follow.

**"This is why I left you in Crete, so that you might put in order what remained to be done"** (toutou charin apelipon se en Krete, hina ta leiponta epidiorthose). The phrase toutou charin ("for this reason, on this account") indicates that Titus's presence in Crete has a defined purpose. The verb apoleipein ("to leave behind, to leave remaining") in the aorist describes a past action: Paul left Titus in Crete at some point and has now departed. The island of Crete (Krete) is named as the location.

The purpose is stated with two infinitives joined by kai ("and"):

(1) Ta leiponta epidiorthose -- "that you might set right what remained" or "put in order what was left unfinished." The verb epidiorthoun ("to set right, to correct, to put in proper order") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of epi ("upon, further") + dia ("through") + orthoun ("to make straight, to set upright"). The prefix epi- may intensify ("thoroughly set right") or indicate continuation ("further set right, complete the setting-right already begun"). The neuter plural participle ta leiponta ("the things remaining, what was left over, the unfinished matters") indicates that Paul began work on Crete but left before completing it. The organizational task was partially done; Titus is to finish it.

(2) The second purpose, joined by kai, follows: appoint elders.

**"And appoint elders in every town as I directed you"** (kai katasteses kata polin presbyterous, hos ego soi dietaxamen). The verb kathistanai ("to appoint, to put in charge, to establish in a position") describes the formal installation of persons in an office. In Acts 6:3, the congregation is told to select seven men "whom we may appoint (katastesomen) over this duty." In the LXX, the verb is used for the appointment of leaders: Exodus 18:21, where Jethro advises Moses to appoint (katasteseis) capable men as officials over the people. The verb implies formal, authorized appointment -- not informal emergence of leadership but deliberate installation.

The persons to be appointed are presbyteroi ("elders, older men, members of the council of elders"). The noun presbyteros in its basic sense means "older person" and carries the connotation of seniority, maturity, and the wisdom that comes with age. In Jewish communal life, the presbyteroi constituted the governing body of a synagogue or community. Elders governed Israel from the earliest times: Exodus 3:16 ("Go and gather the elders of Israel together"); Exodus 18:12; Numbers 11:16-17 ("Gather for me seventy men of the elders of Israel"); Deuteronomy 21:19; Ruth 4:2; 1 Kings 8:1. In the synagogue, the presbyteroi were the recognized leaders who managed the community's affairs, adjudicated disputes, and provided pastoral oversight. The appointment of presbyteroi in the new communities on Crete follows the synagogue pattern: each community needs a recognized body of senior, qualified leaders.

The phrase kata polin ("in every town, city by city") indicates that the appointment is to be comprehensive: not just in one or two locations but across the island, in every community that has been established. This suggests that Paul's work on Crete had resulted in multiple communities in multiple towns, none of which yet had formal leadership.

The clause hos ego soi dietaxamen ("as I directed you") specifies that the qualifications about to be listed are not Titus's invention but Paul's instruction. The verb diatassein ("to direct, to order, to arrange, to give detailed instructions") describes authoritative instruction: Paul laid out specific criteria, and Titus is to follow them. The first person pronoun ego ("I") is emphatic: this comes from Paul's authority.

**"If anyone is above reproach"** (ei tis estin anenkletos). The conditional ei tis ("if anyone") introduces the criteria by which candidates are to be evaluated. The first and overarching qualification is anenkletos -- "above reproach, blameless, not able to be accused, without indictment." The adjective is composed of the alpha-privative (an-) and enkletos (from enkalein, "to bring a charge against, to accuse, to indict"). An anenkletos person is one against whom no legitimate charge can be brought. In Colossians 1:22, Paul writes that Christ's purpose is to present believers "holy and blameless and above reproach (anenklétous) before him." In 1 Corinthians 1:8, Christ "will sustain you to the end, guiltless (anenklétous) in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ." The qualification is not sinless perfection but a life against which no public accusation can stand. This is the baseline: everything that follows specifies what "above reproach" looks like in practice.

**"The husband of one wife"** (mias gynaikos aner). Literally: "a one-woman man" or "a man of one woman." The phrase is identical to 1 Timothy 3:2, where the same qualification appeared for overseers, and 1 Timothy 3:12, where it appeared for deacons. The genitive construction mias gynaikos ("of one wife/woman") describes a characteristic: the elder is a man characterized by faithfulness to one woman. In the first-century context, where polygamy (though rare in the Roman world) still existed in some Jewish communities, and where serial marriage through easy divorce was common in both Jewish and Roman practice, the qualification insists on marital fidelity and singularity. The elder's domestic life must be characterized by undivided commitment to one spouse.

**"And his children are believers and not open to the charge of debauchery or insubordination"** (tekna echon pista, me en kategoria asotias e anypotakta). The elder must have children (tekna) who are pista -- the adjective pistos here can mean either "believing/faithful" (they have embraced the faith) or "trustworthy/reliable" (they are well-behaved and dependable). The context points toward the first sense: the elder's household has responded to the gospel. The logic is similar to 1 Timothy 3:4-5: "He must manage his own household well, with all dignity keeping his children submissive, for if someone does not know how to manage his own household, how will he care for God's church?" The household is the proving ground for leadership: if a man's own children have not responded to his influence and instruction, his capacity to lead a community is in question.

Two negative conditions are specified. The children must not be en kategoria asotias -- "under accusation of debauchery." The noun asotia ("debauchery, profligacy, dissipation, reckless wastefulness") describes a life of excess and moral abandonment. In Ephesians 5:18: "And do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery (asotia)." In Luke 15:13, the prodigal son "squandered his property in reckless living" (zōn asōtōs). The word implies not merely occasional lapses but a pattern of uncontrolled excess. And the children must not be anypotakta -- "insubordinate, unruly, not subject to authority." The same adjective appeared in 1 Timothy 1:9 in the vice list describing those for whom the law is laid down. It will appear again in Titus 1:10 to describe the false teachers. The connection is deliberate: the elder must not have children who exhibit the very quality that characterizes the opponents he is being appointed to confront.

**"For an overseer, as God's steward, must be above reproach"** (dei gar ton episkopon hos theou oikonomon anenkleton einai). The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the reason for the requirements: an overseer must be above reproach because of what an overseer is -- God's steward. The transition from presbyteros ("elder") in verse 5 to episkopos ("overseer") in verse 7 is significant. The two terms refer to the same office, described from different angles. The presbyteros designation emphasizes the person's standing within the community (senior, mature, respected). The episkopos designation emphasizes the person's function (one who watches over, supervises, oversees). The same identification of the two roles appears in Acts 20:17, 28, where Paul summons the presbyterous ("elders") of the Ephesian church and tells them: "Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you episkopous ("overseers"), to care for the church of God." The terms are functionally interchangeable: the elders are the overseers, and the overseers are the elders. One word describes who they are in the community; the other describes what they do.

The overseer is described as theou oikonomon -- "God's steward." The noun oikonomos ("steward, household manager, administrator") is composed of oikos ("house") + nemein ("to manage, to distribute, to administer"). The oikonomos was the person entrusted with managing a household's affairs on behalf of the owner. In Luke 12:42, Jesus asks: "Who then is the faithful and wise steward (oikonomos), whom his master will set over his household, to give them their portion of food at the proper time?" In Luke 16:1-8, the parable of the dishonest steward (oikonomos) explores what happens when a steward mismanages his master's property. In 1 Corinthians 4:1-2: "This is how one should regard us, as servants of Christ and stewards (oikonomous) of the mysteries of God. Moreover, it is required of stewards (oikonomois) that they be found faithful." The designation theou oikonomon -- "steward of God" -- means that the overseer manages not his own household but God's. The community belongs to God; the overseer administers it on God's behalf. This understanding transforms the nature of the authority: the overseer's authority is delegated, not inherent. He acts for the owner, not for himself. And stewardship demands accountability: the steward must give an account to the one whose property he manages.

The verb dei ("it is necessary, it must be") indicates not a suggestion but a requirement. Given what the overseer is (God's steward), the quality that follows (above reproach) is not optional but structurally necessary. A steward who can be credibly accused of misconduct damages the reputation of the one he represents.

The list of qualifications that follows divides into negative (what the overseer must not be) and positive (what the overseer must be):

**Negative qualifications -- what the overseer must not be:**

(1) **"Not arrogant"** (me authade). The adjective authades ("self-willed, arrogant, self-pleasing, stubborn") is composed of autos ("self") + hedesthai ("to please"). An authades person is one who pleases himself, who follows his own desires without regard for others, who insists on his own way. In 2 Peter 2:10, the word describes false teachers who are "bold and willful" (tolmetai authadeis). Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3.30) defines the authades person as one who disregards others' opinions and acts solely for self-gratification. For a steward of God's household, self-will is disqualifying because the steward's task is to serve the owner's purpose, not his own preferences. An arrogant leader will shape the community around his own desires rather than God's instruction.

(2) **"Not quick-tempered"** (me orgilon). The adjective orgilos ("prone to anger, quick-tempered, irascible") describes a disposition, not a single act. The orgē ("anger") that characterizes this person is habitual and easily triggered. Proverbs 22:24 (LXX): "Do not befriend an angry man (andri thymōdei)" -- the wisdom tradition recognizes that an easily angered person is dangerous to associate with and unfit for leadership. The elder must deal with difficult people (the false teachers of 1:10-16); an irascible temperament would escalate rather than resolve conflicts.

(3) **"Not a drunkard"** (me paroinon). The adjective paroinos ("given to wine, drunken, addicted to wine") is composed of para ("beside, alongside") + oinos ("wine"). The same word appeared in 1 Timothy 3:3 among the overseer qualifications there. Drunkenness destroys the self-control (sōphrosynē) that is central to the elder's character.

(4) **"Not violent"** (me plekten). The noun plektes ("striker, bully, violent person") describes one who resorts to physical force. The same qualification appeared in 1 Timothy 3:3. A steward of God's household resolves disputes through instruction and persuasion, not through blows.

(5) **"Not greedy for gain"** (me aischrokerde). The adjective aischrokerdes ("fond of dishonest gain, greedy for shameful profit") is composed of aischros ("shameful, base, disgraceful") + kerdos ("gain, profit, advantage"). The qualification takes on specific urgency in the context of the Cretan false teachers, who are described in verse 11 as teaching "for the sake of shameful gain" (aischrou kerdous charin). The elder must be the opposite of the false teacher: where the false teacher uses his position for profit, the elder must be free from the love of gain.

**Positive qualifications -- what the overseer must be:**

(1) **"Hospitable"** (philoxenon). The adjective philoxenos ("hospitable, loving strangers, welcoming guests") is composed of philos ("loving, fond of") + xenos ("stranger, foreigner, guest"). The same qualification appeared in 1 Timothy 3:2. In the ancient world, where inns were unreliable and often dangerous, hospitality was essential for the functioning of the community and the movement of itinerant workers. Romans 12:13: "Contribute to the needs of the saints and seek to show hospitality" (ten philoxenian diōkontes). Hebrews 13:2: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares." 3 John 5-8 praises Gaius for showing hospitality to traveling brothers. The hospitable elder opens his home to the community and to travelers, making the household a center of ministry rather than a private enclave.

(2) **"A lover of good"** (philagathon). The adjective philagathos ("loving what is good, devoted to good things") is composed of philos ("loving") + agathos ("good"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. It describes a comprehensive orientation toward what is good -- good people, good actions, good purposes. The elder is not merely tolerant of good but actively drawn to it, seeking it out and promoting it.

(3) **"Self-controlled"** (sophrona). The adjective sōphrōn ("of sound mind, self-controlled, temperate, moderate, prudent") is one of the cardinal virtues of the Pastoral Epistles. The sōphrōn-word group appears throughout: sōphrosynē in 1 Timothy 2:9, 15; sōphronismos in 2 Timothy 1:7; sōphronizein in Titus 2:4; sōphronein in Titus 2:6; sōphronōs in Titus 2:12; sōphrōn here and in 1 Timothy 3:2. The concept describes the mental clarity and self-mastery that keeps a person from excess in any direction -- neither panicked nor reckless, neither indulgent nor harsh, but balanced, measured, and in control of his own impulses. This is the antidote to the false teachers' influence: where they upset whole households (1:11), the self-controlled elder maintains stability.

(4) **"Upright"** (dikaion). The adjective dikaios ("just, righteous, upright, fair") describes a person who acts rightly toward others, who gives each person what is due, who conducts his affairs with fairness. In the LXX, dikaios is one of the most important words in the vocabulary of covenant faithfulness: the righteous person lives according to God's standards and treats others justly.

(5) **"Holy"** (hosion). The adjective hosios ("holy, devout, pious, pleasing to God") describes a person set apart for God's purposes, one whose life is characterized by reverence and devotion. In the LXX, hosios often translates the Hebrew hasid ("faithful, devoted, loyal to God"). Psalm 16:10 (LXX 15:10): "You will not allow your holy one (ton hosion sou) to see corruption." Deuteronomy 32:4: "A faithful God (theos pistos) and without injustice; righteous (dikaios) and holy (hosios) is the LORD." The pairing of dikaios and hosios in the present list echoes this pairing in the LXX: the elder is both just toward people and devoted toward God.

(6) **"Disciplined"** (enkrate). The adjective enkrates ("self-controlled, mastering one's desires, having power over") is composed of en ("in") + kratos ("power, strength, might"). The enkrates person has power over himself -- he governs his own appetites and impulses. In Galatians 5:23, enkrateia ("self-control") appears as the final fruit of the Spirit. In 1 Corinthians 7:9, the word describes the capacity to manage one's sexual desires. In 1 Corinthians 9:25, Paul uses the image of an athlete who exercises enkrateia -- self-control in all things -- to win the prize. The elder must be a person who demonstrates mastery over his own life before he can be trusted with the oversight of others' lives.

**"He must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught"** (antechomenon tou kata ten didachen pistou logou). The verb antechesthai ("to hold fast, to cling to, to devote oneself to") in the middle voice describes active, deliberate attachment. The elder does not merely know the word; he holds onto it, grips it, does not let go. The object is tou pistou logou -- "the faithful/trustworthy word." The adjective pistos ("trustworthy, reliable, faithful") describes the word's character: it is dependable, worthy of trust. The phrase kata ten didachen ("according to the teaching, as taught") specifies that this trustworthy word is not the elder's private interpretation but the word as transmitted through apostolic teaching. The elder holds fast to what he has been taught -- the received deposit of sound instruction, the didache that was delivered by Paul and his associates.

This qualification is the climax of the list and the bridge to the section that follows. All the preceding character qualities serve this purpose: the elder must be the kind of person who can hold the word firmly and deploy it effectively. Character qualifications without doctrinal competence would produce good men who cannot teach. Doctrinal competence without character would produce knowledgeable men who cannot lead. Both must be present.

**"So that he may be able to give instruction in sound doctrine and also to rebuke those who contradict it"** (hina dynatos e kai parakalein en te didaskalia te hygiainouse kai tous antilegontas elenchein). The hina clause ("in order that, so that") states the purpose of holding firm to the trustworthy word. The purpose is twofold:

(1) **To give instruction in sound doctrine** (parakalein en te didaskalia te hygiainouse). The verb parakalein ("to exhort, to encourage, to instruct, to comfort, to appeal to") describes the positive ministry of teaching. The teaching is qualified as hygiainousa -- "sound, healthy, in good condition." The medical metaphor that characterizes the Pastoral Epistles appears again: sound teaching is healthy teaching, and it produces health in those who receive it. The verb hygiainein ("to be healthy, to be sound") runs through these letters as the standard against which all teaching is measured (1 Tim 1:10; 6:3; 2 Tim 1:13; 4:3; Titus 1:9, 13; 2:1, 2). Teaching that does not produce health in the community is not sound, regardless of its other qualities.

(2) **To rebuke those who contradict it** (tous antilegontas elenchein). The verb elenchein ("to rebuke, to refute, to convict, to expose, to reprove") describes a confrontational ministry -- not confrontation for its own sake, but the necessary work of correcting those who are wrong. The participle tous antilegontas ("those who speak against, those who contradict, those who oppose") identifies the targets: people who are actively opposing the sound teaching. The elder must not only teach the truth; he must also confront error. The two tasks are inseparable. In a community where no one contradicts sound teaching, only the first task would be needed. But Crete is not such a community -- as the next verses will make clear.

The transition from verse 9 to verse 10 is seamless: the elder must be able to rebuke those who contradict -- "for" (gar, v. 10) there are many such contradictors. The qualifications are not hypothetical but responsive to an existing crisis.

## Verses 10-16: The False Teachers -- Identification, Condemnation, and the Test of Works

"For there are many who are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision party. They must be silenced, since they are upsetting whole households by teaching for shameful gain what they ought not to teach. One of the Cretans, a prophet of their own, said, 'Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons.' This testimony is true. Therefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith, not devoting themselves to Jewish myths and the commands of people who turn away from the truth. To the pure, all things are pure, but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure; but both their minds and their consciences are defiled. They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works. They are detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work."

**What the author was arguing immediately before:** The elder qualifications (1:5-9) culminated in the requirement that elders be able to refute opponents. **What the author argues immediately after:** Chapter 2 opens with "But as for you, teach what accords with sound doctrine" (2:1), turning to the positive content of what Titus himself should teach. **How this section connects the two:** The false teachers (1:10-16) are the reason the elders need the refutation capability described in 1:9, and their teaching is the antithesis of the "sound doctrine" that Titus is to teach in 2:1. The section serves as the dark background against which both the elder qualifications and Titus's own teaching mission are defined. Everything the false teachers are, the elders must not be. Everything the false teachers teach, Titus must counter.

**"For there are many who are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision party"** (eisin gar polloi [kai] anypotaktoi, mataiologoi kai phrenapatal, malista hoi ek tes peritomes). The conjunction gar ("for") connects directly to the need for elders who can refute opponents (v. 9). The reason is that polloi ("many") such persons exist on Crete. The problem is not a few isolated individuals but a widespread phenomenon.

Three terms describe these persons:

(1) **Insubordinate** (anypotaktoi). The adjective anypotaktos ("not subject to authority, rebellious, undisciplined, insubordinate") appeared already in verse 6 among the conditions that disqualify an elder's children. Its reappearance here creates a pointed connection: the very quality that would disqualify an elder's child from the community's respect is the defining quality of these false teachers. They refuse to submit to apostolic authority, to the teaching they have received, or to the order of the community. The word appeared also in 1 Timothy 1:9 in the vice list describing those for whom the law is laid down. Insubordination is the fundamental posture from which all their other errors flow.

(2) **Empty talkers** (mataiologoi). The noun mataiologos ("one who talks idly, speaker of vanities, empty talker") is composed of mataios ("vain, empty, futile, purposeless") + logos ("word, speech"). The related noun mataiologia ("vain discussion, empty talk") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:6, where certain persons "wandered away into vain discussion." The designation identifies the content of their teaching as empty -- not merely wrong in its conclusions but devoid of substance. It produces nothing. It leads nowhere. It is sound without significance.

(3) **Deceivers** (phrenapatal). The noun phrenapates ("mind-deceiver, one who deceives the mind") is composed of phren ("mind, understanding, thinking") + apatan ("to deceive, to cheat, to mislead"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. It describes persons who mislead not through outright lies but through the corruption of the thinking process itself. They distort the way people reason, leading them to conclusions that seem plausible but are false. The deception operates at the level of the mind -- the phren -- the very faculty by which truth is evaluated. This makes the deception especially dangerous: the deceived person does not realize he has been misled because the instrument of evaluation (the mind) has itself been corrupted.

**"Especially those of the circumcision party"** (malista hoi ek tes peritomes). The adverb malista ("especially, particularly, above all, most of all") indicates that the insubordinate empty-talkers are drawn primarily from a specific group: hoi ek tes peritomes -- "those of the circumcision." The phrase identifies ethnic or religious origin: these are Jewish believers (or Jewish associates of the community) who insist on circumcision as a requirement for full participation in the people of God. The same phrase appears in Acts 10:45 and 11:2, where "the circumcision party" (hoi ek peritomes) are shocked that the Holy Spirit has been poured out on uncircumcised Gentiles. In Galatians 2:12, Peter drew back from eating with Gentiles "because he feared those of the circumcision" (tous ek peritomes). In Colossians 4:11, Paul mentions "the only ones of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God."

The identification is specific and significant. The false teaching on Crete is not coming from pagan philosophy or Gentile religious practice. It comes from within the Jewish wing of the community -- from people who bring Jewish interpretive traditions, Jewish purity regulations, and the insistence on circumcision into the community's life in ways that Paul considers contrary to the gospel. This identification will be confirmed by verse 14's mention of "Jewish myths" and by the purity discussion of verse 15.

**"They must be silenced"** (hous dei epistomizein). The verb epistomizein ("to stop the mouth, to silence, to muzzle") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of epi ("upon") + stoma ("mouth"). The word was used for muzzling an animal or stopping a person from speaking. The imperative is strong: these teachers must be prevented from continuing to teach. The verb dei ("it is necessary") indicates that this is not optional but required. The language goes beyond Paul's instruction in 1 Timothy, where Timothy was to "charge" the false teachers not to teach differently (1 Tim 1:3); here the instruction is to silence them entirely. The escalation reflects the situation's urgency: the damage being done is severe enough that the teaching must be stopped, not merely corrected.

**"Since they are upsetting whole households by teaching for shameful gain what they ought not to teach"** (hoitines holous oikous anatrepousin didaskontes ha me dei aischrou kerdous charin). The relative pronoun hoitines ("who are of such a character that") introduces the reason for silencing them. The verb anatrepein ("to overturn, to upset, to subvert, to destroy") describes the effect: they are overturning holous oikous -- "whole households." The noun oikos ("house, household, family") describes the basic social unit of the first-century world: the household, which included the head of household, spouse, children, slaves, and dependents. The early communities met in houses (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2), and households often came to faith as units (Acts 16:15, Lydia's household; Acts 16:33, the jailer's household; 1 Corinthians 1:16, the household of Stephanas). When a false teacher subverts a household, he destroys the basic unit of community life and faith. The adjective holous ("whole, entire") intensifies: they are not picking off individuals from the edges but overturning entire households.

The content of their teaching is described negatively: ha me dei -- "things that it is not necessary to teach" or "things they ought not to teach." The specific content is not detailed here but will be identified in verse 14 as "Jewish myths and commands of people who turn away from the truth."

The motive is exposed: aischrou kerdous charin -- "for the sake of shameful gain." The genitive aischrou kerdous is composed of aischros ("shameful, base, disgraceful") + kerdos ("gain, profit"). The preposition charin ("for the sake of, on account of") identifies the motive. These teachers teach what they teach not out of conviction but for profit. Their ministry is a business, and their doctrine is shaped by what pays. The accusation is devastating: the content of the teaching is driven by the prospect of financial return, not by fidelity to the truth. The elder qualification of verse 7 -- "not greedy for shameful gain" (me aischrokerde) -- was aimed precisely at this contrast: the elder must be the opposite of what these teachers are.

**"One of the Cretans, a prophet of their own, said, 'Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons'"** (eipen tis ex auton, idios auton prophetes, Kretes aei pseustai, kaka theria, gasteres argai). Paul quotes a saying attributed to one of the Cretans' own prophets. The identification of the source as tis ex auton -- "one of them," i.e., a Cretan -- means that the criticism comes from an insider, not an outsider. The title prophetes ("prophet") is applied with the possessive idios auton ("their own"), indicating that this person held a position of cultural authority among the Cretans themselves.

The saying attributed to this prophet is traditionally associated with Epimenides of Crete, a semi-legendary figure of the sixth century BCE whom the Cretans regarded with reverence as a sage, seer, and purifier. Ancient sources credit Epimenides with various oracular and prophetic activities, including the purification of Athens from a plague. By Paul's time, Epimenides was a well-known figure in Greek literary tradition. The designation "prophet" (prophetes) for a pagan sage is notable: Paul uses the word not in the sense of a prophet of Israel's God but in the Greek sense of a divinely inspired speaker, a mantis or oracle. Paul meets the Cretans on their own cultural ground: he cites an authority they themselves recognize.

The saying contains three charges:

(1) **"Always liars"** (aei pseustai). The adverb aei ("always, constantly, at all times") makes the accusation absolute. The noun pseustes ("liar, one who speaks falsely") identifies the Cretans' proverbial dishonesty. The association of Crete with lying was widespread in the ancient Mediterranean: the verb kretizein ("to act like a Cretan, to lie") was a recognized idiom. The Cretans' own claim that Zeus's tomb was on Crete (a claim that scandalized Greeks who believed Zeus was immortal) was cited as a prime example of Cretan mendacity. Callimachus, the third-century BCE poet, wrote: "Cretans are always liars; for a tomb, O Lord, Cretans built for you; but you did not die, for you are forever" (Hymn to Zeus 8-9).

(2) **"Evil beasts"** (kaka theria). The adjective kakos ("evil, bad, harmful") modifies therion ("wild animal, beast"). The charge depicts the Cretans as savage, brutal, operating at the level of animal instinct rather than human reason. The association of Crete with bestial behavior may connect to the island's mythological associations -- the Minotaur, the beast of the labyrinth, was Crete's most famous mythological figure.

(3) **"Lazy gluttons"** (gasteres argai). The noun gaster ("stomach, belly") used metonymically describes a person governed by appetite. The adjective argos ("idle, lazy, inactive, useless") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and ergon ("work"). The combination describes persons who are driven by appetite but unwilling to work -- consumers who produce nothing, devourers who contribute nothing. Paul uses gaster similarly in Romans 16:18: "such persons do not serve our Lord Christ, but their own belly (te heauton koilia), and by smooth talk and flattery they deceive the hearts of the naive." The stomach-as-master is a figure for the life governed by physical appetite rather than higher purposes.

**"This testimony is true"** (he martyria haute estin alethes). Paul endorses the quotation. The noun martyria ("testimony, witness") treats the Cretan prophet's saying as evidence. The adjective alethes ("true, genuine, real") affirms it. Paul does not cite the saying to ridicule an entire ethnic group for its own sake but to characterize the cultural milieu in which the false teachers operate. The false teachers are Cretans (or are operating among Cretans), and the character traits described by their own prophet -- dishonesty, brutishness, appetite-driven laziness -- are visible in the false teachers' behavior. They teach for shameful gain (appetite-driven laziness = lazy gluttons; shameful gain = governed by the belly), they teach what they ought not (deceitfulness = always liars), and they upset whole households (destructiveness = evil beasts). The Cretan prophet's diagnosis fits the false teachers' symptoms.

**"Therefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith"** (di' hen aitian elenche autous apotomos, hina hygiainosin en te pistei). The phrase di' hen aitian ("for which reason, on this account") connects the rebuke to the preceding diagnosis. The verb elenchein ("to rebuke, to reprove, to refute, to convict") repeats from verse 9 -- there, the elder was to rebuke opponents; here, Titus himself is to perform the rebuking until elders are established. The adverb apotomos ("sharply, severely, curtly") is composed of apo ("away from") + temnein ("to cut"). The rebuke is to cut sharply, leaving no room for ambiguity or diplomatic evasion. In 2 Corinthians 13:10, Paul uses the related adverb apotomōs: "I write these things while absent, in order that when present I may not have to be severe (apotomōs)." The severity of the rebuke corresponds to the severity of the situation: gentle correction would be inadequate when whole households are being overturned.

The purpose of the rebuke is stated positively: hina hygiainosin en te pistei -- "that they may be sound in the faith." The verb hygiainein ("to be healthy, to be sound, to be in good condition") returns the medical metaphor to the center of the argument. The false teachers and those they influence are sick -- their teaching is diseased, their behavior is symptomatic. The rebuke aims at health. The goal is not punishment for its own sake but restoration to soundness in the faith (en te pistei). The preposition en ("in") locates the health within the sphere of faith: it is specifically their faith that needs to be restored to health. The fact that the purpose clause exists at all -- "that they may be sound" -- indicates that Paul has not given up on these people. They can still be corrected. The sharp rebuke serves a restorative purpose, like a surgeon's cut that serves healing.

The pronoun autous ("them") is important. Who is being rebuked? In the immediate context, "them" could refer to the false teachers of verse 10 or to the broader Cretan community that has been influenced by them. The purpose clause "that they may be sound in the faith" points more naturally toward the community members who are being led astray: the false teachers are to be silenced (v. 11), but the misled community members are to be rebuked toward health. Both actions serve the same end: the protection and restoration of the community's faith.

**"Not devoting themselves to Jewish myths and the commands of people who turn away from the truth"** (me prosechontes Ioudaikois mythois kai entolais anthropon apostrephomonon ten aletheian). The verb prosechein ("to give heed to, to devote oneself to, to pay attention to") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:4 in the identical construction: "nor to devote themselves to myths and endless genealogies." The parallel is deliberate and connects the two situations: the false teaching in Ephesus and the false teaching in Crete share the same root problem, even if the specific manifestations differ.

The myths are now specified: they are Ioudaikois mythois -- "Jewish myths." The adjective Ioudaikos ("Jewish, pertaining to the Jews") identifies the cultural and religious origin of these stories. They are not Greek myths or Roman myths but elaborations of Jewish scriptural and traditional narratives. As noted in the analysis of 1 Timothy 1:4, the most natural referent is the elaborate midrashic and haggadic tradition that grew around the biblical narratives in the Second Temple period -- speculative stories about patriarchs, angels, cosmic origins, and hidden wisdom that were built on the scaffolding of the biblical text but went far beyond its actual content. The Book of Jubilees, the Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs, portions of 1 Enoch, and similar works represent the kind of elaborate narrative speculation that Paul considers mythoi -- tales that, however interesting, distract from the gospel's core content.

The second element, entolais anthropon apostrephomonon ten aletheian -- "commands of people who turn away from the truth" -- identifies a specific type of teaching: human commands that function as substitutes for divine truth. The noun entole ("command, commandment, instruction") is the word used for God's commandments throughout the LXX (Exodus 20:6; Deuteronomy 5:10; Psalm 19:8 LXX [18:9]; Psalm 119:96 LXX [118:96]). But these commands are not God's; they are anthropon -- "of humans, of people." The genitive anthropon identifies the source: human beings, not God, are the originators of these commands.

The participial phrase apostrephomonon ten aletheian ("who turn away from the truth") further characterizes these human commanders: they are people in the process of turning away from the truth. The verb apostrephein ("to turn away from, to turn one's back on") in the middle/passive describes a self-directed action: they are turning themselves away from the truth. Their commands are the product of their apostasy -- they have turned from the truth and are now generating substitute commands that lead others in the same direction.

The phrase echoes Jesus' critique of the Pharisees in Mark 7:7-8 (quoting Isaiah 29:13): "In vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men (entalmatā anthrōpōn). You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men (tēn paradosin tōn anthrōpōn)." The parallel is precise: human commands that replace divine truth, traditions of human origin that claim divine authority. Paul's critique of the Cretan false teachers follows the pattern Jesus established for critiquing Torah-based tradition that obscures rather than serves God's purpose.

**"To the pure, all things are pure, but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure; but both their minds and their consciences are defiled"** (panta kathara tois katharois; tois de memiammenois kai apistois ouden katharon, alla memiantal auton kai ho nous kai he syneidesis). This is one of the most dense and consequential statements in the chapter. It functions as the theological principle that undercuts the false teachers' program.

The first clause, panta kathara tois katharois -- "all things are pure to the pure" -- is a compressed principle with far-reaching implications. The adjective katharos ("clean, pure, undefiled") in the LXX carries both ritual and moral connotations. Ritually, the clean/unclean distinction governed Israel's life: clean and unclean foods (Leviticus 11), clean and unclean bodily conditions (Leviticus 12-15), clean and unclean persons (Numbers 19), clean and unclean objects. Morally, the clean heart is the undivided heart that is oriented toward God (Psalm 51:10 LXX [50:12]). In this statement, Paul collapses the distinction between the external and the internal: for the person who is katharos (clean, pure), all external things are katharos. The purity of the person determines the purity of the thing encountered. This principle operates in the same sphere as Jesus' teaching in Mark 7:14-23, where Jesus declares: "There is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him, but the things that come out of a person are what defile him" (Mark 7:15). The source of impurity is not the external object but the internal condition.

The relevance to the false teachers' program is immediate. If the false teachers are imposing food laws, purity regulations, or other external commands ("commands of people who turn away from the truth," v. 14), this principle undercuts their foundation: purity is a matter of the person, not of the thing. External regulations cannot produce internal purity. For the person already cleansed -- cleansed by God's action in Christ, cleansed through faith -- external things do not contaminate.

Paul uses a similar principle in Romans 14:14, 20: "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean" (14:14); "Everything is indeed clean, but it is wrong for anyone to make another stumble by what he eats" (14:20). In 1 Timothy 4:3-4: "[They] require abstinence from foods that God created to be received with thanksgiving by those who believe and know the truth. For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving." The consistency across Paul's letters establishes this as a fundamental principle: the creation is good, and the believer's relationship to creation is governed by internal purity, not external regulation.

The second clause introduces the contrast: tois de memiammenois kai apistois ouden katharon -- "but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure." The perfect passive participle memiammenois (from miainein, "to stain, to defile, to pollute, to corrupt") describes persons who have been defiled and remain in that state. The adjective apistois ("unbelieving, faithless, without faith") adds the theological dimension: the defilement is connected to unbelief. The combination is deliberate: defilement and unbelief travel together. For such persons, ouden katharon -- "nothing is pure." No amount of external regulation, dietary restriction, or ritual observance can produce purity for a person whose interior is defiled. The false teachers may insist on food laws and purity codes, but if their minds and consciences are defiled, their elaborate external observances accomplish nothing. They are polishing the outside of a cup while the inside remains filthy (cf. Matthew 23:25-26; Luke 11:39-40).

The final clause specifies the location of the defilement: alla memiantal auton kai ho nous kai he syneidesis -- "but both their mind and their conscience are defiled." Two faculties are named:

(1) **The mind** (ho nous). The nous is the faculty of understanding, reason, and judgment -- the capacity by which a person evaluates, discerns, and makes decisions. In Romans 12:2: "be transformed by the renewal of your mind (tou noos)." In Romans 1:28, those who did not honor God were given up to "a debased mind (adokimon noun)." When the nous is defiled, the capacity to think rightly, to evaluate truth, to discern good from evil is corrupted. The defilement of the mind explains why the false teachers cannot see the truth even when it is presented to them: their instrument of perception is itself contaminated.

(2) **The conscience** (he syneidesis). The syneidesis is the moral awareness, the inner witness, the faculty that evaluates one's own conduct. In 1 Timothy 1:19, the rejection of "good conscience" was the mechanism by which faith was destroyed. In 1 Timothy 4:2, false teachers have "their consciences seared with a hot iron." Here, the conscience is memiantal -- defiled, stained, polluted. A defiled conscience can no longer accurately distinguish right from wrong. It has lost its calibration. The person with a defiled conscience may feel justified in what he does precisely because the faculty that should accuse him has been compromised.

The defilement of both mind and conscience constitutes a comprehensive internal corruption. The mind cannot think rightly; the conscience cannot judge rightly. External purity regulations cannot remedy what is fundamentally an internal problem. This is the theological ground on which Paul opposes the false teachers' program: they prescribe external remedies for an internal disease. Their commands about food, purity, and circumcision address the symptom (contact with things deemed impure) while ignoring the disease (a defiled mind and conscience that can only be cleansed by faith and the work of God).

**"They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works"** (theon homologousin eidenai, tois de ergois arnountai). The verb homologein ("to confess, to profess, to declare publicly, to acknowledge") describes their verbal claim: they say they know God. The verb eidenai ("to know") is the perfect infinitive of oida -- it denotes settled knowledge, intimate acquaintance. Their profession is not timid or hesitant; they claim thorough knowledge of God.

The adversative de ("but") introduces the contradiction: tois ergois arnountai -- "by their works they deny him." The verb arneisthai ("to deny, to repudiate, to disown, to refuse to acknowledge") is the direct opposite of homologein. What the mouth confesses, the life denies. The dative tois ergois ("by their works, through their deeds") identifies the instrument of denial: their actual conduct, their observable behavior. The contrast between profession and practice is absolute: they say one thing and do another. Their verbal confession of knowledge is contradicted by their visible conduct.

This principle -- that works reveal the true content of a person's faith -- has deep roots in Israel's wisdom tradition and in Jesus' own teaching. In Proverbs, the wise person is known by his actions, not merely by his words. In Jesus' teaching in Matthew 7:15-20: "You will recognize them by their fruits... every healthy tree bears good fruit, but the diseased tree bears bad fruit." In Matthew 7:21-23: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven." In James 2:17: "So also faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead." The principle operates here as a diagnostic tool: the false teachers' profession of knowing God is evaluated not by its verbal content but by its practical fruit. Their works deny what their words affirm.

**"They are detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work"** (bdelyktos ontes kai apeitheis kai pros pan ergon agathon adokimoi). Three adjectives deliver the final verdict:

(1) **Detestable** (bdelyktos). The adjective bdelyktos ("abominable, detestable, loathsome") appears only here in the New Testament. It is related to the noun bdelygma ("abomination, detestable thing"), which in the LXX is one of the strongest words for what God finds repulsive. In Deuteronomy 7:25-26: "The carved images of their gods you shall burn with fire... You shall not bring an abominable thing (bdelygma) into your house... You shall utterly detest (bdelygma bdelykse) it." In Proverbs 11:20 (LXX): "Crooked of mind are an abomination (bdelygma) to the LORD." In Proverbs 15:8-9 (LXX): "The sacrifices of the ungodly are an abomination (bdelygma) to the LORD"; "the ways of the ungodly are an abomination (bdelygma) to the LORD." The word carries the full weight of covenant revulsion: what is bdelyktos is what God finds repugnant. The irony is devastating. These people who profess to know God, who impose purity regulations and food laws in the name of godliness, are themselves bdelyktos -- detestable to the God they claim to serve. The pursuit of external purity has produced internal abomination.

(2) **Disobedient** (apeitheis). The adjective apeithes ("disobedient, not persuaded, stubborn, unbelieving") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and peithein ("to persuade, to obey"). The apeithes person is one who will not be persuaded, who resists instruction, who refuses to obey. The word connects back to anypotaktoi ("insubordinate") in verse 10: the false teachers began as insubordinate and remain disobedient. In Romans 1:30, apeitheis appears among the characteristics of those whom God has given over to a debased mind. In Acts 26:19, Paul tells Agrippa: "I was not disobedient (apeithes) to the heavenly vision" -- the implication being that disobedience to divine revelation was a real and terrible possibility that Paul consciously refused.

(3) **Unfit for any good work** (pros pan ergon agathon adokimoi). The adjective adokimos ("not standing the test, failing the test, unqualified, disqualified, rejected upon examination") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and dokimos ("tested, approved, proven, standing the test"). The word describes someone who has been tested and found wanting -- like a coin examined for purity and discovered to be counterfeit, or an athlete subjected to qualification trials and found inadequate. In 1 Corinthians 9:27, Paul writes: "I discipline my body and keep it under control, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified (adokimos)." In 2 Corinthians 13:5-7, Paul urges the Corinthians to test themselves: "Examine yourselves, to see whether you are in the faith. Test yourselves. Or do you not realize this about yourselves, that Jesus Christ is in you? -- unless indeed you fail to meet the test (adokimoi)!" In Romans 1:28, God gives up those who reject him "to a debased mind (adokimon noun)" -- using the same root. The false teachers have been subjected to the test of works -- the test that examines whether profession matches practice -- and they have failed. They are adokimoi: disqualified, rejected, counterfeit.

The sphere of their disqualification is specified: pros pan ergon agathon -- "for every good work." The preposition pros ("toward, for, with respect to") with the accusative indicates fitness or readiness for something. The adjective pas ("every, all, each") makes the disqualification comprehensive: not merely unfit for some good works but for every good work. The phrase ergon agathon ("good work") will become a major theme of the rest of the letter. In 2:7, Titus is to show himself "a model of good works" (typon kalon ergon). In 2:14, Christ "gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works" (zeloten kalon ergon). In 3:1, the community is to be "ready for every good work" (pros pan ergon agathon hetoimos). In 3:8, those who have believed in God are to "devote themselves to good works" (kalon ergon proistasthai). In 3:14, "our people must learn to devote themselves to good works" (kalon ergon proistasthai). The entire letter is oriented toward the production of good works as the visible evidence of genuine faith. The false teachers, who profess to know God but deny him by their works, are the anti-model -- the negative demonstration of what happens when profession replaces practice, when verbal knowledge replaces transformed conduct.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Titus 1 establishes the complete foundation for the letter's mission: organizing the Cretan communities, appointing qualified leaders, and confronting the false teachers who are destroying the communities from within. Each section builds on the preceding one and prepares for what follows.

(1) **Verses 1-4** compress the theological framework of the entire letter into the greeting. Paul's dual identity -- servant of God, apostle of Jesus Christ -- roots his authority in both Israel's history and Christ's commission. His apostleship serves two ends: the faith of God's elect and the knowledge of the truth that produces godliness. These two ends rest on the hope of eternal life, which was promised by the God who cannot lie before time began and has now been manifested through preaching. The greeting establishes the standard against which everything in the letter will be measured: truth that accords with godliness, knowledge that produces faithful living, a promise grounded in God's unchangeable character. The alternation of the title "Savior" between God (v. 3) and Christ (v. 4) announces the theological unity that the letter will maintain throughout.

(2) **Verses 5-9** describe the structural response to the crisis. Titus was left in Crete to complete what Paul began, and the primary task is the appointment of elders in every town. The elder qualifications address character (above reproach, faithful in marriage, children who are believers), temperament (not arrogant, not quick-tempered, not violent), habits (not a drunkard, not greedy), and virtues (hospitable, lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, disciplined). The qualifications culminate in the single most important requirement: the elder must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught, so that he can both teach sound doctrine and refute opponents. This final qualification is the hinge between the elder section and the false teacher section: the elders are needed because opponents exist, and the opponents are the subject of what follows.

(3) **Verses 10-16** identify, diagnose, and condemn the false teachers. They are insubordinate, empty talkers, and mind-deceivers, drawn especially from the circumcision party. They must be silenced because they are overturning whole households by teaching forbidden things for profit. A quotation from the Cretans' own prophet -- "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" -- is endorsed as an accurate description of the cultural soil in which the false teaching grows. The community is to be rebuked sharply, with the goal of restoring them to health in the faith -- specifically by turning them away from Jewish myths and human commands. The theological principle "to the pure all things are pure" undercuts the false teachers' program of external regulation by locating purity in the person rather than in the thing. And the final verdict exposes the fundamental contradiction: these people profess to know God but deny him by their works. They are detestable, disobedient, and disqualified for every good work.

The chapter's architecture is built around the contrast between genuine and counterfeit knowledge of God. Genuine knowledge produces godliness (1:1, kat' eusebeian). It rests on God's truthful promise (1:2, ho apseudes theos). It is transmitted through sound teaching (1:9, te didaskalia te hygiainouse). It is guarded by qualified leaders of tested character (1:5-8). It produces good works (1:8, philagathon; 1:16, ergon agathon by implication of contrast). Counterfeit knowledge, by contrast, issues from Jewish myths and human commands (1:14). It overturns households (1:11). It operates from defiled minds and consciences (1:15). It confesses God verbally while denying him practically (1:16). And it produces not good works but the disqualification from every good work (1:16). The test that distinguishes the genuine from the counterfeit is not the verbal profession but the visible conduct -- not "they profess to know" but "by their works they deny." The chapter makes the case that the communities in Crete need leaders who embody genuine knowledge of God -- leaders whose lives, not merely whose words, demonstrate the truth they teach. The false teachers have words without corresponding works; the elders must have both.
