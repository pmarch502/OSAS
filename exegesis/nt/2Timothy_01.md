# 2 Timothy 1 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**The document's genre and occasion:** This is a letter from Paul to Timothy, written from prison in Rome. The letter's circumstances are markedly different from those of 1 Timothy. In 1 Timothy, Paul had departed for Macedonia and expected to return to Ephesus relatively soon (1 Tim 3:14, "I hope to come to you soon"). The tone of 1 Timothy was official and institutional -- a mandate letter authorizing Timothy to manage the Ephesian community during a crisis of false teaching. In 2 Timothy, the situation has deteriorated on every front. Paul is no longer merely absent; he is imprisoned (1:8, "me his prisoner"; 1:16, "my chain"; 2:9, "bound with chains as a criminal"). His imprisonment is not the relatively open house arrest described in Acts 28:30-31, where Paul received visitors freely and taught without hindrance; this confinement is severe enough that Onesiphorus had to search earnestly to find him in Rome (1:17). Paul's associates have largely abandoned him (1:15, "all who are in Asia turned away from me"; 4:10, "Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me"; 4:16, "At my first defense no one came to stand by me, but all deserted me"). He is aware that his death may be near (4:6-8, "I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come"). The letter reads not as an administrative manual but as a personal, urgent appeal from a father who may be seeing his son for the last time.

The genre of 2 Timothy shares features with what the first-century hearer would recognize as a testamentary exhortation -- the farewell words of a dying figure to his successor. This genre has deep roots in Israel's scriptures. Jacob gathers his sons and blesses them before death (Genesis 49). Moses delivers his final addresses to Israel before dying on Mount Nebo (Deuteronomy 31-33). Joshua delivers a farewell charge (Joshua 23-24). David gives final instructions to Solomon (1 Kings 2:1-9; 1 Chronicles 28-29). In the Second Temple period, the genre was well-developed: the Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs, the Testament of Moses, and similar works present dying patriarchs reviewing their lives, warning their descendants, and committing their legacy to the next generation. The features of the genre include: the speaker faces death, reviews his life and service, gives final charges to successors, warns about future dangers, commends faithfulness, and expresses confidence about the outcome despite present danger. 2 Timothy displays all of these features. Yet it is also a genuine letter with personal details (Timothy's tears at their parting, 1:4; the request for cloak and books, 4:13; greetings to specific individuals, 4:19-21), practical instructions, and the epistolary framework of greeting and closing. It is a letter written in the shadow of death, and this shadow gives every word an urgency absent from 1 Timothy.

The crisis that prompted 1 Timothy -- false teaching in Ephesus -- has not been resolved. It has, if anything, worsened. The desertion of "all who are in Asia" (1:15) suggests that the false teaching has won widespread adherence, or at minimum that the social cost of aligning with Paul has become too high for most to bear. The central question of the letter is no longer institutional management but personal courage: will Timothy stand firm, or will he join the many who have turned away?

**What the author argues immediately after (2 Timothy 2):** Chapter 2 opens with "You then, my child, be strengthened by the grace that is in Christ Jesus" (2:1). The conjunction oun ("then, therefore") connects directly to the contrast that closed chapter 1: the many who turned away versus the one who remained faithful. In light of that contrast, Timothy is to be strengthened -- not in his own resources but "by the grace that is in Christ Jesus." The instruction that follows develops the chapter 1 themes in three directions. First, the deposit of teaching must be transmitted: "what you have heard from me in the presence of many witnesses entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also" (2:2). This extends the chain of custody from 1:13-14 -- Paul gave the pattern to Timothy; Timothy must now pass it to reliable people who will pass it further. Second, suffering is the expected condition of faithful service, illustrated through three metaphors: the soldier who does not entangle himself in civilian affairs (2:3-4), the athlete who competes according to the rules (2:5), the farmer who works before he receives the crop (2:6). Third, Paul returns to the gospel itself: "Remember Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, the offspring of David, as preached in my gospel, for which I am suffering, bound with chains as a criminal. But the word of God is not bound!" (2:8-9). The statement "the word of God is not bound" stands in pointed contrast to Paul's own chains -- the messenger is imprisoned, but the message cannot be. Then comes a "trustworthy saying" that develops the shame-versus-faithfulness theme: "If we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him; if we deny him, he also will deny us; if we are faithless, he remains faithful -- for he cannot deny himself" (2:11-13). The conditional structure makes faithfulness consequential: endurance leads to reigning, denial leads to being denied. This directly extends the choice set before Timothy in chapter 1.

**How chapter 1 connects to what follows:** Chapter 1 establishes the emotional, theological, and exemplary foundation for the entire letter. It begins with Paul's personal longing for Timothy and his affirmation of Timothy's genuine faith (vv. 3-5). It then moves to the central exhortation: do not be ashamed, but share in suffering for the gospel (vv. 6-8). The exhortation is grounded in a compressed statement of God's saving purpose and grace (vv. 9-10). Paul offers himself as a living example of unashamed suffering (vv. 11-12). The charge to guard the deposit (vv. 13-14) specifies what Timothy must protect. And the closing examples -- mass desertion versus one man's faithfulness (vv. 15-18) -- illustrate in concrete terms the choice before Timothy. Every subsequent section of the letter builds on this foundation. The three metaphors of chapter 2 (soldier, athlete, farmer) develop the suffering theme from 1:8. The warning against "quarreling about words" (2:14) and the description of Hymenaeus and Philetus (2:17-18) develop the false teaching crisis that is the backdrop to the desertion in 1:15. The character catalog of 3:1-9 ("in the last days there will come times of difficulty") develops the warning implicit in 1:15's mass desertion. The personal charge of 4:1-5 ("preach the word; be ready in season and out of season") develops the commissioning language of 1:6-8, 13-14. And the final testimony of 4:6-8 ("I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith") is the mature fruit of the confidence expressed in 1:12 ("I am not ashamed, for I know whom I have believed"). Chapter 1 is the seed from which the entire letter grows.

## Verses 1-2: The Greeting -- Apostleship and the Promise of Life

"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God according to the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus, to Timothy, my beloved child: Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord."

**"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God"** (Paulos apostolos Christou Iesou dia thelematos theou). As in 1 Timothy, Paul states his title immediately: apostolos, one sent with authority. But the grounding of the apostleship is expressed differently. In 1 Timothy 1:1, Paul was an apostle kat' epitagen -- "by the command" -- of God our Savior and Christ Jesus our hope. The word epitage ("command, order, decree") is the language of formal mandate, military commission, official dispatch. Here in 2 Timothy, Paul is an apostle dia thelematos theou -- "through the will of God" or "by the will of God." The noun thelema ("will, desire, intention, purpose") is broader and more personal than epitage. It describes not merely an order issued but an intention held, a purpose determined. Paul's apostleship exists because God willed it -- not because Paul chose it, not because a human council approved it, not because circumstances suggested it, but because the will of the living God stands behind it. The shift from "command" to "will" may reflect the letter's more personal and intimate register. In 1 Timothy, where the letter functioned as public authorization, the language of formal command was appropriate. Here, where Paul writes to his beloved child from prison, the warmer language of divine intention takes its place.

The same phrase dia thelematos theou appears in Paul's greetings in 1 Corinthians 1:1, 2 Corinthians 1:1, Ephesians 1:1, and Colossians 1:1. It is Paul's most common way of grounding his apostleship. The consistency of the formula indicates that Paul understands his vocation as originating in and sustained by God's deliberate intention, not by accident, human approval, or personal ambition.

**"According to the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus"** (kat' epangelian zoes tes en Christo Iesou). This prepositional phrase modifies either Paul's apostleship (he is an apostle oriented toward the promise of life) or the entire greeting (he writes in the context of the promise of life). The preposition kata with the accusative ("according to, in connection with, for the purpose of") indicates that the promise of life is the framework or aim within which Paul's apostleship operates.

The noun epangelia ("promise, pledge, undertaking") is one of Paul's most theologically loaded terms. In his usage, it almost always refers to God's promises to Israel -- particularly the promise to Abraham. In Galatians 3:14, the blessing of Abraham comes to the Gentiles "so that we might receive the promised Spirit through faith." In Galatians 3:16-18, Paul argues that the promises were spoken to Abraham and his offspring (singular: Christ), and that the law, which came 430 years later, does not annul the promise. In Romans 4:13-16, the promise to Abraham and his offspring was received through faith, not through law. In Romans 9:4, the promises belong to Israel. In Romans 9:8-9, children of the promise are distinguished from children of the flesh. In Romans 15:8, Christ became a servant to the circumcised "to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs." In Ephesians 2:12, the Gentiles were formerly "strangers to the covenants of promise." The word epangelia thus carries the full weight of Israel's covenantal history: God's promise to Abraham, renewed through Isaac, Jacob, David, and the prophets, now coming to fruition.

What is promised here is zoe -- "life." The promise of life runs through Israel's scriptures as a persistent thread. In Deuteronomy 30:15-20, God sets before Israel "life and good, death and evil" and commands: "Choose life, that you and your offspring may live, loving the LORD your God, obeying his voice, and holding fast to him, for he is your life and length of days." In Psalm 16:11 (LXX 15:11): "You make known to me the path of life; in your presence there is fullness of joy." In Proverbs 3:18, wisdom "is a tree of life to those who lay hold of her." In Daniel 12:2, "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life." In Ezekiel 37, the valley of dry bones receives life from God's breath -- a promise of national resurrection. The "promise of life" is not a new concept introduced by Paul but the culmination of God's oldest and most persistent pledge to his people: I am the God of the living, and my purpose for you is life.

This life is located en Christo Iesou -- "in Christ Jesus." The prepositional phrase defines the sphere in which the promise finds its fulfillment. The promise existed from of old, but its realization is in Christ. This connects directly to verse 10, where Christ is said to have "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel." The promise of life announced in the greeting will receive its explanation in the body of the chapter.

**"To Timothy, my beloved child"** (Timotheō agapetō teknō). In 1 Timothy 1:2, Timothy was addressed as gnesios teknon en pistei -- "genuine/legitimate child in faith." The adjective gnesios emphasized legitimacy: Timothy was Paul's true spiritual offspring, not a pretender. Here, the adjective changes to agapetos -- "beloved, dear, cherished." The shift from "legitimate" to "beloved" reflects the different register of this letter. Where 1 Timothy needed to establish Timothy's credentials before the Ephesian assembly, 2 Timothy speaks from the heart of a father to the heart of a son. The word agapetos carries the full weight of covenantal love: in the LXX, it translates the Hebrew yahid ("only, beloved") and describes Isaac as Abraham's beloved son (Genesis 22:2 LXX, ton huion sou ton agapeton). At Jesus' baptism, the divine voice declares: "This is my beloved Son" (houtos estin ho huios mou ho agapetos, Matthew 3:17; Mark 1:11; Luke 3:22). The word is not casual affection but the deep, committed, covenantal love of a parent for a child.

**"Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord"** (charis eleos eirene apo theou patros kai Christou Iesou tou kyriou hemon). The threefold greeting is identical to 1 Timothy 1:2. The addition of eleos ("mercy") to Paul's standard twofold greeting of "grace and peace" was noted in the analysis of 1 Timothy 1 as anticipating that letter's theme of mercy (1 Tim 1:13, 16). Here in 2 Timothy, mercy will again be prominent: Paul prays for mercy on the household of Onesiphorus (1:16) and for Onesiphorus himself on "that day" (1:18), and the theme of God's compassion toward the faithful amid suffering will pervade the letter. The greeting announces in compressed form what the letter will develop: grace (the unmerited gift of God's favor), mercy (God's covenant faithfulness shown to those in need), and peace (the restored relationship between God and his people) -- all flowing from two sources, "God the Father" and "Christ Jesus our Lord."

## Verses 3-5: Thanksgiving -- Ancestral Faith and Spiritual Heritage

"I thank God whom I serve, as did my ancestors, with a clear conscience, as I remember you constantly in my prayers night and day, longing to see you, remembering your tears, so that I may be filled with joy. I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am persuaded, dwells in you as well."

**"I thank God whom I serve, as did my ancestors, with a clear conscience"** (Charin echo to theo, ho latreuo apo progonon en kathara syneidēsei). The phrase charin echo ("I have thanks, I give thanks") parallels charin echo in 1 Timothy 1:12 ("I thank him who has given me strength"). The verb latreuein ("to serve, to worship, to render sacred service") in the LXX describes Israel's cultic service to God. It is the verb used in Exodus 3:12, where God tells Moses: "When you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall worship (latreusete) God on this mountain." In Deuteronomy 6:13: "You shall fear the LORD your God and serve (latreuseis) him." In Joshua 24:15: "Choose this day whom you will serve (latreusete)." The verb carries the sense of devoted, exclusive religious service -- the worship owed to God alone.

The phrase apo progonon ("from ancestors, from forebears") is critical. Paul is asserting continuity: the God he now serves in the gospel of Christ is the same God his Jewish ancestors served. His faith in Christ is not an apostasy from ancestral religion but its fulfillment. This claim appears in Paul's defense speeches in Acts: "According to the Way, which they call a sect, I worship the God of our fathers, believing everything laid down by the Law and written in the Prophets" (Acts 24:14). "I stand here on trial because of my hope in the promise made by God to our fathers, to which our twelve tribes hope to attain" (Acts 26:6-7). The continuity claim is not incidental; it is foundational. For a first-century Jewish hearer, the charge that the Jesus movement had abandoned the God of Israel would be devastating. Paul answers it here in a single phrase: he serves the God of his progonoi, the ancestors, the ones who came before. The same God, the same service, the same line of faith.

The phrase en kathara syneidēsei ("with a clear/clean conscience") connects to one of 1 Timothy's central themes. In 1 Timothy 1:5, the aim of the charge was love from a pure heart and a "good conscience" (syneidēseōs agathēs). In 1 Timothy 1:19, Hymenaeus and Alexander rejected "good conscience" (agathēn syneidēsin) and made shipwreck of their faith. In 1 Timothy 3:9, deacons must hold the mystery of the faith "with a clear conscience" (en kathara syneidēsei -- the identical phrase used here). Conscience in these letters is the inner moral witness, the capacity to evaluate one's own conduct before God. Paul's conscience is clean: he has not betrayed his calling, has not abandoned his commission, has not distorted the gospel. The man in chains has a clear conscience; the false teachers who walk free have rejected theirs.

**"As I remember you constantly in my prayers night and day"** (hōs adialeipton echō tēn peri sou mneian en tais deēsesin mou, nyktos kai hēmeras). The adjective adialeiptos ("unceasing, constant, without interruption") describes the frequency of Paul's remembrance of Timothy. The word appears also in Romans 1:9 ("without ceasing I mention you always in my prayers") and 1 Thessalonians 1:2 ("we give thanks to God always for all of you, constantly mentioning you in our prayers"). Paul's prayer life is characterized by persistent, unwavering intercession for those he loves. The phrase nyktos kai hēmeras ("night and day") uses the genitive of time to indicate the continuous span during which the praying occurs. In the confinement of prison, with activity restricted and freedom curtailed, prayer would be Paul's primary occupation.

**"Longing to see you, remembering your tears"** (epipothōn se idein, memnēmenos sou tōn dakryōn). The verb epipothein ("to long for, to yearn for, to desire earnestly") expresses intense personal desire. Paul uses the same verb in Romans 1:11 ("I long to see you"), Philippians 1:8 ("I yearn for you all with the affection of Christ Jesus"), and 1 Thessalonians 3:6 (where Timothy's report that the Thessalonians "long to see us" brings Paul comfort). The intensity of the word is not decorative; it conveys the ache of separation between two people who share the deepest bonds.

The tears (dakrya) belong to Timothy. The perfect participle memnēmenos ("remembering, being mindful of") indicates that the memory of those tears is present and vivid in Paul's mind. Timothy wept -- presumably at their parting, when Paul left him or was taken away. The mention of tears accomplishes several things at once. It establishes the depth of the personal relationship. It reminds Timothy (and the reader) that there was a time when the separation was unbearable, when the prospect of Paul's absence moved Timothy to open weeping. And it creates urgency: Paul remembers the tears and longs for the reunion.

**"So that I may be filled with joy"** (hina charas plērōthō). The hina clause states the purpose of Paul's longing: joy. The verb plēroun ("to fill, to make full, to bring to completion") in the passive describes joy that fills the whole person -- not a partial or qualified happiness but a complete filling. Paul in prison wants one thing: to see Timothy and be filled with joy. The emotional texture of the letter is established in these verses: this is not an administrative communication but a cry from the heart.

**"I am reminded of your sincere faith"** (hypomnēsin labōn tēs en soi anypokritou pisteōs). The noun hypomnēsis ("reminder, remembrance, recollection") with the verb lambanein ("to receive, to take") means "having received a reminder" -- something has caused Paul to remember afresh the quality of Timothy's faith. What triggered the reminder is not specified; perhaps a report, perhaps a memory, perhaps the Spirit's prompting.

The adjective anypokritos ("sincere, unfeigned, without pretense") is the same word Paul used in 1 Timothy 1:5 to describe the faith that is the aim of sound teaching: pisteos anypokritou ("sincere faith"). The word is composed of the alpha-privative (an-) and hypokritos ("actor, pretender, one who wears a mask"). Faith that is anypokritos is faith without a mask -- real, not performed, not adopted for social advantage. In the context of 2 Timothy, where many have turned away from Paul because of the cost of association, the emphasis on sincere faith is pointed. Those who abandoned Paul may have had faith that looked genuine when the cost was low; Timothy's faith is the kind that does not depend on favorable circumstances.

**"A faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice"** (hētis enōkēsen prōton en tē mammē sou Lōidi kai tē mētri sou Eunikē). The relative pronoun hētis ("which," qualitative: "which is of such a character that") introduces additional information about this sincere faith. The verb enoikein ("to dwell in, to inhabit, to make one's home in") personifies faith as a resident -- something that takes up dwelling in a person and makes its home there. The same verb will appear in verse 14 for the Holy Spirit "who dwells in us" (tou enoikountos en hēmin). The parallel is deliberate: faith dwells in persons the way the Spirit dwells in persons -- as an inhabitant, not a visitor.

The adverb prōton ("first") establishes chronological priority: the faith dwelt first in Lois, then in Eunice, then (as Paul is about to state) in Timothy. This is three-generation transmission. The grandmother heard, believed, and the faith took up residence in her. The mother heard, believed, and the faith took up residence in her. The son heard, believed, and the faith took up residence in him. The pattern is not automatic -- faith is not genetically inherited -- but it is familial: the household was the primary context in which faith was transmitted in the first-century world.

The names are Greek: Lōis is attested as a name among diaspora Jews, and Eunikē (from eu + nikē, "good victory") was common in the Greco-Roman world and used by Jewish families in the diaspora. Acts 16:1 confirms the family background: "He came also to Derbe and to Lystra. A disciple was there, named Timothy, the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek." Timothy's mother was Jewish and a believer; his father was Greek (and, by implication, not a believer -- the text does not call him one). The faith came to Timothy through the maternal line, through women who believed when the gospel first came to Lystra.

**"And now, I am persuaded, dwells in you as well"** (pepeismai de hoti kai en soi). The perfect tense pepeismai ("I have been persuaded and remain persuaded") expresses a settled, confident conviction. Paul is not uncertain; he does not say "I hope" or "I suspect." He has been persuaded and stands in that persuasion. Yet the very need to state the conviction -- combined with the exhortation to "fan into flame" that immediately follows in verse 6 -- suggests that Timothy's faith, while genuine, may be under pressure. The same faith that was authentic and indwelling may be in danger of being dampened by fear, by the cost of association with a prisoner, by the mass desertion Paul is about to describe. Paul affirms what is true so that Timothy will have the courage to live it out.

## Verses 6-7: The Gift Rekindled -- Power Instead of Cowardice

"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands, for God gave us a spirit not of cowardice but of power and love and self-discipline."

**"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God"** (di' hēn aitian anamimnēskō se anazōpyrein to charisma tou theou). The phrase di' hēn aitian ("for which reason, on this account") connects back to the preceding affirmation of Timothy's sincere faith. Because Paul is persuaded that genuine faith dwells in Timothy, he now calls Timothy to act on it.

The verb anamimnēskein ("to remind, to call to remembrance, to bring back to mind") does not introduce new information but recalls what Timothy already knows. Paul is not teaching Timothy something he has never heard; he is reminding him of what he already possesses. The distinction matters: the problem is not ignorance but dormancy.

The verb anazōpyrein ("to fan into flame, to rekindle, to stir up the embers") is vivid. It is a compound of ana- ("up, again") + zōpyrein ("to kindle to life, to fan a fire"). The word zōpyron was used for the ember or coal kept alive to restart a fire. The metaphor is precise: the gift of God in Timothy is not extinguished -- it still has life, like embers under ash -- but it needs stirring, fanning, active attention to flame back to full intensity. The verb appears only here in the New Testament. In the LXX, the related concept appears in texts about fire that must be tended and not allowed to die out. Leviticus 6:12-13: "The fire on the altar shall be kept burning on it; it shall not go out... Fire shall be kept burning on the altar continually; it shall not go out." The priestly duty was to maintain the fire; letting it die was a failure of stewardship. Timothy has a similar duty: the gift of God requires active tending.

The noun charisma ("gift, grace-gift, endowment") is composed of charis ("grace") + the -ma suffix (indicating the result of an action). A charisma is a concrete expression of grace, a gift that embodies and conveys grace. In Romans 12:6, Paul lists the charismata ("gifts") given to the community: prophecy, service, teaching, exhortation, giving, leading, showing mercy. In 1 Corinthians 12:4, "there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit." The specific charisma Timothy received is not identified in this verse, but its context -- the laying on of hands, the connection to ministry, the call to boldness in the gospel -- suggests it is a gift related to his public commission for ministry.

**"Which is in you through the laying on of my hands"** (ho estin en soi dia tēs epitheseōs tōn cheirōn mou). The gift is located en soi ("in you") -- it is not external to Timothy but indwells him. It came dia ("through, by means of") the laying on of Paul's hands (epitheseōs tōn cheirōn mou). The noun epithesis ("laying on, placing upon") combined with tōn cheirōn ("of the hands") describes the ancient practice of commissioning through physical touch. The laying on of hands has a long history in Israel: Moses laid hands on Joshua to transfer authority and the spirit of wisdom (Numbers 27:18-23; Deuteronomy 34:9). In the early Jesus communities, hands were laid on people for various purposes: healing (Acts 9:17; 28:8), reception of the Spirit (Acts 8:17; 19:6), and commissioning for ministry (Acts 6:6; 13:3).

A comparison with 1 Timothy 4:14 is necessary. There Paul wrote: "Do not neglect the gift you have, which was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you" (meta epitheseōs tōn cheirōn tou presbyteriou). In that passage, the gift came meta ("with, accompanied by") the laying on of hands by the presbyterion ("council of elders, body of elders"). Here in 2 Timothy, the gift came dia ("through") the laying on of Paul's hands (mou, "my hands"). The two descriptions are not contradictory; they describe the same event from different angles. Paul laid his hands on Timothy in the presence of the elders, who also participated. In 1 Timothy, where Paul was establishing Timothy's credentials before the Ephesian community, the corporate action of the elder body was emphasized. Here in 2 Timothy, where Paul writes personally to his beloved child, his own role in the commissioning is emphasized. The event was one; the perspectives are complementary.

**"For God gave us a spirit not of cowardice"** (ou gar edōken hēmin ho theos pneuma deilias). The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the ground of the exhortation: Timothy should fan the gift into flame because the spirit God gave is not characterized by cowardice. The verb edōken ("gave," aorist of didonai) points to a definite past event -- a specific giving that occurred at a specific time, likely the same commissioning event just described.

The word pneuma ("spirit, breath, wind") here could refer to the Holy Spirit whom God gives, or to the disposition or inner orientation that God produces. In the immediate context, where verse 14 will explicitly mention "the Holy Spirit who dwells in us" and where the gift is described as coming from God, the reference is most likely to the Holy Spirit -- but not abstracted from the disposition he produces. The Spirit God gives is characterized by certain qualities, and these qualities become the believer's inner reality. The distinction between "the Holy Spirit" and "the spirit/disposition God gives" would not be sharp for a first-century hearer: the Spirit's indwelling is the source of the believer's new character.

The genitive deilias ("of cowardice, of timidity, of fearfulness") describes the character that this spirit does not have. The noun deilia appears only here in the New Testament. In the LXX, the related adjective deilos ("cowardly, fearful, timid") appears in contexts of military failure. In Deuteronomy 20:8, before battle, the officers say: "Is there anyone who is fearful (deilos) and fainthearted? Let him go back to his house, lest he make the heart of his fellows melt like his own." The cowardly soldier is sent home because cowardice is contagious. In Judges 7:3, God tells Gideon: "Whoever is fearful (deilos) and trembling, let him return home." The military context is significant because the letter has already framed Timothy's task as warfare (1 Tim 1:18, "wage the good warfare"), and 2 Timothy 2:3-4 will develop the soldier metaphor explicitly. Timothy is not to be a warrior sent home for cowardice. He is to stand and fight.

The word also carries weight in another direction. In the words attributed to Jesus in the Gospels, deilia or its cognates describe the failure of the disciples in moments of crisis. In Mark 4:40, after the storm on the sea, Jesus asks: "Why are you so cowardly (deiloi)? Have you still no faith?" Cowardice and faithlessness are linked: to be deilos is to fail in pistis. Paul's statement that God did not give a spirit of cowardice is therefore also a statement about faith: the Spirit God gives produces not faithless fear but faithful courage.

**"But of power and love and self-discipline"** (alla dynameōs kai agapēs kai sōphronismou). Three genitives describe the positive character of the spirit God gives:

(1) **Power** (dynamis). The noun dynamis means "power, ability, capacity, strength, might." In Paul's usage, dynamis is characteristically the power of God at work in and through human agents. In Romans 1:16, "the gospel is the power of God for salvation." In 1 Corinthians 2:4-5, Paul's preaching was "in demonstration of the Spirit and of power, so that your faith might not rest in the wisdom of men but in the power of God." In 2 Corinthians 12:9, the Lord tells Paul: "My power is made perfect in weakness." The power that replaces cowardice is not human assertiveness or self-confidence but God's own operative energy working through a weak vessel. This connects directly to verse 8, where Timothy is to share in suffering for the gospel "according to the power of God" (kata dynamin theou) -- the same power.

(2) **Love** (agape). Love appears because the gospel demands action for the sake of others even when it is costly. Cowardice is self-protective; love is self-giving. The pairing of power with love prevents power from becoming mere force: power exercised in love serves others rather than dominating them. Love was the telos ("aim, goal") of sound teaching in 1 Timothy 1:5 ("the aim of our charge is love from a pure heart"). Here it is part of the character the Spirit produces in those who minister the gospel.

(3) **Self-discipline** (sōphronismos). This noun appears only here in the New Testament. It is related to the sōphron-word group: sōphrosynē ("self-control, sound-mindedness, moderation," 1 Tim 2:9, 15), sōphronein ("to be of sound mind, to think clearly"), sōphronizein ("to bring to one's senses, to train in self-discipline," Titus 2:4). The -ismos suffix suggests the active process or exercise of sōphrosynē -- the discipline of bringing one's mind to soundness, the act of self-correction that keeps a person from either panic on one side or recklessness on the other. Against cowardice, God gives not mere bravado but disciplined self-mastery -- the capacity to assess a situation clearly and act rightly despite danger. The word captures the mental clarity that fear destroys: a coward cannot think straight, but the person with sōphronismos maintains sound judgment under pressure.

The three qualities together compose the character required for ministry in dangerous circumstances. Power supplies the capacity to act. Love supplies the motivation to act for others. Self-discipline supplies the judgment to act rightly. Together they replace cowardice with courage that is both effective and wise.

## Verses 8-12: The Call to Suffer Without Shame -- Grounded in God's Saving Purpose

"Therefore do not be ashamed of the testimony about our Lord, nor of me his prisoner, but share in suffering for the gospel by the power of God, who saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace, which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began, and which now has been manifested through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus, who abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel, for which I was appointed a preacher and apostle and teacher, which is why I suffer as I do. But I am not ashamed, for I know whom I have believed, and I am convinced that he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me."

**"Therefore do not be ashamed of the testimony about our Lord"** (mē oun epaischynthēs to martyrion tou kyriou hēmōn). The conjunction oun ("therefore, then, accordingly") connects this exhortation to the preceding statement about God's gift of power, love, and self-discipline. Because God gave these qualities rather than cowardice, Timothy should not be ashamed. The negative imperative mē epaischynthēs ("do not be ashamed") uses the aorist subjunctive, addressing a specific potential act of shame rather than an ongoing state. The verb epaischynesthai ("to be ashamed of, to be embarrassed by") will become the chapter's controlling word, appearing three times: here (v. 8), in Paul's statement about himself (v. 12, "I am not ashamed"), and in the description of Onesiphorus (v. 16, "he was not ashamed of my chain"). The threefold repetition creates the chapter's spine: shame is the temptation, and the letter presents three responses to it -- Timothy is told not to be ashamed, Paul models not being ashamed, and Onesiphorus demonstrates what not being ashamed looks like in practice.

The object of potential shame is to martyrion tou kyriou hēmōn -- "the testimony about our Lord" or "the testimony of our Lord." The noun martyrion ("testimony, witness, evidence") describes the public declaration about Jesus Christ. In a Roman context, where the Lord the community confessed was a man executed by Roman authority, the "testimony" was socially dangerous. To testify about a crucified criminal as Lord was to invite ridicule, suspicion, and potential legal consequence. The temptation to shame was not merely emotional but practical: being associated with this testimony could result in arrest, imprisonment, or death.

**"Nor of me his prisoner"** (mēde eme ton desmion autou). The negative mēde ("nor, and not") extends the prohibition: not only must Timothy not be ashamed of the testimony itself, he must not be ashamed of Paul, its imprisoned herald. The noun desmios ("prisoner, one in bonds") is modified by autou ("his") -- Paul is the prisoner of Christ, not merely of Rome. The possessive genitive reframes the imprisonment: Paul is in chains not because Rome has captured him but because Christ's service has led him into chains. The one who put Paul there, in the deepest sense, is not the Roman magistrate but the Lord whose gospel Paul proclaims. The reframing matters because it transforms the social meaning of the imprisonment. A man in Roman chains was disgraced -- a failure, a criminal, someone to be avoided. But if the chains are Christ's chains, then the disgrace belongs to those who imposed them, not to the one who wears them.

The temptation to be ashamed of Paul was not hypothetical. Verse 15 will reveal that "all who are in Asia turned away from me." The social cost of association with a condemned prisoner was real, and many had already decided the cost was too high. Paul names this reality and tells Timothy to resist it.

**"But share in suffering for the gospel by the power of God"** (alla synkakopathēson tō euangeliō kata dynamin theou). The strong adversative alla ("but, rather, on the contrary") introduces the positive alternative to shame. The verb synkakopathein ("to suffer together, to share in hardship, to endure affliction with") is a compound: syn ("together with") + kakos ("evil, bad, harmful") + pathein ("to suffer, to experience"). The word appears only here and in 2 Timothy 2:3 in the New Testament. Timothy is commanded not merely to endure his own suffering but to suffer with -- to join Paul in the hardship that the gospel brings. The dative tō euangeliō ("for the gospel") identifies the cause: the suffering is not random or purposeless; it is for the sake of the good news about Christ.

The phrase kata dynamin theou ("by the power of God, according to the power of God") specifies the resource by which this shared suffering becomes possible. The preposition kata ("according to, in accordance with") indicates that the suffering is undergone within the sphere of divine power, enabled by it, sustained by it. Human capacity alone would not be sufficient to endure what Paul describes. But the power (dynamis) is God's -- the same word that appeared in verse 7 as part of the spirit God gives. The connection is explicit: the spirit of power (v. 7) enables the sharing in suffering (v. 8). What God provides in the interior life (power rather than cowardice) is what makes the exterior life (suffering rather than retreat) possible.

**"Who saved us and called us to a holy calling"** (tou sōsantos hēmas kai kalesantos klēsei hagia). The participial phrase identifies the God whose power sustains the suffering. Two aorist participles describe God's completed actions:

(1) **Saved** (sōsantos, from sōzein, "to save, to rescue, to deliver"). The aorist tense treats the saving as a completed event -- something that has already happened. The first-century hearer would understand this in terms drawn from Israel's history: God is the one who saves. The same God who rescued Israel from Egypt (Exodus 14-15), who delivered them from Babylon (Isaiah 43:1-3; 44:6; 45:17), who saved individuals from danger and death throughout the Psalms (Psalm 18:3 LXX [17:3]; 34:6 LXX [33:7]; 107:13 LXX [106:13]) -- this God has now saved Paul and Timothy and all who are in Christ. The verb echoes the greeting's identification of God as the one from whom salvation flows, and connects to 1 Timothy 1:15 ("Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners") and the designation "God our Savior" (1 Tim 1:1; 2:3; 4:10).

(2) **Called** (kalesantos, from kalein, "to call, to summon, to invite, to name"). God has issued a summons. The calling is described as klēsis hagia -- "a holy calling." The noun klēsis ("calling, summons, vocation") in Paul's usage describes God's effectual summons to faith and participation in his purposes. In Romans 11:29, "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable." In 1 Corinthians 1:26, "Consider your calling, brothers." In Ephesians 4:1, "Walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called." In Philippians 3:14, "the upward call of God in Christ Jesus." The adjective hagia ("holy, set apart, sacred") characterizes the calling as belonging to God's sphere -- a summons that separates the called ones from the common and brings them into God's own purposes.

The pronoun hēmas ("us") is important. Who is the "us" God has saved and called? In the immediate context, Paul is addressing Timothy, so the most proximate referent is Paul and Timothy together. But the statement's scope is broader -- it describes the fundamental experience of every person who has received the gospel. The "us" is not private but representative: Paul and Timothy are examples of the universal pattern of God's saving and calling action.

**"Not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace"** (ou kata ta erga hēmōn alla kata idian prothesin kai charin). This is a critical clarification. The saving and calling were not kata ta erga hēmōn -- "according to our works, on the basis of our deeds." The preposition kata ("according to, on the basis of") identifies the ground or basis of the action. God's saving and calling did not respond to or originate from human achievement. The negative ou ("not") is emphatic: it was not works.

The positive ground is stated with the same preposition: alla kata idian prothesin kai charin -- "but according to his own purpose and grace." Two terms describe what motivated God's action:

(1) **Purpose** (prothesis). The noun prothesis ("setting forth, purpose, plan, resolve") is composed of pro ("before, ahead") + thesis ("placing, setting"). It describes something set in place beforehand, a plan established in advance. In Paul's usage elsewhere, the word consistently describes God's sovereign plan. In Romans 8:28, those who love God are "called according to his purpose" (kata prothesin). In Romans 9:11, "in order that God's purpose according to election might continue to stand" (hē kat' eklogēn prothesis tou theou) -- here the purpose is what governs the election of Jacob over Esau, "not because of works but because of his calling." In Ephesians 1:11, God "works all things according to the counsel of his will" (kata tēn prothesin tou ta panta energountos kata tēn boulēn tou thelēmatos autou). In Ephesians 3:11, there is "the eternal purpose" (kata prothesin tōn aiōnōn) that God has realized in Christ Jesus. In every instance, prothesis refers to God's deliberate, premeditated plan -- a purpose that originates in God's own initiative and governs the course of events.

The adjective idian ("his own, personal, private") intensifies: this is God's own purpose, internal to God, not suggested or necessitated by anything outside God. The plan did not originate in human achievement, in cosmic necessity, or in the movement of history. It originated in God.

(2) **Grace** (charis). The noun charis ("grace, favor, gift, kindness") in Pauline usage describes God's free, unmerited favor toward those who do not deserve it. Grace is the mode of God's action: he acts not in response to human merit but out of his own generous purpose. The pairing of prothesis and charis is deliberate: God's plan (prothesis) is executed through grace (charis). The plan is the architect; grace is the mode of construction.

The concept that Israel's God acts according to his own purpose, not in response to human works, has deep roots in Israel's own history. God chose Abraham before Abraham had done anything to merit it (Genesis 12:1-3). God chose Israel not because they were the greatest of peoples but because he loved them and kept the oath he swore to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7-8). God chose Jacob over Esau before they were born or had done anything good or bad (Genesis 25:23; Malachi 1:2-3). The pattern is consistent: God's initiative precedes human response. This text affirms that pattern and applies it to the experience of salvation and calling in Christ.

**"Which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began"** (tēn dotheisan hēmin en Christō Iēsou pro chronōn aiōniōn). The grace described in the previous clause was "given" (dotheisan, aorist passive participle of didonai, "to give") to "us" (hēmin) at a time specified as pro chronōn aiōniōn -- "before the ages of time" or "before eternal times." The phrase is extraordinary. The noun chronos ("time, period, age") in the plural, modified by aiōnios ("eternal, age-long"), describes the vast stretches of cosmic time. The preposition pro ("before") places the giving prior to these ages. Before time as we know it began, God's grace was already given.

The idea that God's plans precede created time is not novel in Jewish thought. Proverbs 8:22-31 describes wisdom as present with God "before the beginning of the earth" and "before the depths" were made. Isaiah 46:10: God declares "the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done, saying, 'My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose.'" Isaiah 25:1: "You have done wonderful things, plans formed of old, faithful and sure." The concept of God's pre-temporal planning was well-developed in Second Temple Judaism: God's purposes exist before creation and govern history toward their fulfillment.

The grace was given en Christō Iēsou ("in Christ Jesus") -- the sphere in which the giving occurs. Christ Jesus is the locus of God's grace. Before the ages began, God's grace was already operative, already given, already residing in the person and purpose of Christ. What was hidden in God's pre-temporal counsel has now become visible in history.

**"And which now has been manifested through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus"** (phanerōtheisan de nyn dia tēs epiphaneias tou sōtēros hēmōn Christou Iēsou). The verb phaneroun ("to make visible, to reveal, to manifest, to bring to light") marks the transition from the hidden to the revealed. What was given before the ages has now (nyn, "now, at this present time") been made manifest. The adverb nyn is emphatic by position: now, at this decisive moment in history, the hidden has been uncovered.

The means of manifestation is dia tēs epiphaneias -- "through the appearing." The noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation, coming into visible presence") was used in Greek literature for the visible manifestation of a deity or the arrival of a king or emperor. The word carried connotations of divine intervention becoming visible in the human sphere. In the Pastoral Epistles, epiphaneia is used both for Christ's first appearing -- his incarnation, ministry, death, and resurrection (here, clearly, since the results described in the next clause are already accomplished) -- and for his future appearing (1 Tim 6:14; 2 Tim 4:1, 8; Titus 2:13). Here the reference is to Christ's historical appearing in the world -- the event through which God's pre-temporal purpose became visible.

The title sōtēros hēmōn ("our Savior") applied to Christ Jesus echoes the greeting's attribution of salvation to God ("God the Father") and completes the theological circuit: God our Savior (1 Tim 1:1; 2:3) works through Christ Jesus our Savior. The title soter ("savior, deliverer, rescuer") applied to Christ identifies him as the agent through whom God's saving purpose is executed.

**"Who abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel"** (katargēsantos men ton thanaton, phōtisantos de zōēn kai aphtharsian dia tou euangeliou). Two aorist participles describe what Christ accomplished through his appearing:

(1) **Abolished death** (katargēsantos ton thanaton). The verb katargein ("to render inoperative, to abolish, to nullify, to put out of action, to make of no effect") is one of Paul's most characteristic and forceful verbs. In Romans 6:6, "our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be rendered inoperative" (katargēthē). In 1 Corinthians 15:26, "the last enemy to be destroyed is death" (katargeitai). In 1 Corinthians 15:54-55, when the perishable puts on the imperishable, "then shall come to pass the saying that is written: 'Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?'" The verb does not mean that death has ceased to exist as a physical phenomenon -- people still die -- but that death's power, finality, and dominion have been broken. Death has been rendered inoperative as the last word. Christ's resurrection is the event that accomplished this: by passing through death and emerging alive, Christ broke death's claim to be the end of the story.

(2) **Brought life and immortality to light** (phōtisantos de zōēn kai aphtharsian). The verb phōtizein ("to illuminate, to shed light on, to make visible, to bring to light") provides the positive complement. The men... de ("on the one hand... on the other hand") construction creates a balanced pair: death abolished on one side, life and immortality illuminated on the other. The verb phōtizein implies that life (zōē) and immortality (aphtharsia, "incorruptibility, imperishability") already existed -- they were present in God's purpose "before the ages began" (v. 9) -- but they were hidden, invisible, in the dark. Christ's appearing turned on the light. What was concealed in God's pre-temporal purpose has now been lit up for all to see.

The noun aphtharsia ("incorruptibility, imperishability, exemption from decay and death") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and phtharsis ("corruption, decay, destruction"). In 1 Corinthians 15:42, 50, 53-54, Paul uses the term for the resurrection body: "What is sown in corruption is raised in incorruption" (speiretai en phthora, egeiretai en aphtharsia). In Romans 2:7, God will give eternal life "to those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality" (aphtharsian). The pairing of zōē and aphtharsia is a hendiadys that describes one reality from two angles: the life God gives is life that does not corrupt, does not decay, does not perish. It is imperishable life.

The means of illumination is dia tou euangeliou -- "through the gospel." The gospel is the lamp by which life and immortality become visible. Christ's appearing accomplished the reality; the gospel proclamation broadcasts it. The chain is: God's purpose (before time) -> Christ's appearing (in time) -> the gospel (to all who hear). The entire sweep of God's action, from pre-temporal purpose to present proclamation, is compressed into these two verses (9-10).

These verses, taken as a unit, contain the chapter's theological core. They describe a movement from eternity to history to proclamation: grace given before the ages, revealed through Christ's appearing, and communicated through the gospel. The purpose originates entirely in God. It is not a response to human achievement. It results in the defeat of death and the disclosure of imperishable life. This is what Timothy is being told not to be ashamed of: a gospel grounded in God's eternal purpose, accomplished by Christ's conquest of death, and making visible the life that God has always intended for his people.

**"For which I was appointed a preacher and apostle and teacher"** (eis ho etethēn egō kēryx kai apostolos kai didaskalos). The relative pronoun ho ("which") refers back to the gospel. Paul was appointed (etethēn, from tithenai in the passive, "to be placed, to be set, to be appointed") for this gospel. Three titles describe his role:

(1) **Preacher** (kēryx). The noun kēryx means "herald, crier, proclaimer" -- one who announces a message on behalf of a superior authority. In the ancient world, the kēryx was the town crier, the royal herald, the one who stood in the public square and proclaimed the king's decree. The herald does not compose the message; he delivers it. Paul is the herald of the gospel, not its author.

(2) **Apostle** (apostolos). One sent with authority, an authorized representative. This repeats the title from verse 1.

(3) **Teacher** (didaskalos). One who instructs, who explains, who trains others in the content of the message. The herald announces; the teacher explains. Paul does both.

These three roles correspond to three aspects of gospel ministry: public proclamation (kēryx), authorized representation (apostolos), and detailed instruction (didaskalos). Together they describe a comprehensive commission to make the gospel known in every dimension.

**"Which is why I suffer as I do"** (di' hēn aitian kai tauta paschō). The phrase di' hēn aitian ("for which reason, on account of which") identifies the cause of Paul's suffering: his appointment to proclaim this gospel. The suffering is not random misfortune; it is the direct consequence of his commission. The demonstrative tauta ("these things") points to the specific sufferings Paul is experiencing: imprisonment, abandonment, danger. The verb paschein ("to suffer, to experience") is stated plainly, without self-pity or elaboration. Paul suffers because he does what he was appointed to do.

**"But I am not ashamed"** (all' ouk epaischynomai). The adversative alla ("but") introduces the contrast: suffering should logically produce shame -- the social disgrace of a prisoner, the humiliation of chains, the abandonment by associates. But Paul is not ashamed. The verb epaischynesthai echoes verse 8 exactly: Paul told Timothy "do not be ashamed" (mē epaischynthēs), and now he models what he commands. He does not merely preach courage; he practices it. The present tense epaischynomai ("I am [not] ashamed") describes Paul's current, ongoing state: right now, in this prison, in these chains, with these desertions, I am not ashamed.

**"For I know whom I have believed"** (oida gar hō pepisteuka). The verb oida ("I know") denotes settled, certain knowledge -- the kind of knowledge that is not shakeable by circumstances. The relative pronoun hō ("whom") is critical: Paul says "I know whom," not "I know what." The object of his confidence is a person, not a proposition. He has believed in someone, and he knows that someone. The perfect tense pepisteuka ("I have believed and continue to believe") describes a past act of faith whose results persist into the present. Paul committed himself to Christ at some point in the past, and that commitment stands unshaken.

This is the chapter's answer to the problem of shame. What enables a person to suffer without shame is not philosophical resolution or psychological toughness but personal knowledge of the one in whom faith has been placed. Paul knows the character, the faithfulness, the power, and the purposes of Christ. Because he knows whom he has trusted, the external circumstances of suffering cannot produce inner shame.

**"And I am convinced that he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me"** (kai pepeismai hoti dynatos estin tēn parathēkēn mou phylaxai eis ekeinēn tēn hēmeran). The perfect tense pepeismai ("I have been convinced and remain convinced") matches the same verb in verse 5, where Paul was convinced of Timothy's sincere faith. The conviction is settled and enduring.

The content of Paul's conviction is that Christ is dynatos ("able, capable, powerful") to guard (phylaxai, "to keep, to protect, to watch over") tēn parathēkēn mou ("my deposit") until ekeinēn tēn hēmeran ("that day"). Several elements require careful attention:

The noun parathēkē ("deposit, something committed to another's care, what is entrusted") is a legal and banking term. In the ancient world, a person could deposit valuables with a trustworthy friend or institution for safekeeping. The depositor expected to receive the deposit back intact. The law protected deposits: Exodus 22:7-13 provides detailed regulations about items left in another's care. The parathēkē is valuable property placed in trusted hands.

The possessive pronoun mou ("my") identifies this as Paul's deposit -- what Paul has entrusted. This is distinct from the parathēkē in verse 14 ("guard the good deposit") and 1 Timothy 6:20 ("guard the deposit"), where the deposit is the gospel that God has entrusted to Timothy. Here the direction is reversed: Paul has placed something in God's keeping. What is Paul's deposit? In context, it is his life, his ministry, his faithfulness, his service -- everything he has invested in the gospel. He has staked his existence on Christ and has entrusted the outcome to God's safekeeping. The confidence is that God is able to protect what Paul has committed to him.

The phrase eis ekeinēn tēn hēmeran ("until that day") points to a specific, known future day. The demonstrative ekeinēn ("that") identifies it as a particular day both Paul and Timothy understand: the day of Christ's return and judgment. The phrase appears again in verse 18 and in 4:8 ("the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day"). "That day" in the prophetic tradition is the Day of the Lord -- the day of God's decisive intervention, judgment, and vindication. Isaiah 2:12: "The LORD of hosts has a day against all that is proud and lofty." Joel 2:31 (LXX 3:4): "The great and awesome day of the LORD." Malachi 4:5 (LXX 3:23): "before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes." Paul's confidence extends beyond his present suffering and beyond his death to that ultimate day when all things will be revealed and all accounts settled.

## Verses 13-14: The Pattern and the Deposit -- What Timothy Must Guard

"Follow the pattern of the sound words that you have heard from me, in the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus. By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you."

**"Follow the pattern of the sound words that you have heard from me"** (hypotypōsin eche hygiainontōn logōn hōn par' emou ēkousas). The noun hypotypōsis ("pattern, outline, model, sketch, prototype") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in 1 Timothy 1:16, where Paul's experience of mercy was described as a hypotypōsis -- a prototype -- for future believers. The word denotes a model to be followed, a template that guides subsequent work. An artist's preliminary sketch, a builder's blueprint, an architect's plan -- these are the analogies. Paul has given Timothy a set of "sound words" (hygiainontōn logōn), and these words function as a pattern that Timothy must have (eche, present imperative of echein, "to have, to hold, to possess") -- not merely remember but hold onto, maintain, keep as a working guide.

The adjective hygiainontōn (from hygiainein, "to be healthy, to be sound, to be in good condition") is the medical metaphor characteristic of the Pastoral Epistles. Sound words are healthy words -- teaching that produces health in those who receive it. The metaphor is developed throughout the letters: 1 Timothy 1:10 ("whatever else is contrary to sound teaching"); 1 Timothy 6:3 ("the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ"); 2 Timothy 4:3 ("the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching"); Titus 1:9, 13; 2:1, 2. Unsound teaching is diseased, producing sickness in the community. Sound teaching is healthy, promoting the well-being of those who hear and follow it.

The source of these words is specified: hōn par' emou ēkousas -- "which you heard from me." The preposition para ("from, from beside, from the presence of") indicates direct personal transmission: Timothy heard these words from Paul's own mouth, in Paul's own presence. The chain of custody is important: the sound words did not arrive through multiple intermediaries but through direct apostolic teaching. Timothy is a first-generation recipient.

**"In the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus"** (en pistei kai agapē tē en Christō Iēsou). The prepositional phrase en pistei kai agapē ("in faith and love") modifies the holding of the pattern. Timothy is not to hold the pattern as a dead letter, a merely formal checklist, a set of propositions maintained without personal investment. He is to hold it in faith (pistei) -- with trust in the God from whom the words come -- and in love (agapē) -- with the self-giving character that is the aim of all sound teaching (1 Tim 1:5). Faith and love are located en Christō Iēsou ("in Christ Jesus"): they are not free-floating virtues but realities that exist within the sphere of Christ.

The instruction guards against two errors. On one side, Timothy might abandon the pattern under pressure -- surrendering the content to fit the expectations of a hostile environment. On the other side, he might hold the pattern rigidly but without the animating qualities of faith and love -- maintaining correct words while losing the life they are meant to carry. Both errors are rejected: he must hold the pattern (preserving the content) and hold it in faith and love (preserving its character).

**"By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you"** (tēn kalēn parathēkēn phylaxon dia pneumatos hagiou tou enoikountos en hēmin). The imperative phylaxon ("guard," aorist imperative of phylassein, "to guard, to watch over, to protect, to keep safe") is direct and urgent. The verb carries the sense of vigilant custody -- the watchfulness of a guard over a treasure or a soldier over a strategic position. In 1 Timothy 6:20, the identical command appeared: tēn parathēkēn phylaxon ("guard the deposit"). The repetition underscores the gravity of the charge: this is not a new instruction but a renewed and intensified version of the charge already given.

The adjective kalēn ("good, noble, beautiful, excellent") describes the deposit. It is not merely a deposit but a good deposit -- kalos, the same adjective used throughout the Pastoral Epistles for things that are worthy and noble: the "good warfare" (1 Tim 1:18), the "good confession" (1 Tim 6:12, 13), the "good foundation" (1 Tim 6:19). The deposit is precious, valuable, worthy of the effort required to guard it.

The noun parathēkē here -- unlike the "my deposit" of verse 12 -- refers to the gospel itself, the body of apostolic teaching that Paul has transmitted to Timothy and that Timothy must now protect and pass on. The direction of entrustment is from God through Paul to Timothy: God entrusted the gospel to Paul (1 Tim 1:11), Paul entrusted it to Timothy (1:14; 2:2), and Timothy will entrust it to faithful men (2:2). The deposit is not Timothy's to modify, reinterpret, or adapt to the preferences of the age. It is to be guarded -- kept intact, protected from corruption, preserved for transmission.

The means of guarding is dia pneumatos hagiou tou enoikountos en hēmin -- "through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us." The preposition dia ("through, by means of") identifies the Holy Spirit as the agent through whom the guarding is accomplished. Timothy is commanded to guard the deposit, but he is not left to do so in his own strength. The Spirit who dwells (enoikountos, from enoikein, "to dwell in, to inhabit, to make one's home in") within the community of believers is the one who makes the guarding possible.

The verb enoikein echoes verse 5, where the same verb described faith dwelling in Lois, Eunice, and Timothy. The parallel is pointed: faith dwells in persons, and the Holy Spirit dwells in persons. The indwelling of the Spirit is what sustains the indwelling of faith. The community is not left alone to protect the gospel through merely human vigilance; the Spirit who inhabits them is the guardian's guardian.

The pronoun hēmin ("us") rather than soi ("you") is significant: the Spirit dwells not only in Timothy but in all who believe. The guarding of the deposit is Timothy's specific responsibility, but the resource for guarding -- the indwelling Spirit -- is a shared possession of the whole community of faith.

## Verses 15-18: Two Responses to Suffering -- Desertion and Faithfulness

"You are aware that all who are in Asia turned away from me, among whom are Phygelus and Hermogenes. May the Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, for he often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain, but when he arrived in Rome he searched for me earnestly and found me -- may the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord on that day -- and you well know all the service he rendered at Ephesus."

**"You are aware that all who are in Asia turned away from me"** (oidas touto, hoti apestraphēsan me pantes hoi en tē Asia). The verb oidas ("you know") indicates that this information is not news to Timothy -- he already knows what happened. The verb apostrephein ("to turn away from, to turn one's back on, to reject") in the passive (apestraphēsan) describes a deliberate reorientation: these people have turned their backs on Paul. The preposition apo (implicit in the compound verb) indicates separation: they have separated themselves from Paul, distanced themselves from him.

The phrase pantes hoi en tē Asia ("all who are in Asia") is striking in its scope. "Asia" (hē Asia) refers to the Roman province of Asia, whose capital was Ephesus -- the very city where Timothy is stationed. The word pantes ("all") is likely a generalization expressing the overwhelming scale of the desertion rather than an absolute count of every individual, since Onesiphorus is about to be named as an exception. But even as hyperbole, the statement is devastating. The mass of the Asian believers has abandoned Paul. The reasons are not specified but can be inferred from the letter's context: association with a condemned prisoner was dangerous. The cost of loyalty was real, and the community that Paul had built in Asia was not willing to pay it.

The significance for Timothy is immediate and personal. He is in Ephesus, the capital of the province where "all" have turned away. The social pressure to join the desertion is enormous. Paul names the reality so that Timothy knows what he faces and can choose deliberately rather than drifting with the current.

**"Among whom are Phygelus and Hermogenes"** (hōn estin Phygelos kai Hermogenēs). Two individuals are named as specific examples of the general desertion. The names appear nowhere else in the New Testament or in known first-century Christian literature. Naming them serves the same function as naming Hymenaeus and Alexander in 1 Timothy 1:20: it exposes them to the community's knowledge and judgment. They are not anonymous figures in a general trend; they are specific people who made a specific choice. Phygelus (possibly from phygē, "flight" -- though the etymology may be coincidental) and Hermogenes ("born of Hermes" -- a standard Greek name) are on record as having turned away from Paul. Timothy knows them; the letter ensures that the community will remember their choice.

**"May the Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus"** (dōē eleos ho kyrios tō Onēsiphorou oikō). The optative dōē ("may he grant, would that he might give") expresses a prayer or wish. Paul prays for mercy (eleos) upon the household (oikos) of Onesiphorus. The noun oikos here means "household" -- the family, dependents, and slaves who constitute Onesiphorus's domestic unit.

The prayer is directed to the oikos rather than to Onesiphorus himself, which may indicate that Onesiphorus is not currently with his household. He has been in Rome (v. 17), and the prayer's structure -- mercy for his household now, mercy for him "on that day" (v. 18) -- suggests he may still be traveling or that he has died. The text does not state explicitly which situation obtains, and certainty is not possible from the text alone. What is clear is that Paul distinguishes between mercy for the household in the present and mercy for Onesiphorus on the eschatological day.

**"For he often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain"** (hoti pollakis me anepsyxen kai tēn halysin mou ouk epaischynthē). The conjunction hoti ("for, because") explains why Paul prays for mercy on this household. The verb anapsychein ("to refresh, to cool, to revive, to give relief") means literally "to breathe again" or "to cool by blowing." It describes physical and emotional renewal -- Onesiphorus brought Paul relief, comfort, and restoration. The adverb pollakis ("often, frequently, many times") indicates that this was not a single visit but a pattern of care: Onesiphorus came to Paul again and again.

The second reason is stated with the chapter's keynote verb: ouk epaischynthē -- "he was not ashamed." The object of potential shame is tēn halysin mou -- "my chain." The noun halysis ("chain, bond, fetter") in the singular likely refers to the chain by which Paul was physically attached to a Roman soldier (cf. Acts 28:20, "it is because of the hope of Israel that I am bound with this chain"; Ephesians 6:20, "for which I am an ambassador in chains"; Philippians 1:7, 13-14, 17). The chain was the visible marker of disgrace. To visit a chained prisoner was to associate oneself with his shame. Onesiphorus was not ashamed of Paul's chain. He looked at the disgrace and was not deterred by it. This is the practical demonstration of what Paul commands Timothy in verse 8: "do not be ashamed of the testimony about our Lord, nor of me his prisoner." Onesiphorus did what Paul asks Timothy to do.

**"But when he arrived in Rome he searched for me earnestly and found me"** (alla genomenos en Rhōmē spoudaiōs ezētēsen me kai heuren). The adversative alla ("but") contrasts Onesiphorus's behavior with the desertion of the Asian believers. Where they turned away, he came looking. The adverb spoudaiōs ("earnestly, eagerly, diligently, with great effort") describes the quality of the search: it was not casual or convenient but determined and effortful. The conditions of Paul's imprisonment apparently made him difficult to find -- he was not in an open, accessible location. Onesiphorus had to work to locate him. The verb zētein ("to seek, to search for, to look for") followed by heuriskein ("to find") creates a pair: he sought and he found. The effort was rewarded.

The detail that Onesiphorus was genomenos en Rhōmē ("having arrived in Rome, having come to be in Rome") indicates that he traveled from Asia to Rome. The distance from Ephesus to Rome was approximately 1,400 miles by land or a significant sea journey. Onesiphorus did not stumble across Paul; he undertook a substantial journey to reach him. This magnifies the contrast with those who were nearby (in Asia) and turned away. They were close and retreated; Onesiphorus was far away and came.

**"May the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord on that day"** (dōē autō ho kyrios heurein eleos para kyriou en ekeinē tē hēmera). Paul prays again, this time for Onesiphorus personally. The prayer is that "the Lord" (ho kyrios) would grant Onesiphorus "to find mercy from the Lord" (heurein eleos para kyriou) "on that day" (en ekeinē tē hēmera). The repetition of kyrios ("Lord") is notable: "may the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord." Whether the two uses of kyrios refer to the same person (Christ) or to different persons (Christ and God the Father) is not determinable from the text alone. The prayer as a whole asks that on the day of final reckoning, Onesiphorus will receive the mercy he showed to Paul. The pattern echoes Jesus' teaching in the Gospels: "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (Matthew 5:7); "For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured to you" (Matthew 7:2). The one who showed mercy to the prisoner will find mercy from the Judge.

The phrase en ekeinē tē hēmera ("on that day") repeats the same expression from verse 12, binding the two prayers together. Paul is confident that God will guard his deposit "until that day" (v. 12); he prays that Onesiphorus will find mercy "on that day" (v. 18). "That day" is the horizon toward which both Paul and Onesiphorus are moving -- the day when all faithfulness will be vindicated and all desertion will be exposed.

**"And you well know all the service he rendered at Ephesus"** (kai hosa en Ephesō diēkonēsen, beltion sy ginōskeis). The final note reminds Timothy that Onesiphorus's faithfulness in Rome was not an isolated act but the continuation of a pattern established in Ephesus. The verb diakonein ("to serve, to minister, to attend to") describes practical service -- the same word used for table service (Acts 6:2), for the collection for Jerusalem (Romans 15:25), and for all the varied forms of concrete care that characterize the community's life. The adverb beltion ("better, more fully, very well") with the pronoun sy ("you") personalizes the point: "you yourself know better than anyone" what Onesiphorus did in Ephesus. Timothy is personally acquainted with this man's track record. The mention of Ephesus brings the example home: Onesiphorus served in the very city where Timothy now stands. His faithfulness is not a distant tale but a local model.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

2 Timothy 1 is a personal, urgent appeal from a prisoner facing possible death to his beloved spiritual son. Every section serves the purpose of equipping Timothy to face the temptation of shame and to choose faithfulness despite its cost.

(1) **Verses 1-2** establish the letter's theological horizon: Paul's apostleship exists in connection with "the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus." The greeting's inclusion of "mercy" between grace and peace signals the letter's concern with God's faithfulness to those who suffer for his sake. Timothy is addressed not as "legitimate child" (as in 1 Timothy) but as "beloved child" -- the register is intimate, not institutional.

(2) **Verses 3-5** ground the letter in personal relationship and spiritual heritage. Paul's service to God stands in continuity with his Jewish ancestors. His conscience is clean. His prayers for Timothy are unceasing. He remembers Timothy's tears and longs for reunion. And he is confident that Timothy possesses the sincere faith that first dwelt in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice. The faith is genuine; the question is whether Timothy will act on it.

(3) **Verses 6-7** issue the letter's first direct exhortation: rekindle the gift. The gift God gave through the laying on of Paul's hands has not been extinguished, but it needs stirring. The basis for courage is stated: God's Spirit produces not cowardice but power, love, and self-discipline. These three qualities compose the character needed for ministry under pressure.

(4) **Verses 8-12** form the chapter's theological center. Timothy is commanded not to be ashamed of the gospel testimony or of Paul the prisoner, but to share in suffering for the gospel by God's power. The basis for this command is a compressed statement of God's saving purpose: God saved and called his people not because of their works but because of his own purpose and grace, given in Christ before time began, now revealed through Christ's appearing, which abolished death and brought imperishable life to light through the gospel. Paul himself models the response: appointed for this gospel, suffering because of it, yet not ashamed -- because he knows the person in whom he has placed his trust and is convinced that God can guard what he has deposited until the day of final reckoning.

(5) **Verses 13-14** specify the duty: hold the pattern of sound teaching received from Paul, in the sphere of faith and love, and guard the good deposit -- not by human effort alone but through the Holy Spirit who dwells within the community of believers. The deposit is the gospel itself, received from Paul, to be preserved intact and passed on.

(6) **Verses 15-18** provide concrete examples that frame the choice before Timothy. On one side stands the mass desertion: all in Asia turned away from Paul, including the named Phygelus and Hermogenes. On the other side stands Onesiphorus, who was not ashamed of Paul's chain, who traveled to Rome and searched earnestly for Paul, who refreshed him often, who served faithfully in Ephesus. Paul prays for mercy on Onesiphorus and his household -- the mercy that the merciful receive.

The chapter's architecture is built around the word "ashamed" (epaischynesthai), which appears three times and creates the letter's controlling contrast. Timothy is told not to be ashamed (v. 8). Paul declares he is not ashamed (v. 12). Onesiphorus was not ashamed (v. 16). Against these three stand all who turned away (v. 15) -- those who chose shame avoidance over faithfulness. The chapter presents Timothy with a binary choice: he can join the many who turned away, or he can follow the pattern of Paul and Onesiphorus. There is no middle ground. The gospel demands open identification with Christ and with his imprisoned apostle. The resources for this identification are God's own purpose and grace (v. 9), the spirit of power, love, and self-discipline (v. 7), and the indwelling Holy Spirit (v. 14). The cost is suffering (v. 8). The hope is "that day" (vv. 12, 18) -- the day when all deposits will be returned, all faithfulness vindicated, and all mercy repaid.
