# Acts 8 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 7):** Stephen stood before the Sanhedrin, charged with speaking against the temple and the Law of Moses (6:13-14). His response was the longest speech in Acts -- a retelling of Israel's entire history organized around a single pattern: Israel repeatedly rejects the agents God sends. The patriarchs rejected Joseph by selling him into Egypt, yet "God was with him" (7:9). The Israelites rejected Moses twice -- first when an individual pushed him away with "Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?" (7:27), then corporately when "our fathers were unwilling to be obedient to him, but repudiated him and in their hearts turned back to Egypt" (7:39). The wilderness generation turned to the golden calf, "rejoicing in the works of their hands" (7:41). Stephen argued that God was never confined to the temple -- the God of glory appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, spoke to Moses from a burning bush in the Midianite wilderness, and traveled with Israel in a portable tabernacle for centuries. Citing Isaiah 66:1-2, Stephen declared that "the Most High does not dwell in houses made by human hands" (7:48). He then turned the charges directly on the Sanhedrin: "You men who are stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears are always resisting the Holy Spirit; you are doing just as your fathers did" (7:51). They had become "betrayers and murderers" of the Righteous One (7:52). The council erupted in rage. Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, saw the heavens opened and "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" (7:56) -- the only use of the title "Son of Man" outside Jesus's own speech in the entire New Testament. The mob dragged him out of the city and stoned him. Stephen died praying for his killers, echoing Jesus's words from the cross. The witnesses laid their cloaks at the feet of "a young man named Saul" (7:58). Stephen "fell asleep" (7:60).

**What the author argues immediately after (Acts 9):** Saul, "still breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord" (9:1), obtains letters from the high priest to arrest believers in Damascus. On the road, a light from heaven flashes around him and a voice says, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting Me?" (9:4). The risen Jesus identifies himself: "I am Jesus whom you are persecuting" (9:5). Saul is blinded, led into Damascus, and after three days receives the laying on of hands from Ananias so that he "may regain his sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit" (9:17). He is baptized (9:18) and immediately proclaims Jesus in the synagogues, "proving that this Jesus is the Christ" (9:22). The chief persecutor becomes a proclaimer. The chapter ends with a summary: "the church throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria enjoyed peace, as it was being built up; and as it continued in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, it kept increasing" (9:31). The three regions named -- Judea, Galilee, and Samaria -- correspond to the geographic mandate of Acts 1:8, and the mention of the Holy Spirit's "comfort" recalls the Spirit's central role in the expansion narrated in chapter 8.

**How chapter 8 connects these:** Acts 7 ended with the first martyr's death and the introduction of the persecutor Saul. Acts 9 will narrate Saul's transformation and end with the church at peace across Judea, Galilee, and Samaria. Acts 8 is the bridge that turns the violence of chapter 7 into the geographic expansion that chapter 9 summarizes. The scattering caused by persecution becomes the mechanism of the gospel's advance beyond Jerusalem. Stephen's argument that God is not confined to the temple is proved in action: the scattered believers preach as they go, and God's Spirit works in Samaria and on a desert road to Gaza. The chapter advances the gospel along the precise trajectory Jesus announced in Acts 1:8 -- from Jerusalem into "all Judea and Samaria, and as far as the remotest part of the earth." Philip goes to Samaria (the second ring), then encounters an Ethiopian official returning toward Africa (the third ring -- the earth's remotest parts). Meanwhile, the chapter returns to Saul three times (8:1, 8:3, and the implied continuation into 9:1), keeping the persecutor in view as the background thread that will become the foreground story in chapter 9. The chapter's two main episodes -- Philip in Samaria and Philip with the Ethiopian -- both demonstrate what Stephen died arguing: that God's presence and God's salvation are not bound to Jerusalem, to the temple, or to ethnic Israel's boundaries.

## Verse 1a: Saul's Consent

"Now Saul approved of putting Stephen to death" (8:1a).

The author's first sentence after Stephen's death returns to the young man introduced at 7:58. The verb syneudokon (from syneudokeo, "to approve together, to consent, to take pleasure in") is stronger than passive acquiescence. The prefix syn- ("together with") indicates shared approval -- Saul joined in the collective judgment that Stephen should die. The word implies active agreement, not reluctant complicity. Paul himself will later confirm this in his own testimony: "when the blood of Your witness Stephen was being shed, I also was standing by approving" (Acts 22:20, using the same verb syneudokon). The author links Saul's name directly to the death of the man whose speech will turn out to be prophetically enacted in Saul's own life: Saul approved killing a man who proclaimed that Israel's leaders always reject God's agents -- and Saul himself will become one of those agents.

## Verses 1b-3: The Persecution and the Scattering

"And on that day a great persecution began against the church in Jerusalem, and they were all scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except for the apostles" (8:1b).

The phrase "on that day" (en ekeine te hemera) ties the persecution directly to Stephen's death. The stoning was not an isolated act of mob rage; it triggered a "great persecution" (diogmos megas) against "the church in Jerusalem" (ten ekklesian ten en Hierosolymois). The word ekklesia ("assembly, congregation") is the same word Stephen used for "the congregation in the wilderness" (7:38) -- the assembly that Moses led. The same word now describes the community under attack. The wilderness congregation faced hostility from within (the golden calf); the Jerusalem congregation faces hostility from without.

"They were all scattered" (pantes diesparesean) -- the verb diaspeiro means "to scatter, to disperse, to sow throughout." The word is the root of "diaspora" (diaspora), the term used for Jews living outside the land of Israel. The believers now experience what Israel experienced in exile: dispersion from the holy city into surrounding territories. But the word diaspeiro also carries an agricultural meaning -- "to sow seed by scattering." The LXX uses forms of this word for scattering in both senses: judgment (Deuteronomy 28:64: "the LORD will scatter you among all peoples") and sowing (the imagery of seed cast broadly). The author may intend both resonances: the believers are scattered like exiles, but they are also scattered like seed. What looks like destruction from the persecutors' perspective is sowing from God's perspective.

"Throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria" (kata tas choras tes Ioudaias kai Samareias) -- the geographic specification is precise and significant. Jesus's commission in Acts 1:8 named three concentric zones: "Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and as far as the remotest part of the earth." The believers had remained concentrated in Jerusalem through the first seven chapters of Acts. The persecution now forces them into the second zone: Judea and Samaria. The advance that should have come through obedient mission comes instead through involuntary displacement. The author presents this without irony -- the mechanism is human violence; the result is divine purpose.

"Except for the apostles" (plen ton apostolon) -- the apostles remain in Jerusalem. The reason is unstated. One possibility is that the persecution targeted specifically the Hellenistic believers associated with Stephen (whose seven companions all bore Greek names, 6:5), while the Aramaic-speaking apostles, who still worshiped in the temple (3:1, 5:42), were less immediately threatened. Another is that the apostles understood their role as requiring continued presence in Jerusalem -- the center from which the concentric expansion of 1:8 proceeds. Whatever the reason, the result is that the geographic expansion is carried out not by the apostles but by ordinary believers: "those who had been scattered went through places preaching the word" (8:4). The work of proclamation is not limited to the Twelve. The scattering democratizes the mission.

"Some devout men buried Stephen, and mourned loudly for him" (8:2).

The "devout men" (andres eulabeis) are distinguished from the fleeing believers by the fact that they stay to bury Stephen. The word eulabes means "reverent, devout, cautious, God-fearing." In Acts 2:5, the same word described the diaspora Jews present at Pentecost: "devout men from every nation." The term may indicate that these are pious Jews (not necessarily believers) who were moved by the injustice of Stephen's death and who performed the duty of burial despite the danger. The verb ekopsanto (from kopto, "to strike, to beat the breast") means "they lamented, they mourned with beating of the breast." The loud mourning (epoiesanto kopeton megan -- "they made a great lamentation") stands in contrast to the violence of the stoning. The community of decency responds to the act of brutality.

"But Saul began ravaging the church, entering house after house; and he would drag away men and women and put them in prison" (8:3).

The verb elymaineto (from lymainomai, "to ravage, to ruin, to destroy") describes devastating, violent action. The same word is used in the LXX of Psalm 80:13 (LXX 79:14) for the wild boar that ravages the vineyard: "A boar from the forest eats it away, and whatever moves in the field feeds on it." The image is of an animal tearing through a field, destroying indiscriminately. Saul's persecution is not surgical but sweeping: "entering house after house" (kata tous oikous eisporeuomenos) indicates a systematic, door-to-door campaign. He "would drag away" (syron) both "men and women" (andras kai gynaikas) -- the verb syro means to drag, to haul by force. The inclusion of women is notable; Saul does not limit his violence to male leaders but targets the entire community regardless of sex. He delivers them "to prison" (eis phylaken). The picture is of a community being torn apart at the household level -- the very households where believers met (2:46, "breaking bread from house to house") are now raided.

Paul himself later confirmed the thoroughness of his persecution: "I persecuted this Way to the death, binding and putting both men and women into prisons" (Acts 22:4); "I used to persecute the church of God beyond measure and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13).

## Verse 4: The Scattered Ones Preach

"Therefore, those who had been scattered went through places preaching the word" (8:4).

The connecting word "therefore" (men oun) signals that what follows is the consequence of the scattering -- but it is a consequence the persecutors did not intend. Those scattered (hoi diasparentes -- the same verb from 8:1) do not go silent. They go "preaching the word" (euangelizomenoi ton logon). The verb euangelizo means "to announce good news, to proclaim the gospel." The "word" (ton logon) is the message about Jesus -- the same "word of God" that "kept spreading" in 6:7. The persecution was designed to silence the message; it multiplies the messengers. The believers do not stop speaking because they have been forced to move. They speak wherever they go.

This verse establishes the principle that governs the rest of the chapter. Everything that follows -- Philip in Samaria, the encounter with the Ethiopian -- is an instance of scattered believers preaching the word. The author will now give two specific examples of what "preaching the word" looked like when the message crossed geographic and ethnic boundaries.

## Verses 5-8: Philip Proclaims the Christ in Samaria

"Philip went down to the city of Samaria and began proclaiming the Christ to them" (8:5).

This Philip is not the apostle Philip (one of the Twelve, listed in 1:13) but Philip the Hellenist, one of the seven selected in 6:5 to oversee the food distribution. He is listed second after Stephen, and like Stephen he was "full of the Spirit and of wisdom" (6:3). Philip is now the second of the seven to take center stage in the narrative. Stephen's work ended in martyrdom in Jerusalem; Philip's work begins with mission beyond Jerusalem.

"Went down" (katelthon) -- Jerusalem sits at approximately 2,500 feet elevation; Samaria lies in the lower hill country to the north. The verb is geographic, not metaphorical. But the destination is loaded with significance. Samaria was the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel, which broke away from Judah after Solomon's death (1 Kings 12). After the Assyrian conquest in 722 BCE, the Assyrian king resettled foreign populations in Samaria who intermarried with the remaining Israelites and practiced a syncretistic religion (2 Kings 17:24-34: "they feared the LORD and served their own gods"). By the first century, Samaritans were regarded by most Jews as half-breeds and heretics. They accepted the Torah (the five books of Moses) but rejected the prophets and the writings. They worshiped on Mount Gerizim rather than in Jerusalem. The hostility between Jews and Samaritans was deep and longstanding -- "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (John 4:9).

For Philip to go to Samaria and proclaim the Messiah there is to cross one of the most charged ethnic and religious boundaries in the first-century Jewish world. The author has prepared for this. Jesus himself had engaged the Samaritan woman (John 4), and his commission in Acts 1:8 explicitly named Samaria as one of the zones of witness. But until this moment, no one had acted on it. The persecution provides the occasion; Philip provides the obedience.

"Proclaiming the Christ" (ekerussen ton Christon) -- the verb kerusso means "to herald, to announce publicly, to proclaim as a herald." The content is "the Christ" (ton Christon) -- the Messiah, the anointed one. For Samaritans, the messianic expectation centered on the Taheb, a figure based on Deuteronomy 18:15-18 -- the "prophet like Moses" whom God would raise up. Stephen had cited this very text in his speech (7:37). The Samaritans, who accepted the Torah, would have known the Deuteronomy promise. Philip's proclamation of "the Christ" would have connected with the Samaritan expectation of the Taheb -- the coming prophet-restorer.

"The crowds were paying attention with one mind to what was being said by Philip, as they heard and saw the signs which he was performing" (8:6).

The word homothymadon ("with one mind, with one accord") was used for the believing community's unity in prayer (1:14, 2:46, 4:24) and for the mob's rush against Stephen (7:57). Here it describes the Samaritans' unified attention to Philip's message. The verb prosechon (from prosecho, "to hold toward, to pay attention, to give heed") indicates focused, sustained attention. The Samaritans attend to Philip's words and to his "signs" (ta semeia). The word semeia ("signs") is the same word used for the apostles' signs (2:43, 5:12), for Stephen's signs (6:8), and for Moses's signs (7:36). The continuity of language links Philip's work in Samaria to the same divine power that operated through Moses, the apostles, and Stephen.

"For in the case of many who had unclean spirits, they were coming out of them shouting with a loud voice; and many who had been paralyzed or limped on crutches were healed" (8:7).

The signs Philip performs are specifically identified: exorcism of unclean spirits and healing of the paralyzed and lame. The unclean spirits come out "shouting with a loud voice" (boonta phone megale) -- an audible, public departure. The healing of the paralyzed and lame echoes Peter's healing of the lame man at the temple gate (3:1-10) and the apostles' healing ministry generally (5:15-16). The same kinds of healings that authenticated the apostolic message in Jerusalem now authenticate Philip's message in Samaria. The power has not diminished by crossing the border from Judea to Samaria.

"So there was much rejoicing in that city" (8:8).

The word chara ("joy, rejoicing") is the community's response to the signs and the message. The same word appears in Jesus's parable of the sower for the seed that falls on rocky ground: "he hears the word and immediately receives it with joy" (Matthew 13:20) -- but that context carries a warning about shallow reception. In the present passage, the author does not attach such a warning. The joy is presented as a genuine response to genuine deliverance. People who were oppressed by unclean spirits, paralyzed, or lame have been healed. The joy is grounded in real, physical liberation.

## Verses 9-13: Simon the Practitioner of Magic

"Now a man named Simon had previously been practicing magic in the city and astonishing the people of Samaria, claiming to be someone great" (8:9).

The author introduces Simon with three characteristics. First, he "had previously been practicing magic" (prouperchen mageuon) -- the verb mageuomai means "to practice magic arts, to exercise sorcery." The imperfect participle with the pluperfect auxiliary (prouperchen) indicates a longstanding, prior activity: Simon had been doing this for a long time before Philip arrived. The Torah explicitly prohibits sorcery: "There shall not be found among you anyone who... uses divination, a soothsayer, one who interprets omens, or a sorcerer" (Deuteronomy 18:10). Whether Samaritans applied this prohibition with the same rigor as the Jerusalem establishment is unclear, but the Torah they accepted contained the prohibition. Simon's practice operated in the space of popular religion, where such boundaries were often ignored.

Second, Simon was "astonishing the people of Samaria" (existanon to ethnos tes Samareias). The verb existemi means "to put out of one's mind, to amaze, to astound." The word ethnos ("nation, people") designates the Samaritan people as a whole -- Simon's influence extended to the entire population, not merely a fringe.

Third, Simon was "claiming to be someone great" (legon einai tina heauton megan -- "saying himself to be someone great"). The reflexive heauton ("himself") indicates self-promotion. Simon made claims about his own status and power.

"And all the people, from small to great, were paying attention to him, saying, 'This man is the Power of God that is called Great'" (8:10).

The phrase "from small to great" (apo mikrou heos megalou) is a Hebrew idiom meaning "everyone without exception" (used in the LXX in Jeremiah 6:13, 31:34, and elsewhere). The title the people assign to Simon -- "the Power of God that is called Great" (he dynamis tou theou he kaloumene megale) -- identifies him in quasi-divine terms. The word dynamis ("power") is the same word used for the power of the Holy Spirit (1:8: "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you") and for the mighty works of the apostles (2:22, 4:33). The Samaritans attributed to Simon a divine power -- specifically, "the Great Power" of God. This may reflect a Samaritan theological category, a designation for a supreme divine emanation or manifestation. The people did not merely admire Simon; they identified him with a divine attribute.

The verb prosechon ("were paying attention") is the same word used in verse 6 for the crowds' attention to Philip. The parallel is deliberate: the Samaritans formerly paid attention to Simon (v. 10-11); now they pay attention to Philip (v. 6). The object of their attention has shifted from a human self-promoter to a herald of the Christ.

"And they were paying attention to him because for a long time he had astounded them with his magic arts" (8:11).

The reason for Simon's hold is duration: "for a long time" (hikano chrono, "for a considerable time"). His influence was established over years, not days. The word mageia ("magic arts, sorcery") categorizes Simon's activity explicitly. This is not prophetic power or apostolic signs; it is mageia -- the arts forbidden by the Torah (Deuteronomy 18:10-12). The author distinguishes sharply between Philip's semeia ("signs") and Simon's mageia ("magic"). Both produce amazement in the crowds, but the source and nature of the power are fundamentally different.

"But when they believed Philip as he was preaching the good news about the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ, both men and women were being baptized" (8:12).

The pivotal conjunction "but" (hote de) marks the turn. The content of Philip's message is described in two phrases: "the good news about the kingdom of God" (euangelizomeno peri tes basileias tou theou) and "the name of Jesus Christ" (tou onomatos Iesou Christou). These two phrases correspond to the two aspects of the apostolic proclamation throughout Acts: the kingdom of God (which Jesus taught about for forty days after the resurrection, 1:3) and the name of Jesus as the Christ (which Peter proclaimed at Pentecost, 2:36). The message is not merely about healing or signs; it is about God's kingdom and about who Jesus is.

The response is faith and baptism. "They believed" (episteusan) -- the verb pisteuo means "to believe, to trust, to place confidence in." "Both men and women were being baptized" (ebaptizonto andres te kai gynaikes) -- the imperfect tense indicates an ongoing process: people were being baptized over time, not in a single event. The inclusion of "both men and women" echoes the scope of Saul's persecution in verse 3 ("dragging away men and women") -- what Saul destroys in Jerusalem, Philip builds in Samaria, and both involve men and women equally.

"Now even Simon himself believed; and after being baptized, he continued on with Philip, and as he observed signs and great miracles taking place, he was repeatedly amazed" (8:13).

The word "even" (kai) emphasizes the surprise: Simon himself -- the man the whole city followed, the one called "the Great Power of God" -- believed and was baptized. The verb episteusen ("he believed") is the same verb used for the Samaritan crowds in verse 12. The author presents Simon's belief with the same vocabulary as everyone else's. He then "continued on with Philip" (en proskarterean to Philippo) -- the verb proskartereo means "to devote oneself to, to persist in, to be constantly attentive to." It is the same word used for the community's devotion to the apostles' teaching and prayer (2:42). Simon attached himself to Philip.

What amazes Simon is described in language distinct from his own former activity: "signs and great miracles" (semeia kai dynameis megalas) -- semeia ("signs") and dynameis ("powers, mighty works"), not mageia ("sorcery"). The man who formerly amazed others (existanon, v. 9, 11) is now himself "repeatedly amazed" (existamenos, v. 13). The same verb is used -- existemi -- but the direction is reversed. The one who astonished is now the one astonished. The power Philip displays surpasses anything Simon had ever produced.

However, the author leaves Simon's inner state ambiguous at this point. The text says he believed and was baptized and continued with Philip. What follows in verses 18-24 will reveal the quality of that belief.

## Verses 14-17: The Apostles Send Peter and John to Samaria

"Now when the apostles in Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent them Peter and John" (8:14).

The news reaches Jerusalem: "Samaria had received the word of God" (dedektai he Samareia ton logon tou theou). The verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to accept") is the same word used in Acts 2:41 for those who "received" Peter's word at Pentecost and were baptized. The phrasing is deliberately parallel: Jerusalem received the word at Pentecost; now Samaria has received the word through Philip. The geographic expansion of Acts 1:8 is unfolding.

The apostles respond by sending Peter and John. The verb apesteilan ("they sent") is significant -- the apostoloi ("sent ones") now send two of their own to confirm and complete what Philip began. The apostles' role is not to replace Philip's work but to authenticate it and to mediate what Philip's ministry alone had not yet accomplished: the giving of the Holy Spirit.

"Who came down and prayed for them that they would receive the Holy Spirit" (8:15).

Peter and John's purpose is specific: they pray for the Samaritan believers "that they would receive the Holy Spirit" (hopos labousin pneuma hagion). The Holy Spirit is what they lack despite having believed and been baptized. This is unusual in the Acts narrative. At Pentecost, Peter promised that those who repented and were baptized would "receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (2:38). The normal sequence was repentance, baptism, and reception of the Spirit. In Samaria, the sequence is different. The author explains why:

"(For He had not yet fallen upon any of them; they had simply been baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus.)" (8:16).

The parenthetical clause is the author's own editorial explanation. The Spirit "had not yet fallen" (oudepo en ep oudeni auton epipeptkos) on any of the Samaritan believers. They had been "simply baptized" (monon bebaptismenoi) -- the word monon ("only, merely") indicates that baptism alone had occurred, without the accompanying reception of the Spirit. The verb epipipto ("to fall upon") is the same word used later for the Spirit's descent on the Gentile household of Cornelius (10:44: "the Holy Spirit fell upon all those who were listening to the message").

The author does not explain why the Spirit was withheld. Several observations from the narrative context suggest a reason. The Samaritans were the first non-Jewish group to receive the gospel. The Jewish-Samaritan division was centuries old and deeply entrenched. If the Samaritans had received the Spirit independently of any connection to the apostolic community in Jerusalem, the result could have been two separate, disconnected movements -- a Jewish church and a Samaritan church with no organic link between them. The delay in the Spirit's arrival until the apostles came from Jerusalem created a visible, undeniable connection: the Samaritan community's experience of the Spirit came through the hands of the same apostles who had received the Spirit at Pentecost. The unity between the Jerusalem community and the Samaritan community was established at its inception, not retrofitted later.

This interpretation is consistent with the author's larger concern throughout Acts. When the Spirit later falls on Cornelius's household without apostolic mediation (10:44), Peter is present to witness it, and his testimony to the Jerusalem community is what resolves the controversy (11:1-18). In each case of boundary-crossing -- Samaritans, Gentiles -- the apostles are present to witness and validate, ensuring that the expanding community remains one body, not multiple competing groups.

"Then they began laying their hands on them, and they were receiving the Holy Spirit" (8:17).

The laying on of hands (epitithentes tas cheiras ep autous) is the physical action through which the Spirit is given. The same gesture was used when the apostles commissioned the seven (6:6) and when Ananias will lay hands on Saul (9:17). The Spirit is described as something they "were receiving" (elambanon) -- the imperfect tense indicates that the reception occurred as hands were laid on individual believers, person by person. The Spirit was not a vague influence but a specific, experienced reality given to each person.

## Verses 18-24: Simon's Attempt to Purchase the Spirit

"Now when Simon saw that the Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles' hands, he offered them money" (8:18).

The verb theaomai ("to see, to behold, to observe") indicates that something visible occurred when the Spirit was given. The author does not specify what Simon saw -- whether it was speaking in tongues (as at Pentecost, 2:4), prophesying, or some other visible manifestation. But the visible effect was clear enough that Simon recognized a connection between the apostles' hands and the Spirit's arrival. His response is to offer money (prosenenken chremata -- "he brought money, he offered payment").

"Saying, 'Give this authority to me as well, so that everyone on whom I lay my hands may receive the Holy Spirit'" (8:19).

Simon's request reveals his understanding -- and his misunderstanding. He grasps that the apostles possess an "authority" (exousia) that produces real results. He wants the same authority. His framework, however, is commercial: power is something that can be acquired through payment. This is the framework of his previous life. In the world of mageia, practitioners purchased techniques, spells, and formulas from one another. The magical papyri of the ancient world are full of instructions that were transmitted for a price. Simon applies the logic of his former trade to the apostolic ministry: if the apostles have a power that works, it can be bought.

The request also reveals what Simon values. He does not ask for the Holy Spirit for himself. He asks for the authority to bestow the Spirit on others. He wants to be the mediator, the conduit, the one through whom power flows. He wants to regain the position he held before Philip arrived -- the person to whom everyone pays attention, the "Great Power." His interest is in the authority, not in the Spirit's transformative presence.

"But Peter said to him, 'May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could acquire the gift of God with money!'" (8:20).

Peter's response is a curse formula. The phrase "May your silver perish with you" (to argyrion sou syn soi eie eis apoleian -- literally, "may your silver be with you unto destruction") invokes destruction upon both the money and the man. The word apoleia ("destruction, perdition, ruin") is not a mild rebuke. Peter is saying: may you and your money go to ruin together. The severity matches the offense.

The reason: "because you thought you could acquire the gift of God with money" (hoti ten dorean tou theou enomisas dia chrematon ktasthai). The key word is dorean ("gift, free gift"). The Holy Spirit is called "the gift of God" (ten dorean tou theou) -- the same phrase Peter used at Pentecost: "you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (2:38). A dorean, by definition, cannot be purchased. The moment it is bought, it ceases to be a gift. Simon's fundamental error is a category mistake: he treats a gift as a commodity. Peter's statement defines the Spirit as irrevocably gratuitous -- it is given, never sold.

"You have no part or share in this matter, for your heart is not right before God" (8:21).

"No part or share" (ouk estin soi meris oude kleros) -- the words meris ("portion, part, share") and kleros ("lot, allotment, inheritance") are both used in the LXX for Israel's inheritance in the land. In Deuteronomy 12:12 and 14:27, 29, the Levites are described as having "no part or inheritance" (meris kai kleros) in the land because the Lord is their inheritance. Peter's words echo the language of covenant exclusion: Simon has no share in "this matter" (en to logo touto -- "in this word/matter"). The word logos here can mean "message," "matter," or "reality." Simon is excluded from the apostolic enterprise because his heart is the problem.

"Your heart is not right before God" (he kardia sou ouk estin eutheia enanti tou theou) -- the word eutheia means "straight, upright, right." The concept of a heart that is "straight before God" echoes the language of Psalm 78:37 (LXX 77:37): "their heart was not steadfast with him" (he kardia auton ouk eutheia met autou). Psalm 78 is itself a retelling of Israel's history of rebellion -- the same history Stephen recounted in Acts 7. The psalm describes a people who "did not keep the covenant of God" (78:10), who "tested God in their heart" (78:18), and whose hearts were "not steadfast." Peter diagnoses Simon's condition in the language of Israel's own history of unfaithfulness: a heart that is not straight before God.

"Therefore, repent of this wickedness of yours, and pray to the Lord that, if possible, the intention of your heart will be forgiven you" (8:22).

Peter's command has two parts: repent (metanoeson -- the same imperative Peter used at Pentecost in 2:38) and pray (deetheti tou kyriou). The call to repentance means that Simon's condition, though severe, is not irreversible. Peter does not pronounce a final sentence; he prescribes a remedy. But the remedy is qualified: "if possible" (ei ara aphetesetai soi he epinoia tes kardias sou). The phrase "if possible" (ei ara) introduces genuine uncertainty. Peter does not guarantee forgiveness; he holds it out as a possibility contingent on genuine repentance. The word epinoia ("thought, intention, purpose") describes what is in Simon's heart -- the deep disposition, the underlying aim. It is not merely the outward act of offering money that needs forgiveness but the heart-level intention that produced it.

The conditional "if possible" is striking. In the Acts narrative so far, forgiveness has been offered unconditionally to those who repent. Peter told the Pentecost crowd: "Repent, and each of you be baptized... for the forgiveness of your sins" (2:38) -- no conditions, no qualifications. To Simon, Peter adds the qualifier. The difference is in the nature of the offense. At Pentecost, the crowd was guilty of complicity in Jesus's death -- a grave sin, but one committed in ignorance (3:17: "I know that you acted in ignorance"). Simon's offense is different: he has witnessed the Spirit's power firsthand, has been baptized, and yet has attempted to commodify what he experienced as a free gift. The sin is not ignorance but calculated exploitation of the sacred. Peter's "if possible" reflects the gravity of attempting to purchase God's gift after having already received the message about it.

"For I see that you are in the gall of bitterness and in the bondage of unrighteousness" (8:23).

Peter's diagnosis uses two phrases, both drawing on Old Testament language.

"The gall of bitterness" (eis cholen pikrias) echoes Deuteronomy 29:18 (LXX): "lest there be among you a root bearing poisonous fruit and bitterness" (riza ano phyousa en chole kai pikria). The Deuteronomy context describes an Israelite who hears the words of the covenant curse and yet "considers himself fortunate in his heart, saying, 'I will do well though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart'" (Deuteronomy 29:19). Such a person becomes a "root" that produces poison within the community -- an internal source of corruption. Peter identifies Simon as this kind of figure: someone within the believing community whose heart intention is poisonous, a root of bitterness that could corrupt others.

"The bondage of unrighteousness" (eis syndesmon adikias) -- the word syndesmos means "bond, fetter, ligament, that which binds together." Simon is "bound" by adikia ("unrighteousness, injustice, wrongdoing"). The image is of a person held captive by a pattern of wrongdoing. Despite his profession of belief and his baptism, Simon remains in bondage to the same disposition that drove his former sorcery: the desire for power over others, the instinct to acquire and control spiritual authority for personal advantage. His external actions (belief, baptism, following Philip) have not broken the internal chain.

"But Simon answered and said, 'Pray to the Lord for me yourselves, so that nothing of what you have said may come upon me'" (8:24).

Simon's response is ambiguous, and the author leaves it that way. Simon does not say "I repent." He asks Peter and John to pray on his behalf. His concern, as stated, is that "nothing of what you have said may come upon me" (hopos meden epelthe ep eme hon eirekate) -- he fears the consequences Peter described. Whether this fear reflects genuine repentance or merely self-preservation, the text does not say. The author does not narrate Simon's final fate. He drops out of the Acts narrative at this point. The unresolved ending serves the author's purpose: Simon stands as a warning example of someone who believed and was baptized yet whose heart was "not right before God." The story illustrates that belief and baptism do not automatically resolve every disposition of the heart. The call to repentance continues after baptism. The heart must be genuinely transformed, not merely externally compliant.

The Simon episode has significant structural function within Acts 8. It stands in contrast to the Ethiopian eunuch episode that follows. Simon was a powerful insider in Samaria -- known by all, followed by all, called "the Great Power of God." He believed and was baptized but proved to have a heart that was not right before God. The Ethiopian eunuch, by contrast, will be an outsider in every sense -- a foreigner, a eunuch, a court official from a distant land. He will believe and be baptized, and will go on his way rejoicing. The contrast is between a well-positioned insider who fails to grasp the nature of the gift and a marginal outsider who receives it with unqualified joy.

## Verse 25: The Return to Jerusalem

"So, when they had solemnly testified and spoken the word of the Lord, they started back to Jerusalem, and were preaching the gospel to many villages of the Samaritans" (8:25).

Peter and John's ministry in Samaria is summarized: they "solemnly testified" (diamartymenoi -- the verb diamartyromai means "to testify earnestly, to declare solemnly") and "spoken the word of the Lord" (lalesantes ton logon tou kyriou). Their work was proclamation and testimony, not merely administration.

On their return to Jerusalem, they did not travel silently. "They were preaching the gospel to many villages of the Samaritans" (pollas te komas ton Samareiton euengelizonto). The imperfect tense indicates ongoing activity: as they traveled south through Samaritan territory, they preached in village after village. The apostles who came to Samaria to validate Philip's work now do Philip's work themselves. The boundary that Jewish-Samaritan hostility had maintained is being dismantled, village by village, by the very leaders of the Jerusalem community. Peter and John, who once asked Jesus if they should "command fire to come down from heaven" to destroy a Samaritan village that rejected Jesus (Luke 9:54), now walk through Samaritan villages proclaiming the good news. The transformation is complete.

## Verses 26-29: Philip Sent to the Desert Road

"But an angel of the Lord spoke to Philip, saying, 'Get ready and go south to the road that descends from Jerusalem to Gaza.' (This is a desert road.)" (8:26).

The narrative shifts abruptly. Philip is redirected from the fruitful Samaritan ministry to a desert road. The instruction comes from "an angel of the Lord" (angelos kyriou) -- the same agency that opened the prison doors for the apostles (5:19) and that appeared to Moses at the burning bush (7:30). The angel directs Philip southward, toward the road descending from Jerusalem to Gaza. Gaza was the southernmost of the five Philistine cities, located near the Mediterranean coast on the route to Egypt and beyond.

The author's parenthetical note -- "this is a desert road" (haute estin eremos) -- underscores the strangeness of the instruction. Philip is being sent from a productive urban ministry (crowds responding, signs occurring, people being baptized) to an empty road in the wilderness. The contrast echoes the pattern of God's activity throughout Acts 7: God appeared to Moses not in a city but in the wilderness (7:30), spoke to Abraham not in a temple but in Mesopotamia (7:2), and was "with" Joseph not in the promised land but in Egypt (7:9). God's purposes are pursued not only in the places where large crowds gather but also on empty roads in the desert.

"So he got ready and went" (kai anastas eporeuthee) -- Philip obeys without recorded hesitation. The verb anastas ("having risen, having gotten up") followed by the verb of motion indicates immediate compliance. No questions, no objections, no negotiation.

"And there was an Ethiopian eunuch, a court official of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who was in charge of all her treasure; and he had come to Jerusalem to worship" (8:27).

The author introduces this figure with unusual detail, stacking four descriptors.

First, he is "Ethiopian" (Aithiops). Ethiopia (Cush in the Hebrew scriptures) referred to the kingdom of Nubia/Meroe, south of Egypt, in modern-day Sudan. In the first-century imagination, Ethiopia represented the southern limit of the known world. The Table of Nations in Genesis 10:6 places Cush as a son of Ham. The prophetic tradition included Ethiopia in God's future purposes: "From beyond the rivers of Ethiopia My worshipers, My dispersed ones, will bring My offerings" (Zephaniah 3:10); "Cush will quickly stretch out her hands to God" (Psalm 68:31). For a first-century Jewish reader, an Ethiopian coming to Jerusalem to worship and receiving the gospel would have resonated as the fulfillment of these prophetic expectations. The gospel is reaching toward "the remotest part of the earth" (Acts 1:8).

Second, he is a "eunuch" (eunouchos). The word could refer to a castrated male or, in a broader sense, to a court official (since eunuchs commonly served in Near Eastern royal courts). However, the author uses the word four additional times in this passage (vv. 34, 36, 38, 39), making it a defining characteristic of the man -- it is unlikely to be merely a title. If the man is literally a eunuch, his situation is theologically complex. Deuteronomy 23:1 states: "No one who is emasculated or has his male organ cut off may enter the assembly of the LORD." A eunuch was excluded from full participation in Israel's covenant community. This exclusion explains a detail the author provides: the eunuch "had come to Jerusalem to worship" (8:27) -- he had traveled a vast distance to worship at the temple, but as a eunuch, he would have been barred from entering the inner courts. He was a worshiper who could not fully worship. He came to the house of God but could not fully enter it.

However, the prophetic tradition anticipated the reversal of this exclusion. Isaiah 56:3-5 directly addresses the eunuch's situation: "Nor let the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree.' For this is what the LORD says: 'To the eunuchs who keep My Sabbaths, and choose what pleases Me, and hold firmly to My covenant, to them I will give in My house and within My walls a memorial, and a name better than that of sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name which will not be eliminated.'" The prophet envisions a time when the eunuch's exclusion will be overturned -- when the eunuch will receive something "better than sons and daughters" (the very thing a eunuch cannot produce). The fact that the eunuch was reading Isaiah (v. 28) when Philip encountered him places the scene squarely within the Isaianic vision of restored access. The eunuch is reading the prophet who promised that people like him would be included.

Third, he is "a court official of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who was in charge of all her treasure" (dynastes Kandakes basillisses Aithiopon, hos en epi pases tes gazes autes). The word dynastes means "a ruler, a man of power, a court official of high rank." "Candace" (Kandake) was not a personal name but a dynastic title -- the queen mother of the Meroitic kingdom, analogous to "Pharaoh" for Egypt's kings. This man held the highest financial office in a foreign kingdom. He is a person of significant worldly authority and responsibility.

Fourth, "he had come to Jerusalem to worship" (hos elelythei proskynesaon eis Ierousalem). Despite being a foreigner, a eunuch, and a high official with demanding responsibilities, he had traveled the enormous distance from Meroe to Jerusalem for the purpose of worship. The verb proskyneo means "to worship, to prostrate oneself, to pay homage." This man is a God-fearer -- a Gentile drawn to the God of Israel, who worships Israel's God without being a full proselyte (conversion being impossible for a eunuch under Deuteronomic law). He occupies the outermost circle of those attached to Israel's faith: too far away, too physically disqualified, too ethnically other to belong fully -- yet compelled to travel hundreds of miles to worship.

"And he was returning and sitting in his chariot, and was reading Isaiah the prophet" (8:28).

He is on his way home. The worship in Jerusalem is behind him. Whatever he experienced there -- standing in the outer courts, excluded from the inner precincts, worshiping from the margins -- he is now leaving. He sits in his chariot reading Isaiah. The detail that he is reading indicates literacy and access to a scroll -- a significant investment. An Isaiah scroll would have been costly and rare. This is a man of means and serious devotion.

The fact that he is reading Isaiah is not incidental to the author's purpose. Isaiah contains both the exclusion passage (eunuchs cannot enter, which the Deuteronomic law states) and the restoration passage (eunuchs will be given a name and a place, Isaiah 56:3-5). Isaiah also contains the suffering servant passage that the eunuch will shortly ask Philip about (Isaiah 53:7-8). The author is presenting a reader who is in the right book, reading the right prophet, at the right moment, on the right road.

"Then the Spirit said to Philip, 'Go up and join this chariot'" (8:29).

Now it is the Spirit, not an angel, who directs Philip. The verb proskolletheti (from proskollao, "to join to, to attach to, to glue to") means "attach yourself to, stay close to." The Spirit's instruction is specific: go to this particular chariot, this particular person. The entire encounter is orchestrated -- angel, road, timing, reader, Spirit, herald.

## Verses 30-35: Philip Interprets Isaiah for the Eunuch

"Philip ran up and heard him reading Isaiah the prophet, and said, 'Do you understand what you are reading?'" (8:30).

Philip "ran up" (prosdrameon) -- the verb prostrechoo means "to run toward." Philip is urgent. The fact that Philip "heard him reading" indicates the ancient practice of reading aloud. Silent reading was uncommon in the ancient world; texts were normally vocalized. Philip heard the words of Isaiah being spoken.

Philip's question -- "Do you understand what you are reading?" (ara ge ginoskeis ha anaginoskeis) -- contains a wordplay in Greek: ginoskeis ("do you know/understand?") and anaginoskeis ("are you reading?"). The question is not condescending; it is an offer of help. The verb ginosko means "to know, to understand, to perceive the meaning." Reading and understanding are distinguished -- one can read without comprehending.

"And he said, 'Well, how could I, unless someone guides me?' And he invited Philip to come up and sit with him" (8:31).

The eunuch's response is honest and humble: "How could I, unless someone guides me?" (pos gar an dynmaimen ean me tis hodoegesei me). The verb hodogeo means "to guide along the way, to lead, to direct" -- from hodos ("way, road") and hegeomai ("to lead"). The word is appropriate to the setting: they are on a road, and the eunuch needs a guide. The word also echoes the language of teaching and discipleship -- a guide who leads someone along the path of understanding.

The eunuch's response expresses a principle that recurs throughout Acts: the message requires interpretation. The Ethiopian has the text of Isaiah but lacks the interpretive key. Philip has the key -- the events of Jesus's life, death, and resurrection that unlock the meaning of the prophetic text. Scripture and interpreter together produce understanding.

"Now the passage of Scripture which he was reading was this: 'He was led like a sheep to slaughter; and like a lamb that is silent before its shearer, so He does not open His mouth. In humiliation His justice was taken away; who will describe His generation? For His life is taken away from the earth'" (8:32-33).

The eunuch is reading Isaiah 53:7-8 (from the LXX). The passage describes a figure who suffers without resistance and without complaint. The key elements:

"He was led like a sheep to slaughter" (hos probaton epi sphagen echthe) -- the simile compares the figure to a sheep being led to its death. The sheep does not resist; it is led. The word sphage ("slaughter") specifies the purpose: this is not casual leading but leading to killing.

"Like a lamb that is silent before its shearer, so He does not open His mouth" (hos amnos enantion tou keirantos auton aphonos, houtos ouk anoigei to stoma autou) -- a second simile reinforces the first. The figure is as silent as a lamb being shorn. The word aphonos means "voiceless, without sound, silent." The figure does not protest, does not cry out, does not defend himself. In the full context of Isaiah 53, this silence is not weakness but voluntary submission: "He was oppressed and afflicted, yet He did not open His mouth" (Isaiah 53:7).

"In humiliation His justice was taken away" (en te tapeinosei autou he krisis autou erthe) -- the LXX of Isaiah 53:8 differs from the Hebrew in emphasis. The Hebrew reads approximately "by oppression and judgment he was taken away," where the "judgment" is the judicial process that condemned him. The LXX rendering emphasizes that justice was denied to him -- his krisis ("judgment, justice, right") was "taken away" (erthe, from airo, "to lift, to remove, to take away"). The figure was denied justice; his legal rights were stripped from him. The word tapeinosis ("humiliation, low estate") describes the condition in which this injustice occurred.

"Who will describe His generation?" (ten genean autou tis diegesetai) -- the word genea can mean "generation" (the people of his time), "posterity" (his descendants), or "life span" (his age, his era). The question asks who can adequately describe or account for this. In the Hebrew, the sense is closer to "who among his generation considered that he was cut off?" -- a question about the failure of his contemporaries to understand what was happening. The LXX rendering opens the meaning wider: who can tell the story of this figure's generation?

"For His life is taken away from the earth" (hoti airetai apo tes ges he zoe autou) -- the figure's life is removed from the earth. The verb airetai ("is taken away") is in the present tense, giving the statement an ongoing, vivid quality. The death of this figure is presented as a removal from earthly existence.

For a first-century reader who knew the full context of Isaiah 53, the passage continues beyond what the eunuch was reading: the suffering figure bears the sin of many (53:12), is counted with wrongdoers (53:12), and yet will "see his offspring" and "prolong his days" (53:10) -- a sequence of death followed by continued life. The full chapter describes a figure who dies unjustly, whose death benefits others, and who is somehow vindicated after death. This is precisely the apostolic proclamation about Jesus.

"The eunuch answered Philip and said, 'Please tell me, of whom does the prophet say this? Of himself, or of someone else?'" (8:34).

The eunuch's question is the interpretive question that had occupied Jewish readers of Isaiah 53 for centuries. The passage describes an unnamed "he" who suffers. Who is this figure? The eunuch identifies two possibilities: the prophet himself (Isaiah, speaking autobiographically) or "someone else" (heteros tinos, another person). The Targum Jonathan on Isaiah 53 identifies the suffering figure as the Messiah in some verses and as Israel in others, reflecting a longstanding interpretive tension. The eunuch does not resolve the question himself; he asks for guidance.

"Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture he preached Jesus to him" (8:35).

The phrase "opened his mouth" (anoixas to stoma autou) is a Hebraic expression for deliberate, authoritative speech (the same phrase introduces Peter's speech at Cornelius's house, 10:34, and Jesus's sermon on the mount, Matthew 5:2). Philip begins "from this Scripture" (apo tes graphes tautes) -- the Isaiah 53 passage is the starting point, not the totality. The word arxamenos ("beginning from") indicates that Philip moved forward from Isaiah 53 into a broader exposition. The content is summarized in two words: "he preached Jesus" (euengelisato auto ton Iesoun -- "he announced the good news about Jesus to him").

Philip's interpretive move is the same one the entire apostolic community has been making: reading the prophetic scriptures as pointing to Jesus. Peter did this at Pentecost with Psalm 16 and Psalm 110 (Acts 2). Peter and John did this with Psalm 118 (Acts 4). Stephen did this with Deuteronomy 18 (Acts 7). Now Philip does it with Isaiah 53. In each case, the scripture provides the framework and Jesus provides the fulfillment. The suffering figure of Isaiah 53 -- led like a sheep to slaughter, silent before his shearers, denied justice, his life taken from the earth -- is identified as Jesus, who was handed over to death, did not resist, was condemned unjustly, and was crucified.

For the eunuch specifically, the identification carries additional significance. If the figure of Isaiah 53 is Jesus, then the broader context of Isaiah becomes relevant. The same prophet who described the suffering servant also promised that eunuchs would be given "a name better than sons and daughters" (Isaiah 56:5) and that foreigners would be brought to God's "holy mountain" (Isaiah 56:7). The same prophet who described the servant's humiliation and death also envisioned the inclusion of precisely the kind of person the eunuch is -- a foreigner and a eunuch. Philip's proclamation of Jesus from Isaiah would naturally have opened the door to these broader Isaianic themes of inclusion.

## Verses 36-39: The Eunuch's Baptism

"As they went along the road they came to some water; and the eunuch said, 'Look! Water! What prevents me from being baptized?'" (8:36).

The eunuch's question is spontaneous: "What prevents me?" (ti koloyei me baptisthenai -- "what hinders me from being baptized?"). The verb kolyo means "to hinder, to prevent, to forbid." The question implies that the eunuch understands baptism as part of the response to the gospel (Philip must have included baptism in his teaching about Jesus), and it also implies that the eunuch is aware of potential obstacles. As a eunuch, he has already experienced being prevented -- prevented from entering the assembly of the Lord (Deuteronomy 23:1), prevented from full participation in temple worship. His entire religious life has been defined by barriers. So when he asks "What prevents me?" the question carries the weight of a lifetime of exclusion.

The answer, implicitly, is: nothing. There is no barrier. The water is there. The message has been received. The response can be immediate. The Deuteronomic exclusion that barred eunuchs from the assembly is overridden by the Isaianic promise of inclusion. The prophet whose suffering servant Philip has just explained is the same prophet who said the eunuch would no longer be a "dry tree."

(Verse 37 is absent from the earliest manuscripts and is generally regarded as a later scribal addition. It reads: "And Philip said, 'If you believe with all your heart, you may.' And he answered and said, 'I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.'" The author's original text moves directly from verse 36 to verse 38.)

"And he ordered that the chariot stop; and they both went down into the water, Philip as well as the eunuch, and he baptized him" (8:38).

The eunuch commands the chariot to stop. He is a man of authority -- he orders (ekeleusen) the halt. The action is mutual: "they both went down into the water" (katebesan amphoteroi eis to hydoor, ho te Philippos kai ho eunouchos). Philip goes in with the eunuch. The baptism is by immersion -- they go "down into" (katebesan eis) the water. Philip baptizes him (ebaptisen auton).

The scene is remarkable for what it lacks. There is no apostolic authorization. There is no laying on of hands. There is no interval between belief and baptism. There is no waiting for Peter and John to arrive from Jerusalem. The delay that occurred in Samaria -- where the Spirit required apostolic mediation -- does not occur here. The eunuch believes, water is available, and baptism happens immediately. The author does not note a delay of the Holy Spirit as he did in Samaria (8:16). The simplicity of the scene is its message: the gospel has reached a person whom the old boundaries would have excluded, and the response is immediate and unobstructed.

"When they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord snatched Philip away; and the eunuch no longer saw him, but went on his way rejoicing" (8:39).

The verb herpasen (from harpazo, "to seize, to snatch away, to carry off") describes a sudden, forceful removal. Philip is physically relocated by the Spirit of the Lord. The word carries the sense of supernatural transportation -- the same verb is used in the LXX for Elijah's miraculous movements (1 Kings 18:12: "the Spirit of the LORD will carry you where I do not know"; 2 Kings 2:16). Philip's removal recalls Elijah's prophetic ministry and reinforces the divine direction of the entire encounter. The meeting was arranged by God (angel, Spirit), and it ends by God's direct intervention (the Spirit snatches Philip away).

"The eunuch no longer saw him" (ouk eiden auton ouketi ho eunouchos) -- Philip simply vanishes. There is no farewell, no ongoing mentoring, no plan for follow-up. The eunuch is left alone with the message, the baptism, and the joy.

"But went on his way rejoicing" (eporeueto gar ten hodon autou chairon) -- the word chairon ("rejoicing") is the last descriptor of the eunuch. He goes home to Ethiopia rejoicing. The word chara/chairo appears throughout Luke-Acts as the marker of genuine encounter with God's salvation: the angels announced "great joy" at Jesus's birth (Luke 2:10); the seventy returned "with joy" from their mission (Luke 10:17); the Samaritans experienced "much rejoicing" when Philip preached (Acts 8:8). The eunuch's joy is not superficial excitement but the response of a man who has received what he traveled hundreds of miles to find -- and more. He came to Jerusalem to worship from the margins; he leaves having been fully included. He came reading a prophet he could not understand; he leaves with the meaning unlocked. He came as a eunuch excluded from the assembly; he leaves as a baptized member of a community that has no such exclusion.

The contrast with Simon is pointed. Simon believed and was baptized (8:13), but his heart was "not right before God" (8:21), and he is last seen asking others to pray for him out of fear (8:24). The eunuch believes and is baptized, and he goes away rejoicing (8:39). Both responded to the gospel. Both were baptized. One was found to be in "the gall of bitterness and in the bondage of unrighteousness" (8:23); the other went away in joy. The difference is not in the external acts but in the condition of the heart. The author places these two stories side by side to show that believing and being baptized is the beginning of the journey, not its automatic completion. The heart must be genuinely turned, not merely externally compliant.

## Verse 40: Philip's Continuing Mission

"But Philip found himself at Azotus, and as he passed through he kept preaching the gospel to all the cities, until he came to Caesarea" (8:40).

Azotus is the Greek name for Ashdod, another of the old Philistine cities, located north of Gaza on the coastal plain. Philip has been transported from the desert road near Gaza to Azotus -- a distance of approximately 20 miles. From Azotus, he travels northward along the coast "preaching the gospel to all the cities" (euengelizeto tas poleis pasas) until he reaches Caesarea. The verb is imperfect (euengelizeto), indicating continuous, ongoing proclamation. Caesarea Maritima was a major Roman administrative center on the coast, built by Herod the Great. Philip will be found there much later in Acts (21:8: "we entered the house of Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven"), indicating that he settled in Caesarea and continued his ministry. The coastal cities between Azotus and Caesarea included Jamnia, Joppa, Apollonia, and others -- Philip preaches in every one.

The verse completes the picture of chapter 8's geographic expansion. The gospel has moved from Jerusalem (chapters 1-7) to Samaria (8:5-25) to the desert road toward Africa (8:26-39) to the Philistine coastal plain (8:40). The movement is centrifugal -- outward from Jerusalem in multiple directions simultaneously.

## Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **Acts 8 enacts what Stephen argued in Acts 7.** Stephen's speech demonstrated from Israel's history that God is not confined to Jerusalem, the temple, or the boundaries of ethnic Israel. God appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, accompanied Joseph in Egypt, spoke to Moses in the Midianite wilderness, and traveled with Israel in a portable tent. Acts 8 proves the point in action: God's Spirit works in Samaria, on a desert road, and through a man whom Deuteronomic law would have excluded from the assembly. Stephen's theological argument about God's freedom from geographic and institutional confinement becomes historical reality in the chapter that follows his death.

2. **The persecution that was meant to destroy the movement becomes the mechanism of its expansion.** Saul ravages the church in Jerusalem; the scattered believers preach wherever they go. The author presents this as consistent with the pattern throughout Acts: opposition produces the opposite of what its perpetrators intend. The Sanhedrin forbade the apostles to speak (4:18); they kept speaking (4:29-31). The Sanhedrin arrested and flogged the apostles (5:40); they went out "rejoicing that they had been considered worthy to suffer shame for His name" (5:41). Now the great persecution scatters the believers, and the scattering produces geographic expansion. The persecutors are unwitting agents of the very advance they seek to prevent.

3. **The chapter advances the gospel along the trajectory of Acts 1:8.** Jesus said, "You shall be My witnesses both in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and as far as the remotest part of the earth." Chapters 1-7 covered Jerusalem. Chapter 8 covers Judea and Samaria (8:1, 4-25) and takes an initial step toward "the remotest part of the earth" through the Ethiopian eunuch, who returns southward toward the edge of the known world (8:26-39). The author structures the chapter to match the concentric expansion Jesus announced.

4. **Philip's ministry demonstrates that the work of proclamation is not limited to the apostles.** Philip is one of the seven chosen for food distribution (6:5), not one of the Twelve. Yet he preaches, performs signs, baptizes, and crosses ethnic boundaries. The scattering of chapter 8 does the same for others: "those who had been scattered went through places preaching the word" (8:4). The author is showing that the Spirit empowers the entire community for witness, not just the designated leaders. The mission is carried forward by ordinary believers displaced by persecution.

5. **The Samaria episode establishes the principle that boundary-crossing expansion requires connection to the existing community.** The delay of the Spirit until Peter and John arrive from Jerusalem is not arbitrary. It ensures that the Samaritan believers are organically connected to the Jerusalem community through the apostolic witness. The gospel crosses the Jewish-Samaritan boundary, but the resulting community is one body, not two separate movements. This principle will recur when the gospel crosses into the Gentile world (chapters 10-11, 15).

6. **The Simon episode demonstrates that believing and being baptized does not automatically resolve the disposition of the heart.** Simon believed, was baptized, and attached himself to Philip -- yet Peter diagnosed him as having a heart "not right before God," being in "the gall of bitterness and in the bondage of unrighteousness." His desire to purchase the Spirit revealed that his old framework -- power as commodity, authority as something acquired for personal advantage -- had survived his external profession of faith. Peter's call to repentance after baptism shows that the transformation the gospel demands is ongoing and internal, not completed by the initial external response. Baptism initiates entry into the community; it does not guarantee that the heart has been fully turned.

7. **The Ethiopian eunuch episode demonstrates the removal of old covenant barriers in light of prophetic promises.** The eunuch was a foreigner, excluded by Deuteronomy 23:1 from the assembly of the Lord. Yet he was a worshiper of Israel's God who traveled vast distances to pray at the temple. He was reading the very prophet (Isaiah) who both described the suffering servant and promised that eunuchs and foreigners would be included in God's house (Isaiah 56:3-7). Philip's proclamation of Jesus from Isaiah 53 unlocked not only the identity of the suffering servant but also the Isaianic vision of radical inclusion. The eunuch's question -- "What prevents me from being baptized?" -- is answered by the entire prophetic trajectory: nothing prevents you. The barriers have been removed by the one whose humiliation and death Isaiah described.

8. **The contrast between Simon and the eunuch reveals the author's understanding of authentic versus inauthentic response to the gospel.** Both heard the message. Both believed. Both were baptized. But Simon's response was self-serving -- he wanted the authority to bestow the Spirit for his own prestige. The eunuch's response was receptive and humble -- he asked for guidance, received the teaching, requested baptism, and departed in joy. The author places these episodes in sequence to show that the outward acts of belief and baptism can accompany radically different heart conditions. The decisive factor is not the external response but the internal orientation: is the heart turned toward God's purposes or toward one's own?

9. **The chapter introduces the pattern of Spirit-directed mission that will govern the rest of Acts.** An angel directs Philip to the desert road (8:26). The Spirit tells Philip to approach the chariot (8:29). The Spirit snatches Philip away after the baptism (8:39). The mission is not self-directed; it is orchestrated by divine agents at every step. This pattern will intensify: the Spirit will direct Peter to Cornelius's house (10:19-20), will set apart Barnabas and Saul for mission (13:2), will redirect Paul from Asia to Macedonia (16:6-10). The Spirit is not merely the power that accompanies the message; the Spirit is the director who determines where the message goes and to whom it is sent.

10. **The chapter's two episodes together demonstrate that the gospel's reach is determined by God's initiative, not by human categories of inclusion and exclusion.** The Samaritans were half-Jews, religiously suspect, ethnically mixed, and historically hostile to Jerusalem. The Ethiopian eunuch was a foreigner, a eunuch, a court official of a distant queen, and physically excluded from the temple's inner courts. Neither fit the expected profile of those who would receive Israel's Messiah. Yet both receive the word, both are baptized, and both come under the Spirit's ministry. The author is dismantling the boundaries -- ethnic, geographic, physical, cultic -- that defined who could belong to God's people. The gospel crosses every line that human religion has drawn, not because human beings decided to be inclusive, but because the Spirit of God directed the messengers to the people the old system excluded.
