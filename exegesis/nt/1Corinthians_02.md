# 1 Corinthians 2 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 1):** Paul opened this letter by identifying himself as "called as an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God" (1:1) and addressing "the assembly of God that is in Corinth, those sanctified in Christ Jesus, called as saints" (1:2). After thanksgiving (1:4-9), he launched immediately into the presenting crisis: factionalism. "It has been reported to me by Chloe's people that there are quarrels among you" (1:11). The quarrels take a specific form: "each one of you says, 'I am of Paul,' or 'I am of Apollos,' or 'I am of Cephas,' or 'I am of Christ'" (1:12). The community has fractured along the lines of allegiance to different teachers. Paul's response is sharp: "Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptized in the name of Paul?" (1:13). The rhetorical questions expose the absurdity of the factions by pointing to the one event that constitutes their community -- the crucifixion of Christ, not the ministry of any teacher.

Paul then made a pivotal turn. He was sent "not to baptize but to proclaim the gospel, and not with wisdom of speech, lest the cross of Christ be emptied" (1:17). The phrase sophia logou ("wisdom of speech" or "eloquent wisdom") introduces the concept that dominates the rest of the chapter and all of chapter 2: sophia -- wisdom. Paul's claim is that a certain kind of wisdom empties the cross. The word kenoo ("to empty, to render void, to make of no effect") is strong: the cross itself loses its power when the message is delivered through impressive human rhetoric.

From 1:18 onward, Paul developed the antithesis between God's wisdom and the world's. "The word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1:18). He quoted Isaiah 29:14 (LXX): "I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the discernment of the discerning I will set aside." He asked three rhetorical questions: "Where is the wise one? Where is the scribe? Where is the debater of this age?" (1:20) -- three categories of recognized wisdom-bearers (Greek philosopher, Jewish Torah scholar, public disputant), all challenged. God made the world's wisdom foolish (1:20). Since the world through its wisdom did not know God, it pleased God through the foolishness of the proclaimed message to save those who believe (1:21). Jews demand signs, Greeks seek wisdom, "but we proclaim Christ crucified -- to Jews a stumbling block and to Gentiles foolishness, but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1:23-24).

The chapter closed with the Corinthians' own example. Paul reminded them of their origins: "not many of you were wise according to the flesh, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth" (1:26). God chose the foolish things to shame the wise, the weak things to shame the strong, the low and despised things and the things that are not, to bring to nothing the things that are -- "so that no flesh may boast before God" (1:28-29). Christ himself became for them "wisdom from God -- righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1:30). The chapter concluded with a quotation from Jeremiah 9:24 (LXX 9:23): "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1:31). The entire argument was designed to undercut the basis of the Corinthians' factionalism: they were evaluating teachers by worldly wisdom and rhetorical skill, the very criteria God has overturned through the cross.

**What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 3):** Paul opens chapter 3 by revealing the point of everything he has argued in chapter 2. He addresses the Corinthians directly: "And I, brothers, could not address you as spiritual people (pneumatikois) but as fleshly people (sarkinois), as infants in Christ (nepiois en Christo)" (3:1). The vocabulary is precise and pointed. The word pneumatikois ("spiritual") is the same word that dominated 2:13-15 -- the pneumatikos person who discerns the things of the Spirit. The word sarkinois ("made of flesh, fleshly") describes someone whose conduct is governed by the flesh rather than the Spirit. The word nepiois ("infants, babes") indicates immaturity rather than wickedness. Paul is saying: despite everything I taught you about the Spirit's wisdom, you are behaving as though you had never received the Spirit at all.

The evidence of their immaturity is stated: "I gave you milk to drink, not solid food, for you were not yet ready for it. And even now you are not yet ready, for you are still fleshly" (3:2-3a). The milk/solid food distinction maps onto the wisdom distinction of chapter 2: the "wisdom spoken among the mature" (2:6) is the solid food; the basic proclamation of Christ crucified (2:2) is the milk. The Corinthians received the milk but have not matured to handle solid food -- not because Paul withheld it, but because their behavior demonstrates they have not grown.

The proof of their fleshliness is precisely the factionalism from 1:12: "For when one says, 'I am of Paul,' and another, 'I am of Apollos,' are you not merely human?" (3:4). The word anthropoi ("human beings, merely human") is Paul's judgment on their behavior. They are acting kata anthropon ("according to a human standard") rather than kata pneuma ("according to the Spirit"). The very act of evaluating teachers by worldly criteria and attaching themselves to human leaders demonstrates that they are operating from natural, fleshly reasoning rather than spiritual discernment.

Paul then corrected their understanding of his role and Apollos's. "What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you believed, as the Lord assigned to each" (3:5). The word diakonoi ("servants, attendants, those who serve") strips the teachers of the status the Corinthians have been attributing to them. Paul planted, Apollos watered, "but God gave the growth" (3:6). Neither the planter nor the waterer is anything; only God who gives the growth matters (3:7). The agricultural metaphor dissolves the factions: the field is God's, the workers are God's agents, and the Corinthians are "God's field, God's building" (3:9). Paul then shifted to a construction metaphor: he laid a foundation -- "Jesus Christ" -- and others build on it (3:10-11). The quality of each builder's work will be tested by fire on "the day" (3:13). The warning applies to the teachers: some may build with gold, silver, and precious stones; others with wood, hay, and straw. The fire will reveal the difference. A builder whose work survives will receive a reward; a builder whose work burns up will "suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire" (3:15). The chapter then issues a solemn warning: "If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple" (3:17).

**How chapter 2 connects these:** Chapter 1 identified the problem (factionalism rooted in worldly wisdom-evaluation) and stated the principle (God overturns human wisdom through the cross). Chapter 3 will apply the principle directly to the Corinthians' situation, naming their behavior as fleshly and infantile. Chapter 2 is the bridge: it explains how God's wisdom actually works, how it is communicated, and why the Corinthians' criteria for evaluating teachers are fundamentally wrong.

The chapter accomplishes this in two movements. In the first (2:1-5), Paul uses his own arrival in Corinth as a case study: he deliberately did not come with impressive rhetoric, so that their faith would rest on God's power, not on human wisdom. This is not merely autobiographical humility; it is an argument against the very criteria the Corinthians are using to rank their teachers. If Paul intentionally refused to deploy rhetorical skill, then evaluating teachers by rhetorical skill misses the entire point.

In the second movement (2:6-16), Paul introduces a category the Corinthians may not have expected: there is a genuine wisdom, spoken among the mature. But this wisdom is not the world's wisdom -- it is God's hidden wisdom, revealed by the Spirit, inaccessible to natural human reasoning. The person who operates from natural faculties alone (the psychikos anthropos) cannot discern spiritual realities. Only the person who has received the Spirit (the pneumatikos) can evaluate and discern them. The chapter climaxes with the declaration that "we have the mind of Christ" (2:16) -- the community that has received the Spirit possesses the capacity to evaluate all things from Christ's perspective. This sets up chapter 3's devastating application: if you have the mind of Christ, why are you evaluating things by the world's criteria? Your factionalism proves that you are not thinking with the mind you have been given.

## Verses 1-5: Paul's Manner of Coming to Corinth

"And I, when I came to you, brothers, did not come proclaiming the testimony of God to you with lofty speech or wisdom" (2:1).

The phrase kago elthon pros hymas ("and I, when I came to you") is personal and emphatic. The kago ("and I" or "I also") connects Paul's own practice to the principle established in chapter 1. Paul was not merely articulating a theory about the irrelevance of worldly wisdom; he enacted it in his own ministry among the Corinthians. The words kath' hyperochen logou e sophias ("with superiority of speech or wisdom") describe precisely what Paul avoided. The noun hyperoche ("superiority, preeminence, excess, surpassing quality") indicates speech that rises above, that towers over the audience through its rhetorical brilliance. The combination of logos ("speech, eloquence, rhetorical expression") and sophia ("wisdom") captures both the form (impressive delivery) and content (philosophical depth) of what the Greek world valued in its orators and teachers.

The word to martyrion tou theou ("the testimony of God") identifies what Paul did proclaim. Some manuscripts read mysterion ("mystery") instead of martyrion ("testimony"), and this variant is significant because mysterionis the word Paul will use in verse 7. Either reading works within the argument: Paul came to deliver God's witness (martyrion) or God's mystery (mysterion), not to showcase his own rhetorical abilities. The genitive tou theou ("of God") is either subjective (the testimony God gives) or objective (the testimony about God). In either case, the message belongs to God, not to Paul.

The Corinthian context makes Paul's statement pointed. Corinth was a city saturated with competitive rhetoric. The Greco-Roman world valued rhetorical prowess as one of the highest social goods. Traveling orators (sophists) drew large crowds, charged fees, competed for students, and built reputations through their speaking skill. The Corinthians, shaped by this culture, were naturally inclined to evaluate teachers the way they evaluated orators: by the impressiveness of their speech. Paul's statement that he deliberately refused this approach strikes at the root of their value system.

"For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (2:2).

The verb ekrina ("I decided, I judged, I resolved") indicates a deliberate decision, not an inability. Paul was not incapable of rhetorical sophistication -- his letters themselves demonstrate considerable argumentative skill, familiarity with Greek literary conventions, and complex reasoning. His avoidance of "lofty speech" was a choice, made for theological reasons. The phrase ou gar ekrina ti eidenai en hymin ("for I decided not to know anything among you") uses the infinitive eidenai ("to know") with the emphatic negation: Paul resolved to act as though he knew nothing -- no philosophical system, no rhetorical technique, no competing wisdom tradition -- except one thing.

The exception: ei me Iesoun Christon kai touton estauromenon -- "except Jesus Christ and him crucified." The word order places Iesoun Christon ("Jesus Christ") first and then intensifies: kai touton estauromenon -- "and this one crucified." The perfect passive participle estauromenon ("having been crucified") describes a past event with continuing results. Jesus was crucified, and he remains the crucified one. The cross is not a stage Christ passed through on the way to something more palatable; the crucifixion is the permanent identity of the one Paul proclaims. This is the moria ("foolishness") of 1:23 -- Christ crucified, a scandal to Jews and absurdity to Greeks. Paul's deliberate limitation of his message to the crucified Messiah is itself an enactment of the principle of 1:17-25: God's power works through what the world considers foolish and weak.

"And I was with you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling" (2:3).

Paul describes his own condition among the Corinthians with three terms. The word astheneia ("weakness, frailty, lack of strength") is the opposite of the dynamis ("power") the Corinthians valued. The word phobos ("fear") and tromos ("trembling") together form a pair that appears in the LXX for the human response to overwhelming circumstances: in Exodus 15:16, "terror and dread fall upon them" (phobos kai tromos); in Psalm 55:5 (LXX 54:6), "fear and trembling come upon me"; in Isaiah 19:16, "Judah shall become a terror to Egypt." The combination phobos kai tromos in Paul's own usage appears in Philippians 2:12 ("work out your salvation with fear and trembling") and 2 Corinthians 7:15 (Titus was received "with fear and trembling"). It describes the posture of a person who is acutely aware of the gravity of the task and their own inadequacy for it.

Paul's arrival in Corinth, as recorded in Acts 18:1-4, confirms this picture. He arrived alone, after the relative failure in Athens, and supported himself by manual labor as a tentmaker. He was rejoined by Silas and Timothy only later (Acts 18:5), and it was only after a divine vision promising "I have many people in this city" (Acts 18:10) that he stayed for an extended period. The weakness, fear, and trembling were not rhetorical exaggeration; they described the actual condition of a man who came to a cosmopolitan commercial city without companions, without financial backing, and without the social status that Corinthian culture respected.

"And my speech and my message were not in plausible words of wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power" (2:4).

Paul distinguishes two modes of persuasion. The first -- en peithois sophias logois ("in persuasive words of wisdom") -- describes the orator's stock in trade. The adjective peithos ("persuasive, plausible, enticing") comes from peitho ("to persuade") and describes speech that wins assent through rhetorical technique. This is how the sophists operated: they constructed arguments designed to be irresistible through their verbal artistry, regardless of whether the content was true. Paul says his logos ("speech") and his kerygma ("proclamation, message") were not in this mode.

The second mode: en apodeixei pneumatos kai dynameoos -- "in demonstration of the Spirit and of power." The word apodeixis ("demonstration, proof, showing forth") is itself a rhetorical term -- it refers to proof or evidence that establishes a conclusion. But the proof Paul offers is not verbal brilliance; it is pneumatos ("of the Spirit") and dynameoos ("of power"). The genitive construction could be objective (proof consisting of the Spirit and power -- the Spirit's activity and God's power were the evidence) or subjective (proof that the Spirit and power produce). In either case, the demonstration was not Paul's doing; it was the Spirit's activity manifesting God's power. Paul does not specify what form this demonstration took -- it may refer to the Corinthians' own experience of the Spirit (which chapters 12-14 will describe in detail: tongues, prophecy, healings, and other manifestations), to transformed lives, to the very existence of the community as evidence that God was at work despite the absence of impressive rhetoric.

"So that your faith might not rest on the wisdom of men but on the power of God" (2:5).

The purpose clause (hina) reveals the telos of Paul's deliberate strategy. The word pistis ("faith, trust, reliance") is what Paul is concerned to protect. Faith has a ground -- something it stands on, something it rests in. Paul's concern is that the Corinthians' faith should rest (e, "might be") on the right foundation. If Paul had come with impressive wisdom, their faith might have been grounded in sophia anthropon ("wisdom of human beings") -- they would have believed because they were impressed by Paul's speaking, and their faith would collapse whenever a more impressive speaker came along. This is, in fact, exactly what has happened: Apollos arrived, and he was "eloquent" (logios) and "mighty in the Scriptures" (dynatos en tais graphais, Acts 18:24). Some Corinthians now prefer Apollos to Paul, precisely because Apollos is the more impressive speaker. Their faith has been resting, at least partially, on human wisdom rather than on divine power.

Paul's goal was that their faith rest on dynamei theou ("the power of God"). Power, not wisdom, is the foundation. This echoes 1:18: the word of the cross "is the power of God" (dynamis theou estin). The power of God and the wisdom of God converge in the crucified Christ (1:24), but Paul insists on using the word dynamis because the Corinthians associate sophia with rhetorical skill. The power of God is what changes people, what forms communities, what overcomes sin and death. Rhetorical skill does none of these things.

## Verses 6-9: God's Wisdom in a Mystery

"Yet among the mature we do speak wisdom, though it is not a wisdom of this age or of the rulers of this age, who are being brought to nothing" (2:6).

The word de ("yet, however, but") introduces an important qualification. Paul has not said there is no wisdom at all; he has said he did not come with the world's wisdom. Now he clarifies: sophian de laloumen en tois teleiois -- "we do speak wisdom among the mature." The verb laleo ("to speak, to talk, to utter") is straightforward; the claim is direct. Paul does speak wisdom. The location of this wisdom-speaking is en tois teleiois -- "among the mature."

The word teleiois ("mature, complete, fully developed, perfect") comes from telos ("end, goal, completion"). In the LXX, teleios describes what is complete or whole -- an animal without blemish fit for sacrifice (Exodus 12:5, LXX: probaton teleion), a heart wholly devoted to God (1 Kings 8:61, LXX: kardia teleia). In Paul's own usage, the word describes the person who has progressed beyond initial stages of growth. In Philippians 3:15, Paul writes: "Let those of us who are mature (teleioi) think this way" -- where the "mature" are those who press forward toward the goal rather than resting on past attainments. In 1 Corinthians 14:20, Paul will urge: "in your thinking be mature (teleioi)" -- do not be children (paidia) in understanding. The teleioi are not an elite spiritual class within the community; they are believers who have grown past infancy into the capacity for deeper understanding. The contrast with nepioi ("infants") that Paul will use in 3:1 is essential: the Corinthians should be teleioi but are behaving as nepioi. Paul speaks wisdom among those who are ready for it, but the Corinthians are not ready.

The wisdom Paul speaks is qualified by two negations. First: ou tou aionos toutou -- "not of this age." The phrase ho aion houtos ("this age") in Jewish apocalyptic thought designates the present era of human history -- the age under the power of sin, death, and rebellion against God -- as opposed to ho aion ho mellon ("the age to come"), the age of God's full reign. Paul used the same phrase in 1:20: "Where is the debater of this age?" The wisdom of "this age" is the wisdom that operates within the horizons of the present world order, ignorant of God's purposes, unable to perceive what God is doing.

Second: oude ton archonton tou aionos toutou ton katargoumenon -- "nor of the rulers of this age, who are being brought to nothing." The word archontes ("rulers, authorities, those who exercise power") requires careful attention. In the LXX, archontes translates the Hebrew sarim and describes human rulers: the princes of Egypt (Genesis 12:15), the rulers of Israel (Exodus 16:22), the officials of the nations (Psalm 2:2). In Acts, the word describes human authorities who acted against Jesus: in Acts 3:17, Peter says to the people of Jerusalem, "I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers (archontes)"; in Acts 4:26, the apostles quote Psalm 2: "the rulers (archontes) gathered together against the Lord and against his Anointed"; and in Acts 13:27, Paul tells the synagogue in Pisidian Antioch that "the inhabitants of Jerusalem and their rulers (archontes), because they did not recognize him nor understand the utterances of the prophets, fulfilled them by condemning him." In all these passages, the archontes who acted against Jesus are the human political and religious authorities -- the Sanhedrin, Pilate, Herod.

Paul will say in verse 8 that these archontes are the ones who "crucified the Lord of glory." Since crucifixion was carried out by identifiable human authorities acting within the political structures of the Roman province of Judea, the most immediate referent for "the rulers of this age" is the human powers who condemned and executed Jesus -- the chief priests, the Sanhedrin, and the Roman governor. The qualifier "of this age" identifies them with the present world order and its wisdom, and the participial phrase ton katargoumenon ("who are being brought to nothing") uses the present passive participle of katargeo ("to render inoperative, to abolish, to bring to nothing"). The same verb appears in 1:28, where God chose the things that are not "to bring to nothing the things that are" (katargese). The rulers of this age, for all their present power, are in the process of being abolished. Their authority is temporary; their wisdom is passing away. Paul uses the same verb in 15:24-26 for the final defeat of "every rule (arche) and every authority (exousia) and power (dynamis)" when Christ hands over the kingdom to the Father. The present tense of the participle indicates that the abolishing is already underway -- it began with the cross and will be completed at the consummation.

"But we speak God's wisdom in a mystery, the hidden wisdom that God decreed before the ages for our glory" (2:7).

The conjunction alla ("but, rather") introduces the positive counterpart. What Paul does speak is theou sophian en mysterio -- "God's wisdom in a mystery." The word mysterion ("mystery, secret, hidden thing") does not mean "mysterious" in the sense of puzzling or incomprehensible. In Paul's usage, a mysterion is something that was hidden in God's plan but has now been revealed. In Romans 16:25-26, Paul speaks of "the mystery that was kept secret for long ages but has now been disclosed and through the prophetic writings has been made known to all nations." In 1 Corinthians 4:1, Paul and the other apostles are "stewards of the mysteries of God" -- their role is to dispense what was hidden but is now entrusted to them. The mystery is not esoteric knowledge for an elite; it is God's plan that was concealed within the age's wisdom structures and has now been uncovered through the cross and the Spirit.

The mystery is further described as ten apokekrymmenen ("the hidden one") -- a perfect passive participle of apokrypto ("to hide, to conceal, to keep secret"). The hiddenness was not accidental; God concealed this wisdom. The verb proorisen ("decreed beforehand, predestined") is the same verb Paul uses in Romans 8:29-30 for God's predetermining purpose. The phrase pro ton aionon ("before the ages") places God's decree before the succession of world-ages, before history itself. This wisdom is not a response to circumstances; it is the eternal plan.

The goal of the decree is eis doxan hemon -- "for our glory." The word doxa ("glory, splendor, honor, radiance") is the consummation that Paul described in Romans 8:17-18 -- the glory to be revealed, the glory that creation itself awaits. God's hidden wisdom, decreed before the ages, has as its goal the glorification of those who receive it. The pronoun hemon ("our") includes Paul and the Corinthians -- this glory belongs to the community that has received the mystery.

"None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (2:8).

The verb egnoken (perfect active of ginosko, "to know, to understand, to recognize") with the negation oudeis ("none, not one") is absolute: not a single one of the rulers of this age grasped God's hidden wisdom. Their ignorance was comprehensive. The conditional clause ei gar egnosan ("for if they had known") states a contrary-to-fact condition: they did not know, and the evidence of their ignorance is stated in the result clause -- ouk an ton kyrion tes doxes estaurosan ("they would not have crucified the Lord of glory").

The title ton kyrion tes doxes ("the Lord of glory") is extraordinary. In the LXX, the phrase appears in the Psalms. Psalm 24:7-10 (LXX 23:7-10) asks: "Who is this King of glory (basileus tes doxes)?" and answers: "The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory." The divine title "King of glory" is transferred to the crucified Jesus, but Paul uses kyrios ("Lord") rather than basileus ("King"). The identification is staggering: the one the rulers crucified was the Lord whose glory fills the temple, the Lord whom the heavens proclaim. Their ignorance was not merely a failure to recognize a teacher or prophet; they failed to recognize the Lord of glory himself. The crucifixion, from the perspective of this age's wisdom, was a political execution of a troublesome provincial subject; from the perspective of God's hidden wisdom, it was the murder of the Lord of glory -- an act so catastrophically misguided that it could only have occurred through ignorance of who the victim was.

The irony Paul constructs is devastating. The very act by which the rulers demonstrated their power -- executing a condemned man by crucifixion, the most shameful death Rome could inflict -- was simultaneously the act by which God accomplished his hidden plan. The rulers' supreme exercise of worldly power and worldly wisdom (they judged Jesus to be a threat and eliminated him) was the instrument of their own defeat. The cross, which this age's wisdom interprets as evidence of Jesus' weakness and failure, is in God's wisdom the mechanism of redemption and the revelation of divine glory. The rulers' ignorance did not frustrate God's plan; it unwittingly served it.

"But, as it is written, 'What no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor the heart of man imagined, what God has prepared for those who love him'" (2:9).

The introductory formula kathos gegraptai ("as it is written") signals a scriptural citation, but the quotation that follows does not correspond exactly to any single passage in the LXX. The closest parallel is Isaiah 64:4 (LXX 64:3): "From of old we have not heard, nor have our eyes seen a god besides you, and your works which you will do for those who wait for you" (apo tou aionos ouk ekousamen oude hoi ophthalmoi hemon eidon theon plen sou kai ta erga sou ha poieseis tois hypomenousin eleon). Paul's version differs significantly: he restructures the quotation around three organs of perception -- eye (ophthalmos), ear (ous), and heart (kardia) -- and replaces "a god besides you and your works" with "what God has prepared."

The triple negation -- eye has not seen, ear has not heard, heart has not conceived -- represents the totality of human cognitive capacity. Sight, hearing, and the heart's ability to conceive or imagine exhaust the ways a human being can acquire knowledge. What God has prepared exceeds all three. The word hetoimasen ("has prepared, has made ready") is the aorist of hetoimazo, used in the LXX for God's preparation of good things: Psalm 31:19 (LXX 30:20), "How abundant is your goodness, which you have stored up for those who fear you, which you have prepared for those who take refuge in you." The beneficiaries are tois agaposin auton ("those who love him") -- the same phrase that appeared in Romans 8:28 to describe those for whom "all things work together for good."

Paul's adaptation of the Isaiah text serves his argument precisely. The wisdom of this age relies on what can be seen, heard, and conceived by human intelligence. God's wisdom consists of what cannot be accessed by any of these means. The rulers of this age did not understand God's plan because it was beyond the reach of their faculties -- not because they lacked intelligence, but because the plan was of a kind that no human faculty could reach. The quotation creates a problem, however: if this wisdom is beyond all human perception, how can anyone know it? Paul answers this in the next verse.

## Verses 10-13: The Spirit as the Revealer of God's Depths

"These things God has revealed to us through the Spirit. For the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God" (2:10).

The word hemin ("to us") is emphatic by position and pivotal to the argument. The things that no eye saw, no ear heard, and no heart conceived -- these inaccessible realities -- God apekalypsen ("revealed, uncovered, disclosed") to us. The verb apokalypto ("to reveal, to uncover") is the opposite of apokrypto ("to hide, to conceal"), the verb used in verse 7 for the hidden wisdom. What was hidden has been uncovered. The agent of revelation is dia tou pneumatos -- "through the Spirit." The Spirit is the means by which the gap between divine hiddenness and human perception is bridged. What human faculties cannot reach, the Spirit delivers.

The reason the Spirit can do this is stated: to gar pneuma panta erauna, kai ta bathe tou theou -- "for the Spirit searches all things, even the depths of God." The verb erauna (or ereunao, "to search, to examine, to investigate thoroughly") is the same verb used in the LXX for God's searching of human hearts (Psalm 139:1, LXX 138:1: ereunas me; Jeremiah 17:10: etazon kardias kai dokimazon nephrous). In Romans 8:27, it was God who searched hearts and knew the mind of the Spirit; here the Spirit searches the depths of God. The relationship is reciprocal: God knows the Spirit's mind (Romans 8:27), and the Spirit knows God's depths (1 Corinthians 2:10). The word bathe ("depths, deep things") indicates the most interior, most hidden aspects of God's being and purpose. The Spirit has access to what is deepest and most concealed in God.

The implications for the Corinthians' factionalism are significant, though Paul will not draw them explicitly until chapter 3. If the only access to God's wisdom is through the Spirit, then no human teacher -- however eloquent -- can serve as the source of that wisdom. Paul, Apollos, and Cephas are all channels at best; the Spirit is the source. Evaluating teachers by their rhetorical skill is therefore evaluating the wrong thing.

"For who knows a person's thoughts except the spirit of that person, which is in him? So also no one comprehends the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God" (2:11).

Paul offers an analogy from common experience. The word ta tou anthropou ("the things of the person, the person's thoughts and inner life") describes the interior world of a human being -- thoughts, intentions, feelings, deliberations. The question tis gar oiden anthropon ta tou anthropou ("for who among human beings knows the things of a human being?") is rhetorical. The answer is ei me to pneuma tou anthropou to en auto -- "except the spirit of the person that is in him." Only the spirit (pneuma) that indwells a person has access to that person's inner world. Another person may observe behavior and hear words, but the thoughts themselves remain private, accessible only to the indwelling spirit.

The analogy is then applied: houtos kai ta tou theou oudeis egnoken ei me to pneuma tou theou -- "so also, no one has known the things of God except the Spirit of God." The parallel is precise. As the human spirit alone has access to the person's interior, so the divine Spirit alone has access to God's interior. No creature, no angel, no ruler of this age, no human philosopher can know ta tou theou ("the things of God") -- God's purposes, God's plan, God's hidden wisdom -- unless the Spirit of God grants that knowledge.

The analogy also establishes an important point about the Spirit's relationship to God. The human spirit is not something external to the person; it is the person's own interior reality. By analogy, the Spirit of God is not an external agent reporting about God from the outside; the Spirit is God's own interior reality -- the aspect of God that knows God from within. This is why the Spirit can search "the depths of God" (v. 10): the Spirit is within those depths.

"Now we have received not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God, that we might understand the things freely given us by God" (2:12).

The pronoun hemeis ("we") is emphatic: we -- the apostles and the believing community -- have received (elabomen, aorist of lambano) not to pneuma tou kosmou ("the spirit of the world") but to pneuma to ek tou theou ("the Spirit that is from God"). The phrase "the spirit of the world" is not further defined, but its meaning is clear from context: it is the governing principle of the kosmos in its present configuration -- the mindset that evaluates by human wisdom, that judges by appearances, that operates within the horizons of "this age." The Corinthians, in their factionalism, have been operating by the spirit of the world even though they have received the Spirit from God.

The purpose of receiving the Spirit is stated: hina eidomen ta hypo tou theou charisthenta hemin -- "so that we might know the things graciously given to us by God." The verb eidomen (aorist subjunctive of oida, "to know, to perceive, to understand") describes knowing in the sense of comprehension and perception. The verb charisthenta (aorist passive participle of charizomai, "to give freely, to grant as a gift, to bestow graciously") is built on charis ("grace"). The things God has given are grace-gifts -- freely bestowed, not earned or purchased. The Spirit's role is to make the recipients aware of what they have already been given. The problem is not that God has withheld his gifts but that the recipients may not perceive what they have received. The Spirit opens their eyes to the realities that are already theirs in Christ -- the righteousness, sanctification, and redemption of 1:30, the glory of 2:7, the inheritance that belongs to God's people.

"And we speak of these things in words not taught by human wisdom but taught by the Spirit, interpreting spiritual things to spiritual people" (2:13).

The clause ha kai laloumen ouk en didaktois anthropines sophias logois ("which things we also speak, not in words taught by human wisdom") returns to the contrast between human wisdom and divine revelation, now applied specifically to the mode of communication. The adjective didaktois ("taught, instructed, learned") is passive: the words Paul uses are not words he learned from a human wisdom tradition. The phrase all' en didaktois pneumatos ("but in words taught by the Spirit") identifies the Spirit as the teacher. The Spirit not only reveals the content (v. 10) but provides the vocabulary -- the words and categories adequate to express what has been revealed.

The final phrase pneumatikois pneumatika synkrinontes is difficult and has been read multiple ways. The verb synkrino can mean "to compare, to combine, to interpret, to explain." The word pneumatikois can be either neuter ("spiritual things") or masculine ("spiritual people"), and pneumatika is neuter plural ("spiritual things"). The possible readings include: (a) "combining spiritual things with spiritual words" -- expressing spiritual realities in Spirit-taught language; (b) "interpreting spiritual things to spiritual people" -- communicating spiritual realities to those who are spiritually equipped to receive them; (c) "comparing spiritual things with spiritual things" -- interpreting one spiritual reality by reference to another. All three readings are consistent with the argument. Paul is saying that the entire communication chain -- the content, the words, and the audience -- must be spiritual. The message comes from the Spirit, is expressed in Spirit-taught language, and is received by Spirit-equipped people.

## Verses 14-16: The Natural Person and the Spiritual Person

"The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit of God, for they are folly to him, and he is not able to understand them because they are spiritually discerned" (2:14).

The word psychikos ("natural, soulish, pertaining to the soul/psyche") is the critical term. It derives from psyche ("soul, life, natural life"), which designates the animating principle of natural human existence. A psychikos anthropos is a person operating from the soul -- from natural human faculties of reason, perception, and judgment -- without the illumination of God's Spirit. The term is not the same as sarkikos or sarkinos ("fleshly"), which Paul will use in 3:1-3 for the Corinthians. The psychikos person is not necessarily immoral; they may be highly intelligent, well-educated, culturally refined, and ethically admirable. But they are operating without the Spirit and therefore lack the capacity to perceive what the Spirit reveals.

In the LXX, psyche translates the Hebrew nephesh -- the life-breath, the animate self. The psychikos person is the nephesh-person, the person whose resources for knowledge are limited to what the natural self can achieve. This is the person described in verse 9 for whom the things God has prepared are beyond what eye has seen, ear has heard, or heart has imagined. The psychikos person's faculties are intact but insufficient for the task of perceiving God's hidden wisdom.

The psychikos person ou dechetai ta tou pneumatos tou theou ("does not accept the things of the Spirit of God"). The verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to accept, to take in") indicates more than intellectual comprehension; it involves a willing reception, an embrace of what is offered. The psychikos person does not merely fail to understand; he or she does not welcome, does not receive, does not take in what the Spirit offers. The reason is stated: moria gar auto estin -- "for they are foolishness (moria) to him." The word moria is the same word from 1:18 (the word of the cross is moria to those who are perishing) and 1:23 (the crucified Christ is moria to Gentiles). From the perspective of natural human wisdom, the Spirit's things look foolish -- they do not conform to the categories and standards by which the natural mind evaluates truth.

The further explanation: kai ou dynatai gnonai ("and he is not able to know/understand"). The verb dynatai ("is able") with the negation ou indicates incapacity, not mere unwillingness. The psychikos person cannot understand, because the things of the Spirit hoti pneumatikos anakrinetai ("are discerned spiritually" or "because they are examined by spiritual means"). The verb anakrino ("to examine, to investigate, to judge, to discern, to evaluate") is a legal and forensic term -- it describes the process of examination that leads to a judgment. The adverb pneumatikos ("spiritually, by means of the Spirit") specifies the instrument required. Spiritual realities require spiritual examination. They cannot be evaluated by the tools available to the natural person. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is a claim about epistemological domains. As one cannot taste colors or hear textures -- the faculty does not match the object -- so one cannot discern spiritual realities with natural faculties alone. A different capacity is required, and that capacity comes only from the Spirit.

"The spiritual person judges all things, but is himself judged by no one" (2:15).

The pneumatikos ("spiritual person") is the counterpart of the psychikos. The pneumatikos is the person in whom the Spirit of God dwells (cf. Romans 8:9-11), who has received "the Spirit who is from God" (v. 12), and who therefore possesses the faculty necessary for spiritual discernment. This person anakrineipanta -- "examines/judges all things." The verb anakrino is the same as in verse 14, but now its subject is the spiritual person rather than the spiritual method. The spiritual person has the capacity to evaluate everything -- both the spiritual realities that the psychikos cannot access and the natural realities that are available to all. The pneumatikos sees more, not less, than the psychikos. The spiritual person can evaluate the natural world and the spiritual world; the natural person can evaluate only the natural world.

The second clause: autos de hyp' oudenos anakrinetai -- "but he himself is examined/judged by no one." The spiritual person is not subject to evaluation by the psychikos. This does not mean the spiritual person is above criticism or accountability (Paul will spend considerable portions of this letter correcting the Corinthians, who are supposed to be pneumatikoi). It means that the psychikos person lacks the capacity to evaluate the spiritual person's judgments. The natural person, lacking the Spirit, cannot assess what the spiritual person perceives. The judgment of the spiritual person on spiritual matters stands because the tools required to overturn that judgment are not available to the one who lacks the Spirit.

This verse carries an implicit warning to the Corinthians, though Paul will not make it explicit until chapter 3. The Corinthians have been evaluating Paul and Apollos and Cephas by natural criteria -- rhetorical skill, social prestige, wisdom-tradition credentials. They have been acting as psychikoi, using psychikos categories to judge pneumatikoi. Paul's argument here says: that evaluation is invalid. You are using the wrong instrument. You cannot assess what is happening in the community of the Spirit by the standards of the world's wisdom. The factionalism itself is evidence that the Corinthians are not exercising the spiritual discernment they should possess.

"'For who has known the mind of the Lord so as to instruct him?' But we have the mind of Christ" (2:16).

The quotation is from Isaiah 40:13 (LXX): tis egno noun kyriou, kai tis autou symboulos egeneto, hos symbibai auton -- "Who has known the mind of the Lord? And who has been his counselor, who will instruct him?" In the original context of Isaiah 40, the passage is part of the great comfort oracle that opens Second Isaiah. The prophet declares God's incomparable greatness: no one has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand or marked off the heavens (40:12); no one directed the Spirit of the LORD or served as his counselor (40:13); the nations are like a drop from a bucket (40:15). The rhetorical question expects the answer "no one" -- no human being has ever known the mind of the LORD well enough to instruct him.

Paul quotes this text and then makes a startling claim: hemeis de noun Christou echomen -- "but we have the mind of Christ." The word nous ("mind, understanding, intellect, way of thinking") is what Isaiah said no one could know when it belonged to kyrios (the LORD). Paul identifies Christos with the kyrios of Isaiah 40:13 -- the Lord whose mind is incomprehensible to unaided human intelligence. And then he says: we have that mind. The pronoun hemeis ("we") is emphatic by position, standing in contrast to the implied "no one" of the Isaiah quotation. No one has known the mind of the Lord -- but we have it.

How? The entire preceding argument (vv. 10-15) provides the answer. The Spirit, who searches the depths of God (v. 10), who alone knows the things of God (v. 11), has been given to the believing community (v. 12) precisely so that they might know what God has freely given (v. 12). The "mind of Christ" is not a separate endowment; it is the consequence of having received the Spirit who knows God's interior life and communicates it to those who receive him. To have the Spirit is to have access to the mind of Christ. The nous Christou is the faculty of spiritual discernment -- the capacity to evaluate all things (v. 15) from the perspective of God's hidden wisdom rather than from the perspective of this age.

The substitution of "Christ" for "Lord" in the Isaiah quotation is theologically loaded. The kyrios of Isaiah 40 is YHWH, Israel's God. Paul applies this title to Christ without comment or apology. Christ is the kyrios whose mind no one can know -- except by the Spirit. This identification, made in passing, reveals how thoroughly Paul has identified Jesus with the God of Israel's scriptures.

The verse also completes the argument against the Corinthians' factionalism, though the application will come in chapter 3. If the community has the mind of Christ, they possess the capacity to evaluate everything -- teachers, messages, practices, disputes -- from Christ's perspective. The problem is not that they lack the capacity; the problem is that they are not using it. They are evaluating by the world's criteria instead of by the mind of Christ. They have been given nous Christou but are exercising nous kosmou. Paul's entire argument in chapters 1-2 has been building to this point: you have been given the most extraordinary epistemological gift imaginable -- the mind of the Lord himself -- and you are using it to pick teams based on who is the most impressive speaker.

## Summary of the Argument's Movement

Paul's argument in chapter 2 moves through four stages, each building on the previous:

First (vv. 1-5): Paul's own practice in Corinth embodied the principle of chapter 1. He deliberately avoided impressive rhetoric so that the Corinthians' faith would rest on God's power rather than on human wisdom. This is not merely autobiography; it is evidence that evaluating teachers by rhetorical skill contradicts the very foundation of the Corinthian community's existence.

Second (vv. 6-9): There is a genuine wisdom, but it is God's wisdom, hidden from the rulers of this age and inaccessible to natural human perception. The rulers' ignorance of this wisdom led them to crucify the Lord of glory -- the supreme demonstration that worldly wisdom, however powerful, cannot perceive what God is doing. What God has prepared exceeds the capacity of eye, ear, and heart to discover.

Third (vv. 10-13): The Spirit bridges the epistemological gap. What cannot be discovered by human faculties has been revealed by the Spirit, who alone knows the depths of God. The believing community has received this Spirit precisely so that they might understand what God has given them. The entire communication of God's wisdom -- content, language, and reception -- is the Spirit's work.

Fourth (vv. 14-16): Two categories of persons are defined by their capacity for spiritual discernment. The psychikos person, operating from natural faculties alone, cannot receive or understand the things of the Spirit; they appear foolish to the natural mind. The pneumatikos person, indwelt by the Spirit, can evaluate all things and possesses what no one else can claim: the mind of Christ.

The argument is designed to dismantle the Corinthians' basis for factionalism from the inside. They have been evaluating teachers by the criteria of this age -- rhetorical skill, philosophical sophistication, social prestige. Paul has argued that these criteria belong to a wisdom that is being abolished, that failed to recognize the Lord of glory, and that is fundamentally incapable of perceiving what God is doing. The only adequate evaluative instrument is the Spirit, and the community that has received the Spirit should be using the mind of Christ, not the mind of the world, to assess everything -- including their teachers. Chapter 3 will draw the devastating conclusion: their factionalism proves they are not doing this.
