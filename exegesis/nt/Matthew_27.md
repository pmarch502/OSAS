# Matthew 27 -- First-Century Reading

## Context Within the Author's Argument

**What came before (Matthew 26):** Chapter 26 is the author's account of the events leading from Jesus's final Passover meal through his arrest and trial before the Sanhedrin. The chapter opens with Jesus's own prediction -- "You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified" (26:2) -- placing the passion events under Jesus's foreknowledge and stated purpose. The chief priests and elders convene at the house of Caiaphas and plot to arrest Jesus "by stealth" (26:4), but Jesus's movements are governed by his own timetable, not theirs. The anointing at Bethany (26:6-13) is identified by Jesus as preparation for his burial, with the declaration that the woman's act "will also be told in the whole world" (26:13). Judas agrees to betray Jesus for thirty pieces of silver (26:14-16) -- a sum that the author will connect to Zechariah in chapter 27. The Passover meal becomes the occasion for Jesus to reinterpret the bread and cup as "my body" and "my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:26-28), explicitly linking his impending death to covenant sacrifice and to forgiveness. The exodus from the supper leads to the Mount of Olives, where Jesus predicts the disciples' scattering (citing Zechariah 13:7, "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered," 26:31) and Peter's threefold denial (26:34). In Gethsemane, Jesus prays three times for the cup to pass from him, each time submitting to the Father's will (26:39, 42, 44), while the disciples sleep. Judas arrives with a crowd bearing swords and clubs, sent from the chief priests and elders, and betrays Jesus with a kiss (26:47-49). Jesus's arrest includes his refusal of violent resistance and his declaration that "all this has taken place that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled" (26:56). The disciples all flee (26:56b). Before the Sanhedrin, Jesus is silent under false testimony until the high priest adjures him by the living God to say whether he is the Christ (26:63). Jesus responds with the affirmation "You have said so" (26:64) and adds the declaration from Daniel 7:13 and Psalm 110:1: "From now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (26:64). The high priest tears his robes and declares blasphemy; the council condemns Jesus as deserving death (26:65-66). The chapter closes with the physical abuse of Jesus by the council's attendants and Peter's threefold denial in the courtyard, culminating in Peter's bitter weeping when the rooster crows (26:75).

**What comes after (Matthew 28):** Chapter 28 opens with the discovery of the empty tomb by Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" (28:1). An angel descends, rolls back the stone, and announces: "He is not here, for he has risen, as he said" (28:6). The women are sent to tell the disciples, and Jesus himself meets them on the way, repeating the instruction (28:9-10). The author then records that the chief priests bribe the soldiers who had been guarding the tomb to spread the story that the disciples stole the body (28:11-15), noting that "this story has been spread among the Jews to this day" (28:15) -- an editorial comment directed at the first-century audience familiar with this counter-narrative. The document closes with Jesus meeting the eleven disciples on the mountain in Galilee, declaring that "all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18), and commissioning them to "make disciples of all nations" (28:19), with the promise: "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (28:20). The declaration of universal authority in 28:18 fulfills the Daniel 7:13-14 vision invoked at 26:64 -- the Son of Man who was condemned by the Sanhedrin now possesses the dominion Daniel described. The commission to "all nations" (panta ta ethne) completes the trajectory announced in 24:14 ("this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world as a testimony to all nations").

**How chapter 27 connects them:** Chapter 26 ended with Jesus condemned by the Sanhedrin and Peter weeping in the courtyard. Chapter 27 picks up "when morning came" (27:1), carrying the narrative forward without interruption into the next stage: the transfer of Jesus to Pilate, the Roman governor, for execution. The entire chapter narrates what the author presents as the fulfillment of Jesus's own predictions -- his delivery to the Gentiles (20:19), his crucifixion (20:19, 26:2), and his death. At the same time, the chapter is structured to demonstrate that every element of the passion enacts patterns established in Israel's scriptures: the thirty pieces of silver invoke Zechariah, the silence before the governor invokes the Isaianic servant, the crucifixion between two criminals echoes Isaiah 53:12, the cry of dereliction quotes Psalm 22, and the phenomena attending Jesus's death -- the torn curtain, the earthquake, the opened tombs -- signal that the events carry cosmic and covenantal significance. Chapter 27 is the hinge between the condemnation of chapter 26 and the vindication of chapter 28. The one who is mocked as king throughout chapter 27 is revealed as possessing "all authority" in chapter 28. The one whose body is placed in a sealed and guarded tomb in 27:57-66 is the one the tomb cannot hold in 28:1-6. The author's argument requires both: the death must be real, public, and verified before the resurrection can carry its weight.

## The Council's Decision and the Transfer to Pilate (vv. 1-2)

"When morning came (proias de genomenes), all the chief priests and the elders of the people took counsel against Jesus to put him to death (symboulion elabon kata tou Iesou hoste thanatosai auton). And they bound him (desantes auton) and led him away and delivered him over to Pilate the governor (paredokan Pontio Pilato to hegemoni)."

The temporal marker "when morning came" links directly to the nighttime trial of 26:57-68. The Sanhedrin had already condemned Jesus as "deserving of death" (26:66), but the council does not possess the authority to execute. Josephus records that under Roman administration, capital punishment was reserved to the Roman governor (War 2.117; cf. John 18:31). The verb paradidomi (to deliver over, to hand over) continues the chain of "delivering" that runs through the passion narrative: Judas "delivered" Jesus to the chief priests (26:15-16), the chief priests now "deliver" him to Pilate. The same verb was used in Jesus's predictions of his passion: "The Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him over to the Gentiles" (20:18-19). The text presents the transfer as fulfilling Jesus's own words precisely -- delivered first to Jewish authorities, then to Gentile authority.

The verb desantes (having bound) indicates that Jesus is treated as a criminal being transported for sentencing. The binding echoes the treatment of prisoners in Israel's history -- Samson bound by the Philistines (Judges 16:21), Zedekiah bound and taken to Babylon (2 Kings 25:7), Jeremiah bound (Jeremiah 40:1). The one who holds all authority (28:18) is led away in bonds.

Pontius Pilate served as prefect (or governor, hegemon) of Judea from approximately 26-36 CE, a historical figure attested by Josephus (Antiquities 18.55-89), Philo (Embassy to Gaius 299-305), and the Pilate inscription found at Caesarea Maritima. The author identifies him by name and title, grounding the narrative in a specific historical moment.

## The Death of the Betrayer and the Blood Money (vv. 3-10)

"Then when Judas, his betrayer, saw that Jesus was condemned (tote idon Ioudas ho paradidous auton hoti katekrithe), he changed his mind (metamelomai) and brought back the thirty pieces of silver (triakonta argyria) to the chief priests and the elders, saying, 'I have sinned by betraying innocent blood (hemarton paradous haima athoon).' They said, 'What is that to us? See to it yourself (sy opse).'"

The narrative inserts the account of Judas between the transfer to Pilate and the trial before Pilate, creating a deliberate parallel between two failures: Judas who betrayed Jesus and Peter who denied him (26:69-75). Both experience remorse. Peter "wept bitterly" (26:75); Judas "changed his mind" (metamelomai). The verb metamelomai (to feel regret, to change one's mind after the fact) is distinct from metanoeo (to repent, to turn around), which is the word the author uses for genuine repentance (3:2, 4:17). The distinction matters: Judas felt regret about the outcome but the text does not describe a repentance that turns toward God. The regret leads to self-destruction, not restoration.

Judas confesses: "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood" (haima athoon -- blood that is without guilt). The phrase "innocent blood" carries significant weight in the Hebrew scriptures. Deuteronomy 19:10, 13 and 21:8-9 address the communal guilt incurred by shedding innocent blood. Jeremiah 26:15 records the prophet's warning: "If you put me to death, you will bring innocent blood upon yourselves and upon this city." The shedding of innocent blood pollutes the land and brings judgment on those responsible. Judas's confession that the blood is "innocent" (athoos) functions as a legal testimony to Jesus's guiltlessness from the mouth of the one most intimately involved in the betrayal.

The chief priests' response -- "What is that to us? See to it yourself" (sy opse) -- is a refusal to accept any responsibility for the moral consequence of their action. The phrase sy opse (literally "you will see [to it]") places the burden entirely on Judas. It will recur on Pilate's lips in a different form at verse 24.

"And throwing down the pieces of silver into the temple (rhipsas ta argyria eis ton naon), he departed, and he went and hanged himself (apelthon apegxato)."

Judas throws the silver into the naos -- not the broader temple complex (hieron) but the sanctuary itself, the inner temple where only priests could enter. The act is one of revulsion: the money is returned to the place from which it was paid, flung back at the institution that authorized the transaction. Judas's death by hanging echoes the death of Ahithophel, David's counselor who betrayed him and then "went and hanged himself" (apegxato in the LXX, 2 Samuel 17:23). Ahithophel was the trusted advisor who conspired with Absalom against David, and when his counsel was not followed, he went home, set his affairs in order, and hanged himself. The parallel is precise: a trusted intimate of the anointed one who betrays him and then destroys himself.

"But the chief priests, taking the pieces of silver, said, 'It is not lawful to put them into the treasury (eis ton korbanan), since they are blood money (time haimatos).' So they took counsel (symboulion labontes) and bought with them the potter's field (ton agron tou kerameos) as a burial place for strangers."

The irony is sharp. The chief priests who arranged the payment of blood money and refused to accept responsibility when Judas returned it now demonstrate scrupulous concern about the ritual propriety of placing the coins back in the treasury. They are careful about the technicality (Deuteronomy 23:18 prohibits bringing the "wages of a dog" -- tainted money -- into the house of the LORD) while having already violated the substance. They strain out the gnat and swallow the camel (23:24). The word korbanan (treasury, from the Hebrew qorban, an offering to God, cf. Mark 7:11) is used only here in the document, identifying the specific temple fund for sacred purposes.

Their solution -- purchasing a potter's field for burying strangers -- uses the blood money for a purpose connected to death. The field becomes a place for the dead, purchased with death's price. The author notes: "Therefore that field has been called the Field of Blood (agros haimatos) to this day" (v. 8). The phrase "to this day" (heos tes semeron) is an authorial aside directed at the reader, indicating that the name persisted into the time of writing -- a well-known landmark in Jerusalem that first-century readers could verify.

"Then was fulfilled what had been spoken by the prophet Jeremiah (tote epleerothe to rethen dia Ieremiou tou prophetou), saying, 'And they took the thirty pieces of silver, the price of him on whom a price had been set by some of the sons of Israel, and they gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord directed me.'"

The author attributes the quotation to Jeremiah, though the language primarily reflects Zechariah 11:12-13: "So they weighed out as my wages thirty pieces of silver. Then the LORD said to me, 'Throw it to the potter' -- the lordly price at which I was priced by them. So I took the thirty pieces of silver and threw them into the house of the LORD, to the potter." In Zechariah's enacted parable, the prophet represents a shepherd of Israel who is rejected by the flock; the "thirty pieces of silver" is the insultingly low wage assessed by the people for his service (thirty shekels was the price of a slave in Exodus 21:32). The rejected shepherd throws the silver into the temple, to the potter. The pattern is replicated: Jesus, the shepherd of Israel (cf. 2:6, 9:36, 25:32, 26:31), is valued at the price of a slave, and the money ends up in the temple and is used to purchase a potter's field.

The attribution to "Jeremiah" rather than Zechariah has been noted since antiquity. Several possibilities present themselves from a first-century perspective. Jeremiah 19:1-13 describes Jeremiah purchasing a potter's earthen flask and then smashing it in the valley of the son of Hinnom (Topheth), declaring that the place would be renamed "the Valley of Slaughter" because of the blood shed there, and that Jerusalem would become "like Topheth" -- a place of death and burial. Jeremiah 32:6-15 records Jeremiah's purchase of a field as a prophetic sign. The author may be combining Zechariah's specific language about the thirty pieces of silver with Jeremiah's broader thematic context of the purchase of a field, the potter, and the renaming of a place of death. In some first-century textual traditions, the Book of the Twelve (minor prophets) was sometimes subsumed under the heading of a major prophet who preceded them. The author's citation practice treats the combined allusion as primarily Jeremianic in its thematic scope, even though the verbal details come from Zechariah.

## Jesus Before Pilate: The Question of Kingship (vv. 11-14)

"Now Jesus stood before the governor (ho de Iesous estathe emprosthen tou hegemonos), and the governor asked him, 'Are you the King of the Jews (sy ei ho basileus ton Ioudaion)?' Jesus said, 'You have said so (sy legeis).'"

The scene shifts to the Roman judicial proceeding. The charge before Pilate is political: "King of the Jews" (basileus ton Ioudaion). The Sanhedrin condemned Jesus on the religious charge of blasphemy (26:65); before Pilate, the charge is framed as a claim to royal authority -- the only kind of charge that would matter to a Roman governor. A messianic claimant asserting kingship over Judea would constitute sedition against Rome.

Jesus's response "You have said so" (sy legeis) is the same form used at 26:25 (to Judas: "You have said so") and 26:64 (to the high priest: "You have said so"). The phrase is neither a simple affirmation nor a denial. It acknowledges the statement while distancing Jesus from the meaning Pilate attaches to it. Jesus is indeed king, but the nature of his kingship is not what the term "King of the Jews" means in Pilate's Roman framework.

"But when he was accused by the chief priests and elders, he gave no answer (ouk apekrinato... ouden). Then Pilate said to him, 'Do you not hear how many things they testify against you (posa sou katamartyrosin)?' But he gave him no answer, not even to a single charge (ouk apekrithe auto pros oude hen rhema), so that the governor was greatly amazed (hoste thaumazein ton hegemona lian)."

Jesus's silence before accusation invokes Isaiah 53:7: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth." The Isaianic servant is the one who suffers in silence, bearing the sins of others. The word rhema (word, utterance) emphasizes the completeness of the silence: not even a single word in response. The governor's amazement (thaumazo) indicates that silence in the face of capital charges was abnormal. A defendant who refused to defend himself defied the logic of Roman judicial procedure, in which self-defense was a right and an expectation. Pilate interprets Jesus's silence not as guilt but as something extraordinary.

## The Release of Barabbas and the Condemnation of Jesus (vv. 15-26)

"Now at the feast the governor was accustomed to release for the crowd any one prisoner whom they wanted (hena desmion hon ethelon). And they had then a notorious prisoner called Barabbas (desmion episemon legomenon Barabban)."

The custom of releasing a prisoner at the festival is attested by the author as an established practice (kata heorten -- "at the feast"). The name Barabbas (Bar-Abba) means "son of the father" in Aramaic. The irony in the author's narrative is structural: the crowd will choose the "son of the father" (Barabbas) and reject the Son of the Father (Jesus, identified as God's Son at 3:17, 17:5). Some early manuscripts (attested by Origen) give Barabbas the name "Jesus Barabbas," which would sharpen the irony further: the crowd chooses between two men named Jesus, one the "son of the father" by name only, the other the Son of the Father by nature.

"So when they had gathered, Pilate said to them, 'Whom do you want me to release for you: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ (Iesoun ton legomenon Christon)?' For he knew that it was out of envy (dia phthonon) that they had delivered him up."

Pilate perceives the motive: "envy" (phthonos). The word denotes resentment of another's position, authority, or success. The chief priests and elders delivered Jesus to Pilate not because of a genuine legal violation but because of their jealousy of his influence. Pilate's insight functions as another testimony to Jesus's innocence from a hostile or indifferent source -- Judas declared the blood "innocent" (v. 4), Pilate recognizes the motive as envy rather than justice.

"Besides, while he was sitting on the judgment seat (kathemenou autou epi tou bematos), his wife sent word to him, 'Have nothing to do with that righteous man (to dikaio ekeino), for I have suffered much because of him today in a dream (kat' onar).'"

Pilate's wife provides yet another testimony to Jesus's innocence, calling him "that righteous man" (ho dikaios ekeinos). Her warning comes through a dream (kat' onar) -- the same mechanism by which God communicated to Joseph at the beginning of the document (1:20, 2:12, 13, 19, 22). In the author's narrative framework, dreams function as divine communication. A Gentile woman receives divine guidance about the identity of the righteous one, while the religious leadership of Israel has condemned him. The pattern echoes the magi in chapter 2: Gentiles receive divine direction, while Herod (the ruler) plots destruction.

"Now the chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd (epeisan tous ochlous) to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus (hina aiteosontai ton Barabban ton de Iesoun apoleseosin)."

The verb peitho (to persuade, to convince) indicates active effort by the leadership to sway the crowd. The crowd does not spontaneously choose Barabbas; the chief priests and elders work to produce this outcome. The word apollymi (to destroy, to kill, to lose) is the same word used for Herod's attempt to destroy the child Jesus in 2:13 -- the author uses the same verb for the threat at the beginning and at the end of the narrative.

"The governor again said to them, 'Which of the two do you want me to release for you?' And they said, 'Barabbas (ton Barabban).' Pilate said to them, 'Then what shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ (ti oun poieso Iesoun ton legomenon Christon)?' They all said, 'Let him be crucified (staurotheto)!' And he said, 'Why? What evil has he done (ti gar kakon epoiesen)?' But they shouted all the more, 'Let him be crucified!'"

Pilate's question "What evil has he done?" constitutes yet another declaration of innocence -- this time from the judge himself. The governor who is supposed to condemn cannot identify the crime. The escalation from "let him be crucified" to the repeated shout "let him be crucified" portrays a crowd driven by the momentum of mob persuasion, not reasoned judgment. The verb stauroo (to crucify) appears here for the first time as a crowd demand, though Jesus predicted it at 20:19 and 26:2.

"So when Pilate saw that he was gaining nothing (ouden ophelei), but rather that a riot was beginning (alla mallon thorybos ginetai), he took water and washed his hands before the crowd (apenipsato tas cheiras apenanti tou ochlou), saying, 'I am innocent of this man's blood (athoos eimi apo tou haimatos toutou); see to it yourselves (hymeis opsesthe).'"

Pilate's hand-washing invokes Deuteronomy 21:6-7, where the elders of a city nearest to an unsolved murder wash their hands over a heifer and declare: "Our hands did not shed this blood, nor did our eyes see it shed." The ritual is Israelite in origin, and the author places it in the hands of a Roman governor. Pilate's declaration "I am innocent of this blood" (athoos eimi) uses the same word Judas used of Jesus -- athoos (innocent, guiltless). Pilate claims innocence of Jesus's blood while having the authority and obligation to prevent an unjust execution. His words "see to it yourselves" (hymeis opsesthe) echo precisely what the chief priests said to Judas: "see to it yourself" (sy opse, v. 4). Both the religious leaders and the political ruler attempt to deflect responsibility, using the same phrase.

"And all the people answered, 'His blood be on us and on our children (to haima autou eph' hemas kai epi ta tekna hemon)!'"

The crowd's declaration assumes the legal language of blood guilt. In 2 Samuel 1:16, David says to the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul: "Your blood be on your head." In Joshua 2:19, Rahab is told that if anyone in her house goes into the street, "his blood shall be on his own head." The phrase indicates the acceptance of legal responsibility for the consequences of bloodshed. The crowd invokes this liability upon themselves "and upon our children" (epi ta tekna hemon), extending the responsibility across a generation. In the author's narrative, this connects directly to Jesus's warnings in chapter 23: "Upon you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth... Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation" (23:35-36). The crowd's self-imprecation and Jesus's prediction converge: the blood guilt they accept and the judgment Jesus announced upon "this generation" point to the same historical consequences. For the first-century reader who knew the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, the crowd's words carry the weight of fulfilled warning.

The author is not attributing guilt to an ethnic group in perpetuity but recording a specific crowd's specific declaration, which the author connects to a specific historical consequence that falls "upon this generation" -- a temporally bounded judgment, not an eternal curse. The text's own framework, established by Jesus's repeated use of "this generation" (11:16, 12:39-45, 16:4, 17:17, 23:36, 24:34), confines the scope to the contemporaries.

"Then he released for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus (ton de Iesoun phragellosas), delivered him to be crucified (paredoken hina stauerothe)."

The scourging (phragellosas, from the Latin flagellum) was a Roman preliminary to crucifixion, involving a leather whip embedded with bone or metal fragments. Josephus records instances where scourging alone caused death (War 2.306-308). The verb paradidomi (to deliver over) appears again -- Jesus is "delivered" yet another time in the chain that began with Judas and continued through the chief priests to Pilate. Each link in the chain fulfills Jesus's prediction at 20:18-19.

## The Soldiers' Mockery of the King (vv. 27-31)

"Then the soldiers of the governor took Jesus into the governor's headquarters (eis to praitorion), and they gathered the whole battalion (holen ten speiran) before him."

The praetorium (praitorion, from Latin praetorium) was the governor's residence and administrative center in Jerusalem, likely either the Antonia Fortress adjoining the temple or Herod's palace on the western hill. A "cohort" (speira) at full strength numbered 480-600 soldiers, though the word can denote a smaller detachment. The gathering of the whole unit indicates that the mockery is a collective act, not the impulse of a few individuals.

"And they stripped him and put a scarlet robe on him (chlamyda kokkinon), and twisting together a crown of thorns (stephanon ex akanthon), they put it on his head and put a reed in his right hand (kalamon en te dexia autou). And kneeling before him, they mocked him, saying, 'Hail, King of the Jews! (chaire, basileu ton Ioudaion)'"

The mockery is structured as a parody of royal investiture. Each element corresponds to a genuine feature of kingship: the scarlet robe (chlamys kokkine) substitutes for the purple of royalty (Roman soldiers' cloaks were scarlet; the resemblance to purple made it useful for the parody); the crown of thorns replaces the laurel wreath or diadem; the reed (kalamos) replaces the royal scepter; the kneeling (gonypeteo) parodies the act of homage; and the greeting "Hail, King of the Jews" (chaire, basileu ton Ioudaion) parodies the acclamation "Ave, Caesar" (chaire, Kaisar). Every element of genuine kingship is inverted: the robe is a soldier's castoff, the crown is made of thorns that wound, the scepter is a fragile reed, and the homage is performed in mockery.

The author's sustained irony operates at every level: the soldiers mock what they do not know to be true. The reader, who has followed the narrative from the angelic announcement of 1:21 ("he will save his people from their sins") through the magi's question "Where is he who has been born king of the Jews?" (2:2), through the entry into Jerusalem when the crowds hailed him as "Son of David" (21:9), and through Jesus's own claim before the Sanhedrin to be "the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" (26:64), understands that the soldiers are unwittingly enacting the truth. Jesus is the King of the Jews. The crown, the robe, the scepter, and the acclamation are all real -- but in a register the soldiers cannot perceive.

"And they spit on him (eneptyon eis auton) and took the reed and struck him on the head (etypton eis ten kephalen autou). And when they had mocked him, they stripped him of the robe and put his own clothes on him and led him away to crucify him (apegagon auton eis to staurosai)."

The spitting recalls Isaiah 50:6: "I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting." This is the third of the Isaianic servant songs, where the servant endures humiliation without resistance, trusting in the LORD's vindication. The striking with the reed -- the mock scepter used as a weapon -- encapsulates the entire scene: the symbol of authority is turned into an instrument of violence against the one who holds the authority.

## The Crucifixion (vv. 32-44)

"As they went out, they found a man of Cyrene, Simon by name (Simona Kyrenaion). They compelled this man to carry his cross (egareusan hina are ton stauron autou)."

The verb agareuo (to compel, to requisition, to conscript) is a loan word from Persian, referring to the authority of royal couriers to commandeer labor, animals, or goods. It appeared at 5:41: "And if anyone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles." The same word describes Roman compulsion of a civilian -- Simon of Cyrene is conscripted to carry the horizontal beam (patibulum) of the cross. Cyrene was a city in North Africa (modern Libya) with a significant Jewish population (cf. Acts 2:10, 6:9, 11:20). Simon is identified by name and origin, suggesting he may have been known to the early community.

"And when they came to a place called Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull, Kraniou Topos), they offered him wine to drink, mixed with gall (oinon meta choles memigmenon), and when he tasted it, he would not drink it (ouk ethelesen piein)."

The location name "Place of a Skull" (Kraniou Topos, translating the Aramaic Golgotha/Gulgolta) likely refers to the appearance of the rock formation, a bare promontory outside the city walls. The wine mixed with gall (chole) draws on Psalm 69:21 (LXX 68:22): "They gave me poison (chole) for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." The Psalm 69 connection is explicit through the word chole (gall, bile, poison). In the Psalm, the sufferer is a righteous one surrounded by enemies who heap abuse upon him. Jesus tastes the drink but refuses it -- he will undergo the suffering without sedation or dulling of consciousness.

"And when they had crucified him (staurosantes de auton), they divided his garments among them by casting lots (ebalon kleron). Then they sat down and kept watch over him there."

The division of garments by lot directly fulfills Psalm 22:18 (LXX 21:19): "They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." Psalm 22 is a lament of a righteous sufferer who is surrounded by enemies, pierced in hands and feet, and exposed to public shame, yet who declares his trust in God and anticipates vindication. The Psalm will be explicitly quoted by Jesus in verse 46. The author does not add a fulfillment formula here, letting the action speak for itself to the reader who recognizes the Psalm.

"And over his head they put the charge against him, which read, 'This is Jesus, the King of the Jews (houtos estin Iesous ho basileus ton Ioudaion).'"

The titulus -- the placard identifying the condemned person's crime -- was standard Roman practice. The charge (aitia) is kingship. The inscription is the third identification of Jesus as "King of the Jews" in the chapter (Pilate's question at v. 11, the soldiers' mockery at v. 29, and now the official Roman charge). The irony continues: the Roman legal system posts as a criminal charge what the author has been demonstrating to be true since the genealogy of chapter 1. The inscription functions simultaneously as condemnation (in Pilate's intention) and proclamation (in the author's design).

"Then two robbers were crucified with him (dyo lestai), one on the right and one on the left."

The word lestai (robbers, bandits, insurrectionists) is the term used by Josephus for the Zealot fighters and revolutionary bandits who plagued Judea (War 2.253-254). At 26:55, Jesus used the same word when he was arrested: "Have you come out as against a robber (leistes), with swords and clubs to capture me?" The placement of Jesus between two lestai presents the man who refused violent revolution (26:52, "Put your sword back into its place") being executed as one of them. Isaiah 53:12 provides the scriptural background: "He was numbered with the transgressors" (meta ton anomon elogisthe in the LXX).

"And those who passed by derided him (hoi de paraporeuomenoi eblasphemoun auton), wagging their heads (kinountes tas kephalas auton) and saying, 'You who would destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save yourself (soson seauton)! If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross.'"

The mockery from the passersby echoes Psalm 22:7 (LXX 21:8): "All who see me mock me; they make mouths at me; they wag their heads." The verb blasphemeo (to revile, to slander) is the same word the high priest used when declaring Jesus guilty of "blasphemy" (26:65) -- the charge of blasphemy is now being committed against Jesus by his mockers. The taunt "If you are the Son of God" (ei huios ei tou theou) replicates the temptation language of 4:3, 6: "If you are the Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread" and "If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down." The wilderness temptation and the crucifixion mockery share the same structure: a challenge to prove divine sonship by exercising power for self-preservation. In both cases, Jesus's identity as Son of God is demonstrated precisely by his refusal to act as the tempter demands.

The reference to "destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days" recalls the false testimony at 26:61. The passersby have heard a distorted version of Jesus's words and throw them back at him. The irony is that Jesus's body is the temple that will be destroyed and raised in three days (cf. John 2:19-21 for the explicit identification) -- the mockers unknowingly describe what is about to happen.

"So also the chief priests, with the scribes and elders, mocked him, saying, 'He saved others; he cannot save himself (allous esosen, heauton ou dynatai sosai). He is the King of Israel; let him come down now from the cross, and we will believe in him (pisteusomen ep' auton). He trusts in God (pepoithen epi ton theon); let God deliver him now, if he desires him (ei thelei auton). For he said, "I am the Son of God."'"

The chief priests, scribes, and elders -- the same groups who plotted Jesus's death (26:3-4) -- now mock him publicly. Their taunt "He saved others; he cannot save himself" is another instance of unwitting truth-telling. Jesus saved others (the verb sozo -- to save, to heal, to rescue -- describes his healing ministry throughout the document: 9:21-22, 14:30). He "cannot save himself" not because he lacks the power but because saving himself would contradict the purpose for which he came. At 20:28, Jesus declared: "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." Self-rescue would negate the ransom.

The taunt "let God deliver him now, if he desires him" quotes Psalm 22:8 (LXX 21:9): "He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him; let him rescue him, for he delights in him." The chief priests speak the words of the mocking enemies in David's psalm without recognizing that they are placing themselves in the role of the psalm's adversaries. The psalm does not end with the sufferer abandoned -- it ends with vindication and universal praise (Psalm 22:22-31). By invoking the psalm's language of mockery, the religious leaders unwittingly invoke its conclusion: the sufferer will be vindicated, and "all the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD" (Psalm 22:27).

"The robbers who were crucified with him also reviled him in the same way (to d' auto kai hoi lestai hoi systaurothentes syn auto oneidizon auton)."

In the author's account, even the criminals crucified alongside Jesus join the abuse. The unanimity of the mockery is total: passersby, religious leaders, and fellow condemned criminals all participate. The isolation is complete -- there is no human voice raised in Jesus's defense at the cross.

## The Death of Jesus and Its Attendant Signs (vv. 45-56)

"Now from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land (skotos egeneto epi pasan ten gen) until the ninth hour."

The sixth hour is noon; the ninth hour is 3:00 PM. Darkness covers "all the land" (pasan ten gen -- either "all the earth" or "the whole land," with the same ambiguity as in 24:30). Three hours of midday darkness invoke the plague of darkness over Egypt in Exodus 10:22: "There was thick darkness in all the land of Egypt three days." They also recall Amos 8:9: "And on that day, declares the Lord GOD, I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight." In Amos, the darkness at noon accompanies God's judgment on Israel and is followed by the declaration: "I will make it like the mourning for an only son" (Amos 8:10) -- the same language Zechariah 12:10 uses for the mourning over the pierced one. Joel 2:31 also connects darkened sun with the day of the LORD. The darkness is the prophetic sign of divine judgment being enacted. The cosmic disruption language Jesus used in 24:29 ("the sun will be darkened") is now physically realized at the crucifixion.

"And about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice (phone megale), saying, 'Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?' that is, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? (thee mou, thee mou, hina ti me egkatelipes)'"

Jesus quotes the opening verse of Psalm 22 (LXX 21:2) in Aramaic (or Hebrew, depending on the form Eli/Eloi). This is the psalm that has been providing the scriptural framework for the entire crucifixion scene: the mockery (22:7-8), the division of garments (22:18), the wagging of heads (22:7), the taunt about trusting God (22:8). By quoting its opening line, Jesus identifies himself with the psalm's speaker -- the righteous sufferer who experiences abandonment by God.

The cry is not an expression of theological despair disconnected from the psalm's larger arc. In the first-century synagogue, citing the opening line of a psalm invoked the entire psalm. Psalm 22 begins with the cry of abandonment (vv. 1-2), moves through the description of suffering and mockery by enemies (vv. 6-18), and then turns decisively at verse 22: "I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will praise you." The psalm concludes with the declaration that "all the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD, and all the families of the nations shall worship before you" (22:27), and that "posterity shall serve him; it shall be told of the Lord to the coming generation; they shall come and proclaim his righteousness to a people yet unborn, that he has done it" (22:30-31). The psalm's trajectory is from abandonment to vindication, from suffering to universal worship, from death to the proclamation of God's righteousness to future generations. Jesus's cry invokes this entire arc.

"And some of the bystanders, hearing it, said, 'This man is calling Elijah (Elian phonei houtos).'"

The mishearing of "Eli" (my God) as "Elias" (Elijah) may reflect genuine confusion between the Aramaic and the proper name, or it may be a further element of mockery. In first-century Jewish expectation, Elijah was to return before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5). The suggestion that Jesus is calling for Elijah treats his cry as a plea for rescue by the eschatological forerunner.

"And one of them at once ran and took a sponge, filled it with sour wine (oxous), and put it on a reed (kalamo) and gave it to him to drink. But the rest said, 'Wait, let us see whether Elijah will come to save him (sosonta auton).'"

The sour wine (oxos) again evokes Psalm 69:21 (LXX 68:22): "For my thirst they gave me sour wine (oxos) to drink." The offer may be an act of minimal mercy (sour wine, or posca, was a common soldier's drink) or another gesture of mockery. The reed (kalamos) -- the same word used for the mock scepter in verse 29 -- is now the instrument for delivering the drink. The bystanders' comment "let us see whether Elijah will come to save him" treats the moment as a spectacle, waiting to see if a miracle will intervene.

"And Jesus cried out again with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit (apheken to pneuma)."

The second cry is described but its words are not recorded in this account. The verb aphiemi (to release, to send away, to let go) is used for Jesus's death: he "released his spirit" (apheken to pneuma). The language is deliberate. Jesus does not merely die; he actively releases his spirit. The verb aphiemi has been used throughout the document for forgiveness (aphiemi -- to release from obligation, to forgive: 6:12, 14, 15; 9:2, 5, 6; 12:31-32; 26:28) and for leaving or departing (4:20, 22; 19:27, 29). The death is presented as a voluntary act -- consistent with 20:28 ("to give his life as a ransom for many") and 26:53-54, where Jesus declared he could summon twelve legions of angels but chose not to, so that the scriptures might be fulfilled.

"And behold, the curtain of the temple was torn in two (to katapetasma tou naou eschisthe eis dyo), from top to bottom (ap' anothen heos kato). And the earth shook, and the rocks were split (he ge eseisthe kai hai petrai eschisthesan)."

The tearing of the temple curtain (katapetasma) is a sign of enormous significance. The naos (sanctuary, inner temple) had two curtains: one at the entrance and one separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (the inner veil, cf. Exodus 26:31-33). The inner veil separated the place of God's presence from all human access; only the high priest entered beyond it, once a year on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:2, 12-15). The tearing "from top to bottom" indicates divine agency -- the direction is from God's side, not from human hands. The barrier between God's presence and the people is removed at the moment of Jesus's death. What the high priest's annual entry behind the veil symbolized -- access to God and atonement for sin -- is now accomplished through Jesus's death. The torn curtain declares that the temple's mediating function is superseded.

The verb schizo (to tear, to split, to rend) connects the tearing of the curtain to the earthquake that splits the rocks. The same action -- splitting apart -- occurs in both the sacred structure and the natural world. The earthquake (seismos -- from seio, to shake) connects to the language of 24:29 where "the powers of the heavens will be shaken" (saleuthesontai). The shaking of the earth at Jesus's death is the concrete realization of the cosmic disruption the prophets associated with divine intervention.

"The tombs also were opened (ta mnemeia aneochthesan). And many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised (polla somata ton kekoimemenon hagion egerthe), and coming out of the tombs after his resurrection (meta ten egersin autou) they went into the holy city and appeared to many."

This passage is unique to the author's account. The opened tombs and raised saints are presented as signs accompanying Jesus's death -- though the note "after his resurrection" (meta ten egersin autou) clarifies that their appearance in Jerusalem occurred after Jesus himself was raised. The phrase "saints who had fallen asleep" (hagioi kekoimemenoi) uses the common Jewish and early Christian euphemism for death (cf. Daniel 12:2: "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake"). The connection to Daniel 12:2 is significant: Daniel's vision of resurrection was associated with "a time of trouble, such as never has been since there was a nation" (Daniel 12:1) -- the same text Jesus drew on in 24:21. The raising of the dead saints functions as a sign that Jesus's death inaugurates the eschatological reality Daniel prophesied.

The term "holy city" (hagian polin) for Jerusalem was used at 4:5, where the devil took Jesus to "the holy city" and set him on the pinnacle of the temple. The dead saints enter the holy city and are seen by many -- the private event of Jesus's death produces public consequences that anticipate the general resurrection.

"When the centurion and those who were with him, keeping watch over Jesus, saw the earthquake and what took place (ta genomena), they were filled with awe (ephoebethesan sphodra) and said, 'Truly this was the Son of God (alethos theou huios en houtos)!'"

The confession comes from the Roman centurion and his soldiers -- Gentiles, agents of the occupying power, the very men who carried out the crucifixion. The phrase "Son of God" (theou huios) has been contested throughout the document: affirmed by God at the baptism (3:17) and transfiguration (17:5), confessed by Peter (16:16), used as a weapon in the temptation (4:3, 6) and in the mockers' taunts (v. 40, 43), charged as blasphemy by the Sanhedrin (26:63-65). Now it is confessed by the executioners. The word alethos (truly, really, in truth) marks this as a genuine recognition, not mockery. What the religious leaders rejected, the Gentile soldiers affirm. The pattern of the document -- Gentiles recognizing what Israel's leaders do not (the magi in chapter 2, the centurion at Capernaum in 8:5-13, the Canaanite woman in 15:21-28) -- reaches its climax here.

"There were also many women there (gynaikes pollai), looking on from a distance (apo makrothen theorousai), who had followed Jesus from Galilee, ministering to him (diakonousai auto). Among whom were Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James and Joseph and the mother of the sons of Zebedee."

The women are identified as those who "followed" (akoloutheo -- the technical term for discipleship throughout the document: 4:20, 22; 8:22; 9:9; 19:27-28) and "ministered" (diakoneo -- to serve, the same word used of the angels serving Jesus after the temptation in 4:11, and of the Son of Man who "came not to be served but to serve" in 20:28). They are present at the cross when the male disciples have fled (26:56). Their watching "from a distance" (apo makrothen) echoes Psalm 38:11 (LXX 37:12): "My friends and companions stand aloof from my plague, and my nearest kin stand far off." The women become the essential witnesses who will link the death, burial, and resurrection: they see where Jesus dies (v. 55-56), where he is buried (v. 61), and they will be the first at the empty tomb (28:1).

## The Burial (vv. 57-61)

"When it was evening (opsias de genomenes), there came a rich man from Arimathea, named Joseph (Ioseph), who also was a disciple of Jesus (hos kai autos emathetethe to Iesou). He went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus (to soma tou Iesou). Then Pilate ordered it to be given to him."

Joseph is identified by three characteristics: he is rich (plousios), from Arimathea, and a disciple (matheteuo -- to be a disciple, to be enrolled as a learner). The term "rich" connects to earlier teachings about wealth in the document: "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God" (19:24), followed by "With God all things are possible" (19:26). Joseph of Arimathea is an example of the "possible" -- a rich man who acts as a faithful disciple.

Under Roman law, the body of a crucified criminal was ordinarily left on the cross or disposed of in a common burial. Permission to claim the body required a request to the governor. Deuteronomy 21:22-23 required that the body of an executed criminal be buried before nightfall: "His body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God. You shall not defile your land that the LORD your God is giving you." Joseph's request fulfills this Torah requirement.

"And Joseph took the body and wrapped it in a clean linen shroud (sindoni kathara) and laid it in his own new tomb (en to kaino autou mnemeieo), which he had cut in the rock (ho elatomesen en te petra). And he rolled a great stone to the entrance of the tomb (prosekylisen lithon megan te thyra tou mnemeiou) and went away."

The details emphasize newness and purity: the shroud is "clean" (katharos), the tomb is "new" (kainos) -- no one has been buried in it before -- and it is hewn from rock (petra). The tomb carved from rock, sealed with a large stone, corresponds to the burial practices of wealthy Judeans in the first century, as attested by archaeological evidence from Jerusalem (numerous rock-cut tombs with rolling-stone closures have been excavated). The newness of the tomb ensures that no question can arise about the identity of any body found there or missing from it.

Isaiah 53:9 reads: "And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death." The Isaianic servant, condemned and killed among the wicked (cf. the two robbers, v. 38), is given burial with a rich man. Joseph of Arimathea, the rich disciple, fulfills this detail precisely.

"Mary Magdalene and the other Mary were there, sitting opposite the tomb (ekei kathemeriai apenanti tou taphou)."

The two women who witnessed the crucifixion now witness the burial. Their presence establishes the chain of testimony: they know where the body is placed. The verb kathemai (to sit) indicates they remained at the site, watching. They will return in 28:1, and their knowledge of the tomb's location ensures that the empty tomb cannot be attributed to a visit to the wrong site.

## The Guard at the Tomb (vv. 62-66)

"The next day, that is, after the day of Preparation (hetis estin meta ten paraskeuen), the chief priests and the Pharisees gathered before Pilate and said, 'Sir, we remember how that impostor said, while he was still alive (eti zon), "After three days I will rise again (meta treis hemeras egeiromai)."'"

The temporal marker is unusual. The author does not say "on the Sabbath" but describes it indirectly as "after the day of Preparation" (paraskeue -- the day before the Sabbath, when preparations for the day of rest were completed). The chief priests and Pharisees approach Pilate on the Sabbath itself, indicating the urgency they attach to the matter. The irony is once more layered: the religious leaders remember Jesus's prediction of resurrection more clearly than the disciples do. They call him "that impostor" (ho planos -- the deceiver, the one who leads astray), using a word from the same root as planao (to lead astray), which Jesus applied to the false prophets in 24:4-5, 11, 24. The leaders accuse Jesus of being the deceiver, while the author has presented the leaders themselves as the ones who "lead the people astray."

"'Therefore order the tomb to be made secure until the third day (heos tes trites hemeras), lest his disciples go and steal him away (klepsosin auton) and tell the people, "He has risen from the dead" (egerthe apo ton nekron), and the last fraud will be worse than the first (he eschate plane cheiron tes protes).'"

The chief priests anticipate the possibility that the disciples might steal the body and fabricate a resurrection story. Their concern reveals that they take Jesus's prediction seriously enough to act against it -- they believe the prediction could be credibly exploited, even if they do not believe in its fulfillment. The phrase "the last fraud will be worse than the first" (he eschate plane cheiron tes protes) treats both Jesus's ministry ("the first" deception, in their view) and a potential resurrection claim ("the last" deception) as acts of plane (deception, error). This detail is narratively important because it establishes a counter-narrative that the author addresses directly at 28:11-15, where the same leaders will bribe the soldiers to say that the disciples stole the body. By reporting the guard's placement before the resurrection, the author preemptively addresses the objection: the tomb was secured, watched, and sealed, and yet it was found empty.

"Pilate said to them, 'You have a guard of soldiers (echete koustodian). Go, make it as secure as you can (asphalisasthe hos oidate).' So they went and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone and setting a guard (meta tes koustodias)."

The word koustodia (guard) is a Latin loanword (custodia), indicating a Roman military guard. The sealing of the stone (sphragizo -- to seal, to affix a seal) would involve a cord and wax or clay seal across the stone, so that any movement of the stone would break the seal and be immediately evident. The combination of the sealed stone and the military guard makes the tomb maximally secure by human standards. The author's point is clear: every possible precaution against tampering has been taken. The resurrection, when it occurs in chapter 28, cannot be attributed to the disciples' intervention. The security measures established by the opponents become, in the author's narrative design, evidence for the reality of what they were intended to prevent.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **It completes the chain of delivery that Jesus predicted.** At 20:18-19, Jesus said: "The Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him over to the Gentiles to be mocked and flogged and crucified, and he will be raised on the third day." Chapter 27 fulfills every element of this prediction in sequence: delivery to Pilate (v. 2), mockery (vv. 27-31), flogging (v. 26), and crucifixion (v. 35). The third-day rising awaits chapter 28. The narrative demonstrates that Jesus's suffering is not a defeat of his purpose but the fulfillment of it -- he predicted each stage, and each stage occurs as stated.

2. **It accumulates testimonies to Jesus's innocence from every quarter.** Judas declares the blood "innocent" (v. 4). Pilate's wife calls Jesus "that righteous man" (v. 19). Pilate himself asks "What evil has he done?" and washes his hands of "this man's blood" (vv. 23-24). Even the mockers unwittingly testify to Jesus's identity: "He saved others" (v. 42), "He trusts in God" (v. 43), "He said, 'I am the Son of God'" (v. 43). The centurion confesses, "Truly this was the Son of God" (v. 54). The chapter builds its case by allowing every participant -- betrayer, judge's wife, judge, enemies, and executioner -- to speak truths they do not fully understand or intend.

3. **It embeds the crucifixion in Israel's scriptural framework.** The thirty pieces of silver fulfill Zechariah 11. Jesus's silence before Pilate fulfills Isaiah 53:7. The division of garments fulfills Psalm 22:18. The mockery by passersby fulfills Psalm 22:7-8. The gall and sour wine fulfill Psalm 69:21. The darkness at noon fulfills Amos 8:9. The cry of dereliction quotes Psalm 22:1. The burial with the rich man fulfills Isaiah 53:9. The author presents the death of Jesus not as an isolated event but as the convergence point of Israel's entire prophetic tradition -- the righteous sufferer of the Psalms, the silent servant of Isaiah, and the rejected shepherd of Zechariah all find their referent in the same person at the same moment.

4. **It presents Jesus's death as an act that alters the structure of Israel's worship.** The tearing of the temple curtain at the moment of Jesus's death (v. 51) signals that the mediating function of the temple -- its system of sacrifice, priesthood, and restricted access to God's presence -- has been superseded. The curtain that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, through which only the high priest passed once a year, is torn by divine action ("from top to bottom"). The covenant blood of 26:28, poured out "for the forgiveness of sins," achieves what the temple sacrifices pointed toward. The temple whose destruction Jesus predicted in 24:2 is already rendered functionally obsolete at the moment of his death, before its physical destruction occurs forty years later.

5. **It makes blood guilt a central narrative thread.** The chapter is structured around the motif of blood: the "blood money" (time haimatos, v. 6), the "Field of Blood" (agros haimatos, v. 8), Pilate's declaration of innocence from "this man's blood" (v. 24), and the crowd's cry "His blood be on us and on our children" (v. 25). Each reference connects to the warning of 23:35-36: "Upon you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth... all these things will come upon this generation." The blood of Jesus, declared innocent by every witness, is the culmination of the righteous blood that has been shed throughout Israel's history. The crowd's acceptance of the blood guilt is presented by the author as the final act in the pattern of prophet-killing that Jesus denounced in chapter 23. The consequences will fall upon "this generation," as Jesus stated.

6. **It presents the centurion's confession as a turning point in the document's ethnic scope.** The Gentile soldiers' confession "Truly this was the Son of God" at the foot of the cross anticipates the universal commission of 28:19 ("make disciples of all nations"). The pattern has been building throughout the document: the Gentile magi worshipped in chapter 2, the centurion at Capernaum demonstrated faith exceeding anything in Israel (8:10), the Canaanite woman's faith was called "great" (15:28), and Jesus predicted that "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven" (8:11). At the cross, the transition becomes explicit: Israel's leaders mock, while Gentile executioners confess. The death of Jesus breaks open the boundary of Israel's covenant to include the nations -- the same dynamic that the torn curtain enacts in the cultic sphere.

7. **It prepares for the resurrection by establishing that the death was real, witnessed, and secured against fraud.** The author provides multiple layers of verification: the death is observed by the centurion and soldiers (v. 54), by the women who followed from Galilee (vv. 55-56), and by Joseph of Arimathea who handles the body (vv. 57-60). The burial location is witnessed by Mary Magdalene and the other Mary (v. 61). The tomb is sealed and guarded by Roman soldiers at the request of the chief priests (vv. 62-66). Every potential objection to the resurrection -- that Jesus did not really die, that the body was moved to an unknown location, that the disciples stole it -- is addressed by the narrative itself before the resurrection is reported. The chapter functions as the necessary foundation for chapter 28: the more verified the death, the more significant the empty tomb.
