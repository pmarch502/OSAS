# 2 Corinthians 3 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (2 Corinthians 2:14-17):** After recounting his anxious movements from Troas to Macedonia in search of Titus (2:12-13), Paul broke into a thanksgiving that launched a major section on apostolic ministry: "But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads the fragrance of the knowledge of him everywhere" (2:14). Paul described himself and his co-workers as "the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing, to one a fragrance from death to death, to the other a fragrance from life to life" (2:15-16). He then posed a question that governs everything that follows: "Who is sufficient for these things?" (2:16b). The chapter closed with Paul distinguishing himself from "the many" (hoi polloi) who "peddle the word of God" (kapeleuontes ton logon tou theou). Unlike them, Paul speaks "as from sincerity, as from God, in the sight of God, in Christ" (2:17). The question of sufficiency is left hanging. The contrast with peddlers of God's word is asserted but not yet grounded. Both threads carry directly into chapter 3.

**What the author argues immediately after (2 Corinthians 4:1-6):** Chapter 4 opens, "Therefore, having this ministry by the mercy of God, we do not lose heart" (4:1). The phrase "this ministry" refers back to the new covenant ministry described in chapter 3. Paul then extends the themes of transparency and concealment: "We have renounced disgraceful, underhanded ways. We refuse to practice cunning or to tamper with God's word, but by the open statement of the truth we would commend ourselves to everyone's conscience in the sight of God" (4:2). The openness (parresia) Paul claimed in 3:12 is restated in 4:2. Then the veil imagery of chapter 3 reappears: "Even if our gospel is veiled, it is veiled to those who are perishing. In their case the god of this age has blinded the minds of the unbelievers, to keep them from seeing the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God" (4:3-4). The same vocabulary -- veil, glory, image (eikon) -- flows directly from chapter 3 into chapter 4, proving that Paul has not changed subjects. Chapter 4 continues what chapter 3 began, and chapter 3 answers what chapter 2 asked.

**How chapter 3 connects the two:** Chapter 3 occupies a specific position in Paul's argument. At the end of chapter 2, Paul asked "Who is sufficient?" and contrasted himself with those who peddle God's word. Chapter 3 answers the sufficiency question (3:4-6: "our sufficiency is from God"), identifies what Paul's ministry actually is (a ministry of the new covenant, not of the letter but of the Spirit, 3:6), demonstrates the superiority of that ministry through comparison with the Sinai covenant (3:7-11), and explains the consequence of that superiority -- a boldness characterized by unveiled openness rather than concealment (3:12-18). Chapter 4 then draws the conclusion: "Therefore, having this ministry..." The argument moves in an unbroken line from the question of sufficiency in 2:16, through the answer in 3:5-6, through the covenantal comparison in 3:7-11, through the veil midrash in 3:12-18, and into the application in 4:1-6. There is no change of subject, no interruption. Chapter 3 is the theological center of a sustained argument about the nature and character of Paul's apostolic ministry.

## Verses 1-3: The Corinthians as Paul's Letter

"Are we beginning to commend ourselves again? Or do we need, as some do, letters of recommendation to you, or from you?" (3:1).

The question responds to the self-commendation language that has been building since 2:14. Paul has been describing his ministry in exalted terms -- the triumphal procession, the aroma of Christ, speech from sincerity in the sight of God. He anticipates the objection: "There you go again, commending yourself." The word synistanein ("to commend, to recommend, to establish credibility") is a technical term for the ancient practice of introducing someone with an endorsement. Paul will return to this verb repeatedly in the letter (4:2, 5:12, 6:4, 10:12, 10:18, 12:11), each time wrestling with the tension between the need to defend his ministry and the inappropriateness of self-promotion.

"Letters of recommendation" (systatikai epistolai) were standard practice in the ancient world. A person arriving in a new city would carry a letter from a known figure vouching for their character and credentials. In Acts 18:27, when Apollos wished to cross from Ephesus to Achaia, "the brothers encouraged him and wrote to the disciples to welcome him." Paul himself commended Phoebe to the Romans (Romans 16:1-2). The practice was normal and legitimate. But Paul's question implies that "some" (tines) -- unnamed persons -- have come to Corinth bearing such letters, or have required them. These are almost certainly the rival teachers whose presence has been hinted at since the letter began. They arrived with credentials from elsewhere; Paul arrived with none, having founded the community himself.

The phrase "or from you" (e ex hymon) suggests the further possibility that the rivals have asked the Corinthians to provide letters on their behalf -- using the Corinthian community as a base from which to establish credentials elsewhere. Either way, the letters-of-recommendation question positions Paul against rivals who rely on external credentials.

"You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts, to be known and read by all" (3:2).

Paul's answer bypasses the credential system entirely. He does not produce a counter-letter; he declares that the Corinthians themselves are his letter. The community's existence as a believing assembly is the evidence of Paul's legitimate ministry. No external document could say more than the living reality of the community Paul founded.

"Written on our hearts" (engegrammenē en tais kardiais hēmōn) -- the letter is inscribed not on papyrus but on hearts. Some manuscripts read "your hearts" (hymōn) rather than "our hearts" (hēmōn), and the textual variation is significant. If "our hearts," the Corinthians are inscribed in Paul's heart -- he carries them with him wherever he goes, and anyone who encounters Paul encounters his love for this community. If "your hearts," the inscription is in the Corinthians themselves -- they bear the evidence within. Either reading works within the argument; both point to an internal, living reality rather than an external document.

"Known and read by all" (ginōskomenē kai anaginōskomenē hypo pantōn anthrōpōn) -- the wordplay between ginōskō ("to know") and anaginōskō ("to read") echoes the wordplay in 1:13 (anaginōskete/epiginōskete). The Corinthians are a public document. Their transformed lives are legible to anyone who encounters them. The letter of recommendation Paul carries is not hidden in a pouch; it is visible in the community itself.

"And you show that you are a letter of Christ, ministered by us, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of hearts of flesh" (3:3).

The sentence shifts the author of the letter. In verse 2, the Corinthians were "our letter" -- Paul's letter, the evidence of his ministry. Now they are "a letter of Christ" (epistolē Christou). The genitive Christou identifies Christ as the author: Christ wrote this letter; Paul merely served as the deliverer (diakonētheisa hyph' hēmōn, "ministered/delivered by us"). The verb diakoneō ("to serve, to minister, to deliver") is the root of diakonia ("ministry, service") and diakonos ("servant, minister"), words that will dominate the rest of this chapter and the next. Paul's role is not authorship but service: Christ writes; Paul delivers.

Two contrasts define the medium and surface of this letter.

First contrast: "not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God" (ou melani alla pneumati theou zōntos). The medium of this writing is not ink (melan, "black [fluid]") but God's Spirit. The designation "the living God" (theos zōn) carries deep roots in Israel's scriptures. Deuteronomy 5:26: "For who is there of all flesh, that has heard the voice of the living God (theou zōntos) speaking out of the midst of fire, as we have, and has still lived?" Joshua 3:10: "By this you shall know that the living God (theos zōn) is among you." 1 Samuel 17:26, 36: David identifies Goliath's offense as defying "the armies of the living God." Psalm 42:2 (LXX 41:3): "My soul thirsts for God, for the living God." Hosea 1:10 (LXX 2:1): "And in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not my people,' it shall be said to them, 'Children of the living God.'" The designation consistently distinguishes Israel's God from the dead, inert, manufactured gods of the surrounding nations. The Spirit of this living God is the medium of the writing on the Corinthians -- not the dead product of pen and ink but the active, dynamic power of the God who lives.

Second contrast: "not on tablets of stone but on tablets of hearts of flesh" (ouk en plaxin lithinais all' en plaxin kardiais sarkinais). This phrase fuses two strands of Israel's prophetic tradition.

The "tablets of stone" (plakes lithinai) recall Sinai. Exodus 31:18: "And he gave to Moses, when he had finished speaking with him on Mount Sinai, the two tablets of the testimony, tablets of stone, written with the finger of God" (plakes lithinai gegrammenai tō daktylō tou theou). Exodus 34:1: "Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the first." Deuteronomy 9:10: "The LORD gave me the two tablets of stone written with the finger of God." The stone tablets are the medium of the Sinai covenant -- the Torah inscribed by God's own finger on stone.

The "hearts of flesh" (kardiai sarkinai) recall the prophetic promise of Israel's inner renewal. Ezekiel 11:19: "I will remove the heart of stone from their flesh and give them a heart of flesh (kardian sarkinēn)." Ezekiel 36:26: "And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh (kardian sarkinēn)." In Ezekiel, the replacement of stone hearts with flesh hearts is part of the restoration prophecy: God will gather Israel from exile, cleanse them, and put his Spirit within them so that they walk in his statutes (Ezekiel 36:24-27). Jeremiah makes the parallel promise in different terms: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33, LXX 38:33).

Paul's phrase "tablets of hearts of flesh" (plaxin kardiais sarkinais) is a composite image. It merges the tablets from Exodus with the hearts from Ezekiel. The stone tablets from Sinai, inscribed with God's finger, are replaced by flesh hearts, inscribed with God's Spirit. The Corinthian community is the fulfillment of the prophetic vision: the law of God is no longer on external stone but internal, inscribed by the Spirit on living hearts. The community itself -- its existence, its transformation, its new life -- is the evidence that the promises of Jeremiah and Ezekiel have been realized.

The theological move Paul makes in verse 3 is subtle but decisive. He has not yet said the words "old covenant" or "new covenant." But by placing the stone tablets of Sinai alongside the flesh hearts of Ezekiel, he has already set up the covenantal contrast that will govern the rest of the chapter. The two surfaces -- stone and flesh -- represent two covenantal arrangements: the one given at Sinai and the one promised by the prophets. And the Corinthians, as a letter written on hearts of flesh by the Spirit of the living God, are the living proof that the promised arrangement has arrived.

## Verses 4-6: Sufficiency from God and the Ministry of the New Covenant

"Such is the confidence that we have through Christ toward God" (3:4).

The word pepoithēsis ("confidence, trust, reliance") echoes pepoithēsis from 1:15, where Paul's confidence in the mutual relationship prompted his original travel plan. Here the confidence is directed "through Christ toward God" (dia tou Christou pros ton theon). Christ is the mediator of Paul's confidence; God is its object. Paul's confidence is not in himself, not in his eloquence, not in his credentials, but in what God has done through Christ -- namely, the creation of the Corinthian community as evidence of a real, effective ministry.

"Not that we are sufficient in ourselves to claim anything as coming from us, but our sufficiency is from God" (3:5).

This verse answers the question left hanging since 2:16: "Who is sufficient for these things?" (tis pros tauta hikanos). The answer: no one, by their own resources. Paul denies self-sufficiency with precision: "Not that we are sufficient from ourselves to reckon anything as from ourselves" (ouch hoti aph' heautōn hikanoi esmen logisasthai ti hōs ex heautōn). The double use of "from ourselves" (aph' heautōn, ex heautōn) is emphatic. Paul cannot even take credit for his ability to think rightly (logisasthai, "to reckon, to consider, to account") about these matters, let alone accomplish them. The sufficiency (hikanotēs, "adequacy, competence, qualification") comes entirely from God (hē hikanotēs hēmōn ek tou theou).

The word hikanos carries an echo from Israel's foundational story. When God called Moses at the burning bush, Moses protested his inadequacy: "Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh?" (Exodus 3:11). In the LXX, God's response to Moses' inadequacy was to assure him of divine presence: "I will be with you" (Exodus 3:12). The pattern is ancient: the one called is not sufficient; God supplies the sufficiency. Paul stands in this lineage, and the Moses connection, once introduced here, will dominate the rest of the chapter.

"Who has made us sufficient to be ministers of a new covenant, not of the letter but of the Spirit. For the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (3:6).

God is the subject: God "made us sufficient" (hikanōsen hēmas) -- the verb hikanoo, "to make adequate, to qualify, to equip." God is the one who qualifies Paul for ministry. The ministry is specified: "ministers of a new covenant" (diakonous kainēs diathēkēs). The word diakonos ("servant, minister, attendant") describes a role of service, not authority. Paul is a servant of something larger than himself: a new covenant.

The phrase "new covenant" (kainē diathēkē) comes directly from Jeremiah. Jeremiah 31:31 (LXX 38:31): "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant (diathēkēn kainēn) with the house of Israel and the house of Judah." The Jeremiah passage specifies what makes this covenant "new": "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. And no longer shall each one teach his neighbor and each his brother, saying, 'Know the LORD,' for they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest, declares the LORD. For I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (Jeremiah 31:33-34, LXX 38:33-34). The new covenant in Jeremiah is characterized by internalized instruction, direct knowledge of God, and comprehensive forgiveness. Paul has already shown in verse 3 that the first of these -- the internalized instruction, the writing on hearts -- has been realized in the Corinthian community.

The new covenant is further defined by a contrast: "not of the letter but of the Spirit" (ou grammatos alla pneumatos). The word gramma ("letter, written character, written document") here denotes the written code of the Sinai covenant -- the Torah as inscribed text, the commandments carved on stone tablets. The word pneuma ("spirit, breath, wind") denotes the Spirit of God whose activity Paul has just described (the Spirit who writes on hearts, 3:3). The contrast is not between two methods of reading the same text (literal vs. spiritual interpretation). It is between two covenantal arrangements: one defined by external written commandment, the other defined by the indwelling Spirit.

"For the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (to gar gramma apoktennei, to de pneuma zōopoiei). The verb apoktennei ("kills") and the verb zōopoiei ("makes alive, gives life") describe the effects of these two covenantal realities. The written code kills because it states the requirement but does not supply the power to meet it. It defines transgression and thereby condemns the transgressor. This is not a criticism of the Torah's content -- Paul calls the law holy, righteous, and good elsewhere (Romans 7:12). It is a statement about the limitation of written commandment as a covenantal instrument: the commandment can diagnose sin but cannot cure it; it can specify obedience but cannot produce it. The Spirit, by contrast, gives life -- the verb zōopoieō is used in the LXX and in Paul's other letters for God's life-giving power. In 1 Corinthians 15:45, the risen Christ is called "a life-giving spirit" (pneuma zōopoioun). The Spirit does what the written code could not: it produces the obedience the law demanded, from within. This is precisely what Ezekiel prophesied: "I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (Ezekiel 36:27).

## Verses 7-11: The Greater Glory -- Two Ministries Compared

Paul now develops a sustained comparison between the ministry associated with the Sinai covenant and the ministry associated with the new covenant. The comparison is built on a series of qal wahomer arguments -- "from the lesser to the greater" (a fortiori) -- a standard form of rabbinic reasoning. The word doxa ("glory, radiance, splendor, weight, honor") appears in nearly every clause. This is the most concentrated use of the word "glory" in any single passage in Paul's letters.

"Now if the ministry of death, carved in letters on stone, came with such glory that the Israelites could not gaze at Moses' face because of its glory, which was being brought to an end" (3:7).

Paul identifies the Sinai covenant ministry with startling directness: "the ministry of death" (hē diakonia tou thanatou). The service mediated by the stone tablets is called a ministry that produces death. This is not an insult to Moses or to the Torah; it is a statement about the covenantal effect of written commandment upon a transgressing people. The written code, by defining what is forbidden, brings the knowledge of sin (Romans 3:20) and thereby the sentence of death upon those who violate it. The Torah itself testifies to this: Deuteronomy 30:15, 19 set before Israel "life and death, blessing and curse," and the narrative that follows demonstrates that Israel consistently chose the path that led to death and curse. The stone tablets are magnificent in origin -- carved by the finger of God (Exodus 31:18) -- but the covenantal arrangement they represent, operating upon Israel as Israel actually was, produced death.

"Carved in letters on stone" (en grammasin entetypōmenē lithois) -- the phrase echoes the "tablets of stone" from verse 3 and reinforces the gramma ("letter") from verse 6. The stone-carving language points directly to Sinai: Exodus 32:16 (LXX): "The tablets were the work of God, and the writing was the writing of God, engraved (kekolammenē) on the tablets."

Despite being a ministry of death, the Sinai ministry "came with glory" (egenēthē en doxē). The glory was real. Exodus 34:29-30 (LXX): "When Moses came down from the mountain, the two tablets of the testimony were in the hands of Moses. As he came down from the mountain, Moses did not know that the skin of his face had been glorified (dedoxastai hē opsis tou chrōmatos tou prosōpou autou) when God spoke to him. Aaron and all the sons of Israel saw Moses, and behold, the skin of his face had been glorified (dedoxastai), and they were afraid to come near him." The glory on Moses' face was real, visible, and terrifying. Paul does not deny this glory; he affirms it as the basis for his argument. If the inferior arrangement came with glory, how much more the superior one?

"Which was being brought to an end" (tēn katargoumenēn) -- the participle katargoumenēn ("being rendered ineffective, being annulled, fading") describes the glory on Moses' face. The verb katargeō means "to render inactive, to bring to an end, to abolish, to make of no effect." Paul used the word in 1 Corinthians 13:8-10 to describe prophecy, tongues, and knowledge being superseded when "the perfect" comes. In 1 Corinthians 2:6, he spoke of the rulers of this age who "are being brought to nothing" (tōn katargoumenōn). Here, the participle describes the glory on Moses' face as transient -- it was in the process of being superseded, fading, brought to its termination. This is a crucial interpretive move by Paul: the Exodus narrative itself does not explicitly state that the glory faded. Paul reads the glory's temporariness as implied by the fact that Moses veiled it. He will develop this reading further in verses 12-13.

"How will not the ministry of the Spirit be even more in glory?" (3:8).

The qal wahomer turns on the contrast between "the ministry of death" (diakonia tou thanatou) and "the ministry of the Spirit" (diakonia tou pneumatos). If the ministry that produced death came with real, visible, terrifying glory, how much more must the ministry characterized by the Spirit's life-giving power surpass it in glory? The question expects the obvious answer: immeasurably more. The logic is not: "the old had glory, the new has no glory." It is: "the old had glory; therefore the new must have incomparably greater glory."

"For if there was glory in the ministry of condemnation, the ministry of righteousness must far exceed it in glory" (3:9).

A second qal wahomer, restating the first with new terms. The "ministry of death" is now called "the ministry of condemnation" (hē diakonia tēs katakrisēos). The word katakrisis ("condemnation, judgment against") describes the covenantal function of the written code: it condemns those who fail to keep it. The contrasting term is "the ministry of righteousness" (hē diakonia tēs dikaiosynēs). The written code condemned; the Spirit's ministry produces righteousness (dikaiosynē). The word dikaiosynē here describes right standing, right relationship, the condition of being in right alignment with God's purposes. The ministry of the new covenant does not merely pardon transgression; it produces the righteousness that the written code demanded but could not achieve.

The verb perisseeuei ("exceeds, abounds, overflows") is the same verb Paul used in 1:5 for the overflow of both suffering and comfort. Just as comfort overflowed in proportion to suffering, so the glory of the Spirit's ministry overflows in proportion to -- and far beyond -- the glory of the written code.

"Indeed, in this case, what once had glory has come to have no glory at all, because of the glory that surpasses it" (3:10).

The comparison reaches its sharpest expression. The old glory has not been destroyed or denied; it has been eclipsed. The verb dedoxasmenon ("what has been glorified") acknowledges the reality of the old glory. But the phrase ou dedoxastai ("has not been glorified") does not mean the glory was never real. It means that in the presence of the surpassing glory (hē hyperballousa doxa), the old glory disappears from view. Paul does not use an analogy, but the logic is that of stars disappearing when the sun rises -- the stars have not ceased to exist, but their light is rendered invisible by the overwhelmingly greater light. The old covenant glory was genuine, but it is swallowed up by the new.

"For if what was being brought to an end came through glory, much more will what is permanent come through glory" (3:11).

A third qal wahomer. The old is described as to katargoumenon ("what is being brought to an end") and the new as to menon ("what remains, what is permanent, what abides"). The verb menō ("to remain, to abide, to endure") is a word of permanence. The old covenant arrangement was temporary by design -- the prophets themselves declared that a new covenant was coming (Jeremiah 31:31). The new arrangement is permanent: it abides. If the temporary came "through glory" (dia doxēs), the permanent comes "in glory" (en doxē). The prepositions may indicate a difference: the old came through glory as through a transient medium; the new exists in glory as its permanent condition.

The cumulative effect of verses 7-11 is to establish a single point through three overlapping arguments: the new covenant ministry, of which Paul is a servant, possesses an incomparably greater glory than the Sinai covenant ministry. This is not because the Sinai arrangement lacked glory -- it had real, visible, divine glory. It is because the new arrangement surpasses it in every dimension: life instead of death, righteousness instead of condemnation, permanence instead of transience, and a glory so overwhelming that it renders the former glory invisible by comparison.

## Verses 12-16: Boldness, the Veil, and Hardened Minds

"Since we have such a hope, we use great boldness" (3:12).

"Such a hope" (toiautēn elpida) -- the hope generated by the preceding argument: that Paul's ministry belongs to the permanent, surpassingly glorious new covenant. The word parrēsia ("boldness, openness, freedom of speech, confidence") was a civic virtue in the Greek world: the right of a free citizen to speak openly in the assembly. In the LXX, it appears in contexts of courageous speech before power. Paul claims parrēsia for his ministry: he operates with full openness, holding nothing back, concealing nothing.

This claim of openness responds directly to the charges against Paul that have been running since the letter began. If Paul has been accused of operating by cunning, by hidden agendas, by the kind of strategic concealment that characterized the sophists, he answers here: his ministry is characterized by boldness, not concealment. And he is about to contrast this boldness with Moses' concealment.

"Not like Moses, who would put a veil over his face so that the Israelites might not gaze at the end of what was being brought to an end" (3:13).

Paul now re-reads the veil narrative from Exodus 34. In the Exodus account, Moses veiled his face after speaking to the people because they were afraid of the radiance (Exodus 34:30, 33, 35). The Exodus text does not state the purpose of the veil as concealment of the glory's fading. Paul, however, reads the veil as serving a concealing function: Moses put on the veil "so that the Israelites might not gaze at the end (telos) of what was being brought to an end (tou katargoumenou)."

The word telos can mean "end" (termination), "goal" (purpose), or "outcome." In this context, the most natural reading is "termination" or "cessation": Moses veiled his face so that Israel would not see the fading, the ending, of the glory. The participial phrase tou katargoumenou ("of what was being brought to an end") uses the same verb (katargeō) that described the fading glory in verses 7 and 11. What was being brought to an end is the glory of the old covenant arrangement, which was temporary by design.

Paul's reading of the Exodus narrative is midrashic -- he does not simply repeat what Exodus says but interprets it, finding in the veil a meaning that the Exodus text does not make explicit. This interpretive method was standard in first-century Jewish practice: the Targums and the midrashic collections regularly find layers of meaning in narrative details that the original text leaves unexplored. Paul's innovation is to read the veil not as a concession to Israel's fear (which is the Exodus explanation) but as a concealment of the old covenant's temporary nature. Moses himself, in Paul's reading, recognized the transience of the glory he bore and shielded Israel from seeing its end.

The contrast with Paul's parrēsia is now complete. Moses veiled; Paul does not. Moses concealed the fading of the old glory; Paul openly displays the surpassing glory of the new covenant. The point is not to criticize Moses but to demonstrate that Paul's ministry, because it belongs to the permanent and surpassing covenant, has no need for veiling. What Paul mediates does not fade.

"But their minds were hardened" (3:14a).

"Their minds" (ta noēmata autōn) -- the pronoun "their" (autōn) refers to the Israelites, the "sons of Israel" (hoi huioi Israēl) from verse 13. The word noēma ("mind, thought, understanding, mental disposition") describes the faculty of perception and comprehension. The verb epōrōthē ("were hardened, were petrified, were made callous") is the aorist passive of pōroō, which means "to harden, to cover with a callous, to petrify." The same verb describes the hardening of Pharaoh's heart throughout the Exodus narrative: Pharaoh's heart was hardened (esklerynthē/epōrōthē) so that he would not let Israel go. Now Paul applies the same hardening to Israel itself: their minds were hardened, petrified, made insensible to what was being revealed.

The passive voice ("were hardened") leaves the agent unspecified. Paul does not say here who hardened their minds -- whether God hardened them (as God hardened Pharaoh), or whether they hardened themselves through refusal. The point is the result: a hardened, insensible condition that persists.

"For to this day, when they read the old covenant, that same veil remains unlifted, because only through Christ is it taken away -- or rather, only in Christ is it brought to an end" (3:14b).

"To this day" (achri gar tēs sēmeron hēmeras) -- Paul extends the situation from the Sinai generation to his own present. The hardening was not a one-time event; it has continued. The veil that was on Moses' face has become, in Paul's reading, a veil over the minds of those who hear the old covenant read.

"The old covenant" (hē palaia diathēkē) -- this is the first time in Paul's letters that this phrase appears. The adjective palaia ("old, ancient, former") implies its complement: if there is an old covenant, there must be a new one. The new covenant was named in verse 6; the old is named here. The "reading" (anaginōskō, the same verb from 1:13 and 3:2) refers to the public reading of the Torah in synagogue worship, where the scrolls of Moses are read aloud on the Sabbath (cf. Acts 15:21: "For from ancient generations Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him, for he is read every Sabbath in the synagogues").

"That same veil remains unlifted" (to auto kalymma... mē anakalyptomenon) -- the veil from the Moses narrative has become a metaphor. What was literal in Exodus 34 is now a description of a spiritual condition: an inability to perceive what is actually happening when the old covenant scriptures are read. The text that tells Israel's story -- including the promises of the new covenant, the prophecies of renewal, the pledges of divine faithfulness -- is heard through a veil that prevents full comprehension.

"Because it is in Christ that it is brought to an end" (hoti en Christō katargeitai) -- the veil is katargeitai ("brought to an end, rendered ineffective, abolished") in Christ. The same verb used for the fading glory (vv. 7, 11, 13) now describes the removal of the veil. What abolishes the veil is not human effort, intellectual achievement, or better exegetical method -- it is Christ. The veil is annulled in Christ. For those who are in Christ, the veil has been lifted; the old covenant scriptures can be read and understood in their fullness, because the one to whom they pointed has come.

"Yes, to this day whenever Moses is read, a veil lies over their hearts" (3:15).

The repetition of "to this day" (heōs sēmeron) underscores the present reality of the situation. "Moses" here stands for the Torah -- the five books attributed to Moses, read publicly in the synagogue. The veil has shifted location one more time: it was on Moses' face (Exodus 34), then over their minds (v. 14), and now over their hearts (epi tēn kardian autōn). The heart (kardia) in the LXX is the center of understanding, will, and moral perception -- not merely emotion but the whole inner person. The veil lies over the very faculty by which comprehension occurs.

"But when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed" (3:16).

The sentence echoes Exodus 34:34 (LXX): "And whenever Moses went in before the Lord to speak with him, he would remove the veil" (perieireito to kalymma). In the Exodus narrative, Moses removed the veil when he entered the Lord's presence. Paul takes this detail and universalizes it: whenever anyone (hēnika de ean epistrepsē -- the subject is indefinite: "whenever one turns") turns to the Lord, the veil is removed. What was true of Moses alone -- unveiled access to the divine presence -- becomes available to anyone who turns.

The verb epistrephō ("to turn, to return") is the standard LXX rendering of the Hebrew shuv, the fundamental prophetic word for repentance and return. Hosea 14:1 (LXX 14:2): "Return (epistrephon), O Israel, to the LORD your God, for you have stumbled because of your iniquity." Joel 2:12: "Return (epistrepsate) to me with all your heart." Malachi 3:7: "Return (epistrepsate) to me, and I will return to you, says the LORD of hosts." The turning Paul describes is the turning the prophets called for -- a turning away from hardness and toward the Lord. When that turning happens, the veil is taken away (periaireitai to kalymma). The verb periaireo ("to take away, to remove from around") is the same verb used in Exodus 34:34 (LXX) for Moses removing his veil. Paul's echo of the Exodus text is deliberate: the very action that Moses performed physically when he entered God's presence -- the removal of the veil -- now happens spiritually when anyone turns to the Lord.

---

## Verses 17-18: The Lord, the Spirit, Freedom, and Transformation

"Now the Lord is the Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom" (3:17).

This verse has generated enormous interpretive energy, but in context its function is specific. Paul has just quoted/echoed Exodus 34:34: when Moses went in "before the Lord" (enanti kyriou), the veil was removed. Paul then universalized this: when anyone turns "to the Lord" (pros kyrion), the veil is removed. Now he identifies who this "Lord" (kyrios) is, in the experience of the new covenant: "the Lord is the Spirit" (ho de kyrios to pneuma estin).

The identification is exegetical, not metaphysical. Paul is reading the Exodus text and saying: the Lord before whom the veil was removed -- the Lord to whom one turns for unveiled access -- is, in the new covenant experience, encountered as the Spirit. This is consistent with everything Paul has been arguing in the chapter: the new covenant is "not of the letter but of the Spirit" (v. 6); the new ministry is "the ministry of the Spirit" (v. 8); the Spirit writes on hearts of flesh (v. 3). The Lord who was present on Sinai in fire, cloud, and thundering voice is now present through the Spirit who writes on hearts and gives life. The identification does not collapse distinct realities into one; it identifies the mode by which the Lord of the Exodus narrative is now accessed and experienced.

"And where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom" (hou de to pneuma kyriou, eleutheria). The word eleutheria ("freedom, liberty") contrasts with the veil, the hardening, and the inability to perceive. Those whose hearts are veiled are not free -- they cannot see what is before them, cannot comprehend the scriptures they hear, cannot perceive the glory that has come. Where the Spirit is present, the veil is gone, and freedom replaces bondage to incomprehension. This is not "freedom" in the abstract but freedom from the specific condition Paul has just described: veiled, hardened, unable to see. The Spirit removes the veil and produces the freedom to see, to understand, to approach God without barrier.

Paul used eleutheria in 1 Corinthians in the context of freedom in Christ that must be exercised with care (chapters 8-10). The word also appears prominently in the related letter to the Galatians: "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery" (Galatians 5:1). In both cases, freedom is freedom from a specific bondage: in Galatians, freedom from subjection to the law as a covenantal system that holds people under guardianship (Galatians 3:23-25). Here in 2 Corinthians, freedom from the veiled, hardened condition that prevents understanding. The Spirit is the agent of this freedom in both letters.

"And we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as from the Lord who is the Spirit" (3:18).

This is the climax of the chapter. Every thread Paul has been developing converges here.

"We all" (hēmeis de pantes) -- the pronoun shifts. In verses 1-6, "we" referred primarily to Paul and his co-workers as ministers. In the veil discussion (vv. 12-16), "we" was Paul as minister, contrasted with Moses. Now "we all" expands the reference to include the entire community of those who have turned to the Lord. Paul, the Corinthians, and all who belong to the new covenant are the subject of what follows.

"With unveiled face" (anakekalymmenō prosōpō) -- the perfect passive participle of anakalyptō ("to uncover, to unveil, to remove a veil from") describes a condition: the face has been unveiled and remains unveiled. This stands in direct contrast to Moses, who veiled his face (v. 13), and to Israel, whose hearts remain veiled (vv. 14-15). Those who have turned to the Lord have had their veil removed (v. 16) and now remain in the unveiled condition permanently. Unlike Moses, who put the veil back on after speaking to the people, the community of the new covenant lives continuously unveiled before the Lord.

"Beholding the glory of the Lord" (tēn doxan kyriou katoptrizomenoi) -- the verb katoptrizomai is related to katoptron ("mirror"). In the middle voice, it can mean either "to behold as in a mirror" or "to reflect as a mirror does." Both senses may be present. The community beholds the Lord's glory -- not through a veil, not indirectly, not through the mediated system of the old covenant, but directly, face to face. The LXX precedent for such language is Numbers 12:8, where God says of Moses: "With him I speak mouth to mouth (stoma kata stoma), clearly and not in riddles, and he beholds the form (doxan) of the LORD." What was Moses' unique privilege -- beholding the Lord's glory directly -- is now the privilege of the entire community. The democratization of unveiled access to God's glory is the practical consequence of the new covenant.

"Are being transformed into the same image" (tēn autēn eikona metamorphoumetha) -- the verb metamorphoō ("to transform, to change in form, to transfigure") is in the present passive, indicating an ongoing process initiated by an external agent. The transformation is not instantaneous; it is progressive and continuous. The believers are being reshaped. The word eikōn ("image, likeness, representation") carries deep resonance. Genesis 1:26-27 (LXX): "Let us make humanity according to our image (kat' eikona hēmeteran) and according to our likeness." Humanity was made in the image of God; the transformation Paul describes is a restoration and deepening of that image-bearing. The "same image" (tēn autēn eikona) is the image of the Lord whose glory they behold. By seeing the glory, they are being reshaped into its likeness.

This is not mystical abstraction. It has a concrete referent in the Moses narrative: when Moses beheld God's glory on Sinai, his own face was transformed -- it became radiant, glorified (Exodus 34:29-30). Moses was changed by the encounter. Paul's argument is that the same dynamic operates in the new covenant, but with two crucial differences: first, the transformation is not limited to one mediator (Moses) but includes the whole community ("we all"); second, the transformation is not temporary (Moses' glory faded, v. 7) but progressive, moving "from glory to glory."

"From glory to glory" (apo doxēs eis doxan) -- the phrase describes a trajectory. The movement is from one degree of glory to another, an ongoing increase, a progressive transformation that does not reach a static endpoint in the present. The first "glory" may refer to the glory initially received when one turns to the Lord; the successive "glory" is the ever-increasing transformation into the Lord's image. The preposition apo ("from") and eis ("into, toward") describe a journey, not a destination. The community is in motion -- being moved from where they began toward a glory that keeps expanding.

"As from the Lord who is the Spirit" (kathaper apo kyriou pneumatos) -- the agent of the transformation is identified one final time. The phrase "the Lord who is the Spirit" repeats the identification from verse 17. The transformation is not accomplished by human effort, moral striving, or ascetic discipline. It is accomplished by the Lord, who is encountered and operative as the Spirit. The Spirit who writes on hearts (v. 3), who gives life (v. 6), whose ministry carries surpassing glory (v. 8), who produces freedom where present (v. 17) -- this same Spirit is the one who progressively transforms the community into the image of the Lord whose glory they behold.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

2 Corinthians 3 is a single, sustained argument that answers the question posed at the end of chapter 2: "Who is sufficient for these things?" The answer moves through four connected stages, each building on the one before.

**First (3:1-3): The Corinthians are Paul's credentials.** Paul does not need letters of recommendation from external authorities because the Corinthian community itself is his letter -- a letter authored by Christ, delivered by Paul, written by the Spirit of the living God on hearts of flesh rather than on tablets of stone. The very existence of the Corinthian assembly as a transformed community is the proof of Paul's legitimate ministry. By placing "hearts of flesh" alongside "tablets of stone," Paul has already introduced the covenantal contrast that will govern the rest of the chapter: the new covenant prophesied by Jeremiah and Ezekiel has arrived, and the Corinthians are its evidence.

**Second (3:4-6): Sufficiency comes from God, who has made Paul a servant of the new covenant.** Paul is not self-sufficient; his competence comes from God. And what God has qualified him for is specific: the ministry of a new covenant -- not of the written code that kills but of the Spirit who gives life. The terms of the two covenants are now explicit: gramma (the written law on stone) versus pneuma (the Spirit who writes on hearts); death (the effect of the written code on transgressors) versus life (the effect of the Spirit on those who receive it). The prophetic promises of Jeremiah 31, Ezekiel 36, and Ezekiel 11 are the scriptural foundation of this contrast.

**Third (3:7-11): The glory of the new covenant ministry surpasses the glory of the old.** Through a series of three qal wahomer (lesser-to-greater) arguments, Paul demonstrates that if the Sinai covenant -- a ministry of death and condemnation, with temporary glory -- came with genuine, visible, terrifying splendor, then the new covenant -- a ministry of the Spirit, righteousness, and permanence -- must possess incomparably greater glory. The old glory was real but transient; the new glory surpasses it so completely that the old, by comparison, has no glory at all, as stars are invisible in sunlight.

**Fourth (3:12-18): The consequence of surpassing glory is unveiled boldness and ongoing transformation.** Because the new covenant ministry belongs to the permanent and surpassingly glorious arrangement, Paul operates with parrēsia -- full openness, no concealment. Unlike Moses, who veiled his face to conceal the fading of the old glory, Paul has nothing to hide because what he mediates does not fade. The veil, however, remains over the hearts of those who have not turned to the Lord -- they hear the old covenant scriptures read but cannot perceive what those scriptures ultimately point toward. The veil is removed only in Christ. When anyone turns to the Lord, the veil comes off, and the Lord is encountered as the Spirit who brings freedom. The result, for the entire community of the new covenant, is a progressive, Spirit-driven transformation: with unveiled faces beholding the Lord's glory, they are being reshaped from one degree of glory to another into the very image of the Lord.

Several observations emerge from the chapter's argument.

**The continuity of the argument with its surroundings.** The chapter begins by answering the sufficiency question from 2:16, and chapter 4 draws the consequences: "Therefore, having this ministry by the mercy of God, we do not lose heart" (4:1). The veil imagery of 3:14-18 reappears in 4:3-4, where the veiled gospel and the blinded minds of unbelievers extend the same theme. The word eikōn from 3:18 recurs in 4:4, where Christ is called "the image of God" (eikōn tou theou). There is no break. The argument runs in an uninterrupted line from 2:14 through 4:6 at minimum.

**The pronoun tracking.** "We" shifts across the chapter. In 3:1-6, "we" is Paul and his co-workers, defending their ministry against those who peddle God's word and carry letters of recommendation. In 3:12-13, "we" is Paul in contrast to Moses, claiming parrēsia against Moses' veiling. In 3:18, "we all" expands to include the entire community -- everyone who has turned to the Lord. The shift is deliberate: what begins as a defense of Paul's apostolic credentials becomes a description of the entire community's participation in the new covenant reality. Paul's ministry is validated not because Paul is special but because what Paul serves -- the new covenant of the Spirit -- is the means by which God is transforming an entire community.

**The LXX background is structural, not decorative.** The chapter cannot be understood without Exodus 31-34 (the stone tablets, Moses' glory, the veil), Jeremiah 31:31-34 (the new covenant written on hearts), and Ezekiel 11:19 and 36:26-27 (the replacement of stone hearts with flesh hearts and the gift of the Spirit). Paul does not merely allude to these texts; he builds his entire argument on them. The Corinthians, as a community with the Spirit of the living God inscribed on their hearts of flesh, are the fulfillment of the Jeremiah-Ezekiel promises. The fading glory of Moses' face, the veil, and the stone tablets are the scriptural framework through which Paul interprets both the limitations of the old covenant and the surpassing character of the new. The chapter is an extended midrash -- an interpretive engagement with Israel's scriptures that draws out their implications for the present.

**The "letter kills" in context.** Paul's statement that "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (3:6) is not a general hermeneutical principle about how to read scripture (as though one should always seek a "spiritual" meaning behind the "literal" text). In context, it is a covenantal statement: the written code of the Sinai covenant, inscribed on stone, brought death because it defined transgression and condemned the transgressor without supplying the power to obey. The Spirit of the new covenant gives life because the Spirit produces from within what the external code demanded from without. The contrast is between two covenantal arrangements and their respective effects, not between two methods of reading.

**The veil and the hardened minds.** Paul's description of Israel's veiled hearts (3:14-15) extends the hardening motif from the Exodus narrative itself. Just as Pharaoh's heart was hardened so that he could not respond to what God was doing, Israel's minds were hardened so that they could not perceive the transient nature of the old covenant's glory or the arrival of the new. The veil remains "to this day" when Moses is read in the synagogue. This is not a dismissal of Jewish scripture reading but a diagnosis: without turning to the Lord, the veil prevents full comprehension. The scriptures themselves are not the problem; the veil is. When the veil is removed -- in Christ -- the scriptures become fully legible.

**The transformation is progressive and communal.** The climactic verse (3:18) describes an ongoing process, not a completed event. "Being transformed" (metamorphoumetha, present passive) is continuous. "From glory to glory" (apo doxēs eis doxan) describes a trajectory, not a fixed state. And the subject is "we all" (hēmeis pantes) -- the entire community, not isolated individuals. The transformation is something that happens to a people together, as they behold together the glory of the Lord. The community is in motion, being reshaped by the Spirit into the image of the Lord whose glory they behold. The destination is not described as reached; the movement is the present reality.

**The connection to the letter's larger defense.** The chapter's argument about Paul's sufficiency and the new covenant's surpassing glory serves the letter's overarching concern: the defense of Paul's apostolic legitimacy. Those who have questioned Paul's authority, who have arrived with letters of recommendation, who have measured Paul by the standards of rhetorical skill and personal impressiveness -- they are measuring by the wrong standard. Paul's ministry does not operate by the criteria of the old arrangement (external credentials, visible displays of power, impressive appearances). It operates by the criteria of the new: the Spirit's transformative work, the inscription on hearts, the progressive reshaping of a community into the Lord's image. The letter of recommendation Paul carries is not a document but a people. His sufficiency is not his own but God's. His glory is not fading but increasing. And his boldness is not the self-confidence of a skilled rhetorician but the parrēsia of one who mediates a covenant that has nothing to hide.
