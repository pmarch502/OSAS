# Revelation 20 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 19):** Chapter 19 opened with the heavenly response to Babylon's fall narrated in chapter 18. A great multitude in heaven shouted "Hallelujah!" (19:1), and the content of their praise was explicit: "His judgments are true and righteous; for he has judged the great harlot who was corrupting the earth with her immorality, and he has avenged the blood of his bond-servants on her" (19:2). The verb "has avenged" (exedikesen) answered the martyrs' cry from 6:10: "How long, O Lord, holy and true, will you refrain from judging and avenging our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" Four Hallelujahs punctuated the celebration (19:1, 3, 4, 6). The twenty-four elders and the four living creatures fell down and worshiped (19:4). A voice from the throne called all God's servants to praise (19:5). Then came the announcement: "The marriage of the Lamb has come and his bride has made herself ready" (19:7). The bride was "clothed in fine linen, bright and clean" -- identified as "the righteous acts of the saints" (19:8). John was told: "Write, 'Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb'" (19:9) -- the fourth of the document's seven beatitudes.

The chapter then turned to direct combat. Heaven opened, and a rider on a white horse appeared, called "Faithful and True" (19:11). His eyes were "a flame of fire" and on his head were "many diadems" (19:12) -- attributes drawn from the opening vision of the risen Christ (1:14: "his eyes like a flame of fire"; cf. 2:18). His robe was "dipped in blood" (19:13), recalling Isaiah 63:1-3 where God treads the nations in the winepress and their lifeblood stains his garments. His name was "the Word of God" (ho logos tou theou, 19:13). From his mouth came "a sharp sword, so that with it he may strike down the nations; and he will rule them with a rod of iron; and he treads the wine press of the fierce wrath of God, the Almighty" (19:15). The sharp sword from the mouth appeared in the opening vision (1:16; 2:12, 16) and the rod of iron quoted Psalm 2:9, the same passage used for the male child in 12:5 and the overcomer in 2:26-27. On his robe and thigh was written: "KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS" (19:16).

An angel standing in the sun then summoned the birds to "the great supper of God" (19:17) -- a grim counterpart to the marriage supper of the Lamb (19:9). The beast and the kings of the earth gathered their armies to make war against the rider (19:19). The result was swift and total: "The beast was seized, and with him the false prophet who performed the signs in his presence, by which he deceived those who had received the mark of the beast and those who worshiped his image; these two were thrown alive into the lake of fire which burns with brimstone" (19:20). The rest were killed by the sword from the rider's mouth, and the birds gorged on their flesh (19:21).

Chapter 19 thus accomplished two decisive actions. First, it celebrated Babylon's fall as the vindication of God's servants and the answer to the martyrs' prayer. Second, it destroyed two members of the counterfeit trinity that has dominated the document since chapter 13: the beast (the first beast from the sea, the agent of political-military oppression) and the false prophet (the second beast from the earth, the agent of religious deception). Both were thrown into the lake of fire. But one figure was conspicuously absent from this defeat: the dragon himself -- "the ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan" (12:9). The beast and the false prophet were the dragon's agents; they have been disposed of. But the dragon, the source of their power (13:2: "the dragon gave him his power and his throne and great authority"), remains at large. Chapter 20 addresses this unfinished business.

**What the author argues immediately after (Revelation 21-22):** Chapter 21 opens with a vision of comprehensive renewal: "Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth; for the first heaven and the first earth passed away, and there is no longer any sea" (21:1). The disappearance of the sea -- the realm of chaos from which the beast emerged (13:1) -- signals the end of the hostile order. "I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, made ready as a bride adorned for her husband" (21:2). A loud voice from the throne declares: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is among men, and he will dwell among them, and they shall be his people, and God himself will be among them" (21:3) -- the fulfillment of the covenant formula that runs from Leviticus 26:12 through Jeremiah 31:33 and Ezekiel 37:27. Then: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes; and there will no longer be any death; there will no longer be any mourning, or crying, or pain; the first things have passed away" (21:4) -- drawn from Isaiah 25:8 ("He will swallow up death for all time, and the Lord GOD will wipe tears away from all faces") and Isaiah 65:17-19 ("the former things will not be remembered or come to mind"). The one on the throne declares: "Behold, I am making all things new" (21:5).

The new Jerusalem is then described in lavish detail (21:9-22:5): a city of enormous dimensions, with twelve gates inscribed with the names of the twelve tribes and twelve foundation stones with the names of the twelve apostles (21:12-14), constructed of precious stones, with streets of gold. The city has no temple, "for the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (21:22). It has no sun or moon, "for the glory of God has illumined it, and its lamp is the Lamb" (21:23). The nations walk by its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory into it (21:24). Nothing unclean enters it, "but only those whose names are written in the Lamb's book of life" (21:27). The river of life flows from the throne, and the tree of life bears fruit monthly, with leaves "for the healing of the nations" (22:2). God's servants "will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads" (22:4). And: "They will reign forever and ever" (22:5).

The transition from chapter 20 to chapters 21-22 is the transition from judgment to new creation. Chapter 20 eliminates every remaining source of evil, death, and opposition: the dragon is bound and then destroyed (20:1-3, 7-10), the dead are judged (20:11-15), and Death and Hades themselves are thrown into the lake of fire (20:14). Only after every enemy has been dealt with -- Satan, the beast, the false prophet, Death, Hades, and every person whose name is not in the book of life -- does the new creation appear. The sequence is deliberate: the old order must be completely dismantled before the new order can be established. The new Jerusalem cannot descend while the dragon still prowls, while Death still holds its captives, while the books of judgment remain unopened. Chapter 20 is the necessary clearing of the ground; chapters 21-22 are the construction of the new.

**How chapter 20 connects the surrounding argument:** The flow from chapter 19 into chapter 20 is continuous and requires no change of subject. Chapter 19 destroyed the beast and the false prophet. Chapter 20 deals with the dragon who empowered them both. The sequence follows the order in which these figures were introduced in reverse: the dragon appeared first (chapter 12), the first beast second (chapter 13:1-10), the second beast/false prophet third (chapter 13:11-18). They are destroyed in reverse order: the false prophet and beast first (19:20), the dragon last (20:10). The author systematically eliminates the counterfeit trinity that has opposed God's purposes since the cosmic conflict was unveiled in chapter 12.

The chapter also resolves the document's longest-running tension: the fate of the faithful dead. In 6:9-11, the souls under the altar cried out for vindication and were told to "rest a little while longer." In 14:13, the dead who die in the Lord were declared blessed, their deeds following them. In 20:4-6, the faithful dead -- specifically those beheaded for their testimony -- come to life and reign with Christ. The rest that was promised, the vindication that was requested, and the blessedness that was declared are now enacted. The martyrs are not merely remembered; they are raised and enthroned.

The chapter further resolves the question posed in 6:17: "The great day of their wrath has come, and who is able to stand?" The answer unfolds progressively through the seals, trumpets, and bowls, but reaches its final statement in 20:11-15: no one stands before the great white throne on the basis of their own ability to withstand divine wrath. They stand or fall on the basis of what is written in the books and whether their names appear in the book of life.

## Verses 1-3: The Binding of the Dragon

"Then I saw an angel coming down from heaven, holding the key of the abyss and a great chain in his hand. And he laid hold of the dragon, the serpent of old, who is the devil and Satan, and bound him for a thousand years; and he threw him into the abyss, and shut it and sealed it over him, so that he would not deceive the nations any longer, until the thousand years were completed; after these things he must be released for a short time."

**"Then I saw an angel coming down from heaven, holding the key of the abyss and a great chain in his hand" (kai eidon angelon katabainonta ek tou ouranou, echonta tēn klein tēs abyssou kai halysin megalēn epi tēn cheira autou).** The phrase kai eidon ("and I saw") marks a new visionary unit, continuous with what precedes. The angel descends from heaven -- the movement is from the seat of authority to the domain of the conflict. The angel carries two objects: the key of the abyss and a great chain.

The "key of the abyss" (tēn klein tēs abyssou) has appeared before in this document. In 9:1, "a star from heaven which had fallen to the earth" was given "the key of the shaft of the abyss" (hē kleis tou phreatos tēs abyssou), and the star opened the abyss, releasing smoke and the locust-plague. In 1:18, the risen Christ declared: "I have the keys of death and of Hades" (tas kleis tou thanatou kai tou hadou). Keys in this document represent authority over access -- the power to open and shut, to release and confine. The key of the abyss grants authority over the abyss itself: the power to open it (as in 9:1-2) or to lock it shut (as here in 20:1-3). In 3:7, the risen Christ identified himself as the one "who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut, and who shuts and no one opens" -- language drawn from Isaiah 22:22, indicating absolute authority over access. The key in the angel's hand is delegated authority from the one who holds all keys.

The abyss (abyssos) in this document is the place from which hostile, chaotic powers emerge. In 9:1-2, 11, the abyss is the source of the demonic locust-plague, and Abaddon/Apollyon is "the angel of the abyss." In 11:7, "the beast that comes up out of the abyss" (to thērion to anabainon ek tēs abyssou) kills the two witnesses. In 17:8, the beast is described as the one that "was, and is not, and is about to come up out of the abyss and go to destruction" (ek tēs abyssou kai eis apōleian hypagei). The abyss in the LXX translates the Hebrew tehom -- the primordial deep of Genesis 1:2 ("darkness was over the surface of the deep [abyssou]"), the waters of chaos that God mastered and ordered. In Psalm 71:20 (LXX 70:20): "You who have shown me many troubles and distresses will revive me again, and will bring me up again from the depths (abyssōn) of the earth." In Psalm 107:26 (LXX 106:26): "They mounted up to the heavens, they went down to the depths (abyssous)." The abyss is the cosmic opposite of heaven -- the deep place of confinement, chaos, and imprisonment. The angel carries the key to lock the dragon in this place.

The "great chain" (halysin megalēn) is a restraining instrument. Chains in the LXX and early Jewish literature are the instruments by which hostile spiritual powers are confined. In Jude 6: "Angels who did not keep their own domain, but abandoned their proper abode, he has kept in eternal bonds (desmois aidiois) under darkness for the judgment of the great day." In 2 Peter 2:4: "God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into Tartarus and committed them to chains (seirais) of darkness, reserved for judgment." In 1 Enoch 10:4-6, Raphael is commanded to bind Azazel hand and foot and cast him into the darkness: "Make an opening in the desert which is in Dudael, and cast him therein. And place upon him rough and jagged rocks, and cover him with darkness, and let him abide there forever." In 1 Enoch 10:11-12, Michael is told to "bind Shemhazah and his associates who have united themselves with women... Bind them fast for seventy generations in the valleys of the earth, until the day of their judgment." The binding of rebellious spiritual powers is a well-established tradition in Jewish apocalyptic literature. The angel's action in Revelation 20 follows this pattern: the dragon is seized, chained, and confined.

**"And he laid hold of the dragon, the serpent of old, who is the devil and Satan" (kai ekratēsen ton drakonta, ton ophin ton archaion, hos estin diabolos kai ho Satanas).** The dragon is identified by four names, the same fourfold identification given in 12:9: "the great dragon was thrown down, the serpent of old (ho ophis ho archaios) who is called the devil (diabolos) and Satan (ho Satanas), who deceives the whole world." The repetition of the full identification links this scene directly to chapter 12 and ensures the reader understands that the figure being bound is the same cosmic adversary whose career has been traced since the opening of the cosmic conflict.

The designation "the serpent of old" (ton ophin ton archaion) reaches back to the beginning of the scriptural narrative. In Genesis 3:1 (LXX): "Now the serpent (ho ophis) was more cunning than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made." The serpent in Eden deceived the woman (Genesis 3:13: "The serpent [ho ophis] deceived me [ēpatēsen me]"), and God pronounced a curse: "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed; he shall bruise you on the head, and you shall bruise him on the heel" (Genesis 3:15, LXX). The author of Revelation has already enacted this primordial conflict in chapter 12, where the dragon pursued the woman and her offspring. Now the ancient serpent is seized -- the enmity announced in Genesis 3:15 reaches its resolution.

The name diabolos ("slanderer, accuser") is the LXX translation of the Hebrew satan ("adversary, accuser") in Job 1:6 and Zechariah 3:1. In 12:10, the dragon was identified as "the accuser of our brothers" (ho katēgōr tōn adelphōn hēmōn), "who accuses them before our God day and night." The word Satanas is the transliterated Hebrew satan. The four names -- dragon, serpent of old, devil, Satan -- identify the figure comprehensively: he is the chaos monster of prophetic tradition (dragon), the deceiver from the garden (serpent of old), the accuser and slanderer (devil/diabolos), and the cosmic adversary (Satan). All four identities are now confined together.

**"And bound him for a thousand years" (kai edēsen auton chilia etē).** The binding (edēsen, from deō, "to bind, to tie") is for a specified duration: a thousand years (chilia etē). The number one thousand in the LXX denotes a vast, complete measure. Psalm 90:4 (LXX 89:4): "For a thousand years in your sight are like yesterday when it passes by, or as a watch in the night." Psalm 50:10 (LXX 49:10): "For every beast of the forest is mine, the cattle on a thousand hills." Deuteronomy 7:9: "The faithful God, who keeps covenant and lovingkindness for a thousandth generation with those who love him and keep his commandments." The number one thousand expresses completeness and fullness of measure -- a divinely determined period of sufficient length to accomplish its purpose. Whether the thousand years denotes a literal chronological millennium or a complete, full period of divine determination is a question the text does not explicitly resolve, but the author's consistent use of numbers throughout the document as symbolic expressions (seven churches, seven seals, seven trumpets, seven bowls; 144,000 as 12 x 12 x 1,000; 1,260 days as 42 months as three and a half years; 1,600 stadia as 4 x 4 x 100) suggests that one thousand functions here as it does elsewhere in the document: as a number signifying completeness and divine sufficiency.

**"And he threw him into the abyss, and shut it and sealed it over him" (kai ebalen auton eis tēn abysson kai ekleisen kai esphragisen epanō autou).** The sequence of verbs is precise and cumulative: threw (ebalen), shut (ekleisen), sealed (esphragisen). The dragon is not merely confined but triply secured. The sealing (esphragisen, from sphragizō, "to seal, to secure with a seal") adds a layer of authority beyond physical restraint. Sealing in this document carries the force of divine authentication and inviolability: the scroll was sealed with seven seals (5:1), the 144,000 were sealed on their foreheads (7:3-4), and the seven thunders were sealed up and not written (10:4). A seal in the ancient world was a mark of authority that prohibited unauthorized opening. The seal over the abyss means the dragon's confinement is guaranteed by divine authority -- he cannot break out on his own. Only when the seal is removed by the one who placed it can the dragon emerge.

**"So that he would not deceive the nations any longer, until the thousand years were completed" (hina mē planēsē eti ta ethnē, achri telesthē ta chilia etē).** The purpose clause (hina mē, "so that... not") identifies the specific reason for the binding: to prevent the dragon from deceiving the nations. The verb planēsē (aorist subjunctive of planaō, "to deceive, to lead astray, to cause to wander") is the dragon's signature activity throughout the document. In 12:9: "the one who deceives (ho planōn) the whole world (tēn oikoumenēn holēn)." In 13:14, the false prophet "deceives (plana) those who dwell on the earth." In 18:23, all the nations "were deceived (eplanēthēsan) by [Babylon's] sorcery." In 19:20, the false prophet "deceived (eplanēsen) those who had received the mark of the beast." The binding does not destroy the dragon; it suspends his capacity to deceive. The nations are freed from the deception that characterized the beast's era, the period when the dragon's agents controlled the world's worship, commerce, and political allegiance.

The phrase "the nations" (ta ethnē) echoes the universal scope of the dragon's deception in 12:9 ("the whole world") and the universal scope of Babylon's seduction (18:3: "all the nations have drunk of the wine of the passion of her immorality"). The dragon's binding means the nations are no longer subject to the systematic, comprehensive deception that operated through the beast-and-Babylon system.

**"After these things he must be released for a short time" (meta tauta dei lythēnai auton mikron chronon).** The verb dei ("it is necessary, it must") indicates divine necessity -- the same word used in the document's opening: "what must (dei) soon take place" (1:1). The dragon's release is not an accident or a failure of the binding but part of the divinely ordained plan. The phrase "a short time" (mikron chronon) echoes 12:12, where the dragon's earthly activity was characterized by the same kind of temporal limit: "knowing that he has only a short time (oligon kairon)." The dragon's freedom, both before and after the thousand years, is bounded and brief. The author signals that the release will serve a purpose within God's plan, a purpose that will become clear in verses 7-10.

## Verses 4-6: The First Resurrection and the Reign of the Faithful

"Then I saw thrones, and they sat on them, and judgment was given to them. And I saw the souls of those who had been beheaded because of their testimony of Jesus and because of the word of God, and those who had not worshiped the beast or his image, and had not received the mark on their forehead and on their hand; and they came to life and reigned with Christ for a thousand years. The rest of the dead did not come to life until the thousand years were completed. This is the first resurrection. Blessed and holy is the one who has a part in the first resurrection; over these the second death has no power, but they will be priests of God and of Christ and will reign with him for a thousand years."

**"Then I saw thrones, and they sat on them, and judgment was given to them" (kai eidon thronous, kai ekathisan ep' autous, kai krima edothē autois).** The vision opens with thrones -- not a single throne but multiple thrones, indicating shared rule. The verb ekathisan ("they sat") does not immediately identify who is sitting, creating a momentary ambiguity that the next clause resolves. The word krima ("judgment, verdict, right to judge") was "given" (edothē, divine passive) to them -- they receive judicial authority, the capacity to render verdicts.

The image of thrones given to the faithful draws on Daniel 7:9-10, 22. In Daniel 7:9 (LXX/Theodotion): "I kept looking until thrones (thronoi) were set up (etethēsan), and the Ancient of Days took his seat." In Daniel 7:22: "Judgment (krisis/krina) was given (edothē) in favor of the saints (hagiois) of the Most High, and the time arrived when the saints took possession of the kingdom." The verbal parallels are precise: thrones placed, judgment given, saints receiving the kingdom. The author of Revelation reproduces Daniel's scene and applies it to the faithful who endured the beast's persecution.

Within the document itself, thrones have been promised to the faithful. In 3:21, the risen Christ declared to Laodicea: "He who overcomes, I will grant to him to sit down with me on my throne, as I also overcame and sat down with my Father on his throne." In Matthew 19:28, Jesus told the twelve: "In the regeneration, when the Son of Man will sit on his glorious throne, you also shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel." The thrones of 20:4 fulfill these promises: the overcomers sit and judge.

**"And I saw the souls of those who had been beheaded because of their testimony of Jesus and because of the word of God" (kai tas psychas tōn pepelekismenōn dia tēn martyrian Iēsou kai dia ton logon tou theou).** The word psychas ("souls") is the same word used in 6:9 for the martyrs under the altar: "I saw underneath the altar the souls (tas psychas) of those who had been slain because of the word of God (dia ton logon tou theou) and because of the testimony (tēn martyrian) which they had maintained." The verbal correspondence is deliberate: the souls who cried out in 6:9-10 are the same category of persons who now sit on thrones in 20:4. What was promised to them in 6:11 -- to "rest for a little while longer, until the number of their fellow servants and their brothers who were to be killed even as they had been, would be completed also" -- has now reached its fulfillment. The waiting is over. The number is complete.

The verb pepelekismenōn (perfect passive participle of pelekizō, "to cut with an axe, to behead") is specific: these are people who were executed by decapitation. The pelekys was the executioner's axe. Beheading was a Roman method of execution, reserved in practice for Roman citizens and for certain judicial sentences. The specificity of the method grounds the vision in the concrete reality of persecution: these are not abstract "faithful ones" but people who were killed in a particular way for particular reasons.

The two reasons for their execution match the two-part description of the dragon's targets in 12:17: "those who keep the commandments of God and hold to the testimony of Jesus" (tōn tērountōn tas entolas tou theou kai echontōn tēn martyrian Iēsou). In 12:17, the testimony of Jesus and the commandments of God defined the dragon's enemies. In 20:4, the testimony of Jesus and the word of God identify those who were killed. The same characteristics that made them the dragon's targets now qualify them for enthronement.

**"And those who had not worshiped the beast or his image, and had not received the mark on their forehead and on their hand" (kai hoitines ou prosekynēsan to thērion oute tēn eikona autou kai ouk elabon to charagma epi to metōpon kai epi tēn cheira autōn).** This clause extends the identification. In addition to those beheaded for their testimony, the vision encompasses those who refused the three compulsions of chapter 13: worshiping the beast (13:4, 8, 12), worshiping his image (13:14-15), and receiving his mark on forehead or hand (13:16-17). These are the people who faced the beast's ultimatum -- worship or die, take the mark or be excluded from commerce -- and refused every demand. They are the people addressed by the third angel's warning in 14:9-11 and described in the editorial exhortation of 14:12: "Here is the perseverance of the saints who keep the commandments of God and their faith in Jesus."

The detail "on their forehead and on their hand" (epi to metōpon kai epi tēn cheira) repeats the language of 13:16 precisely. The author deliberately recalls the specific apparatus of the beast's coercive system to identify those who resisted it. These people are the 144,000 of 14:1 who had the Lamb's name and the Father's name on their foreheads instead of the beast's mark. They are the overcomers promised in the seven letters. They are the community whose deeds "follow with them" (14:13).

**"And they came to life and reigned with Christ for a thousand years" (kai ezēsan kai ebasileisan meta tou Christou chilia etē).** The verb ezēsan (aorist of zaō, "to live, to come to life") denotes a transition from death to life. The same verb was used in 2:8 for the risen Christ: "The first and the last, who was dead, and came to life (ezēsen)." The parallel is pointed: the Christ who came to life is now joined by those who came to life with him. They share in his resurrection.

The verb ebasileisan (aorist of basileuō, "to reign, to exercise kingship") is the fulfillment of what the document has promised from its beginning. In 1:6, Christ "has made us a kingdom (basileian), priests to his God and Father." In 5:10, the new song declared: "You have made them a kingdom and priests to our God; and they will reign (basileusousin) on the earth." In 2:26-27, the overcomer at Thyatira was promised: "I will give him authority over the nations; and he shall rule them with a rod of iron." In 3:21, the overcomer at Laodicea was promised a seat on Christ's throne. In 22:5, the servants in the new Jerusalem "will reign forever and ever." The reign of the faithful with Christ is one of the document's central promises, and 20:4 narrates its beginning.

The phrase "with Christ" (meta tou Christou) is significant. This is one of the rare instances in the document where the title Christos ("anointed one, Messiah") is used without the name Iēsous. The title emphasizes the messianic role: the anointed king of Psalm 2, the one installed on Zion (Psalm 2:6), the one to whom the nations are given as an inheritance (Psalm 2:8). Those who reign do so with the Messiah -- their kingship is participatory, derived from his, shared from his throne (3:21).

**"The rest of the dead did not come to life until the thousand years were completed" (hoi loipoi tōn nekrōn ouk ezēsan achri telesthē ta chilia etē).** The phrase "the rest of the dead" (hoi loipoi tōn nekrōn) refers to the dead who are not included in the group just described -- those who were not martyred for their testimony, who did not resist the beast, or who simply died apart from the specific conditions named in verse 4. Their coming to life is deferred until after the thousand years. The verb ezēsan is repeated, indicating the same kind of transition from death to life, but at a different time. The text implies that there will be a general resurrection after the thousand years, which is described in verses 11-15 -- the great white throne judgment, where "the dead, the great and the small" stand before the throne.

**"This is the first resurrection" (hautē hē anastasis hē prōtē).** The author names the event: the coming to life of the martyrs and the beast-resisters is "the first resurrection" (hē anastasis hē prōtē). The word anastasis ("rising up, resurrection") is the standard term for bodily resurrection throughout early Jewish and Christian usage. Martha used it in John 11:24: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection (en tē anastasei) on the last day." Daniel 12:2 (LXX): "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake, these to everlasting life, but the others to disgrace and everlasting contempt." The designation "first" (prōtē) implies a second resurrection -- the general resurrection of "the rest of the dead" after the thousand years, which takes place in verses 11-15. The two resurrections are distinguished by timing (before and after the thousand years) and by participants (the martyrs/beast-resisters versus the rest of the dead).

The numbering system -- "first resurrection" paired with the "second death" -- creates a deliberate structure. There are two deaths and two resurrections. The first death is physical death, which the faithful experience (2:10: "be faithful unto death"; 12:11: "they did not love their life even when faced with death"). The second death is the lake of fire (20:14; 21:8). The first resurrection is the coming to life of the faithful dead before the thousand years. The implied second resurrection is the general raising of the dead for judgment (20:12-13). The one who participates in the first resurrection is exempt from the second death (20:6). The structure is: first death leads to first resurrection leads to exemption from second death. Physical death, for the faithful, is not the end but the passage to resurrection and immunity from ultimate destruction.

**"Blessed and holy is the one who has a part in the first resurrection" (makarios kai hagios ho echōn meros en tē anastasei tē prōtē).** This is the fifth of the document's seven beatitudes (the others: 1:3; 14:13; 16:15; 19:9; 22:7; 22:14). The word makarios ("blessed, fortunate, to be congratulated") declares the true condition of those whom the world would have pitied or scorned -- people who were beheaded, excluded from commerce, subjected to the beast's fury. The addition of hagios ("holy, set apart, consecrated") intensifies the pronouncement: these people are not merely fortunate but sacred, consecrated, set apart by God. The word hagios throughout this document describes what belongs to God: the holy city (11:2; 21:2; 22:19), the holy angels (14:10), the holy apostles and prophets (18:20), the holy ones/saints (hagioi, used throughout for God's people). To be called hagios in the context of resurrection is to be declared consecrated for God's presence and service.

The word meros ("part, share, portion") indicates participation. The faithful have "a part" -- a stake, a share, a participation -- in the first resurrection. The same word appears in 21:8, where those who practice abomination have "their part (to meros autōn) in the lake that burns with fire and sulfur, which is the second death." The contrast is exact: the faithful have their portion in the first resurrection; the unfaithful have their portion in the second death.

**"Over these the second death has no power" (epi toutōn ho deuteros thanatos ouk echei exousian).** The second death (ho deuteros thanatos) was first mentioned in 2:11, where the overcomer at Smyrna was promised: "The one who conquers will not be hurt by the second death." Now the promise is fulfilled: those who participate in the first resurrection are beyond the second death's reach. The word exousia ("power, authority, right") is the same word used throughout the document for the beast's delegated authority (13:2, 4, 5, 7, 12). The second death has no exousia -- no authority, no jurisdiction, no power -- over those who share in the first resurrection. Their exemption is not temporary but definitive.

The second death is defined in 20:14: "This is the second death, the lake of fire." And in 21:8: "the lake that burns with fire and sulfur, which is the second death." The concept has parallels in early rabbinic and Targumic literature. Targum Jonathan on Deuteronomy 33:6 reads: "Let Reuben live in this age and not die the second death (mota tinyana) in which the wicked die in the world to come." Targum on Isaiah 65:6: "Their punishment shall be in Gehinnom, where the fire burns all the day. Behold, it is written before me: I will not give them respite during their lives but will render to them the punishment of their transgressions and will deliver their bodies to the second death (mota tinyana)." The concept of a second death beyond physical death was known in first-century Jewish thought, and the author of Revelation adopts and specifies it: the second death is the lake of fire, and the first resurrection provides immunity from it.

**"But they will be priests of God and of Christ and will reign with him for a thousand years" (alla esontai hiereis tou theou kai tou Christou kai basileusousin met' autou ta chilia etē).** The dual identity -- priests (hiereis) and kings (implied by basileusousin, "they will reign") -- fulfills the programmatic declaration of 1:6: "He has made us a kingdom (basileian), priests (hiereis) to his God and Father." And the new song of 5:10: "You have made them a kingdom and priests (hiereis) to our God; and they will reign (basileusousin) on the earth." Both passages drew on the foundational text of Exodus 19:6 (LXX): "You shall be to me a kingdom of priests (basileion hierateuma) and a holy nation (ethnos hagion)." The priestly-royal identity promised to Israel at Sinai, claimed for the redeemed community in chapters 1 and 5, is now actualized in the millennial reign. The ones who were killed are the ones who reign. The ones who were excluded from the beast's economic system are the ones who serve as priests before God. The inversion is total: what the beast took from them (life, commerce, social standing), God restores with interest (resurrection, priesthood, kingship).

## Verses 7-10: The Release of Satan and the Final Defeat

"When the thousand years are completed, Satan will be released from his prison, and will come out to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them together for the war; the number of them is like the sand of the seashore. And they came up on the broad plain of the earth and surrounded the camp of the saints and the beloved city, and fire came down from heaven and devoured them. And the devil who deceived them was thrown into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet are also; and they will be tormented day and night forever and ever."

**"When the thousand years are completed, Satan will be released from his prison" (kai hotan telesthē ta chilia etē, lythēsetai ho Satanas ek tēs phylakēs autou).** The verb lythēsetai ("will be released," future passive of lyō, "to loose, to unbind") is passive -- Satan does not free himself. He is released by the one who bound him, at the appointed time. The word phylakē ("prison, guard, custody") was used in 18:2 for Babylon as "a prison of every unclean spirit and a prison of every unclean and hateful bird." The abyss has served as Satan's prison during the thousand years. His release is not a failure of the binding but the execution of the divine plan signaled in verse 3: "he must (dei) be released for a short time."

**"And will come out to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog" (kai exeleusetai planēsai ta ethnē ta en tais tessarsin gōniais tēs gēs, ton Gōg kai Magōg).** Satan's first action upon release is what he has always done: deceive (planēsai). The verb is identical to the one used in verse 3 (planēsē) and throughout the document for the dragon's activity. The dragon's nature has not changed during his confinement. Released, he immediately returns to his characteristic work.

"The nations which are in the four corners of the earth" (ta ethnē ta en tais tessarsin gōniais tēs gēs) -- the phrase "four corners of the earth" appears in 7:1, where four angels stood "at the four corners of the earth, holding back the four winds of the earth." The expression denotes the totality of the earth's extent, its farthest reaches. Isaiah 11:12 (LXX): "He will assemble the banished ones of Israel, and will gather the dispersed of Judah from the four corners (tessarōn pterygōn) of the earth."

"Gog and Magog" (ton Gōg kai Magōg) -- this designation draws on one of the most extensive prophetic passages about eschatological warfare: Ezekiel 38-39. In Ezekiel 38:1-3 (LXX): "Son of man, set your face toward Gog (Gōg), of the land of Magog (Magōg), the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal, and prophesy against him." In Ezekiel's oracle, Gog is a hostile ruler from the land of Magog who, in "the latter years" (Ezekiel 38:8) and "the last days" (Ezekiel 38:16), will invade the land of Israel with a vast coalition of nations. The invasion comes against "a land that is restored from the sword" and "a people gathered from many nations, to the mountains of Israel" (Ezekiel 38:8). The attack is against a people "living securely" (Ezekiel 38:8, 11, 14), who have been "gathered from the nations" and are "living in the center of the world" (Ezekiel 38:12, LXX: epi ton omphalon tēs gēs, "on the navel of the earth"). But God intervenes directly: "I will rain on him and on his troops, and on the many peoples who are with him, a torrential rain, with hailstones, fire and brimstone" (Ezekiel 38:22). The destruction is total, and the aftermath requires seven months for burial and seven years for burning weapons (Ezekiel 39:9, 12).

The author of Revelation compresses Ezekiel's extended narrative into a few verses and universalizes it. In Ezekiel, Gog is a single ruler from a specific land; in Revelation, "Gog and Magog" becomes a designation for the hostile nations themselves, gathered from all four corners of the earth. The shift from a specific northern enemy to a universal coalition is consistent with the author's pattern throughout the document: particular prophetic images are expanded to encompass the cosmic and universal dimension of the conflict.

In Ezekiel's oracle, the attack on Israel comes when the people are living securely after restoration. In Revelation, the attack comes after the thousand years of Christ's reign with the saints. The parallel is structural: a period of peace and security, followed by a final assault by enemies from the earth's extremities, followed by divine destruction from heaven. The author follows Ezekiel's narrative sequence but transposes it into his own eschatological framework.

**"To gather them together for the war; the number of them is like the sand of the seashore" (synagagein autous eis ton polemon, hōn ho arithmos autōn hōs hē ammos tēs thalassēs).** The gathering for war (eis ton polemon) echoes 16:14, where demonic spirits gathered "the kings of the whole world together for the war (eis ton polemon) of the great day of God, the Almighty," and 19:19, where the beast and the kings of the earth "gathered together to make war (ton polemon) against the one sitting on the horse." The definite article ton polemon ("the war") suggests a specific, expected conflict -- the final battle anticipated throughout the document.

The comparison "like the sand of the seashore" (hōs hē ammos tēs thalassēs) is one of Israel's standard expressions for an innumerably large multitude. Genesis 22:17: "I will greatly multiply your seed as the stars of the heavens and as the sand (hōs tēn ammon) which is on the seashore." Genesis 32:12: "You said, 'I will surely prosper you and make your descendants as the sand of the sea (hōs tēn ammon tēs thalassēs), which is too great to be numbered.'" Joshua 11:4: armies "as numerous as the sand that is on the seashore (hōs hē ammos hē para to cheilos tēs thalassēs)." The comparison emphasizes the overwhelming numerical superiority of the attacking force. The saints in the beloved city are vastly outnumbered. The disparity between the attackers and the defenders makes the divine intervention all the more decisive.

**"And they came up on the broad plain of the earth and surrounded the camp of the saints and the beloved city" (kai anebēsan epi to platos tēs gēs kai ekykleusan tēn parembolēn tōn hagiōn kai tēn polin tēn ēgapēmenēn).** The phrase "the broad plain of the earth" (to platos tēs gēs) may echo Habakkuk 1:6 (LXX), where the Chaldeans "march through the breadth (to platos) of the earth." The hostile forces spread across the entire surface of the land.

Two targets are named: "the camp of the saints" (tēn parembolēn tōn hagiōn) and "the beloved city" (tēn polin tēn ēgapēmenēn). The word parembolē ("camp, encampment, barracks") is the LXX word for Israel's wilderness camp (Exodus 14:19-20; Numbers 2:2-34; Deuteronomy 23:14). The camp is the community gathered around God's presence, the military encampment of the covenant people on the march. The designation of the saints' location as a "camp" recalls the wilderness period, when Israel lived not in settled cities but in the mobile encampment that followed the pillar of cloud and fire. The saints in the millennial reign are described with wilderness imagery -- they are still pilgrims, still encamped, still organized around God's presence.

"The beloved city" (tēn polin tēn ēgapēmenēn) is Jerusalem. The participle ēgapēmenēn ("beloved," perfect passive participle of agapaō) designates the city as the object of God's love. The description echoes the prophetic tradition in which Jerusalem/Zion is the chosen, loved, and treasured city of God. Psalm 78:68 (LXX 77:68): "He chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion which he loved (hēgapēsen)." Psalm 87:2 (LXX 86:2): "The LORD loves (agapōn) the gates of Zion more than all the dwelling places of Jacob." Isaiah 62:12: "And they will call them, 'The holy people, the redeemed of the LORD'; and you will be called, 'Sought out, a city not forsaken.'" The designation "beloved" identifies the city as the place of God's affection and commitment, the place he will defend.

The attack follows Ezekiel's pattern precisely. In Ezekiel 38:9, 12, 16, Gog's forces come like a cloud covering the land, advancing against "a people living securely" and "the center of the world." In Revelation, the hostile nations surround the camp and the beloved city. The encirclement is complete. Humanly speaking, the situation is hopeless -- an innumerable army surrounds a camp and a city.

**"And fire came down from heaven and devoured them" (kai katebē pyr ek tou ouranou kai katephagen autous).** The divine response is immediate and overwhelming. No battle is narrated. No defense is mounted by the saints. The fire descends directly from heaven and consumes the attackers. The brevity is striking: what could have been an extended battle narrative is compressed into a single clause. The saints do not fight; God fights for them.

The fire from heaven draws on multiple prophetic precedents. First, Ezekiel 38:22 (LXX): "I will rain on him and on his troops, and on the many peoples who are with him, a torrential rain, with hailstones, fire (pyr) and brimstone (theion)." The fire from heaven in Revelation 20:9 directly fulfills Ezekiel's Gog prophecy.

Second, 2 Kings 1:10-14: Elijah called fire down from heaven to consume the soldiers sent to arrest him. "If I am a man of God, let fire come down (katabētō pyr) from heaven and consume you and your fifty" (2 Kings 1:10, LXX). Fire from heaven is the definitive sign of God's direct intervention.

Third, Genesis 19:24: "The LORD rained on Sodom and Gomorrah brimstone and fire (theion kai pyr) from the LORD out of heaven." The destruction of Sodom by fire from heaven became the paradigmatic instance of divine judgment (Deuteronomy 29:23; Isaiah 13:19; Jeremiah 49:18; Amos 4:11; Luke 17:29; Jude 7). The fire from heaven in 20:9 places the destruction of Gog's forces in the same category as Sodom's annihilation: total, divine, irresistible.

The verb katephagen (from katesthiō, "to consume, to devour, to eat up") indicates complete destruction. The hostile force is not defeated, routed, or dispersed -- it is consumed. The same verb was used in 11:5 for the fire that proceeds from the mouth of the two witnesses and "devours (katesthiei) their enemies." The fire is not a weapon of war but an instrument of direct divine judgment.

**"And the devil who deceived them was thrown into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet are also" (kai ho diabolos ho planēsas autous eblēthē eis tēn limnēn tou pyros kai theiou, hopou kai to thērion kai ho pseudoprophētēs).** The dragon now follows his agents into the lake of fire. The beast and the false prophet were thrown there in 19:20. Now the dragon joins them. The counterfeit trinity is reunited -- in destruction. The passive voice eblēthē ("was thrown") indicates that the dragon does not go voluntarily; he is cast in by divine agency.

The word planēsas ("the one who deceived," aorist participle of planaō) serves as the dragon's epithet in this final appearance. His defining characteristic, his signature activity, is deception. He deceived Eve (Genesis 3:13), he deceived the whole world (12:9), he deceived the nations through the beast and false prophet (13:14; 18:23; 19:20), and he deceived Gog and Magog (20:8). He is identified by his deception as his final identification before destruction.

"The lake of fire and brimstone" (tēn limnēn tou pyros kai theiou) -- the word limnē ("lake, pool") in secular Greek describes a body of standing water, a pool or lake. In the LXX, limnē rarely appears, but the combination of fire (pyr) and brimstone (theion, "sulfur") recalls Genesis 19:24 (Sodom), Isaiah 30:33 (Topheth, the burning place prepared for the Assyrian king), and Isaiah 34:9-10 (Edom's judgment, where "its streams will be turned into pitch, and its dust into brimstone, and its land will become burning pitch"). The lake of fire functions in this document as the place of final, irreversible destruction -- distinct from Hades (which holds the dead temporarily and is itself thrown into the lake in 20:14) and distinct from the abyss (which is a place of confinement, not destruction).

**"And they will be tormented day and night forever and ever" (kai basanisthēsontai hēmeras kai nyktos eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn).** The verb basanisthēsontai ("will be tormented," future passive of basanizō) describes intense suffering. The word basanizō originally referred to testing metals on a touchstone (basanos), then extended to torture and torment. The phrase "day and night" (hēmeras kai nyktos) echoes 4:8, where the four living creatures "do not cease day and night" in their worship, and 14:11, where the beast-worshipers "have no rest day and night." The phrase "forever and ever" (eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn, literally "into the ages of the ages") is the document's strongest temporal expression, used for the duration of God's reign (1:6; 4:9-10; 5:13; 10:6; 11:15; 15:7), the praise of God (5:13; 7:12), and the smoke of the beast-worshipers' torment (14:11) and Babylon's destruction (19:3). The same temporal expression that describes God's eternal reign describes the torment of the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet. The destruction is permanent.

## Verses 11-15: The Great White Throne and the Final Judgment

"Then I saw a great white throne and him who sat on it, from whose presence earth and heaven fled away, and no place was found for them. And I saw the dead, the great and the small, standing before the throne, and books were opened; and another book was opened, which is the book of life; and the dead were judged from the things which were written in the books, according to their deeds. And the sea gave up the dead which were in it, and death and Hades gave up the dead which were in them; and they were judged, every one of them according to their deeds. Then death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire. This is the second death, the lake of fire. And if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire."

**"Then I saw a great white throne and him who sat on it" (kai eidon thronon megan leukon kai ton kathēmenon ep' auton).** The throne has been the central image of the document since chapter 4, where God was revealed seated on the throne in heaven. Here the throne is described with two adjectives: megan ("great") and leukon ("white"). The greatness indicates the supreme authority of this judgment. The whiteness carries the same associations it has held throughout the document: purity, righteousness, and divine holiness (white garments: 3:4-5, 18; 6:11; 7:9, 13-14; 19:14; white stone: 2:17; white horse: 6:2; 19:11; white cloud: 14:14).

The identity of "him who sat on it" (ton kathēmenon) is not explicitly named. Throughout the document, "the one seated on the throne" (ho kathēmenos epi tou thronou) has consistently referred to God the Father: in 4:2-3, 9; 5:1, 7, 13; 6:16; 7:10, 15; 19:4; 21:5. The author may intend the same referent here, or the identity may be deliberately left open to encompass both God and the Lamb, who share the throne (3:21; 22:1, 3: "the throne of God and of the Lamb"). The point is not the precise identity of the seated figure but the authority of the judgment: this is the supreme tribunal.

**"From whose presence earth and heaven fled away, and no place was found for them" (hou apo tou prosōpou ephygen hē gē kai ho ouranos, kai topos ouch heurethē autois).** The fleeing of earth and heaven from the face of the judge is one of the most dramatic images in the document. The word prosōpon ("face, presence") indicates direct confrontation: earth and heaven cannot endure the unmediated presence of the judge. The verb ephygen ("fled," aorist of pheugō) describes flight -- earth and heaven themselves flee from the divine presence.

The image draws on Psalm 114:3, 7 (LXX 113:3, 7): "The sea looked and fled (ephygen); the Jordan turned back... Tremble, O earth, before the Lord, before the God of Jacob." In Psalm 114, the sea and the Jordan flee at God's presence during the Exodus. Nahum 1:5 (LXX): "The mountains quake before him and the hills dissolve; the earth is upheaved before him -- the world and all the inhabitants in it." Isaiah 51:6: "The heavens will vanish like smoke, and the earth will wear out like a garment."

The phrase "no place was found for them" (topos ouch heurethē autois) echoes Daniel 2:35, where Nebuchadnezzar's image is shattered and the wind carries the pieces away: "no place was found (ou topos heurethē) for them." In Daniel, the old order represented by the image is obliterated without a trace. In Revelation, the old creation itself -- earth and heaven -- disappears without a trace. The present cosmic order cannot survive the direct manifestation of divine judgment. This prepares for 21:1: "I saw a new heaven and a new earth; for the first heaven and the first earth passed away."

**"And I saw the dead, the great and the small, standing before the throne" (kai eidon tous nekrous, tous megalous kai tous mikrous, hestōtas enōpion tou thronou).** The dead are identified without distinction of status: "the great and the small" (tous megalous kai tous mikrous). This pairing appears repeatedly in the document as a merism for all people without exception: 11:18 ("the time to reward your servants the prophets and the saints and those who fear your name, the small and the great"); 13:16 ("the small and the great, and the rich and the poor, and the free men and the slaves"); 19:5 ("Give praise to our God, all you his bond-servants, you who fear him, the small and the great"); 19:18 ("the flesh of all men, both free men and slaves, and small and great"). The phrase encompasses every human being regardless of social status, power, wealth, or earthly significance. Before this throne, all distinctions collapse.

The dead are "standing" (hestōtas) before the throne. They have been raised -- they are no longer dead in the sense of being in the grave but are now upright before the judge. The raising is assumed, not narrated: the text moves directly from "the dead" (tous nekrous) to their standing before the throne. The raising is confirmed in verse 13, where the sources of the dead are enumerated.

**"And books were opened; and another book was opened, which is the book of life" (kai biblia ēnoichthēsan, kai allo biblion ēnoichthē, ho estin tēs zōēs).** Two categories of books are opened. First, biblia (plural: "books, scrolls") -- records of deeds. Second, a single allo biblion ("another book"), identified as "the book of life" (to tēs zōēs, literally "the one of life").

The books of deeds draw on Daniel 7:10 (LXX): "The court sat in judgment, and the books (biblia) were opened." In Daniel's vision, the opening of books precedes the judgment of the fourth beast and the giving of the kingdom to the saints. The books contain the records on the basis of which judgment is rendered. The concept of heavenly books recording human actions is widespread in Israel's tradition. Psalm 56:8 (LXX 55:9): "You have stored my wanderings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book (en tē biblō sou)?" Malachi 3:16: "A book of remembrance (biblion mnēmosynou) was written before him for those who fear the LORD and who esteem his name." Isaiah 65:6: "Behold, it is written before me: I will not keep silent, but I will repay." The books are the divine record of every person's deeds, the comprehensive account from which no action is missing and no action is forgotten.

The book of life has been a recurring presence throughout the document. In 3:5, the overcomer's name will not be blotted out of the book of life. In 13:8, all who dwell on the earth will worship the beast -- everyone whose name has not been written in the book of life of the Lamb. In 17:8, those whose names have not been written in the book of life from the foundation of the world will wonder at the beast. In 21:27, only those whose names are written in the Lamb's book of life enter the new Jerusalem. The book of life is God's register of his own -- those who belong to him, those counted among the living, those whose names he has inscribed.

The presence of two categories of books establishes a dual basis for judgment: deeds and inscription. The dead are judged "from the things written in the books, according to their deeds" (ek tōn gegrammmenōn en tois bibliois kata ta erga autōn) -- this is the assessment of conduct. And the book of life determines final destiny: "if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire" (20:15) -- this is the assessment of belonging. The two assessments are complementary, not contradictory. Deeds are the basis of judgment; inscription in the book of life is the basis of final outcome. The document has consistently maintained both emphases: deeds matter (2:2, 23; 3:1, 8, 15; 14:13; 20:12-13; 22:12), and belonging to the Lamb matters (3:5; 13:8; 17:8; 21:27).

**"And the dead were judged from the things which were written in the books, according to their deeds" (kai ekrithēsan hoi nekroi ek tōn gegrammenōn en tois bibliois kata ta erga autōn).** The word erga ("works, deeds") is one of the most frequently used words in the document. The risen Christ says to each assembly: "I know your works" (oida ta erga sou, 2:2, 19; 3:1, 8, 15). To Thyatira: "I will give to each one of you according to your deeds (kata ta erga)" (2:23). In 14:13, the faithful dead rest "from their labors, for their deeds (erga) follow with them." In 22:12: "Behold, I am coming quickly, and my reward is with me, to render to every man according to what he has done (to ergon autou)."

The judgment according to deeds has deep prophetic roots. Jeremiah 17:10: "I, the LORD, search the heart, I test the mind, even to give to each man according to his ways (kata tas hodous autou), according to the fruit of his deeds (kata tous karpous tōn epitēdeumatōn autou)." Psalm 62:12 (LXX 61:13): "You recompense a man according to his work (kata ta erga autou)." Proverbs 24:12: "Will he not render to man according to his work (kata ta erga autou)?" Ezekiel 18:30: "I will judge you, O house of Israel, each according to his conduct (kata tas hodous autou)." The principle of judgment according to deeds is not unique to this document but is a foundational element of Israel's understanding of divine justice. God does not judge arbitrarily or capriciously; he judges on the basis of what has been done. The books provide the evidence; the deeds constitute the case.

**"And the sea gave up the dead which were in it, and death and Hades gave up the dead which were in them" (kai edōken hē thalassa tous nekrous tous en autē, kai ho thanatos kai ho hadēs edōkan tous nekrous tous en autois).** Three repositories of the dead are named: the sea, Death, and Hades. The sea gives up its dead -- those who perished at sea, whose bodies were never recovered, who received no burial. Death and Hades give up their dead -- the personified powers introduced in 6:8 ("one sitting on [the pale horse] had the name Death, and Hades was following with him") and claimed by the risen Christ in 1:18 ("I have the keys of death and of Hades"). Death is the agent that killed them; Hades is the realm that held them. Now both are compelled to release their captives.

The comprehensiveness of the enumeration is the point: no dead person is excluded from this judgment. Those who drowned at sea are included. Those who died on land and descended to Hades are included. The grave, the deep, the realm of the dead -- all yield their inhabitants. The judgment is universal. No death is so remote, no burial so obscure, no drowning so deep that it falls outside the scope of this tribunal.

**"And they were judged, every one of them according to their deeds" (kai ekrithēsan hekastos kata ta erga autōn).** The phrase "every one of them" (hekastos, "each one") individualizes the judgment. This is not a mass verdict but an individual assessment. Each person stands before the throne and is judged on the basis of his or her own deeds. The corporate identity (nations, peoples, tribes) that has characterized much of the document yields here to individual accountability. In the end, each person faces the judge alone, with nothing to present except what was done.

**"Then death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire" (kai ho thanatos kai ho hadēs eblēthēsan eis tēn limnēn tou pyros).** Death and Hades -- the personified powers that have operated since the fall, the last enemies, the pair that accompanied the fourth horseman -- are now destroyed. They follow the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet into the lake of fire. The destruction of Death fulfills the promise of Isaiah 25:8 (LXX): "He has swallowed up death (katepiein ton thanaton) in victory (eis nikos)." Paul quoted this text in 1 Corinthians 15:54: "Death is swallowed up in victory." Hosea 13:14 (LXX): "From the hand of Hades I will rescue them, and from death I will redeem them. Where is your penalty, O death (pou hē dikē sou, thanate)? Where is your sting, O Hades (pou to kentron sou, hadē)?" The destruction of Death and Hades in the lake of fire is the enactment of what Isaiah and Hosea prophesied: the final abolition of death as a power in the created order.

The significance of this act cannot be overstated. Death has been the constant companion of the document's narrative. The Lamb was slain (5:6, 9, 12). The martyrs were killed (6:9; 11:7; 13:15; 20:4). The horseman Death rode across the earth (6:8). The beast made war on the saints and overcame them (13:7). Babylon was drunk with the blood of the saints (17:6). Throughout the document, death has been the dragon's primary weapon and the saints' constant threat. Now Death itself is destroyed. The power that killed the Lamb, that slaughtered the martyrs, that rode the pale horse -- that power is thrown into the lake of fire and abolished. After 20:14, death does not appear again in the document. In 21:4: "there will no longer be any death."

**"This is the second death, the lake of fire" (houtos ho thanatos ho deuteros estin, hē limnē tou pyros).** The author defines his term. The second death is the lake of fire. This definitional statement clarifies what has been referenced since 2:11. The second death is not mere physical death (that is the first death) but the final, irreversible destruction represented by the lake of fire. It is the ultimate consequence -- the destination of the dragon (20:10), the beast and false prophet (19:20), Death and Hades (20:14), and those whose names are not in the book of life (20:15). In 21:8, the same definition will be extended: "the cowardly and unbelieving and abominable and murderers and immoral persons and sorcerers and idolaters and all liars, their part will be in the lake that burns with fire and sulfur, which is the second death."

**"And if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire" (kai ei tis ouch heurethē en tē biblō tēs zōēs gegramenos, eblēthē eis tēn limnēn tou pyros).** The final verse of the chapter states the criterion of destiny in its starkest terms. The verb heurethē ("was found," aorist passive of heuriskō) implies a search -- the book of life is examined, and each person's name is either found or not found. The passive voice indicates that the finding or not-finding is performed by the judge, not by the person being judged.

The book of life has been the standard of belonging throughout the document. In 3:5, the overcomer's name is guaranteed to remain: "I will not blot out his name from the book of life." In 13:8, those whose names are written worship God rather than the beast; those whose names are not written worship the beast. In 17:8, those not written in the book of life marvel at the beast. In 21:27, only those written in the book of life enter the new Jerusalem. The book of life is the ultimate register. The question at the great white throne is not merely what a person did (the books of deeds answer that) but whether a person belongs (the book of life answers that). Deeds are the evidence; the book of life is the verdict.

The verb eblēthē ("was thrown," aorist passive of ballō) is the same verb used for Satan's casting into the lake of fire (20:10) and for Death and Hades' casting into the lake of fire (20:14). The consistency of the verb binds all three final dispositions together: Satan is thrown, Death and Hades are thrown, and those not in the book of life are thrown -- all into the same destination, by the same divine act.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Revelation 20 accomplishes the final elimination of every enemy of God's people and God's purposes, completing the sequence of judgments that began with the fall of Babylon (chapters 17-18), continued with the destruction of the beast and false prophet (19:19-20), and now concludes with the binding and ultimate defeat of the dragon, the judgment of all the dead, and the abolition of Death itself.

The chapter's internal structure moves through four scenes, each marked by kai eidon ("and I saw"):

First (20:1-3), an angel binds the dragon -- identified by his full fourfold name: dragon, serpent of old, devil, Satan -- and confines him in the abyss for a thousand years. The stated purpose of the binding is to prevent the dragon from deceiving the nations. The dragon's signature activity -- deception -- is suspended for the duration. But the text signals that the binding is temporary: "after these things he must be released for a short time."

Second (20:4-6), thrones appear and the faithful dead are raised. Those who were beheaded for the testimony of Jesus and the word of God, and those who refused to worship the beast or receive his mark, come to life and reign with Christ for the thousand years. This is named "the first resurrection." Those who participate in it are declared blessed and holy, exempt from the second death, and serving as priests of God and of Christ. The rest of the dead do not come to life until the thousand years are completed. This scene fulfills the promises made throughout the document: the martyrs who cried out in 6:9-10 receive their vindication; the overcomers who were promised thrones (3:21) sit on them; the priestly kingdom announced in 1:6 and 5:10 is established; the blessedness pronounced over the faithful dead in 14:13 is enacted.

Third (20:7-10), Satan is released after the thousand years, immediately resumes his characteristic work of deception, and gathers the nations from the four corners of the earth -- called Gog and Magog, from Ezekiel 38-39 -- for a final assault on "the camp of the saints and the beloved city." The attacking force is innumerably vast. But there is no battle: fire descends from heaven and devours them. The devil is then thrown into the lake of fire where the beast and false prophet already are, and they are tormented forever. The counterfeit trinity -- dragon, beast, false prophet -- is finally and permanently united in destruction.

Fourth (20:11-15), the great white throne appears. Earth and heaven flee from the presence of the one seated on it. The dead -- every person without exception, great and small -- stand before the throne. Books of deeds are opened, and the book of life is opened. The dead are judged according to their deeds. The sea, Death, and Hades give up their dead. Death and Hades themselves are thrown into the lake of fire -- which is identified as the second death. Anyone whose name is not found in the book of life is thrown into the lake of fire.

The chapter's message to its first-century hearers addresses the questions that have accumulated throughout the document. What happens to the martyrs? They are raised and reign with Christ. Does their death mean defeat? No -- it is the pathway to the first resurrection and exemption from the second death. What happens to the dragon, the ultimate source of all the evil that has afflicted them? He is bound, confined, and ultimately destroyed. What about the beast and false prophet? Already destroyed in chapter 19, they are joined in the lake of fire by their master. What about Death itself -- the constant threat, the fourth horseman, the power that took the martyrs' lives? Death is abolished, thrown into the same lake of fire. What about the judgment of every person who ever lived? It takes place before the great white throne, on the basis of deeds recorded in books and names inscribed in the book of life.

The judgment operates on a dual basis that the author has maintained throughout the document. Deeds matter: the dead are judged according to their works. This is consistent with every assessment the risen Christ made in the seven letters ("I know your works"), with the Spirit's declaration in 14:13 ("their deeds follow with them"), and with the promise of 22:12 ("my reward is with me, to render to every man according to what he has done"). But belonging also matters: the book of life is the final determinant. Deeds and inscription together constitute the judgment. The books of deeds provide the evidence of how a person lived; the book of life determines whether that person is counted among those who belong to God.

The structural logic that connects the four scenes is one of progressive finality. The dragon is bound (temporary restraint), then destroyed (permanent). The saints are raised and reign (the first resurrection), establishing their vindication before the general judgment. The nations' final rebellion is crushed instantly (fire from heaven), demonstrating that even after a thousand years of peace, the dragon's deception produces nothing but catastrophe. The great white throne judgment encompasses all remaining persons and settles every case. Death and Hades -- the last hostile powers standing -- are destroyed. After chapter 20, nothing remains to be judged, no enemy remains to be defeated, no power of death remains to threaten God's people. The ground is cleared for the new creation of chapters 21-22.

The chapter also completes the resolution of the document's central paradox: that the saints conquer by being conquered. In 13:7, the beast "was given to make war with the saints and to overcome them." In 12:11, the brothers "overcame him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, and they did not love their life even when faced with death." In 20:4, the beheaded ones -- the very people whom the beast overcame -- come to life and reign. Their conquest through death is vindicated by their resurrection to kingship. The beast overcame their bodies; God overcame their death. The beast took their lives; God gave them thrones. The apparent contradiction between earthly defeat and heavenly victory, which has run through the entire document, is resolved in the first resurrection. The ones who lost everything -- including their lives -- gain everything: life, priesthood, kingship, and immunity from the second death.

The Ezekiel 38-39 pattern that governs verses 7-10 adds a final prophetic layer. In Ezekiel, God gathers a restored Israel from the nations, settles them securely in their land, and then permits a final assault by Gog from the north -- only to destroy the attackers with fire and brimstone, displaying his glory and holiness before all the nations (Ezekiel 38:23; 39:21-29). The result in Ezekiel is that Israel knows the LORD is their God (39:22) and that the nations know God's purpose (39:23). The author of Revelation follows the same sequence: restoration (the millennial reign), final attack (Gog and Magog), divine destruction (fire from heaven), and the definitive manifestation of God's justice. The pattern is not invented but inherited from Israel's prophetic tradition and applied to the eschatological climax.

The chapter's final image -- the lake of fire -- draws together every strand of judgment in the document. Into it are cast: the beast and false prophet (19:20), the dragon (20:10), Death and Hades (20:14), and all whose names are not in the book of life (20:15). The lake of fire is the second death (20:14; 21:8) -- the absolute termination from which there is no return. It is the antithesis of the river of life that flows from the throne in the new Jerusalem (22:1). Where the lake of fire consumes, the river of life sustains. Where the second death ends all possibility, the tree of life bears fruit every month (22:2). The chapter closes with destruction so that the next chapter can open with creation. The old order -- characterized by the dragon's deception, the beast's tyranny, Babylon's seduction, and Death's dominion -- is entirely eliminated. What remains is the new heaven, the new earth, and the God who declares: "Behold, I am making all things new" (21:5).
