# John 4 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (John 3):** The preceding chapter contains two major movements. First, Nicodemus -- a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, a teacher of Israel -- comes to Jesus "by night" and is told that no one can see or enter the kingdom of God without being "born from above" (gennethe anothen, 3:3, 5). Nicodemus misunderstands, taking "from above" as "again" in the sense of physical re-entry into the womb. Jesus rebukes him: "Are you the teacher of Israel and you do not understand these things?" (3:10). The discourse moves to the themes of believing, heavenly testimony rejected, the Son of Man who descended from heaven, and the one "lifted up" like the bronze serpent (Numbers 21:8-9), leading to the declaration that God loved the world and gave his only-begotten Son so that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life (3:16). The chapter then returns to John the Baptist for his final testimony, in which John declares himself only the friend of the bridegroom, not the bridegroom (3:29), and concludes: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (3:30). The chapter closes with a summary: the one who comes from above is above all; the one who comes from the earth speaks of the earth. The Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand. "The one who believes in the Son has eternal life; but the one who does not obey the Son will not see life, but the wrath of God remains on him" (3:36).

**What the author argues immediately after (John 5):** Jesus returns to Jerusalem for a feast and heals a man who has been paralyzed for thirty-eight years at the pool of Bethesda, on the Sabbath. When confronted by the Jewish authorities, Jesus responds: "My Father is working until now, and I am working" (5:17). This provokes the charge that he is "making himself equal with God" (5:18), and Jesus delivers an extended discourse on the relationship between Father and Son -- the Son does nothing on his own but only what he sees the Father doing (5:19), the Father has given all judgment to the Son (5:22), whoever hears the Son's word and believes the one who sent him has eternal life and does not come into judgment (5:24), the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God and live (5:25). The discourse appeals to multiple witnesses: John the Baptist, the works Jesus performs, the Father himself, and the scriptures -- "You search the scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life, and it is they that testify about me" (5:39). The chapter ends with Moses as a witness against those who claim to follow him but reject the one Moses wrote about (5:45-47).

**How chapter 4 connects the surrounding argument:** Chapter 3 presented a named, male, Jewish ruler and teacher of Israel who came to Jesus under cover of darkness and could not grasp the requirement of birth "from above." He fades from the scene without a confession, without a public declaration, without bringing others to Jesus. Chapter 4 now presents an unnamed, female, Samaritan, socially marginal woman who encounters Jesus in broad daylight (noon, v. 6) and -- despite initial misunderstanding -- arrives at a question about whether Jesus might be the Messiah, leaves her water jar, goes to her city, and brings the entire town to him. The contrast is deliberate and structural. The teacher of Israel failed to comprehend what this outsider grasps. The man who came by night could not testify; the woman in the noon sun becomes a witness.

At the same time, chapter 4 continues the geographic movement that runs through this section of the document. In chapters 1-2, Jesus is in Galilee (Cana), then goes to Jerusalem for Passover (2:13). In chapter 3, he is in the Judean countryside with his disciples, baptizing. Chapter 4 traces his departure from Judea back to Galilee, but the route runs through Samaria -- territory that many Jews avoided by crossing the Jordan and traveling through Perea. The author insists that Jesus "had to" (edei) pass through Samaria (4:4). This necessity is not merely geographic; it serves the author's argument that the revelation of God through Jesus is breaking beyond Jewish boundaries. John 3:16 declared that God loved "the world" (ton kosmon); chapter 4 begins to show what that looks like in practice.

The chapter also advances the author's running theme of what constitutes true belief. Nicodemus believed on the basis of signs (3:2: "no one can do these signs unless God is with him") but could not move beyond that. The Samaritans will begin with belief based on the woman's testimony and then move to belief based on their own encounter: "We no longer believe because of what you said, for we have heard for ourselves and know that this one is truly the Savior of the world" (4:42). The chapter ends with the royal official whose son is healed, and the author explicitly identifies this as "the second sign" (4:54), connecting it to the first sign at Cana (2:11) and resuming the question of what signs produce and whether they can lead to authentic belief.

The transition into chapter 5 continues the geographic rhythm (back to Jerusalem), but the content shifts to direct confrontation with the Jewish authorities over Jesus's identity and authority. Chapter 4 is a hinge: the revelation that was offered to Israel's teacher in chapter 3 (and largely not understood) is now received by Samaritans and a Gentile-associated royal official, before the confrontation with official Judaism intensifies in chapter 5.

## Verses 1-3: Departure from Judea

"Therefore, when the Lord knew that the Pharisees had heard that Jesus was making and baptizing more disciples than John -- although Jesus himself was not baptizing, but his disciples were -- he left Judea and went away again into Galilee."

The opening connects directly to the preceding narrative. The growing number of Jesus's disciples has come to the Pharisees' attention, creating a potential confrontation that the author signals but does not elaborate. The parenthetical correction -- "although Jesus himself was not baptizing, but his disciples were" -- is the kind of precise clarification characteristic of this author (see 2:21, 6:6, 7:39, 11:13, 12:33, 21:19). It guards against a misunderstanding while maintaining the flow of the narrative.

The verb "left" (apheken) and the destination "Galilee" set up the geographic frame. But the significant element is the route, specified in the next verse.

## Verses 4-6: The Necessity of Samaria and Jacob's Well

"And he had to pass through Samaria. So he came to a city of Samaria called Sychar, near the piece of land that Jacob gave to his son Joseph; and Jacob's well was there. So Jesus, being wearied from his journey, was sitting thus by the well. It was about the sixth hour."

**"He had to pass through Samaria" (edei auton dierchesthai dia tes Samareias).** The word edei ("it was necessary") carries theological weight throughout this document. The author uses it for divine necessity: "the Son of Man must (dei) be lifted up" (3:14), "you must (dei) be born from above" (3:7), "he must (dei) increase" (3:30). The geographic route from Judea to Galilee through Samaria was the most direct, but many Jews took the longer route through Perea to avoid Samaritan territory (Josephus, Antiquities 20.118, records violent incidents between Jews and Samaritans along this route). The author's use of edei signals more than convenience; there is a divine appointment at work.

**Samaria and the Samaritans.** The historical background is essential for a first-century reader. After the Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE, the Assyrians deported many Israelites and settled foreign populations in their place (2 Kings 17:24-41). These settlers intermarried with the remaining Israelites and adopted a form of Yahweh worship mixed with their own religious practices. The resulting Samaritan community accepted only the five books of Moses (the Pentateuch) as authoritative scripture and rejected the prophets, the writings, and the centrality of Jerusalem. They built their own temple on Mount Gerizim, which John Hyrcanus destroyed in 128 BCE (Josephus, Antiquities 13.256). The animosity between Jews and Samaritans was deep and mutual -- a religious, ethnic, and political hostility stretching back centuries. Sirach 50:25-26 expresses the Jewish attitude: "With two nations my soul is vexed, and the third is no nation: those who live on Mount Seir, the Philistines, and the foolish people that live in Shechem." The Samaritans are not even dignified as a nation.

**"Near the piece of land that Jacob gave to his son Joseph" (plēsion tou chōriou ho edōken Iakōb tō Iōsēph tō huiō autou).** The author locates the scene with genealogical precision. Genesis 33:18-20 records Jacob purchasing land near Shechem. Genesis 48:22 records Jacob giving Joseph "one portion (shechem) above your brothers." Joshua 24:32 records that the bones of Joseph, brought up from Egypt, were buried at Shechem, in the piece of ground Jacob had bought. This is ancestral ground for both Jews and Samaritans -- the territory of Joseph, the patriarch shared by both communities. The location is not neutral; it is the exact ground where the family history of Israel and Samaria intersects.

**"Jacob's well was there."** This well (pege, v. 6, though phrear, "cistern/well," in vv. 11-12) connects to the Jacob references the author established at the end of chapter 1. In 1:47, Jesus identified Nathanael as "truly an Israelite in whom there is no deceit" -- a deliberate allusion to Jacob the deceiver. In 1:51, Jesus promised Nathanael a vision of angels ascending and descending "on the Son of Man," replacing Jacob's ladder. Now Jesus sits at Jacob's actual well, on Jacob's actual land. The Jacob motif continues: the author is systematically presenting Jesus as the one who fulfills, supersedes, and replaces what Jacob represented. Jacob gave this well; Jesus will offer water that makes wells unnecessary.

Moreover, well scenes in Israel's scriptures function as betrothal type-scenes -- recurring narrative patterns in which a man meets his future bride at a well in a foreign land. Abraham's servant finds Rebekah at a well (Genesis 24:10-61). Jacob meets Rachel at a well (Genesis 29:1-12). Moses meets Zipporah at a well (Exodus 2:15-21). The pattern includes several standard elements: the man travels to a foreign land, arrives at a well, encounters a woman, water is drawn, the woman runs to tell her family, and a marriage results. A first-century reader steeped in Torah would recognize this pattern operating in the background of John 4. Jesus travels to foreign territory (Samaria), arrives at a well, encounters a woman, the conversation centers on water, the woman runs to tell her city, and a union results -- not a marriage, but a covenantal joining of the Samaritans to the one who is the true bridegroom (a title just applied to Jesus in 3:29, where John declared himself the "friend of the bridegroom"). The betrothal type-scene is being transformed: the bride is not an individual woman but a community; the marriage is not biological but spiritual.

**"Jesus, being wearied from his journey, was sitting thus by the well."** The detail of weariness (kekopiakos, "having labored, being fatigued") is striking. The author who opened his document by declaring that the Word was God and created all things now records that this same Word, in flesh, is tired from walking. The incarnation declared in 1:14 -- "the Word became flesh" -- is here shown in its mundane reality. The one through whom all things were made experiences the limitations of created human existence: fatigue, thirst (v. 7), the need to sit down.

**"About the sixth hour" (hora hōs hektē).** Approximately noon, counting from sunrise. This is the hottest part of the day, when drawing water was unusual -- women typically came to the well in the cool of morning or evening. The woman's arrival at noon suggests she is avoiding the other women of the town, which the narrative will explain through her marital history. The time marker also creates a contrast with Nicodemus, who came to Jesus "by night" (3:2). Darkness and light are not merely temporal details in this document; they carry thematic weight (3:19-21: "people loved the darkness rather than the light because their deeds were evil"). Nicodemus approached in darkness; this woman encounters Jesus in full daylight.

## Verses 7-9: The Request for Water

"A woman of Samaria came to draw water. Jesus said to her, 'Give me a drink.' For his disciples had gone away into the city to buy food. Then the Samaritan woman said to him, 'How is it that you, being a Jew, ask a drink of me, a Samaritan woman?' (For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans.)"

Jesus initiates the conversation with a request -- "Give me a drink" (dos moi pein). The request crosses multiple social boundaries simultaneously:

1. **Ethnic/religious boundary.** Jews and Samaritans did not share common vessels, and many Jews considered Samaritans ritually unclean. The author's parenthetical -- "For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (ou gar synchrōntai Ioudaioi Samaritais) -- may refer specifically to the sharing of vessels used for eating and drinking (the verb synchraomai can mean "use together with," particularly in the sense of sharing utensils). The Mishnah (later codification, but reflecting earlier attitudes) considers Samaritan women as permanently unclean (Niddah 4:1).

2. **Gender boundary.** In the conventions of the time, a rabbi would not typically engage a woman in extended public conversation. The disciples' astonishment when they return (v. 27: "they were amazed that he was speaking with a woman") confirms that this violates social expectation.

3. **Moral/social boundary.** As the conversation will reveal, this particular woman has had five husbands and is currently living with a man who is not her husband. She is socially marginal even within her own community, which may explain her solitary trip to the well at noon.

Jesus's request places himself in a position of need -- he is thirsty, he has no bucket (v. 11), he depends on her cooperation. The one who will offer "living water" begins by asking for ordinary water. The pattern mirrors the incarnation itself: the one who gives life enters a condition of need.

The woman's response is sharp and perceptive. She identifies two relevant facts immediately: he is a Jew (presumably from his speech, dress, or appearance), and she is a Samaritan woman. She names the social violation explicitly. Her question is not hostile but genuinely puzzled -- why would he cross this boundary?

## Verses 10-15: Living Water

"Jesus answered and said to her, 'If you knew the gift of God, and who it is who says to you, "Give me a drink," you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water.' The woman said to him, 'Sir, you have nothing to draw with and the well is deep; where then do you get that living water? You are not greater than our father Jacob, are you, who gave us the well and drank from it himself, and his sons and his livestock?' Jesus answered and said to her, 'Everyone who drinks of this water will thirst again; but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never thirst -- ever; but the water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water springing up to eternal life.' The woman said to him, 'Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty nor come all the way here to draw.'"

**"The gift of God" (tēn dōrean tou theou).** Jesus frames the encounter in terms the woman has not yet grasped. The "gift" is not identified yet; the reader must track the conversation to discover it. The phrase "gift of God" appears in Israel's tradition in connection with the provisions God gave Israel -- the manna (Exodus 16), the water from the rock (Exodus 17:1-7, Numbers 20:2-13), the Torah itself (Deuteronomy 4:8). Each was an unearned provision from God for the sustenance of his people.

**"Living water" (hydōr zōn).** The phrase has a double meaning that the woman initially grasps only on its literal level. In ordinary Greek, "living water" (hydōr zōn) means "flowing water" -- water from a spring or stream, as opposed to still, cistern water. The woman hears Jesus claiming access to a spring of fresh running water, which would indeed be superior to well water. But in Israel's scriptures, "living water" carries a far deeper significance:

1. **Jeremiah 2:13:** "My people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters (pēgēn hydatos zōēs in the LXX), and hewn out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water." God himself is the fountain of living water. Israel's sin is abandoning this fountain for self-made alternatives that leak and fail.

2. **Jeremiah 17:13:** "O LORD, the hope of Israel, all who forsake you shall be put to shame... because they have forsaken the LORD, the fountain of living water."

3. **Zechariah 14:8:** "On that day living waters (hydōr zōn) shall flow out from Jerusalem, half of them to the eastern sea and half of them to the western sea." This is an eschatological promise -- in the day of the Lord, living water will flow from God's city.

4. **Ezekiel 47:1-12:** The prophet sees water flowing from the threshold of the restored temple, growing deeper as it flows, turning the Dead Sea fresh, and producing trees whose leaves are for healing. The temple becomes a source of life-giving water for the entire land.

5. **Isaiah 12:3:** "With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation (or deliverance)." This was recited during the feast of Tabernacles water ceremony (Sukkot), which becomes significant later in this document (7:37-38, where Jesus stands and cries out, "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink").

The author is working on both levels simultaneously. The woman hears a claim about superior spring water; the reader, equipped with these scriptural echoes, hears a claim about access to God himself as the source of life. The conversation will gradually move the woman from the literal to the deeper level.

**"You are not greater than our father Jacob, are you?"** The woman's question is phrased to expect a negative answer (mē with the indicative). She assumes the answer is no. But the reader, who has encountered 1:51 (angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man, replacing Jacob's ladder), already knows the answer: yes, he is greater than Jacob. The author has been building the Jacob-supersession motif since chapter 1, and it reaches its climax here at Jacob's own well. Jacob dug a well that provides water requiring repeated return; Jesus offers water that eliminates the need to return.

The woman's reference to "our father Jacob" (tou patros hēmōn Iakōb) is significant. The Samaritans claimed descent from Jacob through Joseph (the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh). She says "our father" -- asserting her community's claim to the patriarchal heritage. The question of who Jacob's true heirs are -- a live issue between Jews and Samaritans -- is subtly present.

**"Everyone who drinks of this water will thirst again; but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never thirst -- ever."** Jesus draws a contrast between the well water (this water, toutou tou hydatos) and the water he gives. The well provides temporary relief; Jesus provides permanent satisfaction. The language of permanent satisfaction echoes Isaiah 49:10: "They will not hunger or thirst, neither will the scorching heat or sun strike them; for he who has compassion on them will lead them and guide them to springs of water." Isaiah 55:1: "Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters." The promise is eschatological: the condition of thirst -- of unsatisfied need, of constant returning -- will be overcome.

**"Will become in him a spring of water springing up to eternal life" (genēsetai en autō pēgē hydatos hallomenou eis zōēn aiōnion).** The water Jesus gives does not remain stagnant in the recipient. It becomes a spring (pēgē) -- the same word used in Jeremiah 2:13 for God as the "fountain" of living water. The recipient becomes a carrier of the life-giving source. The word "springing up" (hallomenou) conveys leaping, surging movement -- this is not a trickle but a vigorous, self-sustaining flow. And its direction is "unto eternal life" (eis zōēn aiōnion), the term the author has introduced in 3:15-16 and will use throughout the document as the central gift offered through Jesus.

The woman's response -- "Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty nor come all the way here to draw" -- remains on the literal level. She wants the practical benefit: no more trips to the well. This is the same pattern of misunderstanding the author used with Nicodemus (3:4: "How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb?"). In both cases, the hearer takes the physical/literal meaning while Jesus speaks of spiritual/heavenly realities. But unlike Nicodemus, who remains stuck in his misunderstanding, this woman will be led forward through the conversation.

## Verses 16-19: The Exposure of Her Situation

"He said to her, 'Go, call your husband and come here.' The woman answered and said, 'I have no husband.' Jesus said to her, 'You have said well, "I have no husband"; for you have had five husbands, and the one whom you now have is not your husband; this you have said truly.' The woman said to him, 'Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet.'"

The conversation takes an abrupt turn. Jesus's instruction -- "Go, call your husband" -- appears to change the subject entirely. But within the flow of the dialogue, it serves a specific function: the woman has asked for living water. Jesus now demonstrates that he knows who she is, fully and specifically. The gift he offers is not impersonal; it comes from one who knows the recipient.

**"You have had five husbands, and the one whom you now have is not your husband."** The statement is precise, factual, and unsoftened. Jesus names her situation without condemnation, without insult, and without euphemism. The five husbands may be the result of death, divorce, or some combination (under both Jewish and Samaritan law, a woman could be divorced by her husband). The current arrangement -- living with a man who is not her husband -- places her outside the boundary of accepted social and religious practice in any first-century community. This is likely the reason for her isolation (coming to the well at noon alone) and for her community's apparent awareness of her situation (v. 29, where her testimony is credible precisely because it includes Jesus's knowledge of her past: "Come, see a man who told me all the things that I have done").

Some interpreters in later centuries read the five husbands as an allegory for the five pagan nations settled in Samaria by the Assyrians (2 Kings 17:24: people from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim), with the "not your husband" representing the impure Yahweh-worship the Samaritans practiced. While a first-century reader familiar with 2 Kings might hear such an echo, the narrative treats the woman as a specific individual with a specific history: Jesus's knowledge of her is presented as the same kind of supernatural perception shown toward Nathanael (1:48), Peter (1:42), and all people (2:24-25). The primary function is to demonstrate that the one offering living water sees her completely and offers it anyway.

**"Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet" (kyrie, theōrō hoti prophētēs ei sy).** Her address shifts from the puzzled "How is it that you..." (v. 9) to a recognition of prophetic authority. In Israel's tradition, prophets were known for supernatural knowledge of hidden things: Samuel knew about Saul's lost donkeys and about his coming kingship (1 Samuel 9-10); Elisha knew what the king of Syria spoke in his bedchamber (2 Kings 6:12); Nathan knew David's hidden sin (2 Samuel 12). The woman's conclusion is natural for someone within the Mosaic tradition that Samaritans shared: a person who knows hidden things must be a prophet.

## Verses 20-24: Worship in Spirit and Truth

"'Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, and you people say that in Jerusalem is the place where one must worship.' Jesus said to her, 'Woman, believe me, an hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father. You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews. But an hour is coming, and now is, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth; for the Father seeks such people to be his worshipers. God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth.'"

**"Our fathers worshiped on this mountain."** "This mountain" is Mount Gerizim, visible from Jacob's well. Mount Gerizim was the central sacred site for Samaritans. In the Samaritan Pentateuch, the tenth commandment (their enumeration differs from the Jewish one) commands the building of an altar on Mount Gerizim. Deuteronomy 11:29 and 27:12 describe the blessings pronounced from Gerizim. The Samaritans built a temple on Gerizim (constructed perhaps in the late fifth or fourth century BCE), which John Hyrcanus destroyed in 128 BCE. Even after its destruction, Gerizim remained the holiest site for Samaritan worship.

The woman's move from the personal exposure of her marital situation to the question of worship is often read as evasion -- changing the subject to avoid uncomfortable personal disclosure. This may be part of the psychology, but the transition also makes natural sense: if this man is a prophet, he can settle the question that divides Jews and Samaritans. The central dispute between the two communities was not about God's existence or the authority of the Torah but about the location where God had chosen to be worshiped. Deuteronomy 12:5-6 commands Israel to worship at "the place that the LORD your God will choose." Jews identified this as Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 7:12-16); Samaritans identified it as Gerizim. If a prophet has appeared, he can adjudicate.

**"You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews."** Jesus does not treat the Jewish-Samaritan dispute as a matter of equal validity. He draws a clear distinction: the Samaritans worship "what they do not know" (ho ouk oidate), while the Jews worship "what they know" (ho oidamen). The pronoun shift is notable -- Jesus includes himself among the "we" who know. The Samaritans, by accepting only the Pentateuch and rejecting the prophets and writings, had limited their knowledge of God's self-revelation. The prophetic tradition -- Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, the Psalms, the wisdom literature -- contained dimensions of God's character and purposes that the Samaritans did not access. Jesus is not being diplomatic; he is being truthful. The fuller knowledge of God resides in the tradition that preserved the fuller scriptures.

**"Salvation is from the Jews" (hē sōtēria ek tōn Ioudaiōn estin).** This statement is striking in context, precisely because the chapter is about to show salvation reaching Samaritans. But the source, the origin, the channel through which God's saving action flows is the Jewish people and their scriptural tradition. The Messiah comes from Judah (Genesis 49:10), the promises run through Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, David, and the prophets. The Samaritans' own expectation of the Taheb (the "restorer," based on Deuteronomy 18:15-18) is rooted in a text from the Torah that was fulfilled through the Jewish lineage. Salvation extends to the world (3:16-17), but it comes through Israel (Romans 9:4-5 will later articulate this same principle, though it is outside this document's scope).

**"An hour is coming, and now is" (erchetai hōra kai nyn estin).** The double temporal marker -- future ("is coming") and present ("now is") -- is characteristic of this author's eschatological framework. The decisive transformation is both imminent and already inaugurated. In this document, "the hour" (hē hōra) is a loaded term, ultimately referring to the hour of Jesus's death and glorification (2:4, 7:30, 8:20, 12:23, 12:27, 13:1, 17:1). The hour that redefines all worship is connected to the hour of the cross.

**"When the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth" (hote hoi alēthinoi proskynētai proskynēsousin tō patri en pneumati kai alētheia).** The adjective alēthinos ("true, real, genuine" -- the same word used in 1:9 for "the true light") identifies not a new category of worshiper but the real, intended kind -- the worshiper that all the patterns pointed toward. The contrast is not between outward and inward worship but between worship tied to a physical location (Gerizim or Jerusalem) and worship made possible by the Spirit and grounded in truth (the full revelation of God through Jesus, who is himself "the truth," as 14:6 will declare).

**"In spirit" (en pneumati)** does not mean "in one's spirit" (internal, subjective worship) as opposed to external forms. The word pneuma in this document consistently refers to the Holy Spirit (1:32-33, 3:5-8, 7:39, 14:17, 14:26, 15:26, 16:13, 20:22). The Spirit was promised as the one who would come after Jesus's glorification (7:39) and lead into all truth (16:13). Worship "in spirit" is worship enabled and animated by the Holy Spirit -- the same Spirit that "remained" on Jesus (1:33) and that Jesus would give to those who believe.

**"And truth" (kai alētheia).** "Truth" (alētheia) is another of this document's central terms. The Word-become-flesh was "full of grace and truth" (1:14). Jesus will declare himself "the truth" (14:6). The Spirit is "the Spirit of truth" (14:17, 15:26, 16:13). Truth in this document is not an abstraction but the reality of God disclosed in the person of Jesus. Worship "in truth" is worship that responds to and is shaped by God's self-revelation in Jesus.

Taken together, "in spirit and truth" describes worship that is made possible by the Spirit's presence and responsive to the full revelation of God in Jesus. The location question -- Gerizim or Jerusalem -- becomes obsolete not because worship becomes purely interior but because the locus of God's presence is no longer a mountain or a building but the person of Jesus and, through him, the Spirit. The tabernacle imagery of 1:14 -- the Word "tabernacled among us" -- reaches its logical conclusion: if God's dwelling has moved from building to person, worship cannot remain tied to a building.

**"For the Father seeks such people to be his worshipers."** The verb "seeks" (zētei) places God in the active role. The Father is not passively waiting for worshipers to figure out the right formula; he is actively searching for them. This is consistent with the author's broader portrait of divine initiative: the Father sends the Son (3:16-17), the Father draws people to the Son (6:44), the Father loves the world (3:16). The seeking Father anticipates the searching shepherd of chapter 10 and the seeking Jesus of chapter 4 itself -- the one who "had to" go through Samaria.

**"God is spirit" (pneuma ho theos).** This is a statement about God's nature, not his substance in a philosophical sense. It affirms that God is not localized, not material, not containable in a building made with hands (an idea that Solomon himself articulated at the temple's dedication: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built," 1 Kings 8:27). Because God is spirit, worship of God cannot be confined to a geographic coordinate. The Spirit-nature of God is the basis for the Spirit-character of true worship.

## Verses 25-26: The Messiah Declaration

"The woman said to him, 'I know that Messiah is coming (he who is called Christ); when that one comes, he will declare all things to us.' Jesus said to her, 'I who speak to you am he.'"

**"I know that Messiah is coming."** The Samaritan expectation of the Taheb ("the restorer" or "the one who returns") was based primarily on Deuteronomy 18:15-18: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers." Since the Samaritans accepted only the Pentateuch, their messianic hope was centered on this prophetic figure rather than the Davidic king of Jewish expectation. The author translates the Samaritan term into its Jewish equivalent: "Messiah... who is called Christ." The woman's understanding of this figure's role is specific: "he will declare all things to us" (anaggelei hēmin hapanta) -- he will explain, reveal, make known. This expectation aligns with the prophetic role (declaring God's word and will) and connects to the prologue's statement that the Word has "made known" (exēgēsato, 1:18) the unseen God.

**"I who speak to you am he" (egō eimi, ho lalōn soi).** This is the most direct, unambiguous self-identification as Messiah in any of the four accounts. In the Synoptic accounts, Jesus is circumspect about the Messiah title, often deflecting or reframing it (Mark 8:29-30, where he accepts Peter's confession but orders silence; Matthew 26:63-64 and Mark 14:61-62, where the response is indirect). Here, to a Samaritan woman at a well, in the middle of the day, Jesus makes the declaration without qualification.

The phrase egō eimi ("I am") carries additional weight in this document. It appears repeatedly in absolute form (6:20, 8:24, 8:28, 8:58, 13:19, 18:5-6) and in the seven "I am" statements with predicates ("I am the bread of life," "I am the light of the world," etc.). In the LXX, egō eimi translates the divine self-identification in Exodus 3:14 ("I AM WHO I AM") and in Isaiah 41:4, 43:10, 43:25, 45:18, 46:4, 48:12 ("I am he"). While the immediate sense in 4:26 is "I am the one you are speaking about" (the Messiah), the echo of the divine name is audible to a reader who has heard the prologue declare that "the Word was God" (1:1).

The fact that this declaration is made to a Samaritan, a woman, and an outsider -- rather than to the teacher of Israel in the preceding chapter -- is the author's commentary enacted in narrative. The teacher of Israel could not understand being born from above; the Samaritan woman is told directly, "I am he." The movement of revelation in this document does not follow the expected channels.

## Verses 27-30: The Disciples Return, the Woman Departs

"At this point his disciples came, and they were amazed that he was speaking with a woman; yet no one said, 'What do you seek?' or 'Why are you speaking with her?' So the woman left her water jar, and went into the city and said to the people, 'Come, see a man who told me all the things that I have done; this is not the Christ, is it?' They went out of the city, and were coming to him."

**"They were amazed that he was speaking with a woman."** The disciples' astonishment confirms the social boundary Jesus has crossed. Their silence -- no one asked "What do you seek?" or "Why are you speaking with her?" -- suggests a pattern already established: the disciples have learned that Jesus's actions, though puzzling, proceed from an authority they do not fully comprehend (cf. 2:22, where they remembered his words after the resurrection; 12:16, where they understood later what they could not grasp at the time).

**"The woman left her water jar" (aphēken oun tēn hydrion autēs).** This detail functions on multiple levels. Practically, leaving the jar would allow others to draw water when they came to the well, or suggest she intended to return. Narratively, it parallels the disciples in the Synoptic accounts who "left their nets" to follow Jesus (Mark 1:18, Matthew 4:20). The jar was the reason she came to the well; she leaves it behind because she has found something that supersedes her original purpose. She came for water; she leaves having encountered the source of living water. The jar -- the vessel for carrying ordinary water -- becomes superfluous.

**"Come, see a man who told me all the things that I have done."** The woman's testimony takes the form of an invitation -- "Come, see" (deute idete) -- the same formula used by Philip to Nathanael (1:46) and by Jesus to his first disciples (1:39). The chain of testimony established in chapter 1 (John testifies, Andrew testifies, Philip testifies) now extends through this Samaritan woman. Her testimony is grounded in personal experience: he knew her. She does not claim to have fully understood him; she asks a tentative question.

**"This is not the Christ, is it?" (mēti houtos estin ho Christos).** The particle mēti introduces a question expecting a negative answer, yet her tone is more open than the grammar strictly requires. She is not asserting; she is inviting others to come and investigate for themselves. Her method of witness is effective precisely because it does not overclaim. She reports what happened to her and lets the encounter speak.

## Verses 31-38: Food, Harvest, and the Work of the Sent One

"Meanwhile the disciples were urging him, saying, 'Rabbi, eat.' But he said to them, 'I have food to eat that you do not know about.' So the disciples were saying to one another, 'No one brought him anything to eat, did he?' Jesus said to them, 'My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work. Do you not say, "There are yet four months, and then comes the harvest"? Behold, I say to you, lift up your eyes and look on the fields, that they are white for harvest. Already the one who reaps is receiving wages and is gathering fruit for eternal life, so that the one who sows and the one who reaps may rejoice together. For in this case the saying is true, "One sows and another reaps." I sent you to reap that for which you have not labored; others have labored and you have entered into their labor.'"

**"I have food to eat that you do not know about."** The same pattern of misunderstanding continues: the disciples take Jesus's statement literally, wondering if someone brought him food while they were away. This mirrors the woman's literal hearing of "living water." The author consistently presents the disciples as slow to grasp the deeper meaning of Jesus's language (see 11:12-13, 14:5, 14:8, 16:17-18).

**"My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work" (emon brōma estin hina poiēsō to thelēma tou pempsantos me kai teleiōsō autou to ergon).** Jesus defines his sustenance in terms of obedience and mission. The language of "the one who sent me" (ho pempsas me) runs throughout this document -- Jesus identifies himself repeatedly as "sent" by the Father (5:23-24, 5:30, 5:36-37, 6:38-39, 6:44, 7:16, 7:28-29, 8:16, 8:18, 8:26, 8:29, 9:4, 12:44-45, 12:49, 13:20, 14:24, 15:21, 16:5, 17:3, 17:18, 17:21, 17:25, 20:21). This is the most frequently repeated description of Jesus's relationship to the Father in the entire document. The will of the sender defines the work of the sent one.

The verb "accomplish" (teleiōsō, from teleioō, "to complete, finish, bring to perfection") anticipates the cry from the cross: "It is finished" (tetelestai, 19:30). The work Jesus was sent to do has a definite end point, and this conversation at the well -- crossing ethnic, gender, and religious boundaries to bring the revelation of God to the Samaritans -- is part of that work.

**"Lift up your eyes and look on the fields, that they are white for harvest."** Whether the saying about four months before harvest is a proverb or a literal seasonal indicator, Jesus's point is that the spiritual harvest is ready now. The most likely visual referent: the Samaritan townspeople, summoned by the woman, are even now coming across the fields toward Jesus. They are the "white harvest" -- ripe, ready to be gathered. The harvest metaphor connects to Israel's prophetic tradition: Joel 3:13 (4:13 in Hebrew) describes a harvest in the context of the day of the Lord. Hosea 6:11: "Also, O Judah, a harvest is appointed for you." But here the harvest is not judgment; it is the ingathering of believers, gathering "fruit for eternal life" (karpon eis zōēn aiōnion).

**"One sows and another reaps."** This saying, which Jesus identifies as proverbial ("the saying is true," alēthinos), describes the division of labor in God's work. The one who sowed -- Moses, the prophets, perhaps John the Baptist, perhaps Jesus himself in this conversation -- prepared the ground. The one who reaps -- the disciples, and ultimately the later community that carries the message -- enters a harvest they did not plant. The Samaritan harvest is a case in point: the Torah (received by the Samaritans), the prophets (whose words prepared the broader field), the woman's testimony, and Jesus's own conversation have all sowed. The reaping is about to happen.

**"I sent you to reap" (egō apesteila hymas therizein).** The past tense "sent" (apesteila, aorist) is striking, since the formal commissioning of the disciples lies in the future (17:18, 20:21). Jesus speaks from the perspective of the mission's completion. The "others" who have labored are debated: they may include Moses, the prophets, John the Baptist, or Jesus himself. The point is that the disciples' harvesting is built upon prior labor -- they are entering a field already cultivated.

## Verses 39-42: The Samaritans Believe

"From that city many of the Samaritans believed in him because of the word of the woman who testified, 'He told me all the things that I have done.' So when the Samaritans came to him, they were asking him to stay with them; and he stayed there two days. Many more believed because of his word; and they were saying to the woman, 'It is no longer because of what you said that we believe, for we have heard for ourselves and know that this one is truly the Savior of the world.'"

**"Many of the Samaritans believed in him because of the word of the woman who testified."** The author uses the vocabulary of testimony (martyreō) for the woman's report, placing her in the same category as John the Baptist, who was introduced as the one who came "to testify" (1:7). The chain of witness -- John, Andrew, Philip, and now this unnamed Samaritan woman -- is the mechanism by which belief spreads. Notably, the woman's testimony is effective: people believe. The teacher of Israel (Nicodemus) produced no followers; this woman's testimony brings a city.

**"They were asking him to stay with them" (ērōtōn auton meinai par' autois).** The verb meinai is from menō -- "to remain, abide, stay" -- the verb the author uses for the Spirit remaining on Jesus (1:33), the disciples remaining with Jesus (1:39), and later for the mutual indwelling of Jesus and the believer (15:4-10). The Samaritans ask Jesus to remain. He does -- "he stayed there two days." For a Jewish teacher to accept Samaritan hospitality, eating their food, sleeping under their roofs, for two full days, would be deeply transgressive by the standards of purity-conscious Judaism. Jesus does it without comment.

**"We have heard for ourselves and know that this one is truly the Savior of the world" (houtos estin alēthōs ho sōtēr tou kosmou).** The Samaritans' confession is remarkable for its scope. They do not call him the Messiah of Israel or the King of the Jews; they call him "the Savior of the world" (ho sōtēr tou kosmou). The title sōtēr ("savior, deliverer") is applied to God in the LXX: Isaiah 45:15 ("Truly you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior"); Isaiah 45:21 ("a righteous God and a Savior"); Psalm 24:5 (23:5 LXX, "the God of his salvation"). In Israel's experience, God was the Savior -- the one who delivered from Egypt, from exile, from enemies. The title is also used in the Greco-Roman world for kings, emperors, and benefactors who delivered cities from danger. Augustus was honored as "savior" in inscriptions throughout the eastern Mediterranean.

The Samaritans apply to Jesus a title that belongs to God in the Jewish scriptures and to the emperor in the political world, and they extend its scope to "the world" (tou kosmou) -- not just Israel, not just Samaria, but the entire human order. This declaration fulfills the prologue's statement that the true light "gives light to every person" (1:9) and that God sent the Son "so that the world might be saved through him" (3:17).

The progression of belief in this passage is instructive. It moves through stages: (1) the woman's testimony creates initial belief; (2) Jesus's own words ("his word," dia ton logon autou) produce deeper and wider belief; (3) the Samaritans arrive at personal knowledge ("we have heard for ourselves and know"). The pattern is precisely what the author advocates throughout the document: testimony leads to encounter, encounter leads to personal conviction. Secondhand testimony is a valid starting point ("many believed because of the woman's word"), but it is meant to lead to firsthand knowledge ("we have heard for ourselves"). This sequence addresses the situation of the author's own readers, who are hearing the testimony of others (the written gospel itself) and are invited to move through it to their own encounter with Jesus.

## Verses 43-45: Reception in Galilee

"After the two days he went forth from there into Galilee. For Jesus himself testified that a prophet has no honor in his own country. So when he came to Galilee, the Galileans welcomed him, having seen all the things that he did in Jerusalem at the feast; for they themselves also went to the feast."

**"A prophet has no honor in his own country" (prophētēs en tē idia patridi timēn ouk echei).** This proverb appears in all four accounts (Matthew 13:57, Mark 6:4, Luke 4:24), but its application here is puzzling and debated. In the Synoptic accounts, it explains rejection in Nazareth or Galilee. Here, the author seems to use it differently. Jesus leaves Samaria (where he was welcomed and confessed as Savior of the world) for Galilee, and the author introduces the proverb about dishonor in one's own country. The logic depends on what the author considers Jesus's "own country" (idia patris). Given the statement in 1:11 that "he came to his own" (eis ta idia ēlthen) -- referring to the Jewish homeland broadly -- and the focus of this document on Jerusalem and Judea as the center of opposition, the author may consider Judea (or more specifically, Jerusalem, the center of Jewish authority) as Jesus's "own country" in the fullest sense. The Galileans welcome him, but their welcome is based on having seen his signs in Jerusalem at the feast (2:23) -- a quality of belief the author has already treated as insufficient (2:23-25: "many believed in his name when they saw the signs... but Jesus did not entrust himself to them"). The proverb may thus explain why Jesus goes to Galilee rather than staying in Judea: in Judea (his "own country" in terms of his messianic and divine identity), he has no honor; in Galilee, he is at least welcomed, even if the welcome is superficial.

## Verses 46-54: The Second Sign -- The Royal Official's Son

"Therefore he came again to Cana of Galilee where he had made the water wine. And there was a royal official whose son was sick at Capernaum. When he heard that Jesus had come out of Judea into Galilee, he went to him and was asking him to come down and heal his son; for he was at the point of death. So Jesus said to him, 'Unless you people see signs and wonders, you will not believe.' The royal official said to him, 'Sir, come down before my child dies.' Jesus said to him, 'Go; your son lives.' The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and started off. As he was now going down, his slaves met him, saying that his son was living. So he inquired of them the hour when he began to get better. Then they said to him, 'Yesterday at the seventh hour the fever left him.' So the father knew that it was at that hour in which Jesus said to him, 'Your son lives'; and he himself believed and his whole household. This is again a second sign that Jesus performed when he had come out of Judea into Galilee."

**"Cana of Galilee where he had made the water wine."** The author brings the narrative full circle, returning to the location of the first sign (2:1-11). The geographic inclusion -- from Cana to Cana -- frames the material between as a unit. Between the first sign and the second sign, the reader has witnessed the temple confrontation (2:13-22), the Nicodemus encounter (3:1-21), John's final testimony (3:22-36), the Samaritan episode (4:1-42), and now returns to where it started.

**"A royal official" (basilikos).** The term basilikos means "one connected with the king" -- a courtier, officer, or official in the service of Herod Antipas, the tetrarch of Galilee. This figure is likely not Jewish in the strictest sense, or at least represents the Herodian court, which was culturally mixed (Herod the Great was Idumean, and his court blended Jewish, Hellenistic, and Roman elements). The progression of the chapter is significant: Jesus has moved from a Jewish teacher (Nicodemus in chapter 3), to a Samaritan woman and her community, and now to a royal official connected to the Hellenistic-Roman political structure. The circle of those who encounter Jesus is expanding outward -- from the center of Jewish religious authority to the margins and beyond.

**"Unless you people see signs and wonders, you will not believe" (ean mē sēmeia kai terata idēte, ou mē pisteusēte).** The plural "you" (idēte, pisteusēte) is addressed not to the official alone but to the Galileans generally. The phrase "signs and wonders" (sēmeia kai terata) echoes the exodus language: Deuteronomy 4:34, 6:22, 7:19, 26:8, 29:3 (28:46 LXX), 34:11 all describe God's acts in Egypt as "signs and wonders." The same phrase appears in Joel 2:30 (3:3 LXX) for eschatological portents. Jesus's statement carries a tone of lament or rebuke: belief should not require spectacular visible proof, yet this is what the Galileans demand. The contrast with the Samaritans is pointed. The Samaritans believed on the basis of the woman's word and then Jesus's own word -- no signs or wonders. The Galileans require visible demonstrations. The chapter implicitly ranks the quality of belief: word-based belief (the Samaritans) is superior to sign-based belief (the Galileans).

**"Sir, come down before my child dies."** The official does not engage with Jesus's rebuke; he simply repeats his plea with greater urgency. His faith at this point is limited -- he believes Jesus can heal but assumes Jesus must be physically present to do so ("come down" to Capernaum).

**"Go; your son lives" (poreuou; ho huios sou zē).** Jesus does not go to Capernaum. He speaks a word, and the word accomplishes the healing from a distance. This is consistent with the prologue's declaration about the creative, effective power of the Word: "All things came into being through him" (1:3). The Word that created the universe does not need proximity to act. The healing by spoken word also echoes the pattern of God's creative speech in Genesis: God spoke, and it was so. Jesus speaks, and the son lives.

**"The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him" (episteusen ho anthrōpos tō logō hon eipen autō Iēsous).** The progression of belief is precisely tracked. First, the official believes Jesus's word and acts on it (he starts for home). Then, when the timing is confirmed (the fever left at the seventh hour, the same hour Jesus spoke), he "believed" in a deeper sense, and "his whole household" with him. The author distinguishes between belief that takes Jesus at his word (v. 50) and the fuller belief that comes when the word is confirmed by experience (v. 53). Both are real; the second is more complete. The household believing echoes the pattern seen in the book of Acts (though written later) and in Israel's tradition, where the head of household's commitment brought the household into the covenant community (cf. Genesis 17:12-13, the circumcision of Abraham's household; Joshua 24:15, "as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD").

**"This is again a second sign" (touto palin deuteron sēmeion).** The author explicitly counts: this is the second sign, linked to the first (water to wine at Cana, 2:11). Both signs take place at Cana, both reveal Jesus's power over the material world (transforming water, healing at a distance), and both produce belief. The counting suggests the author is tracking the signs deliberately. The "sign" (sēmeion) in this document is not merely a miracle but a visible act that points beyond itself to the identity and mission of Jesus. The first sign "revealed his glory" (2:11); the second demonstrates that his word carries life-giving power even across distance -- a power that belongs to the Word who was in the beginning with God.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

The author constructs chapter 4 as a demonstration of the expanding scope of Jesus's revelation and an exploration of what constitutes authentic belief. The chapter moves through three encounters -- the Samaritan woman, the Samaritan townspeople, and the royal official -- each of which reveals something about how people come to recognize who Jesus is.

1. **The Samaritan woman encounter (vv. 1-26) systematically dismantles barriers.** Ethnic, gender, religious, and moral boundaries are crossed by Jesus's initiative. He "had to" go through Samaria; he initiates the conversation; he offers living water to a woman whose life places her outside respectable society. The conversation moves from literal misunderstanding (water, the well, Jacob) through prophetic exposure (the five husbands) to theological disclosure (true worship, the Messiah declaration). The Jacob motif that began in 1:47-51 reaches its climax: Jesus is greater than Jacob; his water supersedes Jacob's well; the true worship he inaugurates supersedes both Gerizim and Jerusalem. The betrothal type-scene pattern subtly communicates that Jesus is the bridegroom (already declared by John in 3:29) who has come to claim a bride -- not an individual but a people.

2. **The Samaritan community's response (vv. 39-42) models the progression from testimony to personal encounter.** The woman's witness brings initial belief; Jesus's own word produces deeper belief; the Samaritans arrive at a confession that surpasses anything offered in the preceding chapters: "the Savior of the world." The title breaks the ethnic boundary entirely. This is no longer about Israel's Messiah alone but about the one who saves the world -- the scope declared in 3:16-17. The Samaritans, despised by Jews as half-pagans, produce the most universal and theologically penetrating confession yet in the narrative.

3. **The royal official's son (vv. 46-54) demonstrates the power of Jesus's word and advances the sign-counting.** The healing occurs by spoken word alone, without physical proximity, connecting to the prologue's theology of the creative, effective Word. The official's progression from desperate petition through trust in Jesus's word to confirmed household belief models the journey the author wants his readers to take. The contrast with the Galilean demand for "signs and wonders" (v. 48) implicitly critiques sign-dependent faith while still affirming the sign as a genuine pointer to Jesus's identity.

4. **The chapter's implicit contrast with Nicodemus is structural.** The male Jewish teacher in chapter 3 came by night, misunderstood, and faded from the scene without confession or witness. The female Samaritan outsider in chapter 4 is met at noon, gradually comprehends, and becomes a witness who brings her entire city to Jesus. The teacher of Israel was told he must be "born from above" and could not understand; the Samaritan woman was offered "living water" and, though she initially misunderstood, was led to a recognition that Nicodemus never reached. The author's point is not that Samaritans are superior to Jews but that the expected recipients of God's revelation (Israel's teachers) rejected or failed to comprehend what unexpected recipients (outsiders, women, Samaritans) embraced. This is the pattern established in the prologue: "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him. But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become children of God" (1:11-12).

5. **The chapter advances the author's redefinition of worship and covenant membership.** Just as the prologue declared that becoming a child of God has nothing to do with blood, flesh, or patriarchal will (1:13), chapter 4 declares that true worship has nothing to do with geographic location. What matters is spirit and truth -- the Spirit's enabling and the revelation of God in Jesus. The well that Jacob dug, the mountain where the Samaritans worshiped, the temple where the Jews worshiped -- all are superseded by the one who is himself the dwelling of God among humans (1:14), the source of living water, and the truth about the Father.

6. **The author continues to track Jesus's signs and their relationship to belief.** The first sign at Cana produced belief among the disciples (2:11). The signs in Jerusalem produced belief that Jesus did not trust (2:23-25). The Samaritans believed without signs. The second sign at Cana produced belief in the official and his household. The author is building a nuanced picture: signs are real and revelatory, but the deepest belief comes through the word -- heard, trusted, and confirmed by experience. The Samaritans who heard Jesus's word and "knew" he was the Savior of the world represent the model the author holds up for his readers, who are themselves encountering Jesus through the testimony of this written document (20:30-31).
