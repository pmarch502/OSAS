# Matthew 22 -- First-Century Reading

## Context Within the Author's Argument

**What came before (Matthew 21):** Jesus arrived in Jerusalem, enacting the prophecy of Zechariah 9:9 by entering on a donkey as a humble king. The crowds shouted "Hosanna to the Son of David" (21:9), drawing on Psalm 118:25-26. Jesus immediately went to the temple and overturned the tables of the money-changers, declaring it should be "a house of prayer" (Isaiah 56:7) but had been made "a den of robbers" (Jeremiah 7:11) -- placing himself in Jeremiah's prophetic role of pronouncing judgment on the temple establishment. He healed the blind and the lame inside the temple, filling the space cleared of commerce with the very people the existing order excluded. The fig tree with leaves but no fruit was cursed and withered -- an enacted parable of the temple system that has all the external apparatus of worship but produces no fruit. The chief priests and elders challenged Jesus's authority ("By what authority are you doing these things?" 21:23), and Jesus countered with a question about John's baptism that they refused to answer because both possible responses were politically dangerous (21:25-27). Jesus then told two parables directed at the leadership: the two sons (21:28-32), where tax collectors and prostitutes enter the kingdom before the chief priests because the first group actually responded to John's message while the leaders only professed obedience; and the tenants (21:33-46), drawn from Isaiah 5's vineyard imagery, where the tenants who killed the owner's servants and his son are destroyed and the vineyard is given to others who will produce its fruit. Jesus declared that "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (21:43). The chief priests and Pharisees understood the parables were about them and sought to arrest Jesus but feared the crowds (21:45-46).

**What comes after (Matthew 23):** Jesus turns from the specific confrontations with Pharisees, Sadducees, and scribes in chapter 22 to a sustained public denunciation of the scribes and Pharisees. He opens by acknowledging their institutional position -- "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat" (23:2) -- and instructs the crowds and disciples to observe what they say but not to imitate what they do, "for they preach, but do not practice" (23:3). This echoes the principle established in the parable of the two sons (21:28-32): speech without corresponding action does not constitute obedience. Jesus then delivers a series of seven "woe" pronouncements (23:13-36) against the scribes and Pharisees, each identifying a specific failure: they shut the kingdom in people's faces, they devour widows' houses, they obsess over tithing minutiae while neglecting "the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness" (23:23), they are like whitewashed tombs -- clean outside, corrupt inside. The denunciation culminates in the charge that they are "sons of those who murdered the prophets" (23:31) and that "upon you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth" (23:35). The chapter closes with the lament over Jerusalem: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing! See, your house is left to you desolate" (23:37-38). The phrase "your house" (ho oikos hymon) echoes the temple as "my house" (ho oikos mou) from 21:13 -- the house that was meant to be God's house of prayer is now "your house," left desolate. Chapter 23 thus completes the indictment that chapters 21-22 have been building.

**How chapter 22 connects them:** Chapter 21 initiated the public confrontation between Jesus and the religious leadership in Jerusalem through prophetic action (the temple disruption, the fig tree cursing) and accusatory parables (the two sons, the wicked tenants). The leadership recognized the parables were about them and wanted to arrest Jesus but feared the crowds. Chapter 22 continues this confrontation on two fronts. First, Jesus tells one more parable -- the wedding feast (22:1-14) -- which extends the vineyard parables by moving from the question of who manages the kingdom to the question of who participates in it and on what terms. Second, the leadership mounts a counter-offensive: the Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Jesus with the question about taxes to Caesar (22:15-22); the Sadducees test him with a puzzle about the resurrection (22:23-33); a Pharisaic lawyer tests him about the greatest commandment (22:34-40). In each case, Jesus answers in a way that silences the questioners and exposes the inadequacy of their understanding. Then Jesus himself takes the offensive with a question of his own about David's son and David's Lord (22:41-46), which no one can answer, and "from that day no one dared to ask him any more questions" (22:46). The chapter thus completes a transition: in chapter 21, the leadership challenged Jesus and Jesus responded with parables that indicted them; in chapter 22, the leadership challenges Jesus with specific test-questions and Jesus defeats each one, then poses a question that silences them entirely. With the question-and-answer phase exhausted, chapter 23 moves to the final stage: direct, unmediated denunciation. The movement from 21 through 22 to 23 is a single sustained arc of confrontation, escalating from parabolic indictment to intellectual combat to prophetic woe.

## The Parable of the Wedding Feast: Invitation, Refusal, and Judgment (vv. 1-14)

"And again Jesus spoke to them in parables (en parabolais), saying, 'The kingdom of heaven may be compared to a king (basilei) who gave a wedding feast (gamous) for his son (to huio autou).'"

The introductory formula "the kingdom of heaven may be compared to" (homoiothe he basileia ton ouranon) has introduced parables throughout the document (13:24, 31, 33, 44, 45, 47; 18:23; 20:1). But the imagery here shifts from landowners and vineyards to a king and a royal wedding. The figure is a king (basileus), not merely a householder (oikodespotes) as in the vineyard parables. The escalation in the parable's central figure reflects the escalation of the argument: the parables in chapter 21 spoke of a man who planted a vineyard; this parable speaks of a king who throws a wedding feast for his son. The stakes are higher, the authority greater, the consequences more severe.

The wedding feast (gamos) for the king's son carries specific resonance. In Israel's prophetic tradition, the relationship between God and his people is frequently described through marital imagery. Hosea 2:16, 19-20: "In that day, declares the LORD, you will call me 'My Husband'... I will betroth you to me forever." Isaiah 54:5: "For your Maker is your husband, the LORD of hosts is his name." Isaiah 62:4-5: "As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you." The wedding feast is the eschatological celebration of the union between God's purposes and his people -- the consummation of the covenant relationship. In this parable, the king gives the feast for his son, introducing a distinction between the king (God) and the son (the one through whom God's purposes are consummated). The author established Jesus as God's son at 3:17 ("This is my beloved Son") and 17:5 ("This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him"). The wedding feast for the king's son is the kingdom-celebration centered on Jesus.

"'And he sent his servants (tous doulous autou) to call those who were invited (tous keklemenous) to the wedding feast, but they would not come (ouk ethelon elthein).'"

The king sends servants (douloi) -- the same term used for the prophets in the vineyard parable (21:34-36). The "those who were invited" (tous keklemenous, perfect passive participle of kaleo, to call) are people who had already been invited. In the customs of the ancient Near East and the Greco-Roman world, a royal invitation was typically extended in two stages: an initial invitation to notify the guests, followed by a second summons when the feast was actually ready. The initial invitation had already gone out; now the servants come with the second call. The invited guests refuse -- "they would not come" (ouk ethelon elthein, literally "they did not will to come"). The refusal is not inability but unwillingness. The verb thelo (to will, to desire) indicates a deliberate choice. This is the same verb used in the vineyard parable when the first son said "I will not" (ou thelo, 21:29). The invited guests, like the second son in the previous parable, refuse to act on their invitation.

"'Again he sent other servants (allous doulous), saying, "Tell those who are invited, See, I have prepared my dinner (to ariston mou hetoimaka); my oxen and my fattened calves are slaughtered, and everything is ready (panta hetoima). Come to the wedding feast (deute eis tous gamous)."'"

The king's patience is remarkable. After the first refusal, he does not punish; he sends more servants with a more detailed and urgent invitation. The preparation is described in generous, even lavish terms -- oxen and fattened calves, everything ready. The word hetoima (ready, prepared) will appear again at 25:10 in the parable of the ten virgins, where "those who were ready went in with him to the marriage feast." Readiness and the wedding feast are linked throughout the author's eschatological teaching. The king's description of his preparations functions as an appeal: everything has been done on the host's side; all that remains is the guests' willingness to come.

"'But they paid no attention (amelesantes) and went away, one to his own farm (eis ton idion agron), another to his business (epi ten emporian autou); while the rest seized his servants, treated them shamefully, and killed them (apokteinan).'"

The verb ameleo (to neglect, to make light of, to disregard) describes the response of those who simply did not care enough to come. The word appears in the LXX at 1 Timothy 4:14 ("do not neglect the gift") and Hebrews 2:3 ("how shall we escape if we neglect such a great salvation"), but in the first-century Jewish world its force is clear: they treated the king's invitation as unimportant. Their alternative activities -- farming and commerce -- are not sinful in themselves; they are the ordinary concerns of daily life. The problem is not that the activities are evil but that they take precedence over the king's summons. The kingdom is refused not only through overt rebellion but through the quiet prioritization of ordinary life over God's invitation.

But some go further: they seize, abuse, and kill the servants. This recapitulates the pattern from the vineyard parable (21:35-36), where the tenants "beat one, killed another, and stoned another." The escalation from indifference to violence mirrors Israel's history with the prophets, from ignoring their message to actively persecuting and killing them (cf. 2 Chronicles 36:15-16: "The LORD, the God of their fathers, sent persistently to them by his messengers... but they kept mocking the messengers of God, despising his words, and scoffing at his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD rose against his people, until there was no remedy").

"'The king was angry (orgistheis), and he sent his troops (ta strateumata autou) and destroyed those murderers and burned their city (ten polin auton enephresen).'"

The king's response is swift and devastating: military destruction of the murderers and the burning of their city. The phrase "burned their city" (ten polin auton enephresen) has been widely recognized as corresponding to the destruction of Jerusalem by Rome in 70 CE, when the city and temple were burned. Whether the author writes before or after 70 CE, the image draws on a well-established pattern in Israel's history: God using foreign armies as instruments of judgment against his own people. In 2 Kings 25:8-9, Nebuzaradan, captain of Nebuchadnezzar's guard, "burned the house of the LORD, and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem." In Isaiah 10:5-6, Assyria is "the rod of my anger" sent against "a godless nation." In Jeremiah 34:2, the LORD says, "I will give this city into the hand of the king of Babylon, and he shall burn it with fire." The king in the parable acts as the God of Israel's history has acted: when those entrusted with his purposes refuse and kill his messengers, he uses the instruments of political-military power to execute judgment. The "troops" (strateumata) are the king's own forces, deployed against those who were his own invited guests. The judgment falls on insiders who became enemies through refusal.

"'Then he said to his servants, "The wedding feast is ready (ho men gamos hetoimos estin), but those who were invited were not worthy (ouk esan axioi). Go therefore to the main roads (tas diexodous ton hodon), and as many as you find, invite to the wedding feast (hosas ean heurete kalesate eis tous gamous)."'"

The feast remains ready. The king's purposes are not thwarted by the refusal of the original guests; the celebration proceeds with different participants. The original invitees "were not worthy" (ouk esan axioi). The word axios (worthy, deserving, fitting) appeared at 3:8, where John the Baptist demanded "fruit worthy (axion) of repentance," and at 10:37-38, where Jesus said, "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy (axios) of me, and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. And whoever does not take his cross and follow me is not worthy of me." Worthiness in the author's usage is not a matter of social status or religious credentials; it is defined by response -- by whether one actually comes when called, produces the fruit that is demanded, and follows when summoned. The original guests' unworthiness consists precisely in their refusal to come.

The servants are sent to "the main roads" (tas diexodous ton hodon -- literally, the exits of the roads, the places where roads lead out, the crossroads or thoroughfares). These are public spaces where anyone might be found -- not the courts of the privileged but the open roads traveled by all sorts of people.

"'And those servants went out into the roads and gathered all whom they found, both bad and good (ponerous te kai agathous). So the wedding hall was filled with guests (anekeimenon).'"

The servants gather "both bad and good" (ponerous te kai agathous). This detail is critical and deliberate. The word poneros (bad, evil, wicked) is a major term in the document: it describes the "evil one" (ho poneros) at 13:19 and 13:38 ("the sons of the evil one"), the "evil eye" at 6:23 and 20:15, and evil people at 5:45 and 12:35. Agathos (good) is its counterpart (cf. 7:11, 17-18; 12:35; 19:17). The gathering of both bad and good at the feast corresponds exactly to the parables of the net (13:47-48, where the net "gathered fish of every kind" and the sorting came later) and the wheat and weeds (13:24-30, 36-43, where both grow together until the harvest). The kingdom in its present phase includes both -- the sorting is not done at the point of invitation but at a later moment. The invitation is indiscriminate; the judgment is subsequent.

The verb anakeimai (to recline at table) describes the posture of guests at a formal banquet. The hall is filled. The king's feast, refused by the original invitees, is now attended by a mixed company from the open roads. The original plan -- a feast for the son -- is fulfilled, though with completely different guests than those first invited.

"'But when the king came in to look at the guests (theasasthai tous anakeimenous), he saw there a man who had no wedding garment (endedymenon endyma gamou). And he said to him, "Friend (hetaire), how did you get in here without a wedding garment?" And he was speechless (ho de ephimosthe).'"

The king inspects the guests. The verb theaomai (to look at, to observe, to inspect) indicates a deliberate examination, not a casual glance. Among the gathered guests, one man lacks an endyma gamou (a wedding garment). The significance of this detail has been debated, but in the context of first-century practice, a royal host providing garments to banquet guests is attested in ancient Near Eastern custom (cf. 2 Kings 10:22, where Jehu commanded, "Bring out vestments for all the worshipers of Baal"). If the king provided garments -- which the universal scope of the invitation ("as many as you find") would seem to require, since people dragged in from the roads would not have their own festive clothing -- then the absence of a wedding garment represents a deliberate refusal to put on what the host provided. The man came to the feast but on his own terms, without accepting what the king offered for proper participation.

The king addresses the man as "Friend" (hetaire). This is the same formal, distancing address the landowner used to the grumbling laborer at 20:13 ("Friend, I am doing you no wrong") and that Jesus will use to Judas at 26:50 ("Friend, do what you have come for"). In each of the three uses in this document, hetaire precedes a moment of exposure or confrontation. It is not hostile, but it is not warm -- it is the address of a superior who is about to call the addressee to account.

The man "was speechless" (ephimosthe -- the verb phimoo, to muzzle, to silence, used in the passive: he was silenced, he was muzzled). This is not the silence of someone who has a defense but chooses not to offer it; it is the silence of someone who has no defense. The man has no explanation for his presence at the feast without the garment. His speechlessness before the king anticipates the silence of those who will have no answer at the final judgment -- the same inability to respond that characterized the Pharisees and Sadducees when Jesus silenced them (22:34, 46).

"'Then the king said to the attendants (tois diakonois), "Bind him hand and foot (desantes autou podas kai cheiras) and cast him into the outer darkness (ekbalete auton eis to skotos to exoteron). In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth (ekei estai ho klauthmos kai ho brygmos ton odonton)."'"

The king commands his attendants (diakonoi -- servants, the same word Jesus used for the one who aspires to greatness at 20:26) to bind the man and cast him into "the outer darkness" (to skotos to exoteron). This phrase appears three times in the document, always in the same formula: 8:12, where Jesus said that "the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness" while many come from east and west to recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; 22:13, here; and 25:30, in the parable of the talents, where the worthless servant is cast into the outer darkness. In each case, the outer darkness is the place of exclusion from the feast -- the place outside the banquet hall where there is no light, no fellowship, no participation in the celebration. The formula "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (ho klauthmos kai ho brygmos ton odonton) appears six times in the document (8:12; 13:42, 50; 22:13; 24:51; 25:30) and functions as the author's signature phrase for the anguish of those who are excluded from the kingdom.

The binding of hands and feet indicates total incapacitation -- the man cannot act, cannot move, cannot help himself. The judgment is comprehensive and irreversible within the parable's frame.

"'For many are called (polloi gar eisin kletoi), but few are chosen (oligoi de eklektoi).'"

This concluding statement is among the most concentrated in the document. The word kletoi (called, invited) comes from kaleo, the same verb used throughout the parable for the king's invitation ("call those who were invited," v. 3; "invite to the wedding feast," v. 9). The word eklektoi (chosen, elect, selected) comes from eklego (to choose, to select). In the LXX, the eklektos terminology is used for Israel as God's chosen people (Isaiah 43:20: "my chosen people," ton laon mou ton eklekton; Isaiah 45:4: "Israel my chosen," Israel ho eklektos mou; Psalm 105:6 LXX: "offspring of Abraham, his servant, children of Jacob, his chosen ones"). The statement does not define a mechanism of predestination; it summarizes what the parable has dramatized. Many were invited -- the original guests received the invitation, and then everyone on the roads received the invitation. But few are among the chosen -- the original guests disqualified themselves by refusing, and even among those who came, one man disqualified himself by refusing to wear the wedding garment. The calling is broad; the eklektoi are those who both respond to the call and accept the terms of participation. The distinction is not between those whom God arbitrarily selects and those he arbitrarily passes over; it is between those who respond appropriately to the invitation and those who do not.

The parable's structure extends the vineyard parables of chapter 21 in a specific direction. The parable of the tenants (21:33-46) addressed the question of who manages the kingdom -- the tenants who killed the prophets and the son are replaced by others who produce fruit. The wedding feast parable addresses the question of who participates in the kingdom, and it answers in two stages. First, those originally invited who refuse are replaced by those gathered from the roads (the kingdom is not limited to those who held the initial invitation). Second, even among those who come, participation requires appropriate response -- the wedding garment. The kingdom is open to "both bad and good," but entrance into the feast is not unconditional. Being called is necessary but not sufficient; being among the eklektoi requires responding to the call on the king's terms.

## The Question about Paying Taxes to Caesar (vv. 15-22)

"Then the Pharisees went and plotted how to entangle him in his words (pos auton pagideusosin en logo)."

The verb pagideuo (to entrap, to ensnare) appears only here in the New Testament. The noun pagis (snare, trap) is used in the LXX for the traps set for animals or for people (Psalm 140:5 LXX: "the arrogant have hidden a trap for me"; Proverbs 7:23: "as a bird rushes into a snare"). The Pharisees' intent is explicit: they are not seeking understanding but designing a verbal trap. The author's narration ensures the reader knows from the outset that the question to follow is not sincere inquiry but a calculated attempt to destroy Jesus with his own words.

"And they sent their disciples to him, along with the Herodians (meta ton Herodianon), saying, 'Teacher (didaskale), we know that you are true (alethes ei) and teach the way of God truthfully (ten hodon tou theou en aletheia didaskeis), and you do not care about anyone's opinion, for you are not swayed by appearances (ou gar blepeis eis prosopon anthropon).'"

The Pharisees send their disciples rather than coming themselves -- possibly to create distance, or to present younger, less recognizable faces. The presence of the Herodians (Herodianoi) is significant. The Herodians were partisans of the Herodian dynasty, politically allied with Rome. The Pharisees and Herodians were ordinarily opposed -- the Pharisees were committed to Torah observance and the Herodians were collaborators with the Roman political order. Their alliance here is a coalition of convenience, united only by shared hostility toward Jesus. This mirrors the coalition noted at Mark 3:6, where Pharisees and Herodians conspired together against Jesus after a Sabbath healing.

The flattering introduction -- "we know that you are true and teach the way of God truthfully" -- is designed to box Jesus in. By publicly acknowledging that he speaks truth without regard for political consequences, they make it harder for him to give an evasive or diplomatic answer. The phrase "you do not look at the face of men" (ou blepeis eis prosopon anthropon) echoes LXX language used for God's own impartiality (Deuteronomy 10:17: "the LORD your God... is not partial and takes no bribe"; 2 Chronicles 19:7: "there is no injustice with the LORD our God, or partiality, or taking bribes"). They are weaponizing his integrity, trying to force a response that will incriminate him regardless of its content.

"'Tell us, then, what you think. Is it lawful (exestin) to pay taxes (kensos) to Caesar, or not?'"

The word kensos (census tax, poll tax) is a Latin loanword (census). This was the tributum capitis, the head tax levied by Rome on every person in the provinces. It was paid directly to the Roman treasury and was distinct from other taxes and customs duties. The tax was deeply resented in Judea. In 6 CE, when Judea became a Roman province and the census for taxation was conducted by Quirinius, a Galilean named Judas led a revolt against the tax, arguing that it constituted an acknowledgment of Roman sovereignty over Israel and was therefore an act of idolatry -- only God was Israel's king (Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.1; Jewish War 2.8.1). The tax remained a flash point in Jewish-Roman relations throughout the first century.

The trap is precisely constructed. If Jesus says it is lawful (exestin -- the same word used in the Sabbath controversies: "is it lawful?" 12:2, 10, 12) to pay the tax, he alienates the crowds who resent Roman occupation and undermines his own prophetic credibility as one who proclaims God's kingdom. If he says it is not lawful, the Herodians are present to report him to the Roman authorities as a seditious agitator -- the charge that will ultimately be brought against him before Pilate (cf. Luke 23:2: "We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding us to give tribute to Caesar"). Either answer appears to destroy him: one politically with the people, the other legally with Rome.

"But Jesus, aware of their malice (ponerian), said, 'Why put me to the test (ti me peirazete), you hypocrites (hypokritai)?'"

Jesus identifies their purpose with two words. Poneria (malice, wickedness, evil intent) is the noun form of poneros, the word that runs through the document for moral evil (cf. 5:37, 39; 6:13; 7:11; 9:4; 12:34, 35, 39, 45; 15:19; 18:32; 20:15). Hypokritai (hypocrites, actors, pretenders) is the term Jesus uses more than any other for the religious leadership -- it will dominate chapter 23, appearing seven times in the woe-pronouncements (23:13, 15, 23, 25, 27, 29). A hypokrites in Greek theatrical usage was an actor, one who wore a mask. Jesus strips the mask: the flattering introduction about truth-telling is itself a performance. They do not want truth; they want a trap.

"'Show me the coin used for the tax (to nomisma tou kensou).' And they brought him a denarius (denarion)."

The coin for the Roman tax was a silver denarius. Jesus does not have one and must ask for one to be produced -- a detail that may itself be significant. The fact that the Pharisees' disciples are carrying a Roman denarius in the temple precincts (the scene is still set in the temple, from 21:23) is ironic: the coin bore the image of the emperor (Tiberius, during Jesus's ministry) and the inscription "Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus" (Ti. Caesar Divi Aug. F. Augustus) along with the title Pontifex Maximus (High Priest). The coin was, in strict Jewish terms, a graven image bearing a blasphemous claim of divinity. Those who challenge Jesus about the propriety of the tax are already carrying the emperor's image and inscription on their persons.

"Then he said to them, 'Whose likeness (eikon) and inscription (epigraphe) is this?' They said, 'Caesar's.' Then he said to them, 'Therefore render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's (apodote oun ta Kaisaros Kaisari), and to God the things that are God's (kai ta tou theou to theo).'"

The word eikon (image, likeness, representation) is the LXX word used in Genesis 1:26-27 for the creation of humanity: "Let us make man in our image (kat' eikona hemeteran), according to our likeness" -- "So God created man in his image (kat' eikona theou), in the image of God he created him." The coin bears Caesar's eikon; the human being bears God's eikon. The question "Whose image is this?" operates on two levels simultaneously. On the surface, it identifies the coin as belonging to Caesar's domain -- it bears his image, his inscription, his claim. The coin is Caesar's; return it to him. But the deeper resonance, which a first-century Jewish hearer steeped in Genesis would perceive, raises the counter-question: if the coin bearing Caesar's image belongs to Caesar, then what bears God's image? The human being. "Render to God the things that are God's" -- that is, yourself. The entire self, created in God's image, belongs to God. The scope of what belongs to Caesar (a coin) and what belongs to God (everything, including the person holding the coin) is radically asymmetric.

The verb apodidomi (to give back, to return, to render) carries the sense of returning what is owed, giving what belongs. It is not a voluntary gift but a payment of obligation. The same verb appeared at 20:8 (paying the laborers their wages) and will appear at 21:41 (tenants who will "give back" the fruits in their seasons). Caesar minted the coin; give it back. God created the person; give yourself back.

The answer escapes the trap entirely. It neither endorses the tax (which would alienate the crowds) nor forbids it (which would incriminate him before Rome). Instead, it reframes the question: the issue is not Caesar versus God, as though they occupy the same plane of authority. Caesar has a limited, derivative claim (the coin that bears his image); God has a total, original claim (the person who bears his image). The response does not resolve the political tension -- it transcends it by asserting a claim so much larger than Caesar's that the tax question becomes secondary.

"When they heard it, they marveled (ethaumasan). And they left him and went away."

The verb thaumazo (to marvel, to be amazed, to wonder) is used throughout the document for astonishment at Jesus's words and works (8:27; 9:33; 15:31; 21:20). Even the hostile questioners are struck by the answer. They have no follow-up, no counter-argument. They simply leave. The trap failed.

## The Sadducees' Question about the Resurrection (vv. 23-33)

"The same day the Sadducees came to him, who say that there is no resurrection (hoi legontes me einai anastasin), and they asked him a question."

The author identifies the Sadducees by their defining doctrinal position: they deny the resurrection (anastasis). This is confirmed by Josephus (Jewish War 2.8.14: "The Sadducees... take away the belief of the immortal duration of the soul, and the punishments and rewards in Hades") and Acts 23:8 ("The Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit"). The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy, closely associated with the temple establishment and the high priestly families. They accepted only the written Torah (the five books of Moses) as authoritative and rejected the oral traditions that the Pharisees valued. Their denial of the resurrection was grounded in the claim that the Torah nowhere teaches it. This is significant for understanding Jesus's response: he will answer them from the Torah itself -- the only scriptural authority they acknowledge.

"'Teacher (didaskale), Moses said, "If a man dies having no children, his brother must marry the widow and raise up offspring for his brother (anastesai sperma to adelpho autou)."'"

The Sadducees cite Deuteronomy 25:5-6, the law of levirate marriage (from Latin levir, brother-in-law). The purpose of the law was to preserve the name and inheritance of a man who died without an heir by having his brother marry the widow and produce children who would legally be counted as the dead man's offspring. The practice is illustrated in the story of Judah and Tamar (Genesis 38) and underlies the book of Ruth (Ruth 4:5-10). The law assumes the importance of continuity within Israel -- the preservation of a man's "name" within the community. The Sadducees invoke this law as the basis for a reductio ad absurdum argument against the resurrection.

"'Now there were seven brothers among us. The first married and died, and having no offspring left his wife to his brother. So too the second, and the third, down to the seventh. After them all, the woman died. In the resurrection (en te anastasei), therefore, of the seven, whose wife will she be? For they all had her.'"

The scenario is constructed to make the resurrection appear logically absurd. If all seven brothers were married to the same woman according to the Torah's own command, and if the resurrection is real, then in the resurrected state the woman would be simultaneously married to seven men -- a result that violates the Torah's marriage laws. The argument assumes that the resurrection is simply a continuation of present life under the same conditions. It is a philosophical puzzle designed not to learn but to embarrass: if Jesus affirms the resurrection, he must explain this impossibility; if he cannot, the resurrection doctrine is discredited.

The number seven may be drawn from Tobit 3:8, 15, where Sarah had been married to seven husbands, each of whom died before consummating the marriage. Whether the Sadducees have this specific story in mind or are simply using seven as a number of completion, the scenario is clearly hypothetical and designed for argumentative effect.

"But Jesus answered them, 'You are wrong (planasthe), because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God (me eidotes tas graphas mede ten dynamin tou theou).'"

Jesus's response opens with a double diagnosis. The verb planao (to lead astray, to deceive, to wander) in the passive means "you are led astray, you are in error, you are deceived." The same verb appears at 24:4-5, 11, 24 for the eschatological deception: "See that no one leads you astray (planese)." The Sadducees are not merely misinformed; they are in a state of fundamental error. The error has two sources: ignorance of the Scriptures (tas graphas -- they do not understand what their own Torah says) and ignorance of the power of God (ten dynamin tou theou -- they have underestimated what God is capable of). The two deficiencies are linked: because they do not understand the Scriptures, they do not grasp the scope of God's power, and because they have limited God's power to what they can imagine, they misread the Scriptures.

"'For in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage (oute gamousin oute gamizontai), but are like angels in heaven (hos aggeloi en to ourano eisin).'"

Jesus dismantles the premise of the Sadducees' argument. The puzzle depends on the assumption that the resurrection reproduces the conditions of present life, including marriage. Jesus denies this assumption: the resurrected state is qualitatively different from the present one. The comparison to angels (aggeloi) is pointed, given that the Sadducees also denied the existence of angels (Acts 23:8). Jesus appeals to a category they reject to describe a reality they reject. The phrase "like angels in heaven" does not mean that resurrected humans become angels but that they share with the angels the characteristic of not being married. Marriage in Israel's Torah served specific purposes: companionship, procreation, preservation of inheritance, continuation of the name. In the resurrected state, these purposes are fulfilled in other ways. The entire framework within which the Sadducees' puzzle operates is a category error -- they are applying the rules of the present age to the age to come.

"'And as for the resurrection of the dead, have you not read what was said to you by God (to rhethen hymin hypo tou theou): "I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (ego eimi ho theos Abraam kai ho theos Isaak kai ho theos Iakob)? He is not God of the dead, but of the living (ouk estin ho theos nekron alla zonton).'"

Having answered the objection, Jesus now makes the positive case for the resurrection, and he does so from the Torah -- the only texts the Sadducees accept as authoritative. The citation is Exodus 3:6, God's self-identification to Moses at the burning bush. The argument turns on the verb tense: God says "I am" (ego eimi), not "I was." At the time God spoke to Moses, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob had been physically dead for centuries. Yet God identifies himself as their God in the present tense. If Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob were simply extinct -- if death were their final state -- then God's self-identification as their God would be meaningless. A God who identifies himself by relationship to persons who no longer exist in any sense is a God of nothing. But God is "not the God of the dead but of the living" -- the patriarchs must therefore be alive in some sense, which entails that death is not the end and that God's covenant relationship with his people persists beyond death. The resurrection is thus grounded not in a speculative philosophical argument but in the character of God's own faithfulness to those with whom he has entered into covenant. If God is Abraham's God, then Abraham must be alive, because God does not have relationships with non-existent beings.

The phrase "what was said to you by God" (to rhethen hymin hypo tou theou) is notable. Jesus does not say "what Moses wrote" or "what Scripture says" but "what was said to you by God." The text is not merely historical record; it is God's living address to the present hearers. The Sadducees, who pride themselves on fidelity to Moses, have failed to hear what God said through Moses.

"And when the crowd heard it, they were astonished at his teaching (exeplessonto epi te didache autou)."

The verb ekplesso (to be struck out of one's senses, to be astonished) is the same word used at 7:28, the conclusion of the Sermon on the Mount: "the crowds were astonished at his teaching." The parallel is significant: just as the Sermon on the Mount demonstrated Jesus's authority as a teacher of Torah, here his handling of a Torah-based challenge from the priestly aristocracy demonstrates the same authority. The crowd's astonishment suggests they recognize that Jesus has done something remarkable -- he has answered the Sadducees from their own accepted texts and silenced them.

## The Question about the Greatest Commandment (vv. 34-40)

"But when the Pharisees heard that he had silenced the Sadducees (ephimosen tous Saddoukaiois), they gathered together (synechthesan epi to auto)."

The verb phimoo (to muzzle, to silence) is the same verb used at v. 12 for the speechless man without a wedding garment. The Sadducees have been muzzled -- silenced, rendered unable to speak. The Pharisees, hearing of this, assemble. The verb synago (to gather together) with the phrase epi to auto (together, in one place) carries echoes of Psalm 2:2 (LXX): "The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together (synechthesan epi to auto), against the LORD and against his Anointed." Whether the author intends this allusion consciously or not, the effect is to present the Pharisees as gathering in concerted opposition.

"And one of them, a lawyer (nomikos), asked him a question to test him (peirazon auton)."

The word nomikos (lawyer, expert in the law) designates a specialist in Torah interpretation. The participle peirazon (testing) uses the same verb (peirazo) used for Satan's testing of Jesus in the wilderness (4:1, 3). The parallel is not accidental: throughout the document, the testing of Jesus by religious authorities mirrors the testing by the adversary. Both seek to discover a point of vulnerability; both fail.

"'Teacher (didaskale), which is the great commandment in the Law (poia estin entole megale en to nomo)?'"

The question about which commandment is "great" (megale) or greatest reflects an actual debate in first-century Judaism. The rabbis counted 613 commandments in the Torah (248 positive, 365 negative), and the question of how to identify a unifying principle or a commandment that encompassed all others was a live discussion. The question is a test, but it is not inherently hostile -- it asks Jesus to demonstrate his competence as a Torah interpreter by identifying the organizing center of the Law.

"And he said to him, 'You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind (agapeseis kyrion ton theon sou en hole te kardia sou kai en hole te psyche sou kai en hole te dianoia sou). This is the great and first commandment (haute estin he megale kai prote entole).'"

Jesus cites Deuteronomy 6:4-5, the Shema -- the foundational confession of Israel's faith, recited twice daily by observant Jews: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might." The Shema was not merely a theological proposition but a performed identity: to recite it was to enact one's membership in Israel and one's submission to Israel's God. Jesus identifies it as "the great and first commandment" -- great in scope (it encompasses the whole person: heart, soul, mind) and first in priority (it is the foundation on which everything else rests).

The author's version specifies "heart" (kardia), "soul" (psyche), and "mind" (dianoia), where the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 6:5 has "heart" (lev), "soul" (nephesh), and "might/strength" (me'od). The substitution of dianoia (mind, understanding, intellectual capacity) for the Hebrew me'od (strength, might, force) may reflect the LXX tradition (some LXX manuscripts read dianoia) and emphasizes that love for God involves the intellect as well as the emotions and the will. The three terms together indicate totality: every faculty, every capacity, every dimension of the person is directed toward God. There is no domain of human existence that falls outside this commandment's scope.

"'And a second is like it (deutera de homoia aute): You shall love your neighbor as yourself (agapeseis ton plesion sou hos seauton).'"

The lawyer asked for one commandment; Jesus gives two, declaring the second "like" (homoia -- similar, resembling, of the same kind as) the first. The citation is Leviticus 19:18: "You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against the sons of your own people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD." In its original context in Leviticus, the commandment is embedded within a series of concrete social obligations -- just wages (19:13), care for the deaf and blind (19:14), impartial justice (19:15), no slander (19:16). Love of neighbor in Leviticus is not an abstraction; it is enacted in specific behaviors of justice and care.

By declaring the second commandment "like" the first, Jesus asserts that the two are inseparable. Love of God that does not produce love of neighbor is incomplete; love of neighbor that is not grounded in love of God lacks its proper foundation. This pairing anticipates the principle that will govern the judgment scene at 25:31-46, where treatment of "the least of these my brothers" is treated as treatment of the Son of Man himself. The vertical (love of God) and the horizontal (love of neighbor) are not parallel tracks but a single reality experienced in two dimensions.

"'On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets (en tautais tais dysin entolais holos ho nomos krematai kai hoi prophetai).'"

The verb krematai (hangs, depends on, is suspended from) creates a vivid image: the entire Torah and all the prophetic writings hang from these two commandments as from a hook or peg. Everything in the Law and the Prophets is an elaboration, specification, or application of these two principles. Remove them, and everything else falls. Jesus is not abolishing or relativizing the other commandments (he explicitly affirmed the Law's permanence at 5:17-19: "I have not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to fulfill them"); he is identifying their structural center. Every commandment in the Torah is an expression of either love for God or love for neighbor or both.

The phrase "the Law and the Prophets" (ho nomos kai hoi prophetai) is a formula the author uses to designate the entire Hebrew Scriptures (cf. 5:17; 7:12; 11:13). At 7:12, Jesus stated, "So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets." The golden rule at 7:12 and the love-commandment pair at 22:37-40 are two formulations of the same reality -- both summarize the entire scriptural tradition in terms of how one relates to God and to others.

## The Question about David's Son: Jesus's Counter-Question (vv. 41-46)

"Now while the Pharisees were gathered together (synegmenon de ton Pharisaion), Jesus asked them a question, saying, 'What do you think about the Christ (ti hymin dokei peri tou Christou)? Whose son is he (tinos huios estin)?' They said to him, 'The son of David (tou Dauid).'"

The initiative shifts. Up to this point in the chapter, Jesus has been answering questions -- the Pharisees about taxes, the Sadducees about resurrection, the lawyer about the greatest commandment. Now Jesus asks the question. The verb synago (to gather) in the passive participle indicates the Pharisees are still assembled from their earlier consultation (v. 34). Jesus addresses them as a group with a question about the identity of the Christ (ho Christos -- the Anointed One, the Messiah).

The question "What do you think about the Christ? Whose son is he?" probes the Pharisees' messianic theology. Their answer -- "the son of David" (tou Dauid) -- is correct as far as it goes. The expectation of a Davidic Messiah was firmly rooted in 2 Samuel 7:12-16, where God promised David, "I will raise up your offspring after you, who shall come from your body, and I will establish his kingdom... I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever." Isaiah 11:1, 10 describes the coming ruler as "a shoot from the stump of Jesse" and "the root of Jesse." Jeremiah 23:5: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch." Ezekiel 34:23-24: "I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David." The Psalms of Solomon (first century BCE) explicitly anticipate a "Son of David" who will purge Jerusalem of Gentiles and rule in righteousness (Psalms of Solomon 17:21). The Pharisees' answer reflects the mainstream of Jewish messianic expectation. The author himself has affirmed it: the genealogy opens with "Jesus Christ, the son of David" (1:1), and the title "Son of David" has been applied to Jesus throughout the document (9:27; 12:23; 15:22; 20:30-31; 21:9, 15).

"He said to them, 'How is it then that David, in the Spirit (en pneumati), calls him Lord (kyrion auton kalei), saying, "The Lord said to my Lord (eipen ho kyrios to kyrio mou), Sit at my right hand (kathou ek dexion mou), until I put your enemies under your feet (heos an tho tous echthrous sou hypokato ton podon sou)"?'"

Jesus cites Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1), which was widely understood in Second Temple Judaism as a messianic text. The psalm is attributed to David. In it, David writes: "The LORD (YHWH, rendered kyrios in the LXX) said to my Lord (adoni, also rendered kyrios in the LXX): 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'" The structure of the citation creates a hierarchy of three figures: YHWH (the one who speaks), "my Lord" (the one addressed), and David (the one who witnesses and reports the conversation). David, the greatest king of Israel, the one from whose line the Messiah is expected, refers to this messianic figure as "my Lord" (kyrios mou).

The phrase "in the Spirit" (en pneumati) indicates that David spoke under prophetic inspiration. The psalm is not merely David's personal reflection; it is a divinely inspired utterance in which David is given to see and describe the status of the coming Messiah. David speaks by the Spirit, and what the Spirit reveals to him is that the Messiah is not merely his descendant but his Lord.

The content of the divine speech -- "Sit at my right hand" (kathou ek dexion mou) -- places the Messiah at the position of supreme honor and authority beside God. This echoes the request of the mother of Zebedee's sons (20:21), who asked for her sons to sit at Jesus's right and left. The seat at God's right hand is not available by human petition; it is given by divine appointment. The subjugation of enemies "under your feet" draws on ancient Near Eastern imagery of a conquering king placing his foot on the neck of a defeated enemy (cf. Joshua 10:24). But the agent of this subjugation is God -- "until I put your enemies" -- not the Messiah himself. The Messiah sits; God acts. The victory is God's, achieved on behalf of and through the Messiah.

"'If then David calls him Lord, how is he his son (pos huios autou estin)?'"

The question does not deny Davidic descent; it complicates it. If the Messiah were merely David's human descendant -- a king from David's bloodline, nothing more -- then David would not call him "Lord." A father does not call his son "Lord" in the patriarchal culture of ancient Israel. The relationship flows the other way: the son honors the father, the descendant honors the ancestor. For David to call the Messiah "my Lord" means that the Messiah holds a status that transcends the ancestor-descendant relationship. He is David's son by lineage but David's Lord by a different kind of authority. The question presses toward a messiahship that exceeds the purely political-national expectations of a restored Davidic kingdom. The "Son of David" title is not wrong, but it is insufficient. The Messiah is something more than a human descendant of David who will sit on David's earthly throne; he is one whom David himself, speaking by the Spirit, recognized as his superior.

This question functions as the theological counterpart to the authority question of 21:23-27. There, the chief priests asked Jesus, "By what authority are you doing these things?" and Jesus refused to answer directly because they would not engage honestly with the evidence. Here, Jesus poses his own authority question: the Messiah's authority does not derive from Davidic descent alone but from a relationship to God that David himself acknowledged. The source of the Messiah's authority is not human lineage but divine appointment -- "The Lord said to my Lord." This reframes the entire question of Jesus's identity: he is not merely a claimant to David's political throne but one whom God has seated at his right hand, to whom David himself gives the title "Lord."

"And no one was able to answer him a word (oudeis edynato apokritheinai auto logon), nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions (oude etolmesen tis ap' ekeines tes hemeras eperrotesai auton ouketi)."

The chapter ends with total silence from the opposition. The verb tolmao (to dare, to have the courage) indicates that the silence is not for lack of questions but for lack of courage. The series of challenges -- taxes, resurrection, the greatest commandment -- has been turned back at every point, and now Jesus's own question has left the Pharisees unable to respond. The intellectual combat is over. The questioners have been exhausted. From this point forward, Jesus will speak without interruption -- the denunciations of chapter 23, the eschatological discourse of chapters 24-25 -- because no one dares to challenge him again. The authority question of 21:23 has been answered, not by declaration but by demonstration: the one who silences every challenger and poses a question none can answer possesses an authority that transcends what any human institution can confer.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **It completes the parabolic indictment of Israel's leadership and extends it to the question of participation in the kingdom.** The wedding feast parable (vv. 1-14) is the third and final parable in the sequence that began at 21:28. The two sons parable addressed response versus profession; the tenants parable addressed the management of the kingdom and the killing of messengers; the wedding feast addresses who ultimately participates in the kingdom celebration. The progression moves from the accusation (the leadership failed to respond), through the judgment (the vineyard is transferred), to the new reality (the feast is filled from the roads with "both bad and good"). Together, the three parables tell a complete story: God invited, the invitees refused and killed the messengers, judgment fell, and new participants were gathered. But participation is not unconditional -- the man without the wedding garment shows that entering the feast requires accepting its terms.

2. **It demonstrates that the distinction between called and chosen is determined by response, not by initial invitation.** The concluding statement of the wedding feast parable -- "many are called, but few are chosen" (v. 14) -- summarizes a principle the author has been developing since the sower parable (13:3-23), where many receive the word but only some bear fruit. The calling is universal and generous; the king invites everyone the servants can find. But being called does not automatically make one eklektos. The original invitees were called and refused. The man without the garment was called, entered, but did not meet the condition of participation. The eklektoi are those who both respond to the invitation and accept the terms -- in this case, the wedding garment. The parable resists both a reading that makes election arbitrary (God calls all, and the response matters) and a reading that makes human effort sufficient (even those who come must be properly clothed by the host's provision).

3. **It shows Jesus defeating every category of opposition through scriptural mastery and theological depth.** The three test-questions represent three distinct sectors of the Jerusalem establishment. The Pharisees and Herodians test him on the political question of Roman taxation (vv. 15-22); the Sadducees test him on the theological question of resurrection (vv. 23-33); a Pharisaic lawyer tests him on the interpretive question of the Law's center (vv. 34-40). Jesus answers each from a different angle: the tax question with a principle that transcends the dilemma; the resurrection question with a Torah text the Sadducees themselves accept; the commandment question with a pairing from Deuteronomy and Leviticus that identifies the Law's structural foundation. In each case, the questioners are silenced. The author presents Jesus as the authoritative interpreter of Israel's own tradition -- the one who understands the Torah, the Prophets, and the Psalms more deeply than those who claim professional expertise in them.

4. **It establishes that what belongs to God is the whole person, not merely a tax payment.** The exchange about Caesar's coin (vv. 15-22) operates on the surface as a political answer, but its deeper logic redefines the question of obligation. The coin bears Caesar's image and belongs to Caesar. The human being bears God's image (Genesis 1:26-27) and belongs to God. "Render to God the things that are God's" is not a parallel obligation alongside "render to Caesar" -- it is a total claim that relativizes every other claim. The passage does not resolve the political question of whether to pay taxes; it subordinates it to the theological question of total allegiance. In the context of the author's argument, where the religious leaders have been exposed as giving God words but not fruit, the demand to render to God what bears his image is a restatement of the fruit-bearing criterion in different terms.

5. **It grounds the resurrection in God's covenant faithfulness rather than in philosophical argument.** Jesus's answer to the Sadducees (vv. 29-32) does not argue for the resurrection from the nature of the soul, from the justice of reward and punishment, or from any philosophical framework. It argues from the character of God as revealed in his own self-identification at the burning bush. God is Abraham's God; therefore Abraham is alive; therefore death does not end God's relationship with his people. The resurrection is a corollary of God's faithfulness. This has implications for the author's larger soteriology: if God's covenant commitments to his people persist beyond death, then the promises of the kingdom (inheriting the earth, entering eternal life, sitting at the feast with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob) are not limited to what can be experienced before death. The God who is the God of the living guarantees the future of those who belong to him.

6. **It redefines the Messiah as one whose authority transcends Davidic lineage.** The question about David's son and David's Lord (vv. 41-46) is the theological climax of the chapter. The Pharisees correctly identify the Messiah as the son of David, but Jesus shows from Psalm 110:1 that David himself called the Messiah "my Lord." The Messiah is not less than David's son -- the author has affirmed the Davidic descent since 1:1 -- but he is more. His authority does not derive from human lineage but from divine appointment: "The Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand." This reframes the authority question that has governed the confrontation since 21:23. The chief priests asked by what authority Jesus acts; the answer, embedded in David's own psalm, is that his authority comes from God's direct appointment to the position at God's right hand. No human institution conferred it; no human challenge can revoke it.

7. **It brings the intellectual confrontation between Jesus and the Jerusalem leadership to its conclusion.** The chapter begins with Jesus telling a parable (vv. 1-14) and ends with the statement that "no one dared to ask him any more questions" (v. 46). Between those two points, every faction of the establishment has taken its best shot and failed. The Pharisees' political trap was escaped. The Sadducees' theological puzzle was dismantled. The lawyer's test was answered with a depth that surpassed the question. And Jesus's own question silenced them all. The narrative effect is to demonstrate that Jesus possesses an authority that cannot be overcome by argument, guile, or institutional power. The only remaining option for the leadership -- since they cannot defeat him intellectually -- is to destroy him physically, which is exactly what the subsequent chapters will narrate. The silence of 22:46 is the hinge between the verbal confrontation of chapters 21-22 and the violent action of chapters 26-27.

8. **It establishes love as the organizing principle of the entire Torah, connecting the vertical obligation to God with the horizontal obligation to neighbor.** The pairing of Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18 (vv. 37-40) identifies the center from which every other commandment derives its meaning. This is not a reduction of the Law but a structural analysis: all 613 commandments "hang" from these two, like branches from a trunk. The declaration that the second is "like" the first makes them inseparable -- love of God without love of neighbor is incomplete, and love of neighbor without love of God is groundless. This pairing will govern the judgment scene at 25:31-46, where service to the hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, and imprisoned is treated as service to the Son of Man himself. The author presents a vision in which the vertical and horizontal dimensions of obligation are not competing priorities but a single reality: to love God with everything is to love neighbor as oneself, and to love neighbor is to render to God what bears his image.
