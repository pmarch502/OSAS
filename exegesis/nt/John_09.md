# John 9 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (John 8):** Chapter 8 continues the Feast of Tabernacles narrative that began in chapter 7. Jesus declares "I am the light of the world; he who follows me will not walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life" (8:12) -- a claim made in the context of the Tabernacles lampstand ceremony, when enormous menorot illuminated the Court of the Women and the city beyond. The Pharisees challenge his self-testimony, and Jesus responds that his testimony is valid because he knows where he came from and where he is going (8:14). The chapter escalates through a series of confrontations: Jesus tells the religious leaders "you will die in your sins" unless they believe "that I am" (8:24); he speaks of his "lifting up" (8:28); he tells those Jews "who had believed in him" (tous pepisteukotas auto) that if they continue in his word, they are truly his disciples and will know the truth that sets free (8:31-32) -- but this group immediately argues with him about Abraham's fatherhood, and the exchange deteriorates until they attempt to stone him (8:59). A fierce dispute about paternity -- Abraham's children, God's children, the devil's children -- produces Jesus's climactic declaration: "Before Abraham was, I am" (ego eimi, 8:58). The authorities take up stones, but Jesus "hid himself and went out of the temple" (8:59). The chapter demonstrates that even among those initially described as having believed, the demand to "continue in his word" (meinete en to logo to emo, 8:31) separates genuine discipleship from nominal or transient adherence. It also intensifies the light-darkness theme: Jesus is the light of the world (8:12), and those who reject him prefer darkness because their deeds are evil (3:19-21, restated in the accusation that they are children of the devil who "was a murderer from the beginning and does not stand in the truth," 8:44).

**What the author argues immediately after (John 10):** The chapter division between 9 and 10 is misleading; chapter 10 begins with no new setting, no new audience, and no transitional phrase. Jesus continues speaking to the same Pharisees addressed in 9:40-41, using the imagery of shepherds and sheep to interpret what has just happened. The one who enters by the door is the shepherd; the one who climbs over the wall is a thief and robber (10:1-2). The shepherd calls his own sheep by name and leads them out, and the sheep follow because they know his voice (10:3-4). The stranger's voice they do not know, and they will not follow a stranger (10:5). This shepherd discourse interprets the events of chapter 9: the man born blind heard Jesus's voice and followed him; the Pharisees acted as strangers and thieves, trying to intimidate the man into rejecting the one who healed him, and when he would not comply, they cast him out. The discourse then develops into two more "I am" declarations -- "I am the door of the sheep" (10:7, 9) and "I am the good shepherd" (10:11, 14). The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (10:11, 15) and has "other sheep that are not of this fold" (10:16). A division (schisma) occurs again among the listeners: "Many of them were saying, 'He has a demon and is insane'" while others said, "These are not the sayings of one demon-possessed. A demon cannot open the eyes of the blind, can he?" (10:20-21). The explicit reference to opening blind eyes confirms that the chapter 10 discourse is a direct continuation of and commentary on the chapter 9 healing.

**How chapter 9 connects the surrounding argument:** Chapter 8 ended with Jesus declaring himself "the light of the world" (8:12) and then being driven out of the temple by men who took up stones. Chapter 9 opens with Jesus, having left the temple (9:1, "as he passed by"), encountering a man blind from birth and performing an act that enacts the claim of 8:12: the light of the world gives light to one who has never seen. The claim that was verbal in 8:12 is now demonstrated in physical reality. The one who said "I am the light of the world" now makes a blind man see.

The chapter also continues the Sabbath controversy that has driven the conflict since chapter 5. In 5:1-18, Jesus healed a paralyzed man at the pool of Bethesda on the Sabbath; in 7:19-24, Jesus defended that healing with a qal va-homer argument from circumcision. Now in 9:14, the author again notes that "it was a Sabbath on the day when Jesus made the clay and opened his eyes." The third Sabbath healing in the document follows the same pattern as the first: Jesus heals, the authorities investigate, the controversy centers not on the healing itself but on the Sabbath violation. But the development is significant. In chapter 5, the healed man did not know who Jesus was (5:13) and, once informed, reported Jesus to the authorities (5:15), after which nothing more is said of his spiritual progress. In chapter 9, the healed man undergoes a progressive transformation from ignorance to worship, moving in the opposite direction from the one expected by the authorities who interrogate him. The two Sabbath healings are deliberately parallel, and the contrast between the two healed men sharpens the chapter's argument about sight, blindness, and response.

The chapter also functions as the dramatic enactment of the judgment theme stated in 3:19-21: "This is the judgment, that the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light, for their deeds were evil." In chapter 9, the light comes literally -- a blind man receives sight -- and the response of the authorities is to reject both the sign and the one who performed it. The man who was blind sees; those who claim to see become blind. Jesus states this explicitly in 9:39: "For judgment I came into this world, so that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may become blind." The reversal of sight and blindness is not metaphorical decoration but the enacted fulfillment of what the author has been arguing since the prologue: "The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it" (1:5).

Finally, the chapter advances the synagogue-expulsion theme that first appeared in 7:13 ("no one was speaking openly of him for fear of the Jews"). In 9:22, the author notes that "the Jews had already agreed that if anyone confessed him to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue" (aposynagogos genetai). This formal threat of excommunication shapes the behavior of the man's parents (9:22-23) and provides the institutional mechanism by which the authorities attempt to prevent confession of Jesus. The term aposynagogos appears only in this document (9:22, 12:42, 16:2) and describes a specific historical reality: exclusion from the communal, social, and religious life that the synagogue represented. The man born blind, who has no fear of this threat, becomes a model of costly confession.

## Verses 1-5: The Disciples' Question, Jesus's Answer, and the Purpose of the Work

"And as he passed by, he saw a man blind from birth. And his disciples asked him, 'Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he would be born blind?' Jesus answered, 'It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him. We must work the works of him who sent me as long as it is day; night is coming when no one can work. While I am in the world, I am the light of the world.'"

**"As he passed by" (kai paragon).** The scene follows directly from 8:59, where Jesus "went out of the temple" after the authorities took up stones. There is no temporal break, no new setting introduced. The participle paragon ("passing by") indicates Jesus in transit, moving through the streets of Jerusalem. The encounter is not sought by the blind man -- he does not call out, does not ask for healing, does not even know Jesus is present. Jesus sees him. The initiative, as throughout this document, belongs to Jesus. In 5:6, Jesus saw the paralytic and knew his condition. In 1:43, Jesus found Philip. In 4:4, Jesus "had to pass through Samaria." The pattern is consistent: the one who brings light does not wait to be summoned by those in darkness.

**"A man blind from birth" (typhlon ek genetes).** The specification "from birth" (ek genetes) is not incidental. It establishes that this blindness is not acquired but congenital -- it has no moment of onset within the man's lived experience. He has never seen. This detail matters for two reasons. First, it eliminates any explanation based on the man's personal conduct: he was blind before he could have committed any sin. Second, it creates a parallel with creation itself: giving sight to one who has never seen is not restoration but origination. It is not healing in the ordinary sense but a new act of creation -- bringing light where there has never been light. The connection to Genesis 1:3 ("Let there be light") and to the prologue's declaration that "in him was life, and the life was the light of men" (1:4) is embedded in the nature of the miracle itself.

**"Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he would be born blind?"** The disciples' question reflects a well-established framework in Jewish thought. The connection between sin and suffering was deeply rooted in Israel's scriptures. Exodus 20:5 declares that God visits "the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me." Deuteronomy 28:15-68 catalogs the afflictions that follow disobedience, and the prophets repeatedly interpreted Israel's national suffering as the consequence of national sin (Jeremiah 5:25, Ezekiel 18:1-4, Isaiah 1:4-9). Psalm 38:3 (LXX 37:4): "There is no soundness in my flesh because of your anger; there is no health in my bones because of my sin." The connection was not merely theoretical; it was the standard interpretive framework.

The phrase "or his parents" acknowledges the Exodus 20:5 principle of generational consequence. But the phrase "this man" raises a different question: how could a person sin before birth so as to be born blind? The question may reflect speculation about prenatal sin -- the possibility that a child could sin in the womb. Genesis 25:22 records that Jacob and Esau "struggled together within her" (Rebecca's womb), and later rabbinic tradition (Genesis Rabbah 63:6, though post-first-century in written form) interpreted this struggling as an indication of the twins' moral tendencies manifesting before birth. Some first-century thinkers may have entertained the notion that sin could occur or be anticipated prenatally. Alternatively, the question may be posed as a logical dilemma: if it is not the parents' sin, then it must be the man's own, even if the mechanics are unclear. The disciples assume one of the two explanations must be correct; Jesus rejects both.

**"It was not that this man sinned, or his parents."** Jesus's response does not deny the general connection between sin and suffering -- he himself drew that connection in 5:14 ("do not sin anymore, so that nothing worse happens to you"). What he denies is that this particular blindness is the consequence of either the man's sin or his parents' sin. The case is specific, not universal. This man's blindness has a different purpose entirely.

**"But that the works of God might be displayed in him" (all' hina phanerothe ta erga tou theou en auto).** The conjunction hina ("so that, in order that") indicates purpose, not cause. The blindness exists so that God's works may be made visible (phanerothe, from phaneroo, "to make manifest, to display, to reveal") in this man. The verb phaneroo is the same word used in 1:31 (John came baptizing "so that he [Jesus] might be manifested to Israel"), 2:11 (Jesus "manifested his glory" at Cana), 3:21 ("his deeds may be manifested as having been worked in God"), and 7:4 (the brothers urged Jesus to "show himself to the world"). The works of God are about to be made visible -- manifested, displayed -- through this man's healing. His blindness is not punishment but occasion.

The statement also carries a broader resonance in Israel's story. God repeatedly used situations of human extremity as occasions for self-revelation. Israel's slavery in Egypt was the occasion for the exodus (Exodus 3:7-8). The Red Sea was the occasion for the demonstration of God's power (Exodus 14:13-14, 31). Elijah's contest on Carmel was the occasion for fire from heaven (1 Kings 18:36-37). The dead son of the Shunammite woman was the occasion for Elisha's life-giving miracle (2 Kings 4:32-37). In each case, the human impossibility became the stage for divine action. The man's lifelong blindness is the same kind of impossibility, and it will become the same kind of stage.

**"We must work the works of him who sent me as long as it is day" (hemas dei ergazesthai ta erga tou pempsantos me heos hemera estin).** The pronoun "we" (hemas) is striking. Some manuscripts read "I" (eme) instead, but "we" has strong textual support and is the more difficult reading. If "we," then Jesus includes the disciples in the work of the one who sent him. The works of God are not Jesus's solitary project; they are a shared labor that the disciples are drawn into. This anticipates 14:12: "He who believes in me, the works that I do, he will do also." The phrase "him who sent me" (tou pempsantos me) is the characteristic Johannine formulation for the Father's commissioning of the Son (4:34, 5:23, 24, 30, 36, 37, 6:38, 39, 44, 7:16, 18, 28, 33, 8:16, 18, 26, 29).

**"As long as it is day; night is coming when no one can work."** The day-night metaphor operates on multiple levels. On the immediate level, it refers to the limited window of Jesus's earthly ministry -- the "little while" he has been speaking about since 7:33. Jesus's presence in the world is the "day" during which the works of God can be performed; his departure (through death, resurrection, and ascension) will bring a kind of "night." On the broader level, the metaphor connects to the prologue's light-darkness framework: the light has come into the world (1:9, 3:19), and while the light is present, its work must be done. The urgency is temporal: the opportunity is not permanent.

**"While I am in the world, I am the light of the world" (hotan en to kosmo o, phos eimi tou kosmou).** This repeats the claim of 8:12 verbatim in compressed form. But now the claim will be enacted. In chapter 8, "I am the light of the world" was a verbal declaration that produced argument and stone-throwing. In chapter 9, it will be demonstrated: the light of the world will bring light to one who has lived in total darkness since birth. The author moves from proclamation to demonstration, from word to sign.

## Verses 6-7: The Healing

"When he had said this, he spat on the ground, and made clay of the spittle, and applied the clay to his eyes, and said to him, 'Go, wash in the pool of Siloam' (which is translated, Sent). So he went away and washed, and came back seeing."

**"He spat on the ground, and made clay of the spittle" (eptyen chamai kai epoiesen pelon ek tou ptysmatos).** The action is deliberate and specific: Jesus spits on the earth, mixes the spittle with the ground to form clay (pelon, "mud, clay"), and applies the clay to the blind man's eyes. The use of clay evokes the creation narrative. In Genesis 2:7, God "formed the man of dust from the ground" (eplasen ho theos ton anthropon choun apo tes ges, LXX). The Hebrew verb yatsar ("to form, fashion") describes a potter's action on clay. The word pelon ("clay, mud") itself appears in the LXX in Job 10:9, where Job addresses God: "Remember that you fashioned me like clay (pelon), and to dust you will return me." Isaiah 64:8 (LXX 64:7): "But now, O LORD, you are our Father; we are the clay (pelos), and you are our potter." Isaiah 45:9: "Woe to him who strives with him who formed him -- a pot among earthen pots! Does the clay (pelos) say to the potter, 'What are you making?'"

Jesus is doing what God did in Genesis: forming something from the dust of the ground. But instead of forming a man, he is forming eyes -- bringing into existence what has never existed. The action is not therapeutic but creative. A first-century reader attentive to the scriptural echoes would recognize the gesture: the one who is applying clay to this man's face is acting as the Creator acted in the beginning.

The detail also carries a Sabbath dimension that will become important in verse 14. The act of kneading -- mixing liquid with dry material to form a paste -- was classified among the thirty-nine categories of work prohibited on the Sabbath (later codified in Mishnah Shabbat 7:2, but reflecting earlier practice). By making clay, Jesus does not merely heal on the Sabbath; he performs an act that fits precisely within the categories of prohibited labor. The violation is not inadvertent but deliberate, just as the command to carry a pallet in 5:8 was deliberate. Jesus does not avoid the Sabbath issue; he forces it.

**"Applied the clay to his eyes" (epechrisen auton ton pelon epi tous ophthalmous).** The verb epichrio ("to smear on, to anoint, to apply") appears only here in the New Testament. In the LXX, the related verb chrio is used for anointing with oil -- the anointing of priests (Exodus 28:41, 29:7), kings (1 Samuel 10:1, 16:13), and prophets (1 Kings 19:16). The Messiah is "the anointed one" (ho christos) -- the one upon whom the anointing has been placed. While the clay application is not a formal anointing, the verb's associations create a subtle resonance: the one who is doing the anointing is the Anointed One himself.

**"Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" (hypage nipsai eis ten kolymbethran tou Siloam).** Jesus sends the blind man to a specific pool. The command involves a journey -- from wherever Jesus encountered him to the pool of Siloam in the southeastern corner of Jerusalem, at the base of the City of David. The healing is not instantaneous upon the application of clay; it requires the man's obedience to a command. He must go, he must wash. The structure parallels the Elisha-Naaman narrative in 2 Kings 5:10-14: Elisha sent Naaman to wash in the Jordan seven times, and Naaman was initially offended by the simplicity and indignity of the command. The healing required obedience to an instruction that seemed arbitrary. Naaman's resistance was overcome, and when he washed, "his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean" (2 Kings 5:14). The man born blind, unlike Naaman, shows no resistance. He goes. He washes.

**"Which is translated, Sent" (ho hermeneuetai apestalmenos).** The author provides an interpretation of the pool's name, as is characteristic throughout this document (1:38, 41, 42; 4:25; 9:7). The Hebrew shiloach (from shalach, "to send") becomes in the author's rendering apestalmenos, "having been sent" -- a perfect passive participle of apostello, the same root that pervades the document for Jesus's relationship to the Father. Jesus is "the one sent" (ho apestalmenos) by the Father. This is the author's most frequently used description of Jesus's identity: the sent one (3:17, 34; 5:36, 38; 6:29, 57; 7:29; 8:42; 10:36; 11:42; 17:3, 8, 18, 21, 23, 25; 20:21). By translating the pool's name as "Sent," the author makes the pool a symbol pointing to Jesus himself. The man is sent to wash in "Sent" -- he is directed to the one whose name the pool bears.

The Pool of Siloam also has specific significance within the Feast of Tabernacles. As described in the analysis of chapter 7, the water libation ceremony involved a priestly procession from the temple down to the Pool of Siloam, where a golden pitcher was filled with water and carried back to be poured on the altar while the crowd sang Isaiah 12:3 ("With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation"). The pool from which the Tabernacles water was drawn -- the water Jesus claimed to fulfill in 7:37-38 -- is now the pool to which the blind man is sent for his sight. The pool that supplied the ceremonial water now supplies the water that washes a blind man's eyes. The Tabernacles connection ties chapters 7, 8, and 9 together: Jesus is the living water (7:37-38), the light of the world (8:12), and the one who sends the blind to wash in the pool whose name means "Sent."

**"So he went away and washed, and came back seeing" (apelthen oun kai enipsato kai elthen blepon).** The sequence is compressed and astonishing in its brevity. Three verbs: he went, he washed, he came back seeing. The author does not describe the moment of sight, the man's reaction, or the experience of seeing for the first time. The narrative is spare to the point of austerity. The verbs carry the weight. The most important is the last: blepon -- "seeing." The present participle indicates his new, ongoing condition. He was blind from birth; he is now seeing. The transformation is total and permanent.

The contrast with the healing in chapter 5 is already visible. The man at the pool of Bethesda was healed instantly by Jesus's word alone, with no intermediary action. The man born blind is healed through a multi-step process: clay applied, a command to go, a specific pool to wash in, and only then sight. The process requires the man's participation and obedience at every step. The theology of this document does not present healing as mechanically uniform; the means vary, but the source -- Jesus -- is constant.

## Verses 8-12: The Neighbors' Inquiry

"Therefore the neighbors, and those who previously saw him as a beggar, were saying, 'Is not this the one who used to sit and beg?' Others were saying, 'This is he,' still others were saying, 'No, but he is like him.' He kept saying, 'I am the one.' So they were saying to him, 'How then were your eyes opened?' He answered, 'The man who is called Jesus made clay, and anointed my eyes, and said to me, "Go to Siloam and wash"; so I went away and washed, and I received sight.' They said to him, 'Where is he?' He said, 'I do not know.'"

**"The neighbors, and those who previously saw him as a beggar."** The man's identity in his community is established: he was known as a beggar, one who "used to sit and beg" (ho kathemenos kai prosaiton). His blindness was public knowledge. The healing cannot be explained away as a misidentification, a trick, or a gradual improvement. The community knew this man and his condition.

**"Is not this the one who used to sit and beg?"** The question introduces the first of several investigations in the chapter. There will be four rounds of inquiry: the neighbors (vv. 8-12), the Pharisees' first investigation (vv. 13-17), the interrogation of the parents (vv. 18-23), and the Pharisees' second interrogation of the man himself (vv. 24-34). The author structures the chapter as a legal proceeding, with successive rounds of testimony that build toward a verdict -- though the verdict the authorities reach is the opposite of what the evidence warrants.

**"Others were saying, 'No, but he is like him.'"** Some of the neighbors cannot reconcile the man they see -- a man who can see -- with the man they knew -- a blind beggar. Their uncertainty is natural: a person who has never used his eyes would look different when those eyes are suddenly alive, focused, responsive. The change in his appearance is evidence of the change in his condition.

**"He kept saying, 'I am the one'" (ekeinos elegen hoti ego eimi).** The pronoun ekeinos ("that one, he himself") is emphatic. The man insists on his own identity: ego eimi, "I am he." The phrase ego eimi, while here simply an assertion of identity ("I am the one"), echoes the divine self-identification that has been running through the document (4:26, 6:20, 8:24, 28, 58). The echo is not intentional on the man's part -- he is simply saying "it's me" -- but the author's ear for this phrase is well established, and the reader hears the resonance.

**"The man who is called Jesus" (ho anthropos ho legomenos Iesous).** When asked how his eyes were opened, the man identifies his healer as "the man who is called Jesus." This is the lowest level of recognition: Jesus is a man with a name. The man does not call him rabbi, prophet, or messiah. He knows only a name and a set of actions. This is the first point in a progression of recognition that will develop through the chapter: "the man called Jesus" (v. 11), "a prophet" (v. 17), "from God" (v. 33), and finally "Lord" with an act of worship (v. 38). The author traces a trajectory from minimal knowledge to full confession, and each stage is forced forward by the pressure of the investigation.

**"Where is he?" "I do not know."** The man does not know where Jesus is. He was sent away to the pool before the healing was complete; he returned seeing but without Jesus present. He has received the benefit of Jesus's action without yet knowing where Jesus is or, in a deeper sense, who Jesus is. This mirrors 5:13, where the man healed at Bethesda "did not know who it was" because Jesus had slipped away. But the two men will take opposite trajectories: the Bethesda man, upon learning who Jesus was, reported him to the authorities (5:15); this man, under increasing pressure from the authorities, will move toward deeper and deeper confession.

## Verses 13-17: The Pharisees' First Investigation

"They brought to the Pharisees the man who was formerly blind. Now it was a Sabbath on the day when Jesus made the clay and opened his eyes. Then the Pharisees also were asking him how he received his sight. And he said to them, 'He applied clay to my eyes, and I washed, and I see.' Therefore some of the Pharisees were saying, 'This man is not from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath.' But others were saying, 'How can a man who is a sinner perform such signs?' And there was a division among them. So they said to the blind man again, 'What do you say about him, since he opened your eyes?' And he said, 'He is a prophet.'"

**"They brought to the Pharisees the man who was formerly blind."** The neighbors take the man to the Pharisees. The verb agousin ("they bring, they lead") may indicate coercion or simply that the community's instinct is to bring unusual events to the religious authorities for adjudication. The Pharisees function as judges and interpreters -- the ones who determine what events mean within the framework of the law.

**"Now it was a Sabbath on the day when Jesus made the clay and opened his eyes."** As in 5:9b, the Sabbath detail is revealed after the healing has been narrated, so that the reader evaluates the healing first and the controversy second. This is the third Sabbath healing in the document (after 5:1-18 and possibly implied in 7:23). The author's specification that Jesus "made the clay" (ton pelon epoiesen) highlights the deliberate nature of the violation: making clay involves kneading, a prohibited Sabbath activity. Jesus could have healed by a word alone, as he did at Bethesda. Instead, he chose a method that involved an act classifiable as work. The Sabbath violation is not incidental but integral to the sign's meaning.

**"He applied clay to my eyes, and I washed, and I see."** The man's testimony is direct and factual. He reports three things: what was done to him (clay applied), what he did (washed), and the result (he sees). The simplicity of the testimony contrasts with the complexity of the interrogation that surrounds it. The man has no theology, no explanation, no defense strategy. He has facts: I was blind; a man put clay on my eyes; I washed; I see.

**"This man is not from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath."** The first group of Pharisees reasons deductively: a man from God would keep the Sabbath; this man does not keep the Sabbath; therefore this man is not from God. The reasoning is formally valid but depends on the premise that no legitimate divine agent would ever act in apparent violation of the Sabbath. Jesus has already challenged this premise in 5:17 ("My Father is working until now, and I am working") and in 7:22-23 (the circumcision argument). The Pharisees operate from the assumption that the Sabbath regulation as they interpret it is the definitive test of a person's relationship to God. Jesus has been arguing that the works themselves -- specifically, the giving of life and wholeness -- are the definitive evidence of the Father's authorization.

**"How can a man who is a sinner perform such signs?"** The second group reasons inductively from the evidence: the sign is real, and signs of this magnitude require divine power. A sinner -- one alienated from God -- could not perform such works. The reasoning echoes Nicodemus's initial approach: "Rabbi, we know that you have come from God as a teacher; for no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him" (3:2). The same logic that drew Nicodemus to Jesus at night now creates dissent within the Pharisaic council.

**"And there was a division among them" (kai schisma en en autois).** The word schisma ("division, split") appeared in 7:43 for the division among the crowd and will appear in 10:19 for the division caused by the shepherd discourse. Jesus produces division wherever the light encounters the darkness. The division is not between believers and random opponents but within the religious establishment itself. Some Pharisees can see the evidence; others cannot. The light is separating those who will acknowledge what they see from those who will not.

**"What do you say about him, since he opened your eyes?"** Unable to resolve the division among themselves, the Pharisees turn back to the man. The question is remarkable: they ask the formerly blind beggar for his theological assessment. The one with the least formal education is asked for his opinion because the ones with the most formal education cannot agree. The irony echoes 7:15, where the authorities marveled at Jesus's learning "having never been educated," and 7:49, where the Pharisees dismissed the believing crowd as "accursed" because they "do not know the Law." Here, the unlearned man is consulted because the learned cannot resolve their dispute.

**"He is a prophet" (prophetes estin).** The man's second identification of Jesus advances from "the man called Jesus" (v. 11) to "a prophet." The step is significant. A prophet in Israel's understanding was one through whom God spoke and acted -- one authorized by God, empowered by God's Spirit, doing God's work. The identification of Jesus as a prophet connects to the same conclusion the crowd reached after the feeding of the multitude ("This is truly the Prophet who is to come into the world," 6:14) and to the declaration of some at the Feast of Tabernacles ("This certainly is the Prophet," 7:40). The man has moved from knowing only a name to making a theological judgment: this man acts with divine authorization.

## Verses 18-23: The Interrogation of the Parents

"The Jews then did not believe it about him, that he had been blind and had received sight, until they called the parents of the very one who had received his sight, and questioned them, saying, 'Is this your son, who you say was born blind? Then how does he now see?' His parents answered them and said, 'We know that this is our son, and that he was born blind; but how he now sees, we do not know; or who opened his eyes, we do not know. Ask him; he is of age, he will speak for himself.' His parents said these things because they feared the Jews; for the Jews had already agreed that if anyone confessed him to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue. For this reason his parents said, 'He is of age; ask him.'"

**"The Jews then did not believe it about him."** The term "the Jews" (hoi Ioudaioi) here, as throughout this document, refers to the religious authorities -- the leadership establishment in Jerusalem. Their initial response to the healing is not investigation but disbelief: they do not believe that the man was actually blind and actually received sight. Their strategy is to disprove the miracle rather than to explain it. If the man was not really blind from birth, the entire controversy evaporates. The shift from "some of the Pharisees" (v. 16) to "the Jews" (v. 18) signals that the investigation has moved from an informal Pharisaic inquiry to an official proceeding.

**"They called the parents."** The parents are summoned as witnesses to establish the foundational fact: was this man born blind? Two questions are put to them: (1) Is this your son, whom you say was born blind? (2) How does he now see? The first question challenges the identity and the condition; the second demands an explanation of the change.

**"We know that this is our son, and that he was born blind."** The parents confirm both facts under questioning: identity (this is our son) and condition (he was born blind). Their testimony eliminates any possibility that the man is an imposter or that his blindness was acquired rather than congenital. The facts are established by witnesses who have no motive to fabricate.

**"But how he now sees, we do not know; or who opened his eyes, we do not know."** The parents' refusal to explain the healing is not based on ignorance -- their son could have told them what happened -- but on fear, as the author immediately explains. They deflect the question to their son: "He is of age" (helikian echei, literally "he has age," meaning he is a legal adult capable of giving testimony). The deflection is self-protective.

**"His parents said these things because they feared the Jews."** The author provides an explicit motive for the parents' evasiveness: fear. The verb ephobountho ("they were fearing," imperfect tense) indicates an ongoing, established fear, not a momentary fright. The parents are afraid of the religious authorities.

**"For the Jews had already agreed that if anyone confessed him to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue."** This is one of the most significant statements in the chapter. The author reveals that a formal decision has been made: anyone who confesses (homologese, from homologeo, "to confess, to acknowledge publicly, to say the same thing") Jesus as the Christ will be expelled from the synagogue. The term aposynagogos ("put out of the synagogue, excommunicated") appears only in this document (9:22, 12:42, 16:2). Its severity in a first-century Jewish context cannot be overstated. The synagogue was not merely a place of worship; it was the center of communal life -- economic, social, educational, and religious. Expulsion from the synagogue meant exclusion from the community, loss of social standing, disruption of commercial relationships, and effective banishment from Jewish life. For a family whose livelihood depended on community participation, the threat was existential.

The word homologese ("should confess") is the aorist subjunctive of homologeo, a word the author uses with precision. In 1:20, John the Baptist "confessed and did not deny, but confessed, 'I am not the Christ.'" In 12:42, "many even of the rulers believed in him, but because of the Pharisees they were not confessing him, so that they would not be put out of the synagogue." Confession (homologia) is the public, vocal acknowledgment of what one believes to be true. The threat is specifically directed against public confession, not private belief. The authorities cannot control what people think, but they can punish what people say. The result is a system that separates belief from confession -- that allows people to believe privately while forbidding them to acknowledge publicly. The parents embody this separation: they may believe or at least suspect, but they will not confess.

**"For this reason his parents said, 'He is of age; ask him.'"** The author repeats the parents' deflection and states the reason a second time, ensuring the reader understands: the parents' caution is not about not knowing but about not risking. Their testimony confirms the miracle (he was born blind; he now sees) but refuses to interpret it (who did it; what it means). They will testify to facts but not to theology, because theology -- specifically, confession of Jesus as Christ -- carries a cost they are unwilling to pay.

The parents stand in contrast to their son, who will shortly demonstrate the opposite posture: he will interpret the healing, identify its theological significance, and accept the consequences. The chapter sets up a choice between the parents' fear-driven silence and the son's courage-driven speech.

## Verses 24-34: The Second Interrogation and the Man's Expulsion

"So a second time they called the man who had been blind, and said to him, 'Give glory to God; we know that this man is a sinner.' He then answered, 'Whether he is a sinner, I do not know; one thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see.' So they said to him, 'What did he do to you? How did he open your eyes?' He answered them, 'I told you already and you did not listen; why do you want to hear it again? You do not want to become his disciples too, do you?' They reviled him and said, 'You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses. We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he is from.' The man answered and said to them, 'Well, this is an amazing thing, that you do not know where he is from, and yet he opened my eyes. We know that God does not hear sinners; but if anyone is God-fearing and does his will, he hears him. Since the beginning of the world it has never been heard that anyone opened the eyes of one who was born blind. If this man were not from God, he could do nothing.' They answered him, 'You were born entirely in sins, and are you teaching us?' And they put him out."

**"Give glory to God" (dos doxan to theo).** This phrase is not an invitation to praise. It is a solemn adjuration, a formula used to place someone under oath to tell the truth. The identical phrase appears in Joshua 7:19 (LXX), where Joshua says to Achan before his confession: "My son, give glory to the LORD, the God of Israel, and give confession to him; and tell me what you have done. Do not hide it from me." The phrase functions as: "Swear before God to tell the truth." The Pharisees are placing the man under a solemn obligation.

**"We know that this man is a sinner" (hemeis oidamen hoti houtos ho anthropos hamartolos estin).** The authorities state their conclusion as established fact. The pronoun hemeis ("we") is emphatic -- we, the authorized interpreters, we who know the Law, we who have the credentials to determine such things. The verdict is pre-established: Jesus is a sinner because he violated the Sabbath. The investigation has been conducted backward -- the conclusion was determined first, and the evidence is being forced into conformity with it. The authorities are not investigating; they are building a case.

**"Whether he is a sinner, I do not know; one thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see" (hen oida hoti typhlos on arti blepo).** The man's response is one of the most rhetorically effective statements in the document. He refuses to engage on the authorities' terrain -- the question of Jesus's sinfulness -- and plants himself on the ground of his own experience: I was blind. Now I see. This is not a theological argument but an empirical fact that no authority can overturn. The man has separated what he does not know (whether Jesus is a sinner by the Pharisees' criteria) from what he does know (the incontestable fact of his own transformation). The contrast between the authorities' confident hemeis oidamen ("we know") and the man's simple hen oida ("one thing I know") exposes the difference between institutional certainty and personal knowledge.

**"I told you already and you did not listen; why do you want to hear it again?"** The man's patience is wearing thin. He has answered the question about how his eyes were opened (v. 15), and the authorities are asking again -- not because they want information but because they want a different answer. The man perceives this: "you did not listen" (ouk ekousate) implies not deafness but refusal.

**"You do not want to become his disciples too, do you?" (me kai hymeis thelete autou mathetai genesthai).** The question, introduced by me (expecting a negative answer), is sarcastic or pointed. The word "too" (kai) is telling -- it implies that the man himself is already, at least functionally, a disciple. Whether he intends this or it slips out under pressure, the effect is the same: he has positioned himself on Jesus's side against the authorities. This is remarkable for a man who, twenty verses earlier, knew nothing about Jesus beyond his name.

**"You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses."** The Pharisees' response draws a line: Jesus's disciples stand on one side; Moses's disciples stand on the other. The pronoun sy ("you") is contemptuous -- you, a blind beggar, are his follower; we, the trained interpreters, follow Moses. The claim to be "disciples of Moses" is not merely sectarian identity; it is an appeal to the foundational authority of the Torah. Moses is the lawgiver, the one through whom God spoke at Sinai, the one whose books are the basis of Israel's entire religious system.

But the author has already undermined this claim. In 5:45-47, Jesus told these same authorities: "Do not think that I will accuse you before the Father; the one who accuses you is Moses, in whom you have set your hope. For if you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me. But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe my words?" The claim to follow Moses while rejecting the one Moses wrote about is, by Jesus's own argument, a contradiction. The Pharisees appeal to Moses as the authority that discredits Jesus; Jesus has already argued that Moses, properly understood, points to him.

**"We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he is from."** The contrast is between the certainty of Moses's divine authorization and the uncertainty of Jesus's origin. "We know" (oidamen) that God spoke to Moses -- this is the bedrock of Jewish faith, grounded in the Sinai revelation (Exodus 19-20, 33:11). "We do not know where he is from" (touton de ouk oidamen pothen estin) -- this is the same objection raised in 7:27, where the Jerusalemites said "we know where this man is from" and used that knowledge to disqualify him. But now the authorities say they do not know where he is from. The irony is that their not-knowing is closer to the truth than the Jerusalemites' knowing was: Jesus's origin is indeed unknown to them, because his true origin is the Father, and they do not know the Father (7:28, 8:19, 55).

**"Well, this is an amazing thing, that you do not know where he is from, and yet he opened my eyes."** The man's response is the theological center of the chapter. He finds it astonishing -- thaumaston, "amazing, marvelous, worthy of wonder" -- that the religious authorities, the experts in discerning God's activity, cannot identify the origin of someone who performed an unprecedented miracle. The word thaumaston echoes Psalm 118:23 (LXX 117:23): "This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous (thaumaste) in our eyes." The thing that should produce wonder and recognition in the experts produces only confusion and hostility.

**"We know that God does not hear sinners; but if anyone is God-fearing and does his will, he hears him."** The man now constructs his own theological argument, and it is remarkably competent. He appeals to a principle the Pharisees themselves would accept: God does not answer the prayers or empower the works of sinners; God hears those who fear him and do his will. The principle is rooted in scripture. Psalm 66:18 (LXX 65:18): "If I had regarded iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened." Proverbs 15:29: "The LORD is far from the wicked, but he hears the prayer of the righteous." Isaiah 1:15: "Even though you multiply prayers, I will not listen. Your hands are full of blood." Job 27:9: "Will God hear his cry when distress comes upon him?" Job 35:13: "Surely God will not listen to an empty cry, nor will the Almighty regard it." The man's argument is constructed from principles embedded in Israel's wisdom and prophetic tradition: God hears the righteous, not sinners. If Jesus were a sinner, God would not have enabled this miracle. Since God manifestly did enable it, Jesus is not a sinner. The argument is a qal va-homer that the Pharisees' own principles require them to accept.

**"Since the beginning of the world it has never been heard that anyone opened the eyes of one who was born blind" (ek tou aionos ouk ekousthe hoti enoixen tis ophthalmous typhlou gegennemenou).** The man escalates his argument by identifying the unprecedented nature of the sign. Opening the eyes of one born blind has no precedent in Israel's history. Elijah raised the dead (1 Kings 17:17-24); Elisha healed leprosy (2 Kings 5:1-14); Moses performed signs that affected nature (Exodus 7-12). But none of Israel's prophets gave sight to a person born without it. The absence of precedent raises the theological stakes: this is not merely a remarkable healing but an act without parallel in the entire scriptural record. The only category adequate to explain it is the creative work of God himself -- the God who said "Let there be light" is the only one who can bring light where there has never been light.

The connection to Isaiah's messianic promises is also in view. Isaiah 29:18: "On that day the deaf shall hear the words of a book, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see." Isaiah 35:5: "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped." Isaiah 42:7: "To open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon, from the prison those who sit in darkness." These are promises attached to God's end-time deliverance -- the coming age of salvation. The opening of blind eyes was a marker of the messianic age. The man's argument, though he may not fully grasp its implications, is pointing toward a messianic conclusion: the one who does what only God promised to do in the age to come must be from God.

**"If this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (ei me en houtos para theou, ouk edynato poiein ouden).** The man's conclusion is stated with the force of logical necessity: if not from God, then incapable of doing anything. The phrase para theou ("from God") is the same language Jesus used of himself: "I am from him" (par' autou eimi, 7:29). The man has arrived, through reasoning from evidence, at a conclusion that echoes Jesus's own self-identification.

**"You were born entirely in sins, and are you teaching us?"** The Pharisees' response abandons argument for insult. They return to the framework Jesus explicitly rejected in verse 3: the man's blindness was caused by sin. "You were born entirely in sins" (en hamartiais sy egenneths holos) reasserts the very assumption Jesus denied. The word holos ("entirely, completely") intensifies the contempt: you are sin from head to foot, from birth to the present. The question "are you teaching us?" (kai sy didaskeis hemas) is an outraged assertion of hierarchy: you, the blind beggar born in sin, presume to instruct us, the authorized teachers of Israel?

The irony, which the reader perceives and the Pharisees do not, is that the man born blind is in fact seeing more clearly than the teachers of Israel. The one they dismiss as unqualified to speak is the one whose argument they cannot answer. They respond to his reasoning not with counter-reasoning but with expulsion.

**"And they put him out" (kai exebalon auton exo).** The verb ekballo ("to cast out, throw out, expel") is strong. It is the same verb used in 6:37, where Jesus promised "the one who comes to me I will certainly not cast out (ou me ekbalo exo)." The Pharisees do what Jesus will not do: they cast out the one who confesses. The expulsion fulfills the threat of 9:22 -- the man is made aposynagogos, expelled from the synagogue community. He has paid the cost that his parents were unwilling to pay. His confession has resulted in his exclusion from the community of Israel as defined by its current leadership.

The expelled man is now outside -- outside the synagogue, outside the community, outside the social and religious structure that has defined Jewish life. This is the condition of the sheep whom the shepherd will find. The thief and robber of 10:1 climb over the wall to control the sheep; the shepherd calls his own by name and leads them out (10:3). The man has been put out by the thieves; he is about to be found by the shepherd.

## Verses 35-38: Jesus Finds the Man

"Jesus heard that they had put him out, and finding him, he said, 'Do you believe in the Son of Man?' He answered, 'Who is he, Lord, that I may believe in him?' Jesus said to him, 'You have both seen him, and he is the one who is talking with you.' And he said, 'Lord, I believe.' And he worshiped him."

**"Jesus heard that they had put him out, and finding him" (ekousen Iesous hoti exebalon auton exo, kai heuron auton).** Jesus seeks out the expelled man. The verb heuron ("finding, having found") indicates that Jesus looked for him. The pattern is the same as 5:14 ("Jesus found him in the temple") but with opposite dynamics. In chapter 5, Jesus found the healed man and warned him; the man then reported Jesus to the authorities. Here, Jesus finds the expelled man and offers him something far greater than physical sight: the opportunity to believe.

The narrative structure is deliberately chiastic with the opening of the chapter. In verse 1, Jesus "saw" the blind man and initiated the healing. In verse 35, Jesus "found" the healed man and initiates the encounter of faith. Both times, Jesus takes the initiative. The man did not seek Jesus for healing; the man does not seek Jesus for faith. Jesus comes to him both times.

**"Do you believe in the Son of Man?" (sy pisteueis eis ton huion tou anthropou).** The question is personal and direct. The pronoun sy ("you") is emphatic: you yourself, having gone through what you have just endured -- do you believe? The title "Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthropou) connects to Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" comes to the Ancient of Days and receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom. It is the title Jesus has used throughout this document for himself (1:51, 3:13-14, 5:27, 6:27, 53, 62, 8:28). The question is whether the man will take the final step -- beyond "a man called Jesus," beyond "a prophet," beyond "from God" -- to believing in the figure who has cosmic authority.

**"Who is he, Lord, that I may believe in him?"** The man's response reveals both his willingness and his ignorance. He does not know who the Son of Man is -- he has never seen Jesus, since Jesus sent him to the pool before his eyes were opened, and he has not seen Jesus since. The word kyrie ("Lord, sir") could be either respectful address or something more; at this stage, the man is using it as a form of respect toward the stranger who is questioning him. But the hina clause -- "that I may believe in him" (hina pisteuso eis auton) -- reveals his disposition: he is ready to believe. He asks not "why should I believe?" but "who is he?" The willingness is established; only the object needs to be identified.

**"You have both seen him, and he is the one who is talking with you."** Jesus's self-identification echoes the pattern of 4:26, where Jesus told the Samaritan woman, "I who speak to you am he" (ego eimi, ho lalon soi). In both cases, the revelation comes to someone who has been on a journey of progressive understanding: the Samaritan woman moved from "a Jew" to "Sir" to "a prophet" to "could this be the Christ" before Jesus declared himself; the blind man has moved from "the man called Jesus" to "a prophet" to "from God" before Jesus declares himself.

The phrase "you have seen him" (kai heorakas auton) carries extraordinary weight in context. The man has "seen" -- and this is the first person in his entire life that he has seen with the eyes Jesus gave him. The verb horao ("to see") in the perfect tense (heorakas) indicates completed action with ongoing result: you have seen him and are seeing him now. The eyes that Jesus created are now looking at their creator.

**"Lord, I believe" (pisteuo, kyrie).** The man's confession is brief and absolute. The word kyrie now carries its full weight: not merely "sir" but "Lord" -- the same address used by Peter in 6:68 ("Lord, to whom shall we go?") and Thomas in 20:28 ("My Lord and my God!"). The verb pisteuo ("I believe") stands without qualification. There is no "I believe, but..." or "I believe if..." The confession is whole.

**"And he worshiped him" (kai prosekynesin auto).** The verb proskyneo ("to worship, to bow down before") is the most significant action in the chapter. In Israel's scriptures, proskyneo is directed to God alone. Exodus 20:5 (LXX): "You shall not worship (proskynesis) them nor serve them." Deuteronomy 6:13: "You shall fear the LORD your God and worship (proskynesis) him." When Cornelius falls at Peter's feet in Acts 10:25-26, Peter says, "Stand up; I too am just a man." When the seer in Revelation 19:10 falls at the angel's feet to worship, the angel says, "Do not do that; I am a fellow servant of yours." But when the man born blind worships Jesus, there is no correction, no rebuke, no redirection. Jesus accepts the worship. In the theology of this document, this acceptance is consistent: the Word who was God (1:1), the only-begotten who is in the bosom of the Father (1:18), the one who claims "Before Abraham was, I am" (8:58), properly receives worship.

The man's trajectory is now complete. He moved from blindness to sight, from "the man called Jesus" to "Lord," from ignorance to worship. Every step of his progression was forced forward by opposition: the neighbors' inquiry prompted him to identify Jesus; the Pharisees' investigation prompted him to call Jesus a prophet; the second interrogation prompted him to argue that Jesus is from God; the expulsion prompted nothing -- but Jesus came to him, and the encounter prompted worship. The man grew not in spite of the pressure but through it. Each confrontation with the authorities drove him deeper into understanding.

## Verses 39-41: Judgment, Sight, and Blindness

"And Jesus said, 'For judgment I came into this world, so that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may become blind.' Those of the Pharisees who were with him heard these things, and said to him, 'We are not blind too, are we?' Jesus said to them, 'If you were blind, you would have no sin; but since you say, "We see," your sin remains.'"

**"For judgment I came into this world" (eis krima ego eis ton kosmon touton elthon).** The word krima ("judgment") appears here rather than krisis (which appears in 3:19, 5:22, 24, 27, 29, 30). The two words are related but not identical: krisis is the process of judging; krima is the result, the verdict, the enacted judgment. Jesus's coming into the world enacts a judgment -- not a future forensic proceeding but a present sorting that occurs through the encounter with him.

This statement must be reconciled with 3:17: "God did not send the Son into the world to judge (krine) the world, but that the world might be saved through him." And with 8:15: "I judge (krino) no one." And with 12:47: "I did not come to judge (krino) the world but to save the world." The reconciliation lies in the author's understanding of how judgment works: Jesus does not come with the explicit purpose of condemning, but his presence creates a crisis that separates people by their response. The judgment is not imposed from outside; it is produced by the encounter. The light does not intend to expose the darkness -- it intends to illuminate -- but its illumination necessarily exposes what the darkness conceals. The judgment in 9:39 is this kind of separating effect: the light reveals who will receive it and who will not. The same principle was stated in 3:19: "This is the judgment (krisis), that the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light."

**"So that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may become blind."** The double purpose clause (hina ... kai hina ...) describes a reversal. Those who do not see (hoi me blepontes) will receive sight. Those who see (hoi blepontes) will become blind. The reversal echoes the pattern of Israel's prophetic tradition. Isaiah 6:9-10 (LXX): "Go, and say to this people: 'You will keep on hearing, but will not understand; you will keep on seeing, but will not perceive.' Make the heart of this people dull, and their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed." This passage is the most frequently quoted Old Testament text in the New Testament documents (Mark 4:12, Matthew 13:14-15, Luke 8:10, Acts 28:26-27, Romans 11:8). Its function is to explain why the revelation of God produces blindness in those who should be most prepared to receive it: the teachers of Israel, the custodians of the scriptures, the ones who claim to see.

The chapter has just enacted this reversal. The man born blind -- the one who literally could not see -- has received both physical and spiritual sight. The Pharisees -- the ones who claim to see, who claim to know the Law, who claim to discern God's activity -- have become blind to what is in front of them. The man who began the chapter in total physical darkness ends it in worship. The authorities who began the chapter confident of their theological vision end it unable to recognize what God has done.

The reversal also echoes Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2:4-8 (LXX): "The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble gird on strength... The LORD makes poor and makes rich; he brings low, he also exalts. He raises the poor from the dust." And Mary's song in Luke 1:51-53 (though outside this document): "He has scattered those who were proud in the thoughts of their heart. He has brought down rulers from their thrones, and has exalted those who were humble." The pattern of divine reversal -- the humbled are exalted, the exalted are brought low -- is a consistent theme in Israel's scriptures and is now being demonstrated in the contrast between the blind beggar and the Pharisees.

**"We are not blind too, are we?" (me kai hemeis typhloi esmen).** The question, introduced by me, expects a negative answer: "Surely we are not blind?" The Pharisees perceive that Jesus's statement about the seeing becoming blind might apply to them, and they push back. The emphatic hemeis ("we") echoes their earlier assertions: "we know that this man is a sinner" (v. 24), "we are disciples of Moses" (v. 28), "we know that God has spoken to Moses" (v. 29). Each use of "we" has been a claim to authoritative knowledge. Now Jesus is suggesting that their knowledge is blindness.

**"If you were blind, you would have no sin; but since you say, 'We see,' your sin remains."** Jesus's response is the chapter's final statement and its theological climax. It establishes a paradox with two parts:

First: if you were blind -- if you genuinely did not know, if you were in the condition of innocent ignorance -- you would have no sin (hamartian ouk eichete). Ignorance mitigates guilt. The man born blind began in ignorance and progressed toward knowledge. His movement was from not-seeing to seeing, and each step brought him closer to the truth. The Pharisees do not have the excuse of ignorance.

Second: since you say "we see" (hoti legete hoti blepomen) -- since you claim to possess knowledge, since you present yourselves as the ones who can see clearly -- your sin remains (he hamartia hymon menei). The verb menei ("remains, abides") is from meno, the same verb that runs throughout this document for the abiding that characterizes the life of the believer (1:33, 38-39; 3:36; 6:56; 8:31; 15:4-10). But here meno describes something that abides to the detriment of those in whom it remains: their sin. The claim to see, when coupled with the refusal to acknowledge what is visible, transforms ignorance into culpability. The Pharisees are not blind in the sense of being unable to see; they are blind in the sense of refusing to see while claiming that they do see. Their sin is not ignorance but the pretense of knowledge that shields them from the truth.

The phrase "your sin remains" carries the weight of a verdict. It is the counterpart to the promise of 8:31-32, where Jesus told those who had believed: "If you continue in my word, you are truly my disciples; and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free." The opposite is also true: if you refuse the word while claiming to know the truth, the sin that enslaves you remains. The Pharisees' condition is not a temporary misunderstanding that will naturally resolve; it is a settled posture -- they say "we see" -- and as long as that posture is maintained, their sin is not removed but remains.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. **The chapter is the dramatic enactment of "I am the light of the world" (8:12).** The claim that was verbal in chapter 8 is demonstrated in chapter 9. The light of the world encounters a man who has never seen light and gives him sight. The sign does not merely illustrate the claim; it enacts it. The light enters the darkness -- the lifelong, congenital, absolute darkness of a man born blind -- and the darkness gives way. But the sign also enacts the other half of the light's effect: it exposes the darkness in those who claim to see. The Pharisees, confronted with an undeniable work of God, refuse to acknowledge it because it contradicts their theological framework. The light reveals both the man's capacity for sight and the authorities' incapacity for recognition. The same act that opens blind eyes closes seeing eyes.

2. **The chapter structures itself as a legal proceeding with four rounds of testimony, and the evidence progressively overwhelms the verdict.** The neighbors establish the man's identity and condition (vv. 8-12). The Pharisees establish the Sabbath violation and produce a division among themselves (vv. 13-17). The parents confirm the congenital blindness and the reality of the healing (vv. 18-23). The man himself delivers an argument the authorities cannot refute (vv. 24-34). At each stage, the evidence for the divine origin of the healing grows stronger. At each stage, the authorities' resistance becomes more desperate. By the end, they have abandoned argument for insult ("you were born entirely in sins") and abandoned persuasion for coercion ("they put him out"). The legal form of the chapter serves the author's theological point: the evidence for Jesus is not lacking; what is lacking is the willingness to follow the evidence where it leads.

3. **The man born blind undergoes a progressive transformation that is the opposite of the authorities' progressive hardening.** The man moves from "the man called Jesus" (v. 11) to "a prophet" (v. 17) to "from God" (v. 33) to "Lord, I believe" with worship (v. 38). At each stage, his understanding of Jesus deepens. The Pharisees move in the opposite direction: from a division among themselves (v. 16) to a unified verdict that Jesus is a sinner (v. 24) to a contemptuous dismissal of both the evidence and the witness (v. 34). The two trajectories cross: the man begins in darkness and ends in worship; the authorities begin in confidence and end in blindness. The chapter dramatizes the principle of 3:20-21: "Everyone who does evil hates the light and does not come to the light, so that his deeds will not be exposed. But he who practices the truth comes to the light, so that his deeds may be manifested as having been worked in God."

4. **The chapter deliberately parallels and contrasts with the healing of chapter 5.** Both chapters narrate a Sabbath healing in Jerusalem. Both involve a man with a long-standing condition. Both produce confrontation with the religious authorities. But the healed men respond in opposite ways. The man at Bethesda did not know who healed him (5:13) and, once told, reported Jesus to the authorities (5:15); the chapter does not record any further spiritual development. The man born blind begins not knowing who healed him but, under pressure from the authorities, moves steadily toward deeper confession until he worships Jesus as Lord (9:38). The man at Bethesda became the instrument of escalation against Jesus; the man born blind became a witness for Jesus against the authorities. The contrast suggests that the same miraculous encounter can produce opposite trajectories depending on the response of the one who is healed. The healing itself does not determine the outcome; the recipient's response does.

5. **The synagogue expulsion (aposynagogos) introduces the cost of confession and the separation between institutional Judaism and those who confess Jesus.** The formal agreement to expel anyone who confesses Jesus as Christ (v. 22) creates a binary: confess and be expelled, or be silent and remain. The parents choose silence and security; the son chooses speech and expulsion. The cost is real -- loss of community, social standing, economic relationships, religious participation. The chapter does not present the cost as trivial or the choice as easy. It presents the choice as a test that reveals what a person values most. The parents value their place in the community; the son values the truth of what has happened to him. The chapter's trajectory suggests that what is lost through expulsion -- communal belonging as defined by the current authorities -- is surpassed by what is gained through encounter with Jesus: a new community defined by the shepherd who calls his own by name (10:3) and who never casts out the one who comes to him (6:37).

6. **The question of Jesus's origin ("where he is from") continues from chapters 7 and 8 and reaches a new resolution.** In 7:27, the Jerusalemites said "we know where this man is from" and used that knowledge to disqualify him. In 7:28, Jesus responded that they do not know the one who sent him. In 9:29, the Pharisees say "we do not know where he is from" and use that ignorance to disqualify him. The irony is complete: whether they claim to know or claim not to know his origin, the result is the same -- rejection. The man born blind provides the answer the authorities will not accept: "If this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (v. 33). He has arrived at the truth about Jesus's origin through reasoning from evidence, while the authorities, who have access to the same evidence and far more scriptural training, refuse to follow the argument to its conclusion.

7. **The creation imagery pervading the healing connects Jesus's work to the work of God in Genesis.** The making of clay from the ground (v. 6) echoes the forming of man from the dust in Genesis 2:7. The giving of sight to one who has never seen echoes the giving of light to a dark creation in Genesis 1:3. The method Jesus chose -- clay, water, the command to go and wash -- is not arbitrary but symbolic: it recapitulates the creative acts that only God performs. The sign does not merely demonstrate Jesus's power; it identifies the kind of power at work. This is not therapeutic skill but creative authority -- the authority of the Word through whom "all things came into being, and apart from him nothing came into being that has come into being" (1:3).

8. **The Pool of Siloam ties the healing to the Tabernacles context and to Jesus's identity as the sent one.** The author's deliberate translation of the pool's name as "Sent" (apestalmenos) connects the healing to the document's central christological claim: Jesus is the one sent by the Father. The pool from which the Tabernacles water was drawn -- the water Jesus claimed to fulfill in 7:37-38 -- is now the instrument through which a blind man receives sight. Water and light converge: the living water of chapter 7 and the light of the world of chapter 8 meet in chapter 9 at the pool whose name means "Sent."

9. **Jesus's statement "your sin remains" (9:41) functions as a verdict that inverts the authorities' verdict against him.** The Pharisees pronounced Jesus a sinner (v. 24). Jesus pronounces that their sin remains. They cast out the man born blind; Jesus will describe them as thieves and robbers who do not enter by the door (10:1). They claimed the authority to determine who is from God and who is not; Jesus declares that their claim to see is the very thing that keeps their sin in place. The judgment of 9:39 is enacted in 9:41: those who claimed to see have become blind, and their blindness is culpable because it is chosen.

10. **The present-tense language of believing continues from earlier chapters.** In verse 35, Jesus asks "Do you believe (pisteueis, present tense) in the Son of Man?" In verse 38, the man responds "I believe (pisteuo, present tense)." The question and the answer are in the present: do you now, at this moment, place your trust? The man's answer is an act in the present moment. His ongoing discipleship will be tested by the ongoing encounter with Jesus. The chapter does not treat belief as a past event to which one looks back; it treats belief as a present posture to which one commits. The worship that follows the confession -- "and he worshiped him" (v. 38) -- is the first act of that ongoing posture.

11. **The chapter demonstrates that encounter with Jesus produces not consensus but separation.** The neighbors are confused (vv. 8-9). The Pharisees are divided (v. 16). The parents are fearful and evasive (vv. 20-23). The man himself moves toward confession and worship, while the authorities move toward hardened rejection. The same event -- a blind man receiving sight -- produces opposite responses in those who witness it. The chapter does not present the evidence as ambiguous; it presents the responses as revealing. The evidence is clear; what differs is the willingness to follow it. This is the enacted form of 7:17: "If anyone is willing to do his will, he will know of the teaching, whether it is of God." The man was willing; the authorities were not. The same light illuminated both, and the same light divided them.
