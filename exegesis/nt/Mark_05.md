# Mark 5 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Mark 4):** After the escalating confrontations of chapters 2-3, the author shifted in chapter 4 to a sustained block of teaching in parables. Jesus taught the crowds about the kingdom of God through images -- the sower, the lamp, the seed growing secretly, the mustard seed. The parables revealed a kingdom that works through the word, that meets with varied responses (some fruitful, some not), that grows by hidden means, and that begins small but becomes immense. The chapter closed with a dramatic demonstration: crossing the sea at evening, Jesus calmed a storm by rebuking the wind and commanding the sea to be still (4:39). The disciples, terrified, asked the question that stands as the hinge between chapter 4 and chapter 5: "Who, then, is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?" (4:41). This question -- not yet answered within the narrative -- drives everything that follows.

**What the author argues immediately after (Mark 6:1-6):** Jesus returns to his hometown and teaches in the synagogue. The people are astonished at his wisdom and miracles but "took offense at Him" (6:3). Jesus observes: "A prophet is not dishonored except in his hometown and among his own relatives, and in his own household" (6:4). The result is devastating: "He could not do any miracle there except that He laid His hands on a few sick people and healed them. And He was amazed at their unbelief" (6:5-6). After the extraordinary displays of authority in chapter 5, the author immediately narrates a scene of rejection and impotence caused by unbelief. The contrast is deliberate.

**How chapter 5 connects them:** The disciples' question at 4:41 -- "Who is this?" -- receives its answer through three escalating demonstrations in chapter 5. The one whom the wind and sea obey also exercises authority over a legion of unclean spirits (5:1-20), over chronic illness and ritual impurity (5:25-34), and over death itself (5:35-43). Each episode answers the question more fully: this is the one whose power extends over every domain that oppresses, defiles, and destroys. But the chapter also prepares for the rejection in 6:1-6 by showing that the response to Jesus's power is not uniform. The Gerasenes witness the evidence and beg him to leave (5:17). The mourners at Jairus's house hear Jesus's word and laugh (5:40). The contrast between those who respond with faith -- the hemorrhaging woman (5:34), Jairus pressed to keep believing (5:36) -- and those who respond with fear or mockery anticipates the hometown's unbelief in chapter 6. The author is building a sustained argument: the kingdom's power is real and comprehensive, but its reception depends on faith, and faith is precisely what those closest to the evidence often lack.

## Verses 1-20: Authority Over a Legion of Unclean Spirits in Gentile Territory

### The Setting (vv. 1-5)

"They came to the other side of the sea, into the region of the Gerasenes."

This single sentence marks a geographic and symbolic boundary crossing. "The other side" (to peran) takes Jesus and the disciples out of Jewish Galilee and into Gentile territory. The "region of the Gerasenes" (Gerasa was a city of the Decapolis, a league of predominantly Greek cities east of the Jordan) places Jesus among pagans for the first time in the narrative. Every detail in the scene that follows is colored by this location.

"When He got out of the boat, immediately a man from the tombs with an unclean spirit met Him."

The author's characteristic "immediately" (euthys) signals the urgency of the encounter. There is no transition, no gradual approach. The moment Jesus sets foot on Gentile soil, he is confronted by the most extreme case of spiritual bondage the document has yet presented.

The description of the man (vv. 3-5) is detailed and cumulative, building a portrait of total dehumanization:

- **"He lived among the tombs"** (en tois mnemasin). In Torah, contact with the dead produces the most severe form of ritual uncleanness. Numbers 19:16 prescribes seven days of impurity for anyone who touches a human bone or a grave. This man does not merely touch a tomb; he lives among them. His dwelling place is a place of death and perpetual uncleanness.

- **"No one was able to bind him anymore, not even with a chain... the chains had been torn apart by him and the shackles broken in pieces; and no one was strong enough to subdue him."** The language of binding and subduing is significant. The community has attempted to restrain this man by force and has failed. The word "subdue" (damasai) means to tame or master, a word used for the subjugation of wild animals. The man has been treated as something less than human -- chained like a beast -- and yet no human power can contain what holds him.

- **"Constantly, night and day, he was screaming among the tombs and in the mountains, and cutting himself with stones."** The picture is of unceasing torment. Night and day, without rest, the man screams and mutilates himself. The self-destruction is driven by the unclean spirit, not by the man's own will. He is a prisoner of a power that uses his own body against him.

The author has constructed a figure of maximal uncleanness and maximal bondage: Gentile territory, tombs, unclean spirit, nakedness (implied by the later detail that he is found "clothed" after healing, v. 15), superhuman violence, self-destruction. Isaiah 65:4 connects tomb-dwelling with pig-eating as marks of those outside the covenant: "Who sit among graves and spend the night in secret places; who eat pig's flesh, and the broth of unclean meat is in their pots." The entire scene is saturated with the imagery of Isaiah's description of paganism at its most degraded. This is what Jesus encounters when he crosses to "the other side."

### The Confrontation (vv. 6-10)

"Seeing Jesus from a distance, he ran up and bowed down before Him; and shouting with a loud voice, he said, 'What business do You have with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I implore You by God, do not torment me!' For He had already been saying to him, 'Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!'"

Several elements require careful attention.

**The man's approach.** Despite the description of uncontrollable violence, the man runs to Jesus and "bowed down before Him" (prosekynesan auto). The verb proskyneo denotes an act of prostration -- falling on one's face before a superior power. The same man whom chains could not restrain runs toward Jesus and falls at his feet. The author presents this as the involuntary response of the spiritual power within the man to the authority it recognizes in Jesus.

**"What business do You have with me" (ti emoi kai soi).** This is the same formulaic expression used by the unclean spirit in the synagogue at Capernaum (1:24): "What have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth?" The phrase is attested throughout the LXX as a protest against unwanted intervention (Judges 11:12; 1 Kings 17:18; 2 Kings 3:13). The demon recognizes that Jesus's presence constitutes an intrusion into its domain.

**"Son of the Most High God" (huie tou theou tou hypsistou).** The title "Most High" (hypsistos) in the LXX appears with particular frequency in contexts where non-Israelites recognize Israel's God. Melchizedek, the Canaanite priest-king, blesses Abraham by "God Most High" (Genesis 14:18-20 LXX). Nebuchadnezzar, the Babylonian king, acknowledges "the Most High God" after Daniel's deliverance (Daniel 3:26 LXX) and again after his own restoration from madness (Daniel 4:2 LXX). The author has a Gentile demon use the Gentile designation for Israel's God. The title is appropriate to the geographic setting: on Gentile soil, the spiritual powers use the name by which pagans recognized the supreme deity of Israel. The author continues the pattern established in 1:24 and 3:11 -- the unclean spirits know Jesus's identity and declare it, while human characters remain uncertain (4:41).

**"I implore You by God, do not torment me!"** The irony is sharp. The demon adjures Jesus "by God" -- it appeals to the authority of the very God whose Son is confronting it. The word "torment" (basanises) is a term used for judicial punishment or torture. The demon perceives Jesus's action as the enactment of a sentence -- not a random encounter but a judgment.

**The sequence of command and dialogue.** Verse 8 is explanatory: "For He had already been saying to him, 'Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!'" The author uses the imperfect tense (elegen), indicating that Jesus had been repeatedly commanding the spirit before the exchange in verse 7. This is the only exorcism in the document where Jesus's initial command does not produce immediate compliance. The delay is not a sign of weakness but sets up the revelation of the scope of the problem: this is not a single unclean spirit.

**"What is your name?" (v. 9).** Jesus asks the spirit's name. In ancient Near Eastern practice, knowing a name conferred power over the named entity. But the dynamic here is not that Jesus needs the name in order to gain power -- he has already commanded the spirit to depart. The question serves a revelatory function for the reader.

**"My name is Legion, for we are many."** The answer is devastating in its implications. "Legion" (Legeon) is a Latin loanword -- the term for the primary unit of the Roman army, comprising approximately 5,000 to 6,000 soldiers. The author uses a Roman military term for the force occupying this man. The word carries inescapable political overtones for a first-century audience living under Roman occupation. The man is described as occupied -- his body colonized by an alien force that has taken away his autonomy, his humanity, and his capacity for community. The name "Legion" frames spiritual oppression in the language of imperial subjugation.

The shift between singular and plural in the demon's speech is notable: "My name is Legion" (singular), "for we are many" (plural). The man and the demons blur together linguistically, reflecting the degree to which the man's identity has been consumed by the possessing powers.

**"He begged Him earnestly not to send them out of the region" (v. 10).** The demons plead not to be expelled from the territory. This suggests a territorial understanding of spiritual power -- the unclean spirits are attached to this region and fear displacement.

### The Pigs and the Sea (vv. 11-13)

"Now there was a large herd of pigs feeding nearby on the mountain. And the demons begged Him, saying, 'Send us into the pigs so that we may enter them.' Jesus gave them permission."

The presence of pigs confirms Gentile territory. Under Torah, pigs are among the paradigmatically unclean animals (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8). No Jewish community would maintain a herd of swine. The demons request transfer into the pigs rather than outright expulsion, and Jesus permits it.

"And coming out, the unclean spirits entered the pigs; and the herd rushed down the steep bank into the sea, about two thousand of them; and they were drowned in the sea."

The number -- about two thousand -- approaches the size of a Roman cohort or the auxiliary forces that would have been familiar to residents of the Decapolis. The image of a "legion" rushing headlong into the sea and being destroyed there carries an unmistakable echo for readers steeped in Israel's foundational narrative. In Exodus 14-15, the Egyptian army -- the occupying military power that had enslaved Israel -- pursued God's people to the sea and was drowned in it. The Song of Moses celebrates: "Pharaoh's chariots and his army He has cast into the sea; and the choicest of his officers are drowned in the Red Sea" (Exodus 15:4). The author may not be making an explicit allegorical equation between Rome and the demons, but the resonance of the image -- a force called "Legion," numbering in the thousands, destroyed in the sea -- would not have been lost on a first-century hearer who knew the Exodus story. The God who drowned Pharaoh's army is at work again.

The pigs' destruction also demonstrates that the demons are genuinely destructive -- given any vessel, they drive it to annihilation. The man's survival is not because the demons were benign but because Jesus's prior authority prevented the destruction they would have accomplished.

### The Response: Fear and Rejection (vv. 14-17)

"Their herdsmen ran away and reported it in the city and in the countryside. And the people came to see what it was that had happened."

The herdsmen flee and spread the report. The townspeople come to investigate. What they find:

"And then they came to Jesus and saw the man who had been demon-possessed sitting down, clothed and in his right mind, the very man who had previously had the 'legion'; and they became frightened."

The contrast with vv. 3-5 is total. The man who was naked is now clothed. The man who could not be subdued is sitting calmly. The man who screamed ceaselessly is "in his right mind" (sophronounta -- a word denoting sanity, self-control, soundness of thinking). The restoration is complete: social reintegration (clothed), physical composure (sitting), mental wholeness (in his right mind). The author uses the participle "the very man who had previously had the 'legion'" to ensure the reader understands this is the same person -- the transformation is that radical.

The townspeople's response is fear. They "became frightened" (ephobethesan). This is the same fear the disciples displayed after the stilling of the storm (4:41 -- "they became very much afraid," ephobethesan phobon megan). But whereas the disciples' fear led to the question "Who is this?" -- a question that, however faltering, remains open -- the Gerasenes' fear leads to rejection:

"And they began to beg Him to leave their region" (v. 17).

The people who have witnessed the liberation of a man from years of torment respond by asking Jesus to go away. The author does not explain their motives. The economic loss of two thousand pigs may be a factor. The disruption of the existing order -- even an order that included a screaming, self-mutilating man among their tombs -- may be more threatening than the bondage was. The author simply records the response: the evidence of Jesus's power produces not gratitude or faith but a desire for him to leave.

### The Commissioned Proclaimer (vv. 18-20)

"And as He was getting into the boat, the man who had been demon-possessed was begging Him that he might accompany Him."

The healed man wants to follow Jesus -- the same impulse that moved the first disciples (1:18, 20; 2:14). But Jesus refuses:

"And He did not let him, but He said to him, 'Go home to your people and report to them what great things the Lord has done for you, and how He had mercy on you.'"

This instruction stands in sharp contrast to Jesus's consistent pattern in Jewish territory, where he commands silence: the leper is told "say nothing to anyone" (1:44), the demons are forbidden to speak "because they knew him" (1:34; 3:12), and Jairus's household will be told that "no one was to know about this" (5:43). Here, in Gentile territory, Jesus commands the opposite: go and tell. The man is to become a herald.

The content of the proclamation is specified: "what great things the Lord has done for you, and how He had mercy on you." Jesus uses "the Lord" (ho kyrios) -- a term that in the LXX renders the divine name YHWH. The man is told to proclaim what God has done.

"And he went away and began to proclaim in Decapolis what great things Jesus had done for him; and everyone was amazed" (v. 20).

The author's editorial note is precise and significant. Jesus told the man to report what "the Lord" had done. The man proclaims what "Jesus" had done. The author allows the substitution to stand without comment, letting the equation speak for itself: what "the Lord" did and what "Jesus" did are the same thing. This is not a theological argument; it is a narrative identification. The language of Psalm 126:2-3 resonates: "Then they said among the nations, 'The LORD has done great things for them.' The LORD has done great things for us; we are glad." The phrase "great things" (megala) in connection with "the Lord" belongs to the vocabulary of Israel's praise for God's saving acts.

The man proclaims "in Decapolis" -- the league of ten Greek cities. He becomes the first herald of Jesus's power in the Gentile world, commissioned by Jesus himself. The mission to the nations, which the document will develop more fully, begins here with a single restored man telling his own story.

## Verses 21-24a: Jairus and the Urgency of Faith

"When Jesus had crossed over again in the boat to the other side, a large crowd gathered around Him; and He stayed by the seashore."

Jesus returns to the Jewish side of the sea. The crowd gathers immediately. The author now begins a two-part story that will be interrupted by a second story -- a literary technique (often called intercalation) that the author employs repeatedly throughout the document (cf. 3:20-35, where Jesus's family comes looking for him and the Beelzebul controversy is inserted between their arrival and Jesus's response; cf. 6:7-30, where the sending of the twelve and their return frame the account of John's death; cf. 11:12-25, where the cursing of the fig tree frames the temple action). The technique is not incidental; it creates interpretive connections between the framing story and the inserted story, so that each illuminates the other.

"And one of the synagogue officials, named Jairus, came, and upon seeing Him, fell at His feet, and pleaded with Him earnestly, saying, 'My little daughter is at the point of death; please come and lay Your hands on her, so that she will get well and live.'"

**Jairus's position.** A synagogue official (archisunagogos) was a lay leader responsible for the administration of synagogue services -- selecting readers, maintaining order, overseeing the building. This was a position of communal standing and authority. That such a person falls at Jesus's feet and pleads indicates the desperation of his situation and his recognition of Jesus's power.

**"My little daughter" (to thygatrion mou).** The diminutive form (thygatrion rather than thygater) conveys tenderness -- "my little girl." She is "at the point of death" (eschatos echei -- literally "is at the last," an idiom for being at the extremity of life).

**"So that she will get well and live" (hina sothe kai zese).** The verb sothe is from sozo, which means both "to heal/make well" and "to save." The author uses this word with deliberate ambiguity throughout the document. In 5:28, the hemorrhaging woman will use it ("I will get well/be saved"); in 5:34, Jesus will use it ("your faith has made you well/saved you"). The same word will appear in 8:35 ("whoever wants to save his life will lose it"), 10:26 ("who then can be saved?"), and at the cross in 15:30-31 ("save yourself... He saved others; He cannot save Himself"). The author is building a network of meaning around sozo that connects physical restoration with something larger. Here, Jairus uses sozo alongside zese ("and live") -- the two verbs together encompass both healing and the continuation of life.

"And He went off with him; and a large crowd was following Him and pressing in on Him" (v. 24).

Jesus goes with Jairus. The crowd presses in. The stage is set for what the author wants the reader to expect: Jesus is on his way to heal a dying child. Then the author interrupts.

## Verses 25-34: The Woman with the Flow of Blood -- Inserted Story

### The Woman's Condition (vv. 25-26)

"A woman who had had a hemorrhage for twelve years, and had endured much at the hands of many physicians, and had spent all that she had and was not helped at all, but instead had become worse."

The author describes the woman's condition with a series of devastating clauses, each one adding to the weight of her suffering:

- **Twelve years of hemorrhaging.** Under Leviticus 15:25-27, a woman with a discharge of blood outside her normal period "will be unclean as long as she has the discharge, just as in the days of her menstrual impurity." Everything she lies on, sits on, or touches becomes unclean. Anyone who touches these objects must wash, bathe, and remain unclean until evening. The woman has lived for twelve years in a state of perpetual ritual exclusion. She cannot participate in communal worship, cannot touch others without rendering them impure, cannot enter the temple courts. Her condition is not merely medical; it is social and religious isolation.

- **"Endured much at the hands of many physicians."** The verb "endured" (pathousa) is the same root (pascho) used elsewhere in the document for Jesus's own suffering (8:31; 9:12). Her encounters with physicians have been a form of suffering.

- **"Spent all that she had."** She is now destitute.

- **"Was not helped at all, but instead had become worse."** The treatments have been futile; her condition has deteriorated. The author has systematically stripped this woman of every resource: health, money, community, religious participation. She has nothing left except the report she has heard about Jesus.

### The Touch and the Healing (vv. 27-29)

"After hearing about Jesus, she came up in the crowd behind Him and touched His cloak. For she had been saying to herself, 'If I just touch His garments, I will get well.'"

The woman approaches from behind, in the crowd, without announcing herself. The reason for her stealth is implicit in her condition: under the purity regulations of Leviticus 15, her touch would render anyone she contacted unclean. She is doing something she is not supposed to do -- touching another person while in a state of impurity. Her approach is an act of transgression driven by desperation and by something the author calls faith (v. 34).

Her internal reasoning -- "If I just touch His garments, I will get well (sothesomai)" -- uses the same verb sozo that Jairus used in verse 23. The two stories are linked by this word. The woman believes that Jesus's power is so potent that even indirect contact -- touching his clothing, not his person -- will be sufficient.

"And immediately the flow of her blood was dried up; and she felt in her body that she was healed of her disease."

The healing is instantaneous. The word "dried up" (exeranthe) is vivid and physical -- the same word used for the withering of plants (4:6, the seed on rocky ground that "withered away"; 11:20-21, the fig tree that "withered"). The source of impurity and suffering is stopped at its root.

### Jesus's Response (vv. 30-34)

"And immediately Jesus, perceiving in Himself that power from Him had gone out, turned around in the crowd and said, 'Who touched My garments?'"

The author describes Jesus as perceiving (epignous) the outflow of power (dynamin). The word dynamis -- power, ability -- is the same word used for the mighty works Jesus performs throughout the document (6:2, 5, 14; 9:39). The healing is presented not as an automatic or impersonal transfer but as something Jesus perceives. The power belongs to him and flows from him. He is aware of its movement.

"And His disciples said to Him, 'You see the crowd pressing in on You, and You say, Who touched Me?'"

The disciples' response is practical to the point of exasperation. The crowd is pressing on Jesus from every side; singling out one touch among hundreds seems absurd. But the author distinguishes between the general press of the crowd and the specific touch of faith. Many are physically pressing against Jesus; one has reached out with the confidence that his power can heal her. The distinction matters.

"And He looked around to see the woman who had done this. But the woman, fearing and trembling, aware of what had happened to her, came and fell down before Him and told Him the whole truth."

The woman comes forward in fear and trembling. Her fear has multiple layers: she has received an extraordinary healing; she has touched a rabbi while ritually unclean; she has been discovered. She falls before Jesus -- the same posture as the Gerasene demoniac (v. 6) and Jairus (v. 22) -- and tells "the whole truth" (pasan ten aletheian). The author notes that she conceals nothing.

"And He said to her, 'Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace and be cured of your disease.'"

Jesus's response contains several elements:

**"Daughter" (thygater).** This is a term of familial inclusion. The woman who has been excluded from community for twelve years is addressed with a word of belonging. Jesus claims her as family -- a striking echo of 3:34-35, where Jesus redefined family as "whoever does the will of God."

**"Your faith has made you well" (he pistis sou sesoken se).** The verb sesoken is the perfect tense of sozo -- "has saved/healed you." Jesus attributes the healing not to the touch itself but to the faith that motivated it. The distinction is critical. It was not a magical transfer triggered by physical contact; it was the woman's trust in Jesus's power that activated the healing. The author uses this declaration to define what faith looks like in practice: it is a desperate reaching toward Jesus in the confidence that he has the power to restore what is broken.

**"Go in peace" (hypage eis eirenen).** The Hebrew equivalent is lek leshalom -- a blessing of wholeness and well-being. The word eirene (shalom) encompasses more than the absence of conflict; it denotes completeness, health, restoration to right relationship. The woman is sent out not merely healed but whole.

**"Be cured of your disease" (isthi hygies apo tes mastigos sou).** The word mastigos literally means "whip" or "scourge" -- the author describes her disease as a beating she has endured. Jesus's final word ensures that the healing is permanent, not temporary.

### The Twelve-Year Connection

The woman has been hemorrhaging for twelve years (v. 25). Jairus's daughter is twelve years old (v. 42). The author places this numerical connection before the reader without comment. The girl has been alive for exactly as long as the woman has been dying. When the girl was born, the woman's affliction began. The two restorations -- of the woman and of the girl -- are bound together by this shared number. The number twelve itself carries the weight of Israel's twelve tribes, and its appearance in both stories suggests a symbolic dimension beyond the biographical: these are stories of the restoration of Israel's people.

## Verses 35-43: Authority Over Death Itself

### The Report of Death (vv. 35-37)

"While He was still speaking, people came from the house of the synagogue official, saying, 'Your daughter has died; why bother the Teacher further?'"

The delay caused by the woman's encounter has had consequences. While Jesus was stopping to address the woman, the girl died. The messengers' words are blunt: "Your daughter has died" (he thygater sou apethanen). Their conclusion follows logically from the premises available to them: death is final; there is no reason to continue troubling Jesus. The word "bother" (skylleis) means to trouble or weary -- they assume Jesus's power has limits, and death exceeds them.

"But Jesus, overhearing what was being spoken, said to the synagogue official, 'Do not be afraid, only believe.'"

The author uses the verb parakousas, which can mean either "overhearing" or "ignoring/disregarding." Both senses may be operative: Jesus hears the report but refuses to let it determine the outcome. His response to Jairus is a direct command: "Do not be afraid, only believe" (me phobou, monon pisteue). The present imperative pisteue calls for ongoing trust -- "keep on believing." The word monon ("only") strips the demand to its essence: nothing is required of Jairus except continued trust. The contrast with the Gerasenes is sharp: they saw the evidence of Jesus's power and responded with fear that led to rejection (v. 17). Jairus is told to do the opposite -- to let faith, not fear, determine his response to the situation.

The woman's story, placed just before this moment, functions as a demonstration for Jairus. She reached out in faith when all other resources were exhausted -- physicians had failed, money was gone, her condition had worsened. And her faith was vindicated. Now Jairus faces the ultimate exhaustion of resources: his daughter is dead. The author has positioned the woman's story to show Jairus (and the reader) what faith looks like when the situation appears hopeless.

"And He allowed no one to accompany Him except Peter, James, and John the brother of James."

Jesus restricts the witnesses. Peter, James, and John form an inner circle who will also be present at the transfiguration (9:2) and at Gethsemane (14:33). The author reserves these three for the moments of greatest revelation: the raising of the dead, the unveiling of glory, and the agony before the cross. The pattern suggests that what is about to happen belongs to a category of revelation that requires a limited, prepared audience.

### At the House (vv. 38-40)

"They came to the house of the synagogue official, and He saw a commotion, and people loudly weeping and wailing."

The mourning has already begun. Professional mourners were customary in first-century Jewish practice; even the poorest family was expected to hire at least two flute players and one wailing woman (Mishnah Ketubbot 4:4, a later codification of existing practice). The wailing and commotion indicate that the household has accepted the finality of the girl's death.

"And after entering, He said to them, 'Why are you making a commotion and weeping? The child has not died, but is asleep.' And they began laughing at Him."

Jesus's statement -- "The child has not died, but is asleep" (to paidion ouk apethanen alla katheudei) -- provokes ridicule. The mourners know the girl is dead. Their laughter is the response of people who consider Jesus's words absurd. The word katheudei (sleeps) is used for ordinary sleep in this document (4:27, the farmer who "goes to bed at night and gets up daily"; 13:36; 14:37, the disciples sleeping in Gethsemane). Jesus uses it here to redefine death in relation to his own power: what is permanent and irreversible to the mourners is temporary and reversible to him. The statement does not deny that the girl has biologically died (the messengers stated she had, and the mourners confirm it); it reframes death as a condition from which Jesus can summon a person back, as easily as waking a sleeper.

"But putting them all outside, He took along the child's father and mother and His own companions, and entered the room where the child was in bed."

Jesus expels the unbelieving mourners. The verb ekballon (putting outside) is the same word used for casting out demons (1:34, 39; 3:15, 22-23). The author uses it without comment, but the parallel is present in the language: unbelief, like an unclean spirit, is removed from the space where Jesus will act. Only five witnesses remain: the parents and the three disciples. The raising of the dead occurs before the smallest audience of any miracle in the document.

### The Raising (vv. 41-43)

"And taking the child by the hand, He said to her, 'Talitha, kum!' (which translated means, 'Little girl, I say to you, get up!')."

**Touch.** Jesus takes the dead girl by the hand. Under Numbers 19:11, "The one who touches the dead body of any person shall be unclean for seven days." For the third time in this chapter, Jesus makes physical contact with a source of ritual impurity -- the demoniac among the tombs (v. 1-2, by proximity and engagement), the hemorrhaging woman (who touched him, vv. 27-30), and now a corpse. In every case, the expected direction of contamination is reversed. Jesus does not become unclean; the source of uncleanness is itself overcome. The dead girl does not defile Jesus; Jesus's touch brings life. This pattern -- established with the leper in 1:41 and extended through the three encounters of chapter 5 -- constitutes one of the author's most consistent arguments: in Jesus, the power of cleanness overpowers the power of uncleanness, the power of life overpowers the power of death.

**"Talitha, kum."** The author preserves Jesus's Aramaic words and provides a translation. This is a practice the author reserves for moments of particular power and significance: "Boanerges" (3:17), "Talitha kum" here, "Ephphatha" (7:34), "Abba" (14:36), and "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani" (15:34). By retaining the original Aramaic, the author gives the reader Jesus's actual spoken words -- the words that the dead girl heard. The phrase is simple, even tender: "Little girl" (talitha -- a term of affection, from the Aramaic tl'itha, a young female lamb), "get up" (kum -- the straightforward imperative "rise").

The author's translation adds a phrase not present in the Aramaic: "I say to you" (soi lego). This insertion emphasizes the personal authority behind the command. It is not a prayer to God asking for the girl's restoration. It is a direct command from Jesus to the dead child: I say to you, get up.

"And immediately the girl got up and began to walk, for she was twelve years old. And immediately they were completely astonished."

The response is instantaneous -- "immediately" (euthys), twice in one verse. The girl rises and walks. The parenthetical note "for she was twelve years old" explains why she walks rather than crawls -- she is old enough to walk on her own. It also completes the numerical link to the hemorrhaging woman's twelve years of illness. The witnesses are "completely astonished" (exestesan ekstasei megale -- literally "they were astonished with a great astonishment," a Semitic construction for emphasis).

"And He gave them strict orders that no one was to know about this, and He told them to have something given her to eat."

Two final details:

**The command to silence.** Back in Jewish territory, the secrecy pattern resumes. In Gentile territory, the healed demoniac was told to proclaim (v. 19). Here, the witnesses of the raising are told to keep silent. The author maintains the tension: Jesus controls the revelation of his identity and power, suppressing testimony in Jewish territory where premature messianic claims would provoke misunderstanding and opposition (as 6:14-16 will show, where Herod hears reports about Jesus and the speculation runs wild -- "John the Baptist has risen from the dead... He is Elijah... He is a prophet").

**"He told them to have something given her to eat."** This practical detail serves as a demonstration of the reality of the resurrection. The girl is not a ghost or an apparition; she is a physical, living child who needs food. The command also reveals something about Jesus's character as the author presents it: in the aftermath of an act of staggering power, his attention turns to the ordinary needs of a child. The same quality appears in 6:31, where Jesus tells the disciples to "come away by yourselves to a secluded place and rest for a while," and in 8:2-3, where he is concerned that the crowd has had nothing to eat.

## The Structure of Mark 5 as an Answer to "Who Is This?"

The three episodes of Mark 5 follow the storm-calming of 4:35-41 and answer the disciples' question -- "Who, then, is this?" -- through a systematic demonstration of Jesus's authority over three domains:

| Episode | Domain | Source of Bondage | Barrier Crossed | Response |
|---------|--------|-------------------|-----------------|----------|
| vv. 1-20 | The demonic realm | A legion of unclean spirits | Gentile territory, tombs, uncleanness | Locals: fear and rejection. Healed man: proclamation |
| vv. 25-34 | Chronic illness/impurity | Twelve-year hemorrhage | Purity boundary (she touches him) | Woman: faith. Jesus: "Your faith has saved you" |
| vv. 35-43 | Death itself | The girl has died | Death as final (the mourners' assumption) | Mourners: laughter/mockery. Jairus: challenged to believe |

The escalation is deliberate. Authority over demons was established already in 1:23-27 and 1:34. Authority over illness was demonstrated in 1:30-31 and 1:40-42. But authority over death is new. The author has saved the most dramatic demonstration for the climax of the series, and in doing so has moved the reader from the question "Who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" to an implicit answer: this is the one who commands every force that holds creation captive -- spiritual bondage, physical affliction, and death itself.

---

## The Reversal of Contamination as a Unifying Theme

All three episodes share a common dynamic: the expected direction of ritual contamination is reversed. In each case, Jesus comes into contact with a source of defilement, and instead of becoming unclean, he overpowers the impurity:

1. **The demoniac** lives among tombs in Gentile territory, saturated in death-uncleanness. Jesus does not become defiled; the unclean spirits are expelled.
2. **The hemorrhaging woman** touches Jesus while in a state of perpetual blood-impurity. Jesus does not become unclean; the flow of blood dries up.
3. **The dead girl** is a corpse. Jesus takes her hand. He does not become unclean; she comes back to life.

This pattern, begun with the leper in 1:40-45, reaches its fullest expression in chapter 5. The author is presenting a consistent argument about the nature of Jesus's power: it operates as the opposite of contamination. Where uncleanness normally transfers from the impure to the pure, Jesus's holiness transfers from the pure to the impure. Cleanness, life, and wholeness flow outward from him and overwhelm the forces of defilement, sickness, and death. The purity system of Leviticus is not abolished or contradicted; it is fulfilled from the other direction. What the system could only quarantine, Jesus heals.

---

## Faith, Fear, and the Question of Response

The chapter presents a spectrum of responses to Jesus's power, and the author uses this spectrum to build an argument about what separates reception from rejection:

- **The Gerasenes** see the evidence and respond with fear that leads to rejection: "They began to beg Him to leave their region" (v. 17).
- **The hemorrhaging woman** acts in faith despite her condition, her poverty, and the social consequences of touching someone while unclean. Jesus validates her: "Your faith has made you well" (v. 34).
- **Jairus** begins in faith (he falls at Jesus's feet and pleads, v. 22), faces the annihilation of his hope (his daughter dies, v. 35), and is commanded to keep believing: "Do not be afraid, only believe" (v. 36).
- **The mourners** hear Jesus's word ("the child is asleep") and respond with ridicule: "They began laughing at Him" (v. 40). They are expelled from the room.

The author positions faith and fear as the defining alternatives. Both are responses to the recognition of power. Fear withdraws from the power and asks it to leave. Faith approaches the power and reaches out for what it can give. The author will develop this theme further in 6:1-6, where unbelief -- not an external enemy but the refusal of those who know Jesus best -- limits what Jesus can do: "He could not do any miracle there... He was amazed at their unbelief."

---

## Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 5

Mark 5 completes the answer to the question the disciples asked after the calming of the storm: "Who, then, is this?" The author answers through three episodes of escalating scope:

1. Jesus crosses into Gentile territory and encounters a man possessed by a legion of unclean spirits -- the most extreme case of spiritual bondage in the entire document. He casts out the spirits, destroys them in the sea (in language that evokes Israel's foundational deliverance at the Exodus), and restores the man to sanity, community, and mission. The Gerasenes respond with fear and rejection. The healed man is commissioned to proclaim what "the Lord" has done -- and proclaims what "Jesus" has done, an equation the author lets stand.

2. A woman excluded from community for twelve years by a condition no human power could heal reaches out to touch Jesus's garment. Instead of her uncleanness contaminating him, his power heals her. Jesus names her faith as the cause and calls her "daughter" -- a word of inclusion for one who has been excluded.

3. A synagogue official's twelve-year-old daughter dies while Jesus is delayed by the woman's encounter. Jesus commands the father to keep believing despite the evidence of death. He enters the room, takes the dead girl by the hand, and commands her to rise. She does. The one whom wind and sea obey is also obeyed by death.

The chapter's argument is cumulative: Jesus exercises authority over the entire range of forces that oppress, defile, and kill. The spiritual powers recognize him and submit ("Son of the Most High God," v. 7). Disease and impurity yield to his power even through the touch of his clothing. Death releases its hold at his spoken word. The author is not merely cataloging miracles; he is building a case for the identity of Jesus -- the identity that will not be fully declared until Peter's confession in 8:29 and not fully understood until the cross and the empty tomb. In the meantime, the evidence accumulates, and the question of response -- faith or fear, reaching out or pulling away -- determines whether individuals receive what Jesus offers or ask him to leave.
