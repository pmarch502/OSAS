# Luke 23 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 22):** Luke 22 traced the mechanisms by which Jesus passed from controlling the temple (the daily teaching pattern of 21:37-38) to being bound and delivered to his enemies. Satan returned -- the adversary who departed in 4:13 "until an opportune time" -- and entered Judas, who arranged the betrayal "apart from the crowd" (22:6). The Passover meal became the site of a radical reinterpretation: Jesus identified the bread as his body "given for you" and the cup as "the new covenant in My blood" (22:19-20), redirecting Israel's foundational remembrance from the Exodus to himself. He predicted Peter's triple denial with precise specificity (22:34) while also praying that Peter's faith would survive the sifting and that he would turn back and strengthen the others (22:32). On the Mount of Olives, Jesus submitted to the Father's will -- "not My will, but Yours be done" (22:42) -- at the cost of agony so severe that his sweat fell like drops of blood. He was arrested; Peter denied him three times; the guards mocked him and demanded he "prophesy" even as his prophecies were being fulfilled in real time. The chapter concluded with the council hearing at daybreak, where Jesus declared that "from now on the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God" (22:69), the council inferred he was claiming to be the Son of God, and they treated his own words as self-incriminating testimony sufficient to condemn him (22:71). The council hearing produced the verdict; chapter 23 must now produce the execution -- but the council lacks the authority to carry out a death sentence. The case must go to Rome.

**What the author argues immediately after (Luke 24):** Luke 24 opens on the first day of the week. The women who observed the burial (23:55-56) come to the tomb with the spices they prepared and find the stone rolled away and the body absent. Two men in dazzling clothing deliver the interpretive key to the entire passion narrative: "Remember how He spoke to you while He was still in Galilee, saying that the Son of Man must be delivered into the hands of sinful men, and be crucified, and the third day rise again" (24:6-7). On the road to Emmaus, the risen Jesus rebukes the two disciples for being "slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken" and asks: "Was it not necessary (edei) that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into His glory?" (24:26). He then opens the scriptures to them, "beginning with Moses and with all the prophets" (24:27). To the gathered disciples he says: "all things which are written about Me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (24:44), and then: "Thus it is written, that the Christ would suffer and rise again from the dead the third day, and that repentance for forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in His name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem" (24:46-47). The disciples are designated as "witnesses of these things" (24:48). The narrative closes with the ascension from Bethany and the disciples returning to Jerusalem "continually in the temple praising God" (24:53).

**How chapter 23 connects these:** Chapter 22 ended with the council's verdict. Chapter 24 opens with the empty tomb and the risen Jesus explaining that everything that happened was scripturally necessary. Chapter 23 is the event itself -- the suffering, death, and burial that the council's verdict set in motion and that the resurrection will vindicate. Every element in chapter 23 serves a dual function: it narrates what happened and simultaneously demonstrates that what happened fulfills what was written. The charges before Pilate reframe the council's religious verdict into political terms. Pilate's triple declaration of innocence establishes that Jesus dies not because guilt was found in him but because the crowd's will prevailed. The address to the daughters of Jerusalem continues the lament over Jerusalem from 19:41-44 and the warnings of 21:20-24, placing the crucifixion within the trajectory that leads to the city's destruction. The crucifixion itself fulfills Isaiah 53:12, which Jesus quoted in 22:37: he is literally "numbered with transgressors." The criminal who asks to be remembered when Jesus comes in his kingdom receives the promise of paradise "today" -- a word (semeron) that has carried the weight of salvific presence throughout the Gospel. Jesus dies quoting Psalm 31:5, committing his spirit to the Father. The temple curtain tears. A centurion declares him innocent. A council member who did not consent to the verdict buries him. The women observe and prepare spices. Every detail prepares for the interpretive claim of chapter 24: that this death was not the failure of God's plan but its fulfillment.

## Verses 1-5: The Charges Before Pilate

"Then the whole body of them got up and brought Him before Pilate" (23:1).

The phrase "the whole body of them" (hapan to plethos auton) refers to the council assembly of 22:66 -- the presbyterion, the chief priests, and the scribes. The entire governing body rises as a unit to deliver Jesus to the Roman governor. The word plethos ("multitude, body, assembly") emphasizes collective action; this is not a faction but the full institutional leadership of the nation acting together. The verb egagon ("they led, they brought") is the same word used for bringing someone before a court. The transition from the council chamber to Pilate's presence marks the shift from Jewish religious authority, which has rendered its verdict, to Roman imperial authority, which alone possesses the ius gladii -- the right to execute.

Pontius Pilate served as prefect (or procurator, as Tacitus terms the office in Annals 15.44) of Judea from approximately 26 to 36 CE. He was the representative of Roman power in the province, responsible for maintaining order, collecting taxes, and adjudicating capital cases. His residence was normally in Caesarea Maritima, but he came to Jerusalem during festivals to manage the crowds. The trial takes place during Passover, when Jerusalem's population swelled and the risk of unrest was highest.

"And they began to accuse Him, saying, 'We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding to pay taxes to Caesar, and saying that He Himself is Christ, a King'" (23:2).

The charges are three and all are political. This is a deliberate translation of the council's religious verdict into categories that demand Roman attention. The council found Jesus claiming to be the Son of God (22:70-71); before Pilate, this becomes three accusations:

First, "misleading our nation" (diastraphonta to ethnos hemon). The verb diastrepho means "to pervert, to distort, to turn aside." The charge is sedition -- corrupting the people, turning them away from proper loyalty. This is the charge the authorities have feared since 19:48, when "all the people were hanging on His words." Jesus's influence over the crowd is recast as subversion.

Second, "forbidding to pay taxes to Caesar" (kolyonta phorous Kaisari didonai). This is a demonstrable falsehood. In 20:20-26, the spies sent by the scribes and chief priests asked Jesus whether it was lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, specifically hoping "to catch Him in some statement, so that they could deliver Him to the rule and the authority of the governor" (20:20). Jesus's answer was: "Then render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (20:25). The author's reader knows the charge is a fabrication. The very encounter designed to trap Jesus into opposing the tax instead produced a statement affirming it. The accusers now claim the opposite of what Jesus said, in the presence of the very governor to whom they wanted to deliver him. The strategy that failed in chapter 20 is repurposed as a lie in chapter 23.

Third, "saying that He Himself is Christ, a King" (legonta heauton Christon basilea einai). The word Christos ("anointed one") is the Greek rendering of the Hebrew Mashiach. By adding basilea ("a king"), the accusers translate the messianic claim into a direct challenge to Caesar's sovereignty. In the Roman system, claiming kingship in a province was an act of rebellion against the emperor. Tiberius was notoriously suspicious of potential rivals (Tacitus, Annals 1.72; Suetonius, Tiberius 58), and any governor who tolerated such claims risked being reported for negligence. The charge is calculated to force Pilate's hand: if a man claims to be a king and you release him, you are no friend of Caesar.

"Pilate asked Him, saying, 'Are You the King of the Jews?' And He answered him and said, 'It is as you say'" (23:3).

Pilate cuts to the charge that matters to Rome: kingship. "Are You the King of the Jews?" (sy ei ho basileus ton Ioudaion). The pronoun sy ("you") is emphatic -- "you, are you the king?" Jesus's response -- sy legeis ("you say it," or "it is as you say") -- parallels his response to the council in 22:70 (hymeis legete hoti ego eimi, "you say that I am"). In both cases, Jesus neither denies the claim nor affirms it in the terms his questioners intend. The answer acknowledges the truth of the title while distancing Jesus from the political meaning the questioner imports into it. The kingship is real; the nature of the kingship is not what Pilate assumes.

"Then Pilate said to the chief priests and the crowds, 'I find no guilt in this man'" (23:4).

This is the first of three declarations of innocence that Pilate will pronounce (the second in v. 14, the third in v. 22). The phrase ouden heurisko aition en to anthropo touto means "I find no cause [for accusation], no guilt, in this man." The word aition ("cause, basis for a charge, guilt") is a legal term -- Pilate is rendering a judicial verdict: the charges do not hold. After hearing Jesus's single, ambiguous response, Pilate concludes there is no basis for prosecution. The implication is that Pilate perceives no political threat. Whatever Jesus means by kingship, it does not register as sedition in Pilate's assessment.

"But they kept insisting, saying, 'He stirs up the people, teaching throughout all Judea, starting from Galilee even to this place'" (23:5).

The verb epischyon ("they kept insisting, they grew stronger in pressing") indicates escalation -- the accusers intensify their pressure when the first attempt fails. They broaden the charge: Jesus "stirs up" (anaseiei -- "incites, agitates") the people through his teaching, and the scope is comprehensive -- "throughout all Judea, starting from Galilee even to this place." The geographical sweep from Galilee to Jerusalem traces the exact route of Jesus's journey narrative (9:51-19:44). What the author presented as the purposeful journey of God's messenger to the holy city, the accusers recast as a traveling campaign of sedition. The mention of Galilee, however, provides Pilate an opening.

## Verses 6-12: Jesus Before Herod Antipas

"When Pilate heard it, he asked whether the man was a Galilean. And when he learned that He belonged to Herod's jurisdiction, he sent Him to Herod, who himself also was in Jerusalem at that time" (23:6-7).

The mention of Galilee triggers a jurisdictional question. Galilee was not under Pilate's direct authority; it was the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, who ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BCE to 39 CE. Pilate seizes the opportunity to transfer the case. The word exousias ("jurisdiction, authority") is a legal term denoting the sphere within which a ruler's power operates. Herod Antipas happens to be in Jerusalem "at that time" (en tautais tais hemerais) -- presumably for the Passover festival. The transfer is both a political maneuver (Pilate avoids responsibility for a troublesome case) and a courtesy (acknowledging Herod's authority over a Galilean subject).

"Now Herod was very glad when he saw Jesus; for he had wanted to see Him for a long time, because he had been hearing about Him and was hoping to see some sign performed by Him" (23:8).

The author has carefully prepared for this moment. In 9:7-9, Herod heard about Jesus's deeds, was "perplexed" because some said John had risen from the dead, and declared: "I myself had John beheaded; but who is this man about whom I hear such things?" The verse concluded: "And he kept trying to see Him" (ezetei idein auton). In 13:31, Pharisees warned Jesus that "Herod wants to kill You," and Jesus responded by calling Herod "that fox" (13:32). Now, after an entire narrative arc of wanting to see Jesus, Herod finally meets him -- not as a teacher or healer but as a prisoner.

Herod's gladness (echare lian -- "he rejoiced greatly") parallels the authorities' gladness when Judas offered to betray Jesus (22:5, echarēsan). In both cases, joy accompanies an opportunity that is predatory rather than genuine. The word echare shares the root of chara ("joy"), which throughout the Gospel accompanies authentic encounters with God's action (1:14, 2:10, 10:17, 15:7, 10). Herod's joy is a counterfeit: he wants entertainment, not truth. He hopes to see a "sign" (semeion) -- precisely the kind of spectacle Jesus has consistently refused. In 11:29, Jesus declared: "This generation is a wicked generation; it seeks a sign, and yet no sign will be given to it but the sign of Jonah." Herod is the embodiment of the sign-seeking generation.

"And he questioned Him at some length; but He answered him nothing" (23:9).

Jesus's silence before Herod is total. The phrase en logois hikanois ("in considerable words," "at some length") emphasizes the volume of Herod's questioning. Against all that speech, Jesus says nothing. The silence has a prophetic precedent. Isaiah 53:7: "He was oppressed and He was afflicted, yet He did not open His mouth; like a lamb that is led to slaughter, and like a sheep that is silent before its shearers, so He did not open His mouth." The servant of YHWH in Isaiah 53 is characterized by his silence before his oppressors. Jesus spoke to the council (22:67-70) and answered Pilate's question (23:3), but before Herod he is silent. The distinction is significant: the council and Pilate were exercising legitimate judicial authority, however corruptly. Herod is seeking a performance. Jesus will not dignify the curiosity of the man who killed John and was called "that fox."

"And the chief priests and the scribes were standing there, accusing Him vehemently" (23:10).

The accusers have followed Jesus to Herod's hearing and intensify their charges. The adverb eutonos ("vehemently, vigorously") indicates fierce, sustained accusation. The chief priests and scribes are not passive observers; they are actively prosecuting before each successive authority.

"And Herod with his soldiers, after treating Him with contempt and mocking Him, dressed Him in a gorgeous robe and sent Him back to Pilate" (23:11).

The word exouthenesas ("treating with contempt, despising, regarding as nothing") is the same word used in the LXX of Psalm 22:6 (LXX 21:7): "I am a worm and not a man, a reproach of men and despised (exouthenemenos) by the people." The mockery takes a specific form: Herod dresses Jesus in an estheta lampran -- a "bright, splendid, gorgeous robe." The word lampros describes something that gleams or shines; it is used in the LXX and in first-century literature for the clothing of the wealthy and powerful (cf. James 2:2-3, where the man with the "bright clothing" receives preferential treatment). The robe is a parody of royal clothing. Herod, the tetrarch who rules by Rome's permission, dresses the prisoner in mock-royal garments. The reader, who has heard the angelic announcement of the true King's birth (2:11), the acclamation at the entry (19:38), and Jesus's own declaration of enthronement at God's right hand (22:69), understands the irony that Herod does not: the robe mocks what is actually true.

Herod sends Jesus back to Pilate. He does not condemn him, does not sentence him, does not find him guilty. Like Pilate, Herod finds no basis for a death sentence -- a fact Pilate will explicitly cite in verse 15.

"Now Herod and Pilate became friends with one another that very day; for before they had been enemies with each other" (23:12).

This notice is unique to Luke and is not a casual aside. The reconciliation of two rulers through the trial of an innocent man carries a bitter irony. Psalm 2:1-2 reads: "Why are the nations in an uproar and the peoples devising a vain thing? The kings of the earth take their stand and the rulers take counsel together against the LORD and against His Anointed." The apostolic community will explicitly cite this psalm in connection with these events in Acts 4:25-28: "For truly in this city there were gathered together against Your holy servant Jesus, whom You anointed, both Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, to do whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur." The reconciliation of Herod and Pilate -- a Jewish tetrarch and a Roman prefect, previously hostile -- is the fulfillment of Psalm 2's picture: rulers who are enemies with each other unite against the LORD's Anointed. Their friendship is forged over the body of the innocent.

## Verses 13-25: Pilate's Attempt to Release Jesus and the Crowd's Demand

"Pilate summoned the chief priests and the rulers and the people, and said to them, 'You brought this man to me as one who incites the people to rebellion, and behold, having examined Him before you, I have found no guilt in this man regarding the charges which you make against Him. No, nor has Herod, for he sent Him back to us; and behold, nothing deserving death has been done by Him'" (23:13-15).

Pilate reassembles the accusers and adds "the people" (ton laon) to the audience. His statement is a formal judicial summary with three components. First, he restates the charge: "you brought this man to me as one who incites the people to rebellion" (hos apostraphonta ton laon -- "as one turning the people away"). Second, he declares his own finding: "having examined Him before you" (anakrinas enopion hymon -- anakrino is the technical legal term for judicial examination), "I have found no guilt in this man regarding the charges which you make against Him" (ouden heuron en to anthropo touto aition hon kategorite kat' autou). This is the second declaration of innocence. Third, he invokes Herod's finding as corroboration: "nor has Herod" (all' oude Herodes). Two independent authorities, one Jewish and one Roman, have examined the prisoner and found nothing warranting death.

"Therefore I will punish Him and release Him" (23:16).

The verb paideusas ("having disciplined, having chastised") refers to flogging -- a lesser punishment that Pilate offers as a compromise. The logic is remarkable in its injustice: Pilate has declared Jesus innocent twice and cited Herod's agreement, and yet he proposes to punish him anyway. The flogging is not justice but politics -- an attempt to satisfy the accusers with a partial penalty while avoiding a capital sentence for which there is no legal basis.

"But they cried out all together, saying, 'Away with this man, and release for us Barabbas!' (He was one who had been thrown into prison for an insurrection made in the city, and for murder)" (23:18-19).

The crowd's response is unanimous: "all together" (pamplethei -- the entire multitude, with one voice). They demand two things: the removal of Jesus and the release of Barabbas. The author provides Barabbas's credentials in a parenthetical note: he was imprisoned "for an insurrection made in the city" (dia stasin tina genomenen en te polei) "and for murder" (kai phonon). The word stasis means "insurrection, uprising, revolt" -- exactly the kind of political agitation that the accusers falsely charged Jesus with. The irony is precise and devastating. Jesus, who told people to render to Caesar what is Caesar's and whose kingship Pilate found non-threatening, is condemned as a seditionist. Barabbas, an actual insurrectionist and murderer, is released. The crowd chooses the violent revolutionary and rejects the one whose response to violence was to heal the severed ear of an enemy's servant (22:51).

The name Barabbas (bar-Abba) means "son of the father." The crowd demands the release of the son of the father and the death of the one whom the heavenly voice called "My beloved Son" (3:22) and whom the council extracted the confession of being "the Son of God" (22:70).

"Pilate, wanting to release Jesus, addressed them again, but they kept on calling out, saying, 'Crucify, crucify Him!'" (23:20-21).

The author emphasizes Pilate's desire to release Jesus (thelōn apolusai ton Iesoun -- "wanting, wishing to release"). Pilate's will is clear; his authority to act on it is overwhelmed by the crowd. The verb epephonoun ("they kept on calling out") is imperfect tense -- continuous, repeated shouting. The demand is stark: staurou staurou auton -- "crucify, crucify Him." The repetition conveys the mob's relentless intensity. Crucifixion was the Roman penalty reserved for slaves, rebels, and the worst criminals. It was designed to be maximally painful and publicly humiliating -- a spectacle of Roman power over those who defied it. Cicero called it "the cruelest and most hideous of punishments" (In Verrem 2.5.64-65).

"And he said to them the third time, 'Why, what evil has this man done? I have found no guilt in Him deserving death; therefore I will punish Him and release Him'" (23:22).

The third declaration of innocence. Pilate's question -- ti gar kakon epoiesen houtos ("What evil has this man done?") -- goes unanswered. No crime is specified because none exists. The three declarations form a structure that the author clearly intends the reader to notice: three times the Roman judge finds no guilt, just as Peter denied three times. The triple form is the emphatic form of legal testimony -- what is established by two or three witnesses stands confirmed (Deuteronomy 19:15). Pilate's triple pronouncement of innocence establishes beyond question that Jesus's death is not a just penalty for a proven crime. It is a judicial murder.

"But they were insistent, with loud voices asking that He be crucified. And their voices began to prevail. And Pilate pronounced sentence that their demand be granted" (23:23-24).

The verb katischyon ("they prevailed, they were gaining strength") is the key: the crowd's voices overpowered Pilate's judgment. The word epekrinon ("he pronounced sentence, he decided, he gave his verdict") is a judicial term -- Pilate renders a formal decision. But the content of the decision is to grant "their demand" (to aitema auton). The sentence is not based on evidence or law but on the volume of the crowd's insistence. Pilate has abdicated his judicial role. The governor who had the authority to release Jesus instead ratifies the mob's demand.

"And he released the man they were asking for who had been thrown into prison for insurrection and murder, but he delivered Jesus to their will" (23:25).

The author restates Barabbas's crimes -- insurrection and murder -- one final time, ensuring the reader cannot miss the exchange. The phrase paredoken de ton Iesoun to thelemati auton ("he delivered Jesus to their will") is loaded. The verb paradidomi ("to hand over, to deliver") is the same word used for Judas's betrayal (22:4, 6, 21, 22, 48) and for the Son of Man being "delivered into the hands of sinful men" (24:7). The chain of handing over runs from Judas to the council to Pilate, and now Pilate hands Jesus to the will (thelema) of the crowd. The word thelema ("will") contrasts with the prayer of 22:42: "not My will (thelema) but Yours (the Father's) be done." Jesus submitted his will to the Father's; Pilate submits his judgment to the crowd's will. Two acts of surrender, opposite in character.

## Verses 26-31: Simon of Cyrene and the Warning to the Daughters of Jerusalem

"When they led Him away, they seized a man, Simon of Cyrene, coming in from the country, and placed on him the cross to carry behind Jesus" (23:26).

The verb epilabomenoi ("having seized, having laid hold of") indicates compulsion -- Simon is pressed into service, not volunteering. Cyrene was a city in North Africa (modern Libya) with a substantial Jewish community (Acts 2:10, 6:9, 11:20, 13:1). Simon is "coming in from the country" (erchomenon ap' agrou), meaning he is arriving from outside the city. The author specifies that Simon carried the cross "behind Jesus" (opisthen tou Iesoun). The phrase opisthen carries significance in light of Jesus's teaching on discipleship: "If anyone wishes to come after Me (opiso mou), he must deny himself, and take up his cross daily and follow Me" (9:23). Simon literally does what Jesus described figuratively: he takes up the cross and carries it behind Jesus. Whether Simon is a disciple or a bystander, his action enacts the image of cross-bearing that Jesus used to define what it means to follow him.

"And following Him was a large multitude of the people, and of women who were mourning and lamenting Him" (23:27).

The procession includes a large crowd, and Luke specifically identifies women (gynaikes) among them who are "mourning and lamenting" (ekoptonto kai ethrēnoun). The verb kopto ("to beat one's breast, to mourn") and thrēneo ("to wail, to lament") are the standard vocabulary of public mourning in the ancient world and in the LXX (cf. Zechariah 12:10-14, where mourning is described for "the one whom they have pierced"). The women's lamentation treats Jesus as already dead -- the mourning has begun before the execution.

"But Jesus turning to them said, 'Daughters of Jerusalem, stop weeping for Me, but weep for yourselves and for your children'" (23:28).

Jesus addresses them as "Daughters of Jerusalem" (thygateres Ierousalem) -- a phrase from the Song of Songs (1:5, 2:7, 3:5, 5:8, 8:4) that denotes the women of the city as a collective. The command is startling: he redirects their grief. "Stop weeping for Me" (me klaiete ep' eme) -- the tears are misplaced. The one being led to crucifixion tells the mourners that they, not he, are the ones who need weeping. "Weep for yourselves and for your children" (eph' heautas klaiete kai epi ta tekna hymon). The children are explicitly included, extending the horizon of what is coming beyond the present generation.

This warning directly continues two earlier passages. In 19:41-44, Jesus himself wept over Jerusalem: "If you had known in this day, even you, the things which make for peace! But now they have been hidden from your eyes. For the days will come upon you when your enemies will throw up a barricade against you, and surround you and hem you in on every side, and they will level you to the ground and your children within you" (19:42-44). In 21:20-24, Jesus predicted: "When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then recognize that her desolation is near... Woe to those who are pregnant and to those who nurse babies in those days; for there will be great distress upon the land and wrath to this people" (21:23). The daughters of Jerusalem passage connects the crucifixion to the coming destruction of the city. The same trajectory that produces Jesus's death will produce Jerusalem's desolation.

"For behold, the days are coming when they will say, 'Blessed are the barren, and the wombs that never bore, and the breasts that never nursed'" (23:29).

In Israel's tradition, barrenness was a source of shame and sorrow (1 Samuel 1:5-8, Genesis 30:1, Isaiah 54:1). To call the barren "blessed" (makariai) is to invert the deepest values of the culture. The only circumstance in which childlessness becomes a blessing is when having children means watching them suffer and die. Jesus is describing a catastrophe so severe that the normal human desire for progeny becomes a curse. The passage echoes Hosea 9:14, where the prophet, in the context of judgment on Israel, cries out: "Give them, O LORD -- what will You give? Give them a miscarrying womb and dry breasts." What Hosea asked as a plea for mercy in judgment -- that at least there would be no children to suffer -- Jesus predicts as a reality.

"Then they will begin to say to the mountains, 'Fall on us,' and to the hills, 'Cover us'" (23:30).

The quotation is from Hosea 10:8: "They will say to the mountains, 'Cover us!' and to the hills, 'Fall on us!'" In Hosea, this is the response of Israel to God's judgment upon the high places of Aven (Beth-aven, a name mocking Bethel) -- when the places of idolatrous worship are destroyed, the people prefer burial under the mountains to facing what comes. The citation places what is coming upon Jerusalem in the same category as the prophetic judgments on Israel's historical sins. The destruction of 70 CE is not a new kind of event; it is the same kind of divine response to covenant failure that the prophets described, now directed at the generation that rejected its Messiah.

"For if they do these things when the tree is green, what will happen when it is dry?" (23:31).

The proverb contrasts a green (living, moist) tree with a dry one. If "they" -- the Romans, or the power structures at work in this moment -- do this to the green tree (a living, innocent person, bearing fruit), what will they do to the dry tree (a guilty, fruitless city ripe for burning)? The image of Israel as a tree is deeply rooted in prophetic literature. Ezekiel 17 presents Israel as a vine that was transplanted and grew. Isaiah 5:1-7 describes Israel as a vineyard that produced bad fruit and was given over to destruction. Jesus himself cursed the barren fig tree in the parallel tradition and told the parable of the fig tree given one more year to bear fruit (13:6-9). The green tree/dry tree proverb says: if innocence receives this treatment, guilt will receive far worse. If Rome crucifies the righteous one, what will Rome do to the rebellious city? The answer came in 70 CE.

## Verses 32-38: The Crucifixion

"Two others also, who were criminals, were being led away to be put to death with Him" (23:32).

The word kakourgoi ("criminals, evildoers" -- literally, "workers of evil") identifies Jesus's companions in execution. The detail fulfills what Jesus himself declared in 22:37, quoting Isaiah 53:12: "He was numbered with transgressors" (meta anomon elogisthe). The Hebrew of Isaiah 53:12 reads: "He poured out Himself to death, and was numbered with the transgressors; yet He Himself bore the sin of many, and interceded for the transgressors." Jesus is literally counted among the criminals -- placed between them, executed by the same method, subjected to the same public degradation. What Isaiah wrote as prophecy and Jesus cited as imminent destiny is now enacted.

"When they came to the place called The Skull, there they crucified Him and the criminals, one on the right and the other on the left" (23:33).

The location is identified only by its name: Kranion ("Skull"), the Greek equivalent of the Aramaic Golgotha. Luke does not explain the name or linger on the geography. The positioning -- one criminal on the right, one on the left -- creates a visual and symbolic tableau: Jesus is at the center, flanked by two who represent the two possible responses to him, as the exchange in verses 39-43 will demonstrate.

Crucifixion itself needs to be understood as a first-century audience would understand it. The victim was typically stripped and nailed or bound to a wooden cross, then lifted up for public display. Death came slowly, often over days, through a combination of shock, exposure, dehydration, and asphyxiation as the body's weight made breathing increasingly difficult. It was a death designed for maximum shame: the victim was naked, helpless, publicly visible, and unable to control any bodily function. In the Jewish context, Deuteronomy 21:23 declared: "cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree" (LXX: kekataramenos hypo theou pas kremamenos epi xylou). A crucified person bore not only Roman condemnation but the scriptural curse. The theological weight of this detail will be explored by Paul (Galatians 3:13), but the Lukan audience would already feel the tension: the one declared to be the Son of God at baptism (3:22) and by the council's own inference (22:70) now hangs under the deuteronomic curse.

"But Jesus was saying, 'Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing.' And they cast lots, dividing up His garments among themselves" (23:34).

Jesus's first words from the cross are a prayer of intercession. He addresses God as "Father" (Pater) -- the same intimate address used in the Mount of Olives prayer (22:42) and the prayer model (11:2). The prayer asks forgiveness (aphes autois -- "release them, let them go") for those who are executing him. The pronoun "them" (autois) most immediately refers to the soldiers carrying out the crucifixion, but within the author's larger frame it encompasses all who are complicit: the council, Pilate, Herod, the crowd. The stated reason -- "they do not know what they are doing" (ou gar oidasin ti poiousin) -- does not excuse the act but identifies ignorance as a mitigating factor. In Acts, Peter will echo this to the people of Jerusalem: "I know that you acted in ignorance, just as your rulers did also" (Acts 3:17). The intercessory prayer from the cross fulfills the final clause of Isaiah 53:12: "He... interceded for the transgressors" (LXX: kai dia tas anomias auton paredothe -- "he was delivered up because of their transgressions"). The same Isaiah 53:12 that was quoted in 22:37 ("numbered with transgressors") now has its full scope enacted: numbered with transgressors and interceding for them.

The division of garments fulfills Psalm 22:18 (LXX 21:19): "They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots" (diemerisanto ta himatia mou heautois kai epi ton himatismon mou ebalon kleron). The author notes this fulfillment without quoting the psalm directly; the reader steeped in Israel's scriptures would recognize the echo. Psalm 22 is the psalm of the righteous sufferer who cries out to God in abandonment and is vindicated. Its imagery pervades the crucifixion accounts across the tradition.

"And the people stood by, looking on. And even the rulers were sneering at Him, saying, 'He saved others; let Him save Himself if this is the Christ of God, His Chosen One'" (23:35).

The author distinguishes two groups. The people (ho laos) stand and watch -- the verb theoron ("looking on, observing") suggests attentive watching without active participation. The rulers (hoi archontes), by contrast, sneer (exemykterizon -- "turned up their noses at, derided, treated with contempt"). The verb is strong and visceral; it appears in the LXX of Psalm 22:7 (LXX 21:8): "All who see me sneer at me" (exemykterisan me).

The content of the mockery has a precise structure: "He saved others" (allous esosen) -- they acknowledge, even in mockery, that Jesus performed acts of salvation/healing. The verb sozo means both "to save" and "to heal" throughout the Gospel (7:50, 8:36, 8:48, 8:50, 17:19, 18:42). The rulers concede the reality of what Jesus did for others. The taunt is: let him now do it for himself. "If this is the Christ of God, His Chosen One" (ei houtos estin ho Christos tou theou ho eklektos) -- the title "Chosen One" (eklektos) echoes the voice at the transfiguration: "This is My Son, My Chosen One; listen to Him" (9:35). The rulers unknowingly use the very title heaven bestowed. Their taunt, like the guards' demand that he "prophesy" in 22:64, employs language that is truer than they know.

The structure of the mockery -- "save yourself" -- echoes the wilderness testing of chapter 4. There, the devil challenged Jesus to use his power for self-preservation: turn stones to bread for yourself, throw yourself down and let angels catch you, take the kingdoms for yourself. At every point, Jesus refused to exercise power for his own benefit. The cross is the final form of the same test: use your power to save yourself. The answer is the same: he will not.

"The soldiers also mocked Him, coming up to Him, offering Him sour wine, and saying, 'If You are the King of the Jews, save Yourself'" (23:36-37).

The soldiers' mockery parallels the rulers' but shifts the title: where the rulers said "Christ of God, His Chosen One," the soldiers say "King of the Jews" -- the political title from Pilate's interrogation (23:3). Each group mocks with the categories relevant to their own sphere: the Jewish leaders use religious titles; the Roman soldiers use the political one. The sour wine (oxos) offered echoes Psalm 69:21 (LXX 68:22): "They also gave me gall for my food, and for my thirst they gave me vinegar (oxos) to drink." The gesture may have been a cruel parody of refreshment, though Roman soldiers routinely drank posca (diluted vinegar wine), and the offering may have been partly practical. The author presents it as part of the mockery.

"Now there was also an inscription above Him, 'THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS'" (23:38).

The titulus -- the placard stating the charge -- was standard Roman practice. The condemned person's crime was written on a board and posted above or carried before them. The inscription houtos estin ho basileus ton Ioudaion ("This is the King of the Jews") states the charge for which Jesus was executed: claiming kingship. The irony, which runs through the entire chapter, is that the charge is true. He is the King of the Jews -- the Anointed One, the one to whom dominion and glory and a kingdom were given (Daniel 7:14), the one seated at the right hand of the power of God (22:69). The instrument of Roman condemnation becomes an inadvertent proclamation. What Pilate meant as a criminal's charge, the author presents as an enthronement announcement.

## Verses 39-43: The Two Criminals

"One of the criminals who were hanged there was hurling abuse at Him, saying, 'Are You not the Christ? Save Yourself and us!'" (23:39).

The verb eblasphemei ("was blaspheming, was hurling abuse") is the same word the author used for the guards' mockery in 22:65 (blasphemountes). The criminal's demand mirrors the rulers' taunt precisely: "save yourself" (soson seauton). He adds "and us" (kai hemas), turning the taunt into a demand for physical rescue. The conditional "if You are the Christ" (ouchi sy ei ho Christos) uses the particle ouchi, which expects an affirmative answer: "You are the Christ, aren't you?" Even in his abuse, the criminal uses the messianic title. His concept of messiahship, however, is identical to the tempter's in chapter 4 and the rulers' in verse 35: the Messiah should use his power for self-preservation and immediate rescue.

"But the other answered, and rebuking him said, 'Do you not even fear God, since you are under the same sentence of condemnation? And we indeed are suffering justly, for we are receiving what we deserve for our deeds; but this man has done nothing wrong'" (23:40-41).

The second criminal's response introduces a dramatic reversal. He begins with a rebuke (epitimon -- the same verb Jesus used when rebuking demons, 4:35, 41; rebuking the wind, 8:24; and rebuking the disciples, 9:55). The rebuke is theological: "Do you not even fear God?" (oude phobe ton theon). The fear of God -- reverence for the divine, the recognition that human judgment is not final -- is the foundation of wisdom in Israel's tradition (Proverbs 1:7, 9:10; Psalm 111:10). The criminal invokes this foundation as the basis for a different response to shared suffering.

He then makes a confession that functions as a fifth declaration of Jesus's innocence (after Pilate's three and Herod's implicit finding): "this man has done nothing wrong" (houtos ouden atopon epraxen). The word atopon means "out of place, improper, wrong" -- a broad term covering any kind of wrongdoing. The criminal's assessment matches Pilate's judicial finding: Jesus is innocent. But the criminal goes further than Pilate. Pilate declared Jesus legally not guilty; the criminal draws a moral distinction: "we are receiving what we deserve for our deeds" (axia gar hon epraxamen apolambanomen) -- our punishment is just. "But this man" (houtos de) -- this man is different. The criminal recognizes the gap between what justice demands for him and what is being done to Jesus. He names what the entire chapter has been demonstrating: Jesus's death is not justice.

"And he was saying, 'Jesus, remember me when You come in Your kingdom!'" (23:42).

The request is remarkable on multiple levels. First, the criminal addresses Jesus by name -- Iesou -- without any title, in the intimate form of direct address. Second, he asks to be "remembered" (mnestheti mou). The word mimnesko ("to remember") in the LXX is the language of God's covenantal remembrance: "God remembered (emnesthē) His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (Exodus 2:24); "He has remembered (emnēsthē) His lovingkindness and His faithfulness to the house of Israel" (Psalm 98:3, LXX 97:3). Hannah prayed that God would "remember" her (1 Samuel 1:11). Nehemiah asked God to "remember" him for good (Nehemiah 13:31). To ask Jesus to "remember" him is to address Jesus with the kind of appeal that is properly directed to God.

Third, the phrase "when You come in Your kingdom" (hotan elthes en te basileia sou -- or eis ten basileian sou, "into Your kingdom," in some manuscripts) is an astonishing statement of faith. The criminal is looking at a man who is naked, nailed to wood, dying by the same method he himself is dying -- and he sees a king. He does not ask for rescue. He does not demand a sign. He asks for a place in a kingdom that is, from every visible indication, nonexistent. The criminal sees what the rulers, the soldiers, and the first criminal cannot see: that the man on the middle cross possesses a kingdom, and that this kingdom has a future beyond the cross.

"And He said to him, 'Truly I say to you, today you shall be with Me in Paradise'" (23:43).

Jesus's response exceeds the request. The criminal asked to be remembered at some future point when the kingdom arrives. Jesus answers with three elements.

First, "today" (semeron). This word is a structural pillar of the Gospel. At the annunciation to the shepherds: "today (semeron) in the city of David there has been born for you a Savior, who is Christ the Lord" (2:11). At the Nazareth synagogue: "today (semeron) this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (4:21). To Zacchaeus: "today (semeron) salvation has come to this house" (19:9). At the Last Supper: "the rooster will not crow today (semeron) until you have denied three times that you know Me" (22:34). In every instance, semeron carries the force of God's action breaking into the present moment. What is promised is not deferred but enacted now. The criminal asked for a future remembrance; Jesus gives a present reality.

Second, "with Me" (met' emou). The criminal asked to be remembered by Jesus; Jesus promises presence with him. The distinction is between being recalled by an absent king and being accompanied by a present one. The preposition meta ("with") denotes personal company, shared location, togetherness.

Third, "in Paradise" (en to paradeiso). The word paradeisos appears only three times in the New Testament (here, 2 Corinthians 12:4, Revelation 2:7). In the LXX, paradeisos translates the Hebrew gan ("garden") and is used for the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:8-10, LXX: paradeison en Edem). In Second Temple Jewish literature, paradise became associated with the place of the righteous dead -- the garden-like realm where the faithful await resurrection (1 Enoch 32:3, 2 Esdras 4:7-8, Testament of Levi 18:10-11). Jesus promises the criminal not merely future remembrance but present entry into the place of divine blessing, and the means of entry is not the criminal's merit but Jesus's company: "with Me."

The two criminals present the two responses to Jesus that have run throughout the Gospel. One demands that Jesus prove himself by acting according to the mocker's expectations. The other recognizes who Jesus is despite all contrary evidence, confesses his own guilt, acknowledges Jesus's innocence, and asks for mercy. The first criminal mirrors the sign-seeking generation of 11:29; the second mirrors the tax collector of 18:13, who "was not even willing to lift up his eyes to heaven, but was beating his breast, saying, 'God, be merciful to me, the sinner!'" Jesus declared that the tax collector "went to his house justified" (18:14). The criminal on the cross receives the same verdict in even more dramatic terms: not merely justified, but promised paradise today.

## Verses 44-49: The Death of Jesus

"It was now about the sixth hour, and darkness fell over the whole land until the ninth hour, because the sun was obscured" (23:44-45a).

The sixth hour is noon; the ninth hour is three in the afternoon. Darkness (skotos) covers the entire land (eph' holen ten gen) for three hours at the height of day. The word gen can mean "earth" or "land" -- in this context, "the whole land" of Israel is the more likely reference, though the author may intend cosmic scope.

The darkness has deep prophetic roots. Amos 8:9-10: "It will come about in that day, declares the Lord GOD, that I will make the sun go down at noon and make the earth dark in broad daylight. Then I will turn your festivals into mourning and all your songs into lamentation." Amos describes darkness at noon during a festival as the sign of God's judgment -- and the crucifixion occurs during Passover, Israel's greatest festival. The correspondence is exact: the sun goes down at noon, and the festival becomes mourning. Joel 2:31 predicted: "The sun will be turned into darkness and the moon into blood before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes." Exodus 10:21-23 records the plague of darkness over Egypt before the first Passover -- three days of thick darkness that could be felt, while the Israelites had light in their dwellings. The Passover connection is significant: at the original Passover, darkness fell upon those under judgment while the people of God had light. Now, at this Passover, darkness falls over the whole land as the Passover lamb (22:7) is being slain.

The phrase "the sun was obscured" (tou heliou eklipontos) uses the verb ekleipo ("to fail, to give out, to be eclipsed") -- the same verb used in 22:32 for the prayer that Peter's faith "may not fail" (ekleipe). The sun's light fails at the moment of Jesus's death, just as Peter's faith nearly failed at the moment of his denial. The verbal echo connects the cosmic darkness to the spiritual darkness of the preceding chapter.

"And the veil of the temple was torn in two" (23:45b).

The katapetasma tou naou ("the curtain of the temple") refers to the inner curtain that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (the Holy of Holies) -- the chamber where the Ark of the Covenant once stood and where God's presence was understood to dwell (Exodus 26:31-34). Only the high priest entered through this curtain, and only once a year, on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), carrying the blood of sacrifice (Leviticus 16:2, 12-15). The curtain was the boundary between the accessible and the inaccessible, between human space and divine space.

The verb eschisthe ("was torn, was split") is passive -- the curtain did not deteriorate; it was torn. The agent is not named, but the passive in this context implies divine action. God tore the curtain. The tearing has at least two dimensions in the author's framework. First, access: the barrier between God's presence and the people is removed. What required priestly mediation and annual ritual is now open. Second, departure: in Ezekiel 10-11, the glory of God left the first temple before its destruction, moving from the inner sanctuary to the threshold, then to the east gate, then to the Mount of Olives (Ezekiel 11:23). The tearing of the curtain may signal a similar departure -- God's presence leaving the temple, which Jesus has already declared will be destroyed (21:6). The curtain no longer separates because the temple's function has ended. The author places this event before Jesus's death, not after -- the curtain tears while Jesus is still alive, linking the temple's symbolic end to the cross.

"And Jesus, crying out with a loud voice, said, 'Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit.' Having said this, He breathed His last" (23:46).

Jesus's final words are from Psalm 31:5 (LXX 30:6): "Into Your hand I commit my spirit" (eis cheiras sou parathēsomai to pneuma mou). The LXX text uses paratithēmi ("to entrust, to deposit, to commit for safekeeping"), the same verb used for entrusting something valuable to another's keeping. Jesus adds the address "Father" (Pater), personalizing the psalm's prayer.

The choice of this psalm is significant in comparison with the tradition. Mark and Matthew record Jesus crying out Psalm 22:1: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" -- the opening of the psalm of abandonment that moves toward vindication. Luke omits this cry and instead presents Jesus dying with Psalm 31 -- a psalm of trust in persecution. Psalm 31 is the prayer of one who is surrounded by enemies (31:4, 8, 13, 15), slandered and conspired against (31:13, 20), but who trusts God to deliver him: "Into Your hand I commit my spirit; You have ransomed me, O LORD, God of truth" (31:5). The psalm's context is remarkably apt: plots, conspiracy, enemies, false accusations -- and through it all, the psalmist's trust in God's faithfulness. Jesus dies not in the language of abandonment but in the language of entrustment. He places his spirit (pneuma) into the Father's hands (cheiras) -- the same hands from which the power of darkness cannot snatch it.

The phrase exepneusen ("He breathed His last," literally "He breathed out, He expired") uses the verb ekpneo, which appears only in the death accounts of Jesus in the Gospels. The simplicity of the statement is striking after the cosmic signs and the torn curtain. The Son of God dies.

"Now when the centurion saw what had happened, he began praising God, saying, 'Certainly this man was innocent'" (23:47).

The centurion (hekatontarchos) is a Roman military officer commanding approximately eighty men. His reaction has two parts. First, he praises God (edoxazen ton theon -- "he glorified God"). This is the response of those who witness God's action throughout the Gospel: the shepherds glorified God (2:20), the people glorified God after healings (5:25-26, 7:16, 13:13, 17:15, 18:43). A Roman soldier sees what has happened at the cross and his response is to glorify the God of Israel.

Second, he declares: ontos ho anthropos houtos dikaios en -- "Certainly this man was righteous/innocent." The word dikaios carries both meanings: "righteous" (morally upright, in right relationship with God) and "innocent" (legally blameless). In the Lukan context, both apply. Where the parallel in Mark has the centurion say "Truly this man was the Son of God" (Mark 15:39), Luke has "this man was righteous." The word dikaios connects to the author's established vocabulary: Simeon was "righteous (dikaios) and devout" (2:25); Joseph of Arimathea will be described as "good and righteous" (agathos kai dikaios, 23:50). The centurion's declaration is the climactic statement of innocence in the chapter -- the fourth (after Pilate's three, and alongside the criminal's assessment in v. 41). A Gentile soldier, the agent of the execution, pronounces the verdict that the Jewish council refused: this man was dikaios.

"And all the crowds who came together for this spectacle, when they observed what had happened, began to return, beating their breasts" (23:48).

The people who gathered to watch (synparagnomenoi) the spectacle (theoria -- "spectacle, sight") respond to what they have seen. They return home "beating their breasts" (typtomenoi ta stethe). Breast-beating is the sign of mourning and self-reproach (cf. 18:13, where the tax collector "was beating his breast, saying, 'God, be merciful to me'"). The crowd's response suggests grief, possibly conviction -- they came to watch an execution and they leave in mourning. The same people whose voices "prevailed" in demanding crucifixion (23:23) now beat their breasts at what their demand produced.

"And all His acquaintances and the women who accompanied Him from Galilee were standing at a distance, seeing these things" (23:49).

The word gnostoi ("acquaintances, those who knew him") and the Galilean women form the witness circle. They stand "at a distance" (apo makrothen) -- the same phrase used of Peter following "at a distance" in 22:54. The women who accompanied Jesus from Galilee have been mentioned in 8:1-3: "Mary who was called Magdalene... Joanna the wife of Chuza, Herod's steward, and Susanna, and many others who were contributing to their support out of their private means." These women, who provided for Jesus during his ministry, now stand as witnesses to his death. Their role as observers is critical for the narrative: they will see where the body is laid (23:55) and return with spices (23:56-24:1), providing the continuity of witness from death to burial to empty tomb.

## Verses 50-56: The Burial

"And a man named Joseph, who was a member of the Council, a good and righteous man (he had not consented to their plan and action), a man from Arimathea, a city of the Jews, who was waiting for the kingdom of God; this man went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus" (23:50-52).

The author's description of Joseph is layered and deliberate. Five things are said about him:

First, he is "a member of the Council" (bouleutes huparchon) -- a member of the Sanhedrin, the very body that condemned Jesus. He is an insider to the institution that produced the death sentence.

Second, he is "good and righteous" (agathos kai dikaios). The word dikaios is the same word the centurion just used for Jesus (v. 47). Joseph is characterized by the same quality as the man he is about to bury. The author places a dikaios man in the role of caring for the body of the dikaios man.

Third -- and the author inserts this parenthetically, as though it must not be missed -- "he had not consented to their plan and action" (houtos ouk en synkatatetheimenos te boule kai te praxei auton). The verb synkatatithemi means "to agree together, to consent, to vote with." Joseph stood against the council's decision. He was present (or at least a member) but did not join the consensus. In 22:71, the council said "what further need do we have of testimony?" as though the verdict were unanimous. Joseph's dissent reveals it was not.

Fourth, he is "from Arimathea, a city of the Jews" (apo Arimathaias poleos ton Ioudaion). The identification places him geographically -- Arimathea is commonly identified with Ramathaim-zophim, the birthplace of Samuel (1 Samuel 1:1). He is from a specifically Jewish city, a man of Israel.

Fifth, he "was waiting for the kingdom of God" (prosdechomenos ten basileian tou theou). The verb prosdechomai ("to wait for, to look forward to, to expect") is the same verb used of Simeon in 2:25: "this man was righteous and devout, looking for (prosdechomenos) the consolation of Israel." Joseph shares Simeon's posture: expectant, waiting for God to act. He is aligned with the faithful remnant within Israel -- those who await what God has promised rather than defending what the institution has built.

Joseph's act of requesting the body from Pilate is socially and politically dangerous. He is a council member publicly associating himself with a condemned criminal, claiming the body that Roman practice normally left to hang or disposed of in a common grave. He is taking a visible stand against his own institution's decision.

"And he took it down and wrapped it in a linen cloth, and laid Him in a tomb cut into the rock, where no one had ever lain" (23:53).

Joseph personally handles the body -- he takes it down (kathelōn) from the cross, wraps it in a sindon (a linen cloth used for burial wrapping), and places it in a rock-cut tomb. The detail "where no one had ever lain" (hou ouk en oudeis oupo keimenos) establishes that the tomb is new, unused. There can be no confusion about whose body is (or is not) in the tomb when the women return.

"It was the preparation day, and the Sabbath was about to begin" (23:54).

The word paraskeuē ("preparation") is the technical term for the day before the Sabbath -- Friday, the day when all preparations for the day of rest had to be completed before sunset. The phrase sabbaton epephosken ("the Sabbath was dawning") uses an unusual verb -- epiphōskō literally means "to dawn, to grow light," but in Jewish reckoning the new day began at sunset. The verb may be used loosely, or it may reflect the practice of beginning the Sabbath by lighting lamps at dusk (the Sabbath "dawned" with its lights). The urgency is practical: the Sabbath is imminent, and burial must be completed before it begins.

"Now the women who had come with Him out of Galilee followed, and saw the tomb and how His body was laid" (23:55).

The Galilean women from verse 49 follow Joseph to the tomb. The author emphasizes what they saw: the tomb itself (to mnemeion) and the manner in which the body was placed (hos etethe to soma autou). They are eyewitnesses to the burial location and the condition of the body. This detail is not incidental -- it establishes that when these same women return in 24:1, they know exactly where to go, and they know what they placed there. Any alternative explanation for the empty tomb must account for their direct observation.

"Then they returned and prepared spices and perfumes. And on the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment" (23:56).

The women prepare aromatic spices (aromata) and perfumes (myra) for the body -- the customary treatment of the dead to honor them and delay decomposition. The final note is significant: "on the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment" (to men sabbaton hesychasan kata ten entolen). Even in the extremity of grief, even with the body of their teacher lying in a borrowed tomb, they observe the Torah's Sabbath command (Exodus 20:8-11, Deuteronomy 5:12-15). The commandment still governs. The law is not set aside by the crucifixion -- at least, not yet. These women live as observant Jews, and the author records their obedience without comment. The spices will have to wait until the first day of the week.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **The trial before Pilate exposes the charges as fabrications built on a true claim.** The accusers charge Jesus with misleading the nation, forbidding taxes to Caesar, and claiming to be Christ, a King. The first two are false -- Jesus has done the opposite. The third is true but not in the sense the accusers intend or Pilate would understand. The entire trial operates in the gap between what "King" means to Rome and what it means in the framework of Daniel 7 and Psalm 110. Pilate senses the gap and finds no guilt; the accusers exploit the ambiguity to force a conviction.

2. **Pilate's triple declaration of innocence establishes that Jesus's death is not justice.** Three times the Roman governor states that Jesus has done nothing deserving death (vv. 4, 14, 22). Herod's failure to condemn adds a fourth implicit finding. The criminal's confession adds a fifth (v. 41). The centurion's declaration adds a sixth (v. 47). The author constructs an overwhelming consensus of innocence from every angle -- Jewish ruler (Herod), Roman governor (Pilate), fellow condemned (the criminal), Roman soldier (the centurion) -- precisely to demonstrate that what kills Jesus is not evidence of guilt but the overpowering of justice by collective will (v. 23, "their voices prevailed").

3. **The Herod episode fulfills the narrative thread of the seeking ruler and the Psalm 2 pattern.** Herod wanted to see Jesus since 9:9. When he finally does, he seeks entertainment, receives silence, and joins in mockery. His reconciliation with Pilate over the body of the innocent enacts Psalm 2:1-2: kings and rulers conspiring together against the LORD's Anointed. The apostolic community in Acts will explicitly interpret these events through Psalm 2 (Acts 4:25-28).

4. **The address to the daughters of Jerusalem places the crucifixion on the trajectory toward Jerusalem's destruction.** Jesus redirects the women's grief from himself to themselves and their children, continuing the lament of 19:41-44 and the warnings of 21:20-24. The green tree/dry tree proverb connects the treatment of the innocent Jesus to the coming treatment of the guilty city. The crucifixion is not an isolated judicial event; it is part of the sequence that ends in 70 CE.

5. **The crucifixion fulfills Isaiah 53 comprehensively.** Jesus quoted Isaiah 53:12 in 22:37 ("numbered with transgressors"), and the chapter enacts the rest of the verse: "He poured out Himself to death" (the crucifixion); "was numbered with the transgressors" (crucified between two criminals, v. 33); "bore the sin of many" (the silence, the suffering, the cup accepted in 22:42); "and interceded for the transgressors" (the prayer of forgiveness, v. 34). The servant song's portrait of vicarious suffering is narrated as historical event.

6. **The two criminals present the two responses to Jesus that define the Gospel's entire moral landscape.** One demands that Jesus prove his messiahship through self-rescue; the other recognizes Jesus's innocence and kingship through the evidence of the cross itself. The first criminal represents the sign-seeking demand that Jesus has rejected throughout the narrative (4:1-13, 11:29). The second represents the faith that sees what is not visible and receives what is not deserved -- the same faith as the tax collector (18:13-14) and Zacchaeus (19:1-10). The promise of paradise "today" extends the Lukan pattern of semeron as the moment of God's salvific action.

7. **The temple curtain's tearing signals the end of the temple's mediatorial function.** The curtain that separated God's presence from the people is torn from within. Whether this represents the opening of access (God now available without priestly mediation) or the departure of presence (God leaving the temple that will be destroyed), or both, the symbolic force is the same: the temple system as the locus of divine-human encounter has reached its terminus. What the curtain guarded is now either universally accessible or no longer there. The prediction of 21:6 ("not one stone upon another") begins its fulfillment in this moment.

8. **Jesus dies in the posture of trust, not abandonment.** The final words -- Psalm 31:5, "Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit" -- present a death characterized by deliberate entrustment. Jesus does not expire passively; he actively places his spirit into the Father's keeping. The prayer of 22:42 ("not My will, but Yours be done") reaches its completion: the will of the Father was the cup of suffering, and Jesus drinks it to the end, then commits the outcome to the Father. The obedience that began at the baptism (3:22) and was tested in the wilderness (4:1-13) and agonized over on the Mount of Olives (22:44) is completed at the cross.

9. **Joseph of Arimathea demonstrates that the institutional verdict was not unanimous.** A council member who "had not consented" to the decision acts on behalf of the condemned. His character -- good, righteous, waiting for the kingdom of God -- aligns him with the faithful remnant (Simeon, Anna, the shepherds, Zechariah and Elizabeth) rather than with the institutional leadership. His dissent fractures the council's claim to unified judgment and provides the condemned Jesus with an honorable burial.

10. **The women provide the chain of witness that connects death to resurrection.** The Galilean women are present at the cross (v. 49), observe the burial (v. 55), prepare the spices (v. 56), and will discover the empty tomb (24:1-3). Their unbroken presence across these scenes makes them the primary witnesses to the continuity of the narrative: the same body they watched die and saw buried is the body they will find absent. The author's careful tracking of their movements is not incidental but evidential -- it establishes that the resurrection claim rests on the testimony of those who were present at every stage.
