# Galatians 6 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**Where this stands in the letter:** Chapter 5 turned from argument to consequence at 5:13 and set the Spirit against the flesh. Chapter 6 carries that instruction into particular cases -- what to do about someone who falls, how responsibility is shared and how it is not, what is owed to teachers, and what a life yields -- and then closes.

**The closing is different in kind from everything before it.** At 6:11 Paul takes the pen from the scribe and writes the rest himself, drawing attention to the fact as he does so. What follows is a compressed restatement of the letter, composed by its author, in his own hand, after everything else has been said. That makes 6:11-18 unusually good evidence for what Paul took the letter to be about -- better than any reader's summary, because it is his own.

**Two structural observations about the ending, both unique to this letter.** Every other letter of Paul closes with greetings, usually to named individuals and sometimes at length: Romans 16 names twenty-six people; Colossians 4:7-17 names ten and gives news of each. Galatians has none. No greeting to anyone, no news, no travel plans, no request for hospitality, no mention of a co-worker being sent or received, no "the churches of Asia greet you." And the letter that opened without a thanksgiving therefore also closes without greetings. Both silences are the author's own, and both are audible against his practice everywhere else.

**What comes before (Galatians 5:13-26):** The freedom argument turned into an obligation -- "through love serve one another as slaves" -- and the whole law was said to stand fulfilled in Leviticus 19:18. The works of the flesh were catalogued, with eight of the fifteen items concerning conflict inside a group, and the fruit of the Spirit was named as a single crop. The section closed on the Galatians provoking and envying one another (5:26), after an earlier warning that they were biting and devouring each other (5:15).

Chapter 6 opens by addressing a congregation in exactly that condition. Its first instruction is about how to handle someone who has fallen, and its first named virtue is gentleness. Neither is an accident of arrangement.

## Verses 1-5: Burdens and Loads

> Brothers, if anyone is caught in any trespass, you who are spiritual should restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness. Watch yourself, lest you too be tempted. Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ. For if anyone thinks he is something when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one test his own work, and then his boast will be in himself alone and not in his neighbor. For each will bear his own load.

**Verse 1a: "Brothers, if anyone is caught in any trespass, you who are spiritual should restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness."**

**Ean kai prolēmphthē anthrōpos en tini paraptōmati.** The verb prolambanō means "to take beforehand," and in the passive "to be caught, overtaken, surprised, detected." Two senses are available and the Greek does not choose between them.

On the first, the man is caught *by* the trespass -- overtaken by it, surprised into it before he saw it coming. This is the more sympathetic reading and it fits the gentleness that follows.

On the second, he is caught *in* it -- discovered, found out by others. This is the more forensic reading and it fits a congregation in which people are watching each other.

Both produce the same instruction, which may be why Paul did not disambiguate.

**Paraptōma** is worth distinguishing from its near-neighbour. It is built on piptō, "to fall," with para-, "beside" -- a false step, a slip, a stumble off the path. Paul's other word, **parabasis** (used at 2:18 and 3:19), is built on bainō, "to go," with para- -- a deliberate stepping across a stated line. Paraptōma is the gentler of the two, and it is the one Paul chose here. Whatever has happened, it is described as a fall rather than a defiance.

The indefinite pronouns are generous: **anthrōpos** ("a person," not "one of you") and **en tini paraptōmati** ("in *any* trespass"). No category of fall is excluded from the instruction, and no category of person.

**Hymeis hoi pneumatikoi** ("you who are spiritual") picks up from 5:25 -- those who live by the Spirit and walk in line with it.

Whether this addresses a subgroup within the congregation or the whole of it is not stated, and the question has been read both ways. Some have heard irony in it, as though Paul were quoting a self-designation back at people who claimed it. Against that: the letter has consistently addressed all of these congregations as possessing the Spirit -- they received it (3:2-5), the Spirit of the Son is in their hearts crying Abba (4:6), they are led by the Spirit (5:18) and live by the Spirit (5:25). On the letter's own terms, "you who are spiritual" describes the readers as such. Reading it as a barb requires importing a tone the letter has not established.

**Katartizete ("restore") is the key word of the section, and the image behind it does the work.** Katartizō means "to put in order, to mend, to repair, to make fit, to bring to a proper condition." Its uses are concrete before they are figurative:

- It is what James and John were doing to their nets when they were called -- "in the boat mending (katartizontas) their nets" (Mark 1:19; Matthew 4:21). A torn net is useless and is not thrown away; it is repaired and put back in the water.
- In medical Greek it is the standard word for setting a broken bone -- restoring the limb to its proper alignment so that it can bear weight again.
- It covers refitting a ship, and equipping a person for a task (the cognate noun appears at Ephesians 4:12, "for the equipping of the saints").

What these share is the return of something to working order. The instruction is therefore not to discipline, to exclude, or to correct doctrinally -- and it is not merely to forgive. It is to **mend**, and mending assumes both that the thing is worth the trouble and that it is expected to function again.

**En pneumati prautētos** ("in a spirit of gentleness"). Prautēs is among the fruit listed at 5:23, and the word does not mean weakness. In Greek usage it describes strength that is under control -- it is used of a horse that has been broken, which has lost none of its power and has gained a rider. Moses is called the meekest man on earth (Numbers 12:3) in the middle of a book in which he confronts Pharaoh and the people repeatedly.

The manner prescribed for mending is thus drawn directly from what the Spirit was said to grow, which ties the instruction to the preceding chapter rather than beginning something new.

**Verse 1b: "Watch yourself, lest you too be tempted."**

**Skopōn seauton** shifts abruptly to the **singular**, mid-sentence, after a plural address. The generalized instruction to a community becomes a warning to each individual who acts on it.

Skopeō is "to look at, to keep an eye on, to fix one's attention on" -- the verb behind "scope" and "telescope." The attention that was directed at the fallen man is to be turned back on the one directing it.

**Mē kai sy peirasthēs** ("lest you also be tempted"). The word peirazō covers both testing and tempting; the noun peirasmos appeared at 4:14 for the trial Paul's illness posed to the Galatians.

The clause concedes something that the whole enterprise of restoration depends on: the one doing the mending stands in the same exposure as the one being mended. Nothing in the instruction places him above it, and the change to the singular makes the point personally rather than as a general maxim.

**Verse 2: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ."**

**Allēlōn ta barē bastazete.** Baros is a weight -- something heavy, and in figurative use something oppressive, a load that presses down. Paul uses it at 1 Thessalonians 2:7 of the weight an apostle could have thrown around, and at 2 Corinthians 4:17 of an "eternal weight of glory."

**Bastazō** is "to carry, to bear, to take up and hold." It is the verb of 5:10 ("he will bear the judgment"), and it will appear twice more in this chapter -- at 6:5 of one's own load, and at 6:17 of the marks Paul carries on his body. Four uses in two chapters, of a judgment, a burden, a load, and a set of scars.

**Ton nomon tou Christou ("the law of Christ")** appears only here in the Pauline letters, and its closest relative is 1 Corinthians 9:21, where Paul describes himself as "not being without law toward God but **ennomos Christou**" -- within Christ's law, in-lawed to Christ. That passage is instructive because it is also a passage about accommodation: Paul says he became as one under law to those under law, and as one outside law to those outside it, and inserts this phrase to forestall the conclusion that he is therefore lawless.

**The phrase here is not defined, but the sentence supplies what fulfils it, and that is a substantial constraint on any definition.**

What fulfils the law of Christ is **carrying each other's weight**. Not believing something, not belonging to something, not observing something. That places the phrase in direct relation to 5:14, where "the whole law is fulfilled in one word: you shall love your neighbour as yourself." Bearing burdens is Leviticus 19:18 performed rather than recited.

Three readings of what the phrase *denotes* are available, and the letter does not decide among them:

*A new code given by Christ*, replacing or superseding the Torah. This reading has the difficulty that the letter nowhere sets out such a code, and that when Paul does state the content of what is to be fulfilled, at 5:14, he quotes Leviticus.

*The Torah as read and embodied by Christ* -- the same instruction, seen through the one who fully performed it. This reading fits 5:14 comfortably, and fits the fact that the "law of Christ" is fulfilled by doing what Leviticus 19:18 commands.

*The pattern of Christ's own conduct functioning as a norm.* The one thing this letter says Christ did is give himself for others -- "who gave himself for our sins" (1:4), "who loved me and gave himself for me" (2:20). Bearing what is not yours to bear is precisely that pattern, and on this reading 6:2 says: do what he did.

The readings are not sharply exclusive, and a reader should notice that most commentaries answer the question on grounds imported from elsewhere in the New Testament rather than from anything Galatians says.

**Anaplērōsete** ("you will fill up completely") is a strengthened form of the plēroō used at 5:14, with ana- adding the sense of filling right to the top. Some manuscripts have the imperative ("fulfil!") rather than the future ("you will fulfil"); the difference is slight, since a future indicative in this position functions much like a command.

**Verses 3-4: "For if anyone thinks he is something when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one test his own work, and then his boast will be in himself alone and not in his neighbor."**

**The connection to verse 2 runs through what obstructs it.** A man who thinks himself something will not stoop to carry another man's weight. Self-assessment is therefore not a change of subject; it is the condition of the instruction just given.

**Phrenapata heauton** ("he deceives his own mind") is a compound of phrēn ("mind, understanding, the seat of thought") and apataō ("to deceive"), found nowhere else in the New Testament and rare in Greek generally. The deception is located in the understanding rather than in the will -- the man is not lying; he has fooled himself.

The construction is stark: **dokei tis einai ti mēden ōn** -- "if anyone supposes himself to be something, being nothing." The two indefinite pronouns, ti ("something") and mēden ("nothing"), are set directly against each other with no qualification.

**Dokimazetō** ("let him test, examine, prove") is the verb for assaying metal -- putting a sample to fire or to the touchstone to determine whether it is genuine and what its content is. The related adjective dokimos means "tested and approved," and adokimos "failed the test, rejected." Paul uses the verb of self-examination at 1 Corinthians 11:28 and 2 Corinthians 13:5.

The object is **to ergon heautou**, "his own work" -- singular, the whole of what a man does rather than a selection from it.

**Kauchēma** ("boast, ground of boasting") is the noun. It anticipates the closing section, where boasting is at issue twice: what the other teachers want to boast in (6:13), and what Paul will boast in (6:14).

The instruction is that any ground of confidence must come from examining one's own work -- **eis heauton monon kai ouk eis ton heteron**, "with reference to himself alone and not to the other." What is being ruled out is the comparative: confidence built by measuring oneself against a neighbour rather than against the work itself. In a congregation described as provoking and envying one another (5:26), this is not an abstract caution.

**Verse 5: "For each will bear his own load."**

**The apparent contradiction with verse 2 dissolves in the Greek, because two different nouns are used, and the difference is not subtle.**

Verse 2: bear one another's **barē**. A weight, and in this context an oppressive one -- the thing that presses a person down and that he cannot manage alone.

Verse 5: each will bear his own **phortion**. A phortion is a consignment -- a ship's cargo, a pack assigned to a soldier or a porter, a merchant's freight. It is what a person is properly given to carry, sized to him and belonging to him. It is the same noun Jesus uses at Matthew 11:30: "my burden (phortion) is light."

The verb is identical in both verses -- bastazō -- so the entire distinction rests on the nouns, which is exactly how a Greek reader would have taken it.

The two instructions are therefore complementary and not in tension. Help with what crushes; carry what is assigned. The first concerns someone else's crisis; the second concerns your own responsibility, and neither cancels the other.

The future tense (bastasei, "will bear") may be a general future -- this is how things are -- or may look toward a final accounting, which would fit the sowing-and-reaping material immediately following.

## Verses 6-10: Sowing and Reaping

> Let the one who is taught the word share all good things with the one who teaches. Do not be deceived: God is not mocked. For whatever a man sows, that he will also reap. For the one who sows to his own flesh will from the flesh reap corruption, but the one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life. And let us not grow weary in doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up. So then, as we have opportunity, let us do good to all, especially to those of the household of faith.

**Verse 6: "Let the one who is taught the word share all good things with the one who teaches."**

**Koinōneitō** is the verb of koinōnia -- to have in common, to share, to participate, to go partners in something. It is the same root as the "fellowship" of the handshake at 2:9, where Paul and Barnabas were given "the right hand of koinōnia."

**Ho katechoumenos** ("the one being instructed") is the passive participle of katēcheō, "to instruct orally" -- built on ēcheō, "to sound," so literally to sound something down into someone. The English words "catechumen" and "catechism" descend from it, and the verb's presence here is evidence that these congregations had settled arrangements for teaching, with recognized teachers.

**En pasin agathois** ("in all good things") means goods and not sentiments. The instruction is that those who receive instruction should support those who give it, materially.

Paul addresses the same obligation at much greater length at 1 Corinthians 9:3-14, where he argues it from four directions -- the soldier who does not pay his own wages, the vine-dresser who eats the fruit, the shepherd who drinks the milk, and the Torah's own ruling that "you shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain" (Deuteronomy 25:4) -- and then declines to make use of the right himself.

Why the instruction appears here, between a warning about self-assessment and a warning about sowing, is not stated. The connection may simply be practical: a congregation in conflict is a congregation whose teachers go unsupported.

**Verse 7: "Do not be deceived: God is not mocked. For whatever a man sows, that he will also reap."**

**Mē planasthe** ("do not be led astray") is from planaō, "to cause to wander" -- the root of "planet," the wandering star. Paul uses this exact formula elsewhere to head off a mistaken assumption (1 Corinthians 6:9; 15:33), and its presence signals that what follows corrects something people are inclined to think.

**Theos ou myktērizetai** ("God is not sneered at"). Myktērizō is built on myktēr, "nostril," and means literally to turn up the nose at someone -- to treat with open and visible contempt. The Greek scriptures use it of contempt for wisdom and for the righteous: "they despised all my reproof" (Proverbs 1:30); "a foolish son mocks his father" (Proverbs 15:20); "the righteous see it and are glad; the innocent laugh them to scorn" (Job 22:19).

The passive with God as its subject is the point. **God is not the sort of thing that can be treated with contempt and not notice.** The sense is not that God takes offence at insults but that the arrangement being described cannot be gamed -- one cannot appear to satisfy it while doing otherwise and expect the appearance to hold.

**The sowing-and-reaping principle is proverbial and runs throughout the scriptures**, always with the same two components:

- "Whoever sows injustice will reap calamity" (Proverbs 22:8)
- "Those who plough iniquity and sow trouble reap the same" (Job 4:8)
- "They sow the wind and they shall reap the whirlwind" (Hosea 8:7)
- "Sow for yourselves righteousness; reap the fruit of steadfast love" (Hosea 10:12)
- "Those who sow in tears shall reap with shouts of joy" (Psalm 126:5)

**The two components are *correspondence* and *delay*.** What comes up is the kind of thing that went into the ground -- a farmer does not sow barley and reap wheat. And the harvest does not arrive with the sowing; there is a season in between during which nothing visible happens and the sower has only his own work to go on. Both components are operative in the verses that follow: verse 8 states the correspondence, verse 9 states the delay.

**Verse 8: "For the one who sows to his own flesh will from the flesh reap corruption, but the one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life."**

The flesh-and-Spirit contrast of 5:16-26 is restated agriculturally, and the restatement makes it a matter of outcome rather than of struggle.

**Eis tēn sarka heautou** ("into his own flesh") -- the reflexive is emphatic. Sowing into the flesh is sowing into oneself, which is a fair description of the vice list at 5:19-21, where more than half the items concern a person's advancement at the expense of the group.

**Phthora** ("corruption, decay, ruin, perishing") is what happens to perishable things by their nature -- the word covers rot, spoilage, and destruction. Paul uses it of the created order's bondage at Romans 8:21 and of what is sown perishable at 1 Corinthians 15:42. The point of the word is that no external punishment needs to be added: what is sown into the perishable yields according to its kind.

**Zōēn aiōnion** ("life of the age") is the standard expression for the life of the age to come. It belongs to the two-age framework the letter announced in its greeting -- deliverance "out of the present evil age" (1:4) -- and it is the only occurrence of the phrase in this letter.

**The structure of the claim deserves attention because it is easy to read past.** Both harvests use the same verb (therisei) in the same tense, and both follow from what was sown. Eternal life is stated here as a **crop reaped**, which is to say as something arrived at rather than something held from the beginning and merely retained.

That is the same grammar the letter has used throughout for what is at stake: a connection severed and a position fallen out of (5:4), an inheritance not entered (5:21), a harvest reaped or not (6:8). None of these describes a possession confiscated or a credit reversed. All describe something departed from, not arrived at, or not brought in.

**Verse 9: "And let us not grow weary in doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up."**

**Mē enkakōmen** ("let us not lose heart, grow weary in, become discouraged"). The compound is en- plus kakos, "bad" -- to behave badly in the sense of flagging, to turn faint-hearted at a task.

**To kalon poiountes** ("doing the good") uses kalos, which means good in the sense of fine, admirable, worthy -- what looks right and is right.

**Kairō gar idiō therisomen** ("for in its own season we shall reap"). The delay component of the proverb is stated plainly. **Kairos** is not clock-time (chronos) but the right time, the appointed moment, the season proper to a thing. A harvest has one, and it is not chosen by the farmer.

**Mē ekluomenoi** ("not fainting, not giving out") is from ekluō, "to loose out, to unloose, to let go slack." It is used of collapsing from hunger (Matthew 15:32), of the strength going out of a person, of a bowstring going slack.

**The conditional is explicit and Paul does not soften it.** The harvest comes in its season **if** we do not give out. The sentence is built as a promise with a condition attached, and both halves are stated.

The pastoral situation this addresses is visible in the verse itself. Between sowing and reaping there is a season in which nothing appears to be happening, and the temptation in that season is not to do evil but to stop doing good because it seems to yield nothing. That is what enkakeō and ekluō both describe.

**Verse 10: "So then, as we have opportunity, let us do good to all, especially to those of the household of faith."**

**Hōs kairon echomen** ("as we have opportunity") plays on the kairō idiō of the previous verse. There is a season for reaping, which is not now, and a season for working, which is. The word is the same; the two seasons are different.

**Ergazōmetha to agathon** ("let us work the good") -- the verb is ergazomai, from ergon, the noun that has appeared throughout the letter in "works of law" (2:16; 3:2, 5, 10) and "works of the flesh" (5:19). Here the same root is used positively and without qualification: work the good.

**Tous oikeious tēs pisteōs** ("those of the household of faith") uses oikeios, "belonging to the house," from oikos. The word describes family members and dependents as distinct from outsiders, and it continues the household imagery that has run from 4:1-7 (heir, guardians, stewards, adoption) through 4:19 (little children) to here.

**The scope is stated as a priority, not as a restriction.** Pros pantas -- to all. Malista de -- especially, to these. The nearer obligation does not withdraw the wider one, and a reader should notice that the verse is often quoted with only its second half.

## Verses 11-18: The Postscript in Paul's Own Hand

> See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand! It is those who want to make a good showing in the flesh who would compel you to be circumcised -- only in order that they may not be persecuted for the cross of Christ. For not even those who are circumcised keep the law themselves, but they want you to be circumcised so that they may boast in your flesh. But far be it from me to boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world. For neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation. And as for all who walk by this rule, peace be upon them and mercy, and upon the Israel of God. From now on let no one cause me trouble, for I bear on my body the marks of Jesus. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. Amen.

**Verse 11: "See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand!"**

**The convention behind this needs stating for the sentence to register.** Letters in this period were normally dictated to a trained scribe, who wrote in a small, practised, even hand. Romans 16:22 preserves such a scribe's own greeting, inserted into the letter: "I Tertius, who wrote this letter, greet you in the Lord."

The sender would commonly add a closing in his own hand, both as authentication and as a personal touch: "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand" (1 Corinthians 16:21); "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand" (Colossians 4:18); "I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it" (Philemon 19). At 2 Thessalonians 3:17 the practice is explicitly described as a security measure: "this is the sign of genuineness in every letter of mine; it is the way I write."

Here Paul draws attention both to the change of hand and to the **size** of his letters. **Pēlikois... grammasin** -- "with what large characters."

Why they are large is not stated, and the proposals are all reconstructions: poor eyesight, which some connect to 4:15 and the offer of the Galatians' eyes; unfamiliarity with the pen in a man who normally dictated; hands coarsened by manual labour (Paul worked with his hands, 1 Thessalonians 2:9; 1 Corinthians 4:12) or damaged by the beatings referred to at 6:17; or simple emphasis -- writing large the way a person raises his voice, so that what follows cannot be skimmed.

**What does not depend on the explanation is the status of what follows.** From verse 11 to the end, this is the author's own hand and his own summary, set down after everything else has been said. What he chooses to restate is therefore evidence of a kind not otherwise available.

**Verse 12: "It is those who want to make a good showing in the flesh who would compel you to be circumcised -- only in order that they may not be persecuted for the cross of Christ."**

**Euprosōpēsai en sarki** ("to put on a good face in the flesh"). The verb is a compound of eu ("well") and prosōpon ("face"), found nowhere else in the New Testament and rare in Greek generally. It denotes making a good outward appearance -- looking right to observers, presenting a face that will pass inspection.

The phrase **en sarki** does the double duty it has done throughout the letter: a good showing in the outward and visible sphere, and a good showing measured in circumcised bodies.

**Anankazousin** ("they compel") is the verb of 2:3 (Titus, who was not compelled) and 2:14 (Peter, compelling Gentiles to Judaize). Its three occurrences in this letter all concern pressure applied to Gentiles about circumcision, and together they form a set that spans the whole document.

**The motive Paul assigns them is the avoidance of persecution**, and the sentence is emphatic about it: **monon hina** -- "*only* in order that" -- tō staurō tou Christou mē diōkōntai.

**What that persecution would have consisted of, and why circumcising Gentile converts would avert it.** The letter does not explain, and any account is reconstruction, but the situation it presupposes is intelligible and worth setting out, because without it the verse is opaque.

A Gentile in a Greek or Roman city was expected to participate in the cults of his household, his trade association, and his city. This was not private religion in a modern sense; it was civic and social obligation, woven into public festivals, guild dinners, family observances and civic loyalty. A Gentile who stopped participating was conspicuous and was liable to be regarded as antisocial or disloyal.

Jewish communities had long-established customary allowances for their own non-participation, recognized in practice by Roman authorities. A Gentile who converted and became a Jew moved into that recognized position. A Gentile who joined a Jewish-derived movement but did *not* convert occupied no recognized position at all: he had abandoned the cults without entering the community whose abstention was tolerated.

There is also pressure to be reckoned from the other direction. A movement that proclaimed a crucified man as Messiah and admitted uncircumcised Gentiles as full members without conversion was liable to bring trouble on the Jewish communities associated with it -- and on any Jewish teachers identified with it.

On either account, circumcising the Gentile converts regularizes an anomaly, and it does so at their expense rather than at the teachers' own.

**This connects directly to 5:11**, where Paul argued from his own persecution that he cannot be preaching circumcision. The two verses are the two ends of one argument: preaching a cross sufficient without conversion draws persecution; adding conversion removes it.

**A caution that should be entered plainly and not buried.** This is Paul's characterization of his opponents' motives, and it is not their account of themselves. This letter is the only surviving source for the dispute. The other party left no writing; we do not have their reasoning, their scriptural arguments, or their self-understanding, and we do not know whether they would have recognized the motive attributed to them here. A reader should hold this verse with that awareness -- not as a reason to disbelieve Paul, but as a fact about the evidence.

**Verse 13: "For not even those who are circumcised keep the law themselves, but they want you to be circumcised so that they may boast in your flesh."**

**Hoi peritemnomenoi is a present participle, and its referent is genuinely disputed.** The form may be middle or passive, and it can be rendered "those who are circumcised" (a settled state), "those who are getting circumcised" (in process), or "those who have themselves circumcised."

Three candidates follow.

*The other teachers, as circumcised men* -- Jews pressing a requirement they were born into. This is the simplest reading, though it strains the present tense somewhat.

*The Galatians themselves*, currently in the process of accepting it. This fits the present tense but makes the sentence awkward: Paul would be saying that the Galatians do not keep the law, which is trivially true of men who have only just started.

*The other teachers as themselves recent proselytes* -- Gentiles who converted and now recruit others. This gives the present participle its full force and would explain several things at once: the eagerness to make converts, the possibility of "boasting" in a tally of them, and a convert's characteristic zeal for the boundary he has just crossed. It is a reconstruction, not a demonstration, and the text does not confirm it.

**Nomon phylassousin** ("they keep the law"). Phylassō is "to guard, to keep watch over, to observe" -- the standard verb for keeping commandments in the Greek scriptures.

The charge that those pressing the programme do not perform it can be heard in two ways, and they are quite different.

*As hypocrisy*: they demand of others what they do not do themselves. This is the ordinary reading and it is a charge about character.

*As an argument that the programme is incoherent*: the covenant being urged is not in fact being kept by those urging it, so what precisely is on offer? On this reading the point is structural rather than personal, and it draws on 5:3 -- circumcision obligates a man to the *whole* law, and if the whole is not being kept by anyone in view, then the transaction does not deliver what it advertises.

**Hina en tē hymetera sarki kauchēsōntai** ("that they may boast in your flesh") supplies the alternative motive to that of verse 12. Converts as a record of achievement -- countable, displayable, and made of other people's bodies. The possessive is pointed: *your* flesh, *their* boast.

**Verse 14: "But far be it from me to boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world."**

**Emoi de mē genoito kauchasthai** uses the letter's strongest negation, mē genoito, which appeared at 2:17 and 3:21 to reject an inference drawn from Paul's position. Here it rejects a possibility about himself.

**Boasting is not a marginal theme in the scriptures Paul is working from.** Jeremiah 9:23-24 sets the terms: "Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, let not the mighty man boast in his might, let not the rich man boast in his riches; but let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me." Paul quotes that passage twice (1 Corinthians 1:31; 2 Corinthians 10:17). The question is never whether to boast but in what.

Paul's answer is the cross -- which, as an object of boasting, is a deliberate outrage. Crucifixion was a slave's death and a rebel's death, designed to humiliate; Roman writers of the period treat it as barely mentionable. To name it as one's sole ground of boasting is to invert the entire economy of honour that the phrase "make a good showing" (v. 12) belongs to.

**Di' hou emoi kosmos estaurōtai kagō kosmō** ("through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world"). Both verbs are perfect -- completed action with standing result -- and the severance runs in both directions. Not merely that Paul has withdrawn from the world's estimation, but that the world has been put to death with respect to him. Neither party is any longer in a position to make claims on the other.

**Kosmos** here names the present ordering of things -- the same register as "the present evil age" (1:4) and "the elements of the world" (4:3). It is not the created order as such but the world as a system of value, standing and appearance, which is exactly what verse 12's "good showing" belongs to.

The three crucifixion statements in the letter now stand together: "I have been crucified with Christ" (2:19-20); "those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh" (5:24); "the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world" (6:14). In each case the severance is decisive, and in each case it is participatory in a death that was not the speaker's own.

**Verse 15: "For neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation."**

The first half repeats 5:6 in substance; the second half differs. Where 5:6 had "faith working through love," this has **kainē ktisis**, "new creation."

**Note again that the negation runs both ways.** Uncircumcision counts no more than circumcision. Whatever the letter is arguing against, it is not Jewish practice as such but the assignment of standing to a bodily condition -- and the sentence rules out both directions with equal force.

**The phrase kainē ktisis admits two readings, and the letter supports both. Neither should be presented as settled.**

*Reading one: the renewed order.* Ktisis is the standard word for the created order or for the act of creating. Paul uses it that way at Romans 8:19-22, where "the whole creation groans" and awaits its liberation from corruption. On this reading, kainē ktisis names the new age into which the present one is giving way -- and the letter would then close where it opened, since 1:4 announced deliverance "out of the present evil age." The inclusio would be deliberate.

Two further supports: the immediately preceding verse has just had a **world** crucified, which is a cosmic statement rather than a personal one; and the letter's whole frame, from 1:4 through the stoicheia of 4:3 and 4:9, is a two-age frame.

*Reading two: the person made new.* The contrast within the sentence is with a bodily alteration -- circumcision, uncircumcision -- which makes an inward or personal renewal an apt antithesis: not a change made *to* the body but a change in what the person *is*. The natural parallel is 2 Corinthians 5:17, "if anyone is in Christ, new creation; the old things passed away, behold, they have become new" -- where the Greek supplies neither a verb nor a pronoun ("he is a new creation" is a translator's addition) and which is itself read both ways.

The two are not mutually exclusive: a person made new may be the first instance of a renewal that is wider than himself, and Romans 8:19-23 sets the two side by side. The verse does not adjudicate, and readers should notice when a translation has adjudicated for them by supplying "he is."

**Verse 16: "And as for all who walk by this rule, peace be upon them and mercy, and upon the Israel of God."**

**Hosoi tō kanoni toutō stoichēsousin** ("as many as will walk in line by this rule").

**Kanon** is a measuring rod, a straight-edge, a rule in the carpenter's sense -- the standard against which straightness is judged. The English "canon" descends from it. **Stoicheō** is the verb of 5:25, from the root that has appeared as stoicheia (4:3, 9) and systoichei (4:25): to stand in a row, to keep formation, to walk in line.

"This rule" most naturally refers to what was just said in verse 15.

**Kai epi ton Israēl tou theou ("and upon the Israel of God") is the most contested phrase in the letter, and the dispute turns entirely on the conjunction kai.**

*Read epexegetically* -- "and that is to say, upon the Israel of God" -- the phrase identifies the group just named, those who walk by this rule, **as** the Israel of God. This has been the dominant reading in the interpretive tradition, and it is the reading most English translations imply by their punctuation.

*Read additively* -- "and also upon the Israel of God" -- it names a **second** group receiving the same blessing alongside the first.

**Three considerations bear on the choice, and they should be laid out rather than assumed.**

*First, kai is normally additive.* That is its ordinary function and what a reader meeting it would expect. The epexegetic use ("and indeed," "that is to say") is well attested and genuinely available, but it is the marked, less common sense. It has to be argued for from context; it cannot simply be asserted, and the burden of proof lies on the party invoking it.

*Second, there is no external usage to appeal to.* The phrase Israēl tou theou occurs nowhere else -- not in the Greek scriptures, not in surviving Jewish writings of the period, not elsewhere in the New Testament, not in any Greek text that has come down to us. There is no established sense to consult.

And Paul uses **Israēl** roughly sixteen times across his letters -- including the sustained argument of Romans 9-11, where an enormous amount turns on the word, and where he distinguishes carefully between Israel and the Gentiles being grafted in -- without ever unambiguously applying it to a mixed or Gentile body.

*Third, the sentence has the shape of a synagogue benediction.* The closing blessing of the Amidah, the central prayer of Jewish worship, asks God to grant "**peace**, goodness and blessing, grace and lovingkindness and **mercy**, upon us **and upon all Israel your people**." Galatians 6:16 runs: peace upon them, and **mercy**, and **upon the Israel of God** -- the same elements, in the same order, ending in the same tail.

The Amidah's fixed wording is later than Paul, so this is a formal parallel and not a demonstrable citation. But the *form* of such a blessing is old, and if a familiar liturgical closing is in view, then "and upon Israel" is a **formula** -- and a formula is not the kind of thing that gets silently redefined halfway through its own recitation.

**None of this settles the verse, and it would be dishonest to present it as settling it.** What the evidence does show is that the additive reading is not the eccentric one, and that the epexegetic reading carries a burden of proof it is frequently not made to bear. A reader should take a position here knowingly, and should notice when a commentary, a translation's punctuation, or a sermon has taken one without saying that a choice was made.

**Verse 17: "From now on let no one cause me trouble, for I bear on my body the marks of Jesus."**

**Tou loipou** ("from now on, henceforth, for the rest") signals a final word on the matter.

**Kopous moi mēdeis parechetō** ("let no one furnish troubles to me"). Kopos is labour, trouble, wearisome effort -- the noun behind kopiaō, the verb Paul used at 4:11 for having laboured over the Galatians to exhaustion. The idiom "to furnish trouble to someone" appears at Matthew 26:10 and Luke 11:7.

**Ta stigmata tou Iēsou ("the marks of Jesus").** A stigma is a brand or a tattoo -- a mark cut or burnt into the skin, permanent and visible. Its uses in this world were specific and they were all about ownership or allegiance:

- **Slaves** were branded, both to show whose they were and to make runaways identifiable and unsaleable.
- **Soldiers** in some contexts bore marks of their unit or their commander.
- **Devotees of certain deities** bore marks identifying whose they were, which is attested in several cults of the period.

In every case the mark answered the question: *whose is this man?*

**Bastazō** ("I bear") is the verb of 6:2 and 6:5, and of Acts 15:10.

**What the marks were.** Paul catalogues them at 2 Corinthians 11:23-25: "far more imprisonments, with countless beatings, and often near death. Five times I received at the hands of the Jews the forty lashes less one. Three times I was beaten with rods. Once I was stoned." A man who had survived that carried it on his skin permanently.

**The placement of this sentence is the letter's final argument, and it contains no theology at all.**

The entire letter has been a dispute about a mark made in the flesh and about what that mark proves. Paul closes by producing a different mark, in his own flesh. It was not chosen, it was not applied in a ceremony, and it confers no status. It is what happened to a man who preached the cross without the rite -- and it answers the same question circumcision was being offered to answer.

The counterpart to circumcision, in other words, turns out not to be another rite. It is scar tissue.

**Verse 18: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. Amen."**

The benediction is standard in its opening -- **charis**, "grace," is the word the letter began with at 1:3 and the sphere the Galatians were called into at 1:6 and were said to have fallen out of at 5:4. The letter ends by wishing them the thing it warned them they were leaving.

Two departures from Paul's usual form.

**Meta tou pneumatos hymōn** ("with your spirit") rather than the more usual "with you all." The same form appears at Philippians 4:23 and Philemon 25.

**Adelphoi** ("brothers") stands at the very end -- after the benediction, immediately before the Amen. It occupies that position in no other Pauline letter.

**The placement is worth registering, and it is the last thing the letter does.** This document contains "I am astonished"; "you foolish Galatians"; "who has bewitched you"; "let him be accursed," twice; "you are severed from Christ"; "you have fallen from grace"; "I am afraid I have laboured over you in vain"; and "I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves."

Its final word before the Amen is *brothers*.

Nothing in six chapters has withdrawn that address. The severity throughout is aimed at a movement the writer believes can still be reversed -- the desertion is in progress at 1:6, the circumcision has not yet happened at 5:2, and the confidence at 5:10 is that they will come round -- and the relationship the letter is written from does not change from the first line to the last.

**Amen** transliterates the Hebrew 'amen, from a root meaning firmness or reliability. It is the word by which a congregation assents to what has been said and makes it its own -- which means the letter ends by inviting the people it has just rebuked to say yes to it.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. **The instruction of 6:1-5 is addressed to a congregation that has been described as biting and devouring one another (5:15) and provoking and envying one another (5:26).** The verb for dealing with someone who has fallen is katartizō -- to mend a net, to set a bone, to restore a thing to working order -- and the manner prescribed is gentleness, taken from the list of what the Spirit grows. Neither the word nor the manner is chosen at random for a congregation in that condition.

2. **The one doing the restoring is warned, in the singular, that he stands in the same exposure.** The abrupt change from plural to singular mid-sentence makes the warning personal rather than general. Nothing in the instruction elevates the mender above the mended, which is the same levelling the letter performed at 2:17 when Jews seeking justification "were found to be sinners."

3. **6:2 and 6:5 are not in conflict, and the two nouns are why.** Baros is a crushing weight, to be shared; phortion is an assigned load -- a ship's cargo, a soldier's pack -- which is properly one's own. The verb is identical in both verses, so the entire distinction rests on the nouns, which is how a Greek reader would have taken it. Help with what overwhelms; carry what is yours.

4. **"The law of Christ" is not defined, but what fulfils it is, and that constrains any definition.** The phrase occurs only here in Paul, and its closest relative is 1 Corinthians 9:21's ennomos Christou. The context supplies the content: bearing one another's burdens. That ties it directly to 5:14, where the whole law stands fulfilled in Leviticus 19:18. Whether the phrase denotes a new code, the Torah as embodied by Christ, or Christ's own self-giving as a pattern, this letter does not say -- and readers should notice that the question is usually answered on grounds imported from elsewhere.

5. **6:7-9 states the outcome as corresponding, delayed, and conditional.** What is sown determines what comes up; the reaping arrives "in its own season" and not before; and it arrives "if we do not give up." Set beside 5:4 and 5:21, the letter has three warnings of a single shape -- a connection severed, an inheritance not entered, a harvest not brought in. None describes a possession confiscated. All describe something departed from or not arrived at.

6. **The postscript is the author's own summary, and what it selects is evidence about what he thought the letter was.** Having taken the pen himself and written large, Paul restates four things: the other teachers' motive, boasting, the cross, and new creation. He does not restate justification, works of law, Abraham, the two covenants, the curse, the promise, or the law's term -- which is to say, none of the material that occupies chapters 2 through 4. What the letter's own conclusion foregrounds is the crisis and the cross.

7. **The motive attributed to the other teachers is the avoidance of persecution, and the situation that would make it intelligible is reconstructible but not stated.** A Gentile who left the civic cults without becoming a Jew occupied no recognized position; circumcising him regularized an anomaly at his expense. And a movement admitting uncircumcised Gentiles could bring trouble on those associated with it. Whether that is what was actually happening, the letter does not say -- and it should be held firmly in view that this is one party's account of the other's motives, preserved because that party's letter survived and the other's did not.

8. **6:15 closes an arc opened at 1:4, on one reading of it, and the reading is genuinely undecided.** The letter began with deliverance "out of the present evil age" and ends with "new creation." Whether ktisis names the renewed order or the person made new is not settled: 2 Corinthians 5:17 is read both ways and supplies no verb of its own. The frame of 1:4 and the crucified kosmos of 6:14 weigh toward the wider reading; the immediate contrast with a bodily alteration weighs toward the narrower. Translations that supply "he is a new creation" have decided the question silently.

9. **6:16 turns on a single conjunction, and while the evidence does not settle it, it does show which reading carries the burden of proof.** Kai is normally additive, and the epexegetic sense is the marked one requiring argument from context. "The Israel of God" appears nowhere else in Greek. Paul nowhere else applies Israēl unambiguously to a mixed body, including throughout Romans 9-11 where the word does enormous work. And the sentence's elements -- peace, mercy, "and upon Israel" -- match the shape of a synagogue benediction, which if in view makes the tail a formula rather than a redefinition. Whichever reading is taken should be taken knowingly.

10. **The letter answers a mark in the flesh with a mark in the flesh, and this is its final argument.** Circumcision has been the subject throughout. Paul closes by producing his own stigmata -- brands of the kind that identified whose a slave was -- carried on his body from beatings, rods and stoning. It answers the same question the rite was offered to answer, and it contains no theology whatever.

11. **The closing has no greetings, and the opening had no thanksgiving; both are unique to this letter among Paul's.** And the final word before the Amen is adelphoi. The two silences measure how serious the writer thought the situation was. The last word measures what he still believed about the people in it, after six chapters of saying the hardest things he ever put in a letter.
