# Matthew 9 -- First-Century Reading

## Context Within the Author's Argument

**What came before (Matthew 8):** After concluding the extended mountain discourse (chapters 5-7), the author presented a sequence of episodes demonstrating Jesus's authority in action. Jesus healed a leper and directed him to show himself to the priest and offer what Moses commanded -- indicating that the Torah's requirements still hold. He healed the centurion's servant, marveling at a Gentile's faith and declaring that "many will come from east and west" to recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom while "the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness." He healed Peter's mother-in-law and cast out spirits, which the author tied to Isaiah 53:4: "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases." Two would-be followers were warned about the cost of following Jesus (no place to lay his head; let the dead bury their own dead). Jesus then stilled a storm on the sea -- prompting his disciples to ask "What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" -- and delivered two demoniacs in the country of the Gadarenes, after which the whole city begged him to leave. Throughout chapter 8, the author organized these episodes around the question of Jesus's authority (exousia), a word that first appeared in the crowds' reaction at 7:29: "He was teaching them as one having authority, and not as their scribes." Chapter 8 demonstrated that this authority extends over disease, nature, and the demonic realm.

**What comes after (Matthew 10):** The author presents the second of the document's five major discourses. Jesus summons the twelve disciples, names them, and sends them out with authority (exousia) over unclean spirits and to heal every disease and affliction. They are sent specifically "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." Jesus gives them detailed instructions about conduct, warns them of persecution, and tells them "I did not come to bring peace, but a sword." The discourse addresses the cost and urgency of the mission, directly continuing the themes of chapter 9's conclusion.

**How chapter 9 connects them:** Chapter 8 demonstrated Jesus's authority through mighty acts but left the question of what this authority ultimately means. Chapter 9 escalates the demonstration in two critical ways. First, Jesus claims authority not only over physical ailments but over sin itself (9:6) -- a claim that provokes the first direct accusation of blasphemy from the scribes and begins the pattern of opposition that will intensify throughout the document. Second, the chapter steadily reveals who Jesus's mission is for -- sinners, not the righteous (9:13); the sick, not the healthy (9:12) -- and ends with the image of crowds who are "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (9:36), which creates the immediate rationale for commissioning the twelve in chapter 10. The movement is from demonstrated authority (chapter 8) to contested authority and clarified mission (chapter 9) to delegated authority (chapter 10).

## Authority Over Sin Demonstrated Through Healing (vv. 1-8)

The chapter opens with Jesus crossing the sea and coming "to his own city" (eis ten idian polin). The author identified Jesus's base as Capernaum at 4:13, so this is a return home after the Gadarene episode that closed chapter 8. The transition is geographic but also thematic: in Gadara the whole city rejected Jesus; in his own city, what follows is a mixture of faith and hostility.

"And behold, some people brought to him a paralytic, lying on a bed. And when Jesus saw their faith (ten pistin auton), he said to the paralytic, 'Take heart, child; your sins are forgiven (aphientai sou hai hamartiai).'"

Several elements demand attention. First, "their faith" (ten pistin auton) -- the pronoun "their" is plural, referring to those who brought the paralytic, not necessarily the paralytic himself. The author presents faith as something that can be exercised on behalf of another. Jesus responds to the faith he sees in the group.

Second, the paralytic comes seeking healing but receives a declaration about sin. "Your sins are forgiven" uses the present passive (aphientai) -- "are being forgiven" or "are herewith forgiven." The passive implies God as the agent: God is forgiving your sins, and Jesus is declaring it. Yet the scribes do not hear it as a mere declaration; they hear it as a claim.

"And behold, some of the scribes said to themselves, 'This man is blaspheming (houtos blasphemei).'" The scribes' reasoning is not stated by the author here but is implied: only God can forgive sins (this is explicit in the parallel accounts and reflects standard Jewish theological conviction; see Isaiah 43:25, "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake"). To pronounce forgiveness is to exercise a divine prerogative. The scribes hear Jesus doing this and conclude he is blaspheming -- claiming for himself what belongs to God alone.

"But Jesus, knowing (eidon, literally 'seeing') their thoughts, said, 'Why do you think evil in your hearts?'" The verb eidon (to see, perceive) echoes 9:2, where Jesus "saw" the faith of the paralytic's friends. Jesus sees both faith and hostility. His perception penetrates to the interior -- to thoughts (enthymeseis, deliberations, inner reasoning) and hearts (kardiais). The author consistently presents the heart as the site where a person's true orientation is revealed (5:8, 5:28, 6:21).

The question Jesus poses is diagnostic: "For which is easier, to say, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Rise and walk'?" The logic is precise. Anyone can say "your sins are forgiven" -- the statement is unverifiable. No one can confirm or deny that forgiveness has actually occurred. But to say "rise and walk" to a paralytic is immediately testable. If the paralytic does not rise, the claim is exposed as empty. Jesus argues from the visible to the invisible: "But that you may know (eidete) that the Son of Man has authority (exousian) on earth to forgive sins" -- and then he turns to the paralytic -- "Rise, pick up your bed, and go home."

The title "Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthropou) appears here for the first time in this document in Jesus's own speech (it appeared at 8:20 in the saying about having nowhere to lay his head). The phrase comes from Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" comes before the Ancient of Days and is given dominion, glory, and a kingdom "that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him." But it also functions in Ezekiel as God's regular address to the prophet (Ezekiel 2:1, "Son of man, stand on your feet"), where it emphasizes the prophet's humanity in contrast to God's transcendence. In this context, Jesus uses it to describe a figure who exercises divine authority "on earth" -- the forgiveness of sins is not deferred to heaven or to the eschatological future but enacted in the present, on the ground, in a specific encounter with a specific paralyzed man.

The phrase "on earth" (epi tes ges) is significant. The model prayer asked for God's will to be done "on earth as it is in heaven" (6:10). Here, the Son of Man's authority to forgive sins is exercised on earth -- it is a present reality, not only a future hope.

The paralytic rose and went home. The crowds' response: "they were afraid (ephobethesan) and glorified (edoxasan) God, who had given such authority to human beings (tois anthropois)." Two elements stand out. First, the crowds fear -- this is not joyful admiration but the trembling response to a display of divine power (phobos in its biblical sense of awe before the numinous). Second, the crowds understand that this authority has been "given" (donta) -- it is delegated, not seized. And they say it has been given "to human beings" (plural, tois anthropois), not merely to Jesus individually. The author may be signaling that the crowd perceives something about authority being entrusted to humans on earth more broadly, which will find its next expression in chapter 10 when Jesus delegates this very authority to the twelve.

## The Calling of a Tax Collector and Table Fellowship with Sinners (vv. 9-13)

"As Jesus passed on from there, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth, and he said to him, 'Follow me.' And he rose and followed him."

The author names the tax collector as Matthew -- this is the same name that will appear in the list of the twelve at 10:3, where the author uniquely adds the descriptor "the tax collector." Tax collectors (telonai) have already appeared in the mountain discourse at 5:46 as an example of the lowest standard of reciprocal love ("Do not even the tax collectors do the same?"). They were despised in first-century Jewish society because they collaborated with the Roman occupying power and typically enriched themselves by collecting more than was owed. To call a tax collector as a disciple is a provocative act, consistent with the pattern emerging throughout chapters 8-9: Jesus's authority is exercised precisely among those whom the religious establishment considers unclean, unworthy, or beyond the pale.

The call itself is strikingly brief: "Follow me" (akolouthei moi). It uses the same imperative form that appeared at 4:19 when Jesus called Simon and Andrew. The response is equally terse: "he rose and followed him" (anastas ekolouthesen auto). There is no negotiation, no qualification. The contrast with the would-be followers of 8:19-22, who proposed conditions or requested delays, is stark. Matthew simply rises from his tax booth -- abandoning his livelihood -- and follows.

The scene shifts immediately to a meal: "And as Jesus reclined at table in the house, behold, many tax collectors and sinners came and were reclining with Jesus and his disciples." The phrase "tax collectors and sinners" (telonai kai hamartoloi) functions as a fixed pair in the author's vocabulary, describing those considered outside respectable society. "Sinners" (hamartoloi) in first-century Jewish usage did not simply mean morally imperfect people (everyone was that); it designated those who lived in flagrant disregard of the Torah's requirements -- people whose way of life placed them outside the covenant community's boundaries. Reclining at table (synaneekeinto) with such people was not merely social contact but table fellowship, which in the Jewish context implied acceptance, intimacy, and shared identity. To eat with someone was to identify with them.

The Pharisees notice and question not Jesus directly but his disciples: "Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?" The word "teacher" (didaskalos) is what outsiders call Jesus in this document; the disciples call him "Lord" (kyrios). The Pharisees' objection is not arbitrary -- table fellowship with the unclean carried implications of contamination. The concern was that associating with sinners would compromise one's own standing before God, and that a teacher who did so was leading others astray.

Jesus's response contains three elements:

First, the physician metaphor: "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick." This is a self-description of Jesus's mission. A physician goes to the sick, not the healthy. The logic is functional: Jesus is where he needs to be because these are the people who need what he brings. The metaphor implicitly categorizes the sinners at the table as sick, not as permanently excluded -- their condition is one that can be addressed.

Second, the scripture citation: "Go and learn (poreuthentes de mathete) what this means: 'I desire mercy and not sacrifice' (eleos thelo kai ou thysian)." This is a direct quotation of Hosea 6:6. The phrase "go and learn" (poreuthentes mathete) was a standard rabbinic formula used when one's opponent had failed to understand a well-known text -- it carries a mild rebuke. The Hosea passage, in its original context, was God's complaint against Israel through the prophet: Israel's loyalty (hesed/eleos) is like morning dew that disappears, and God values steadfast covenant loyalty over ritual observance performed without genuine relational faithfulness.

The author's choice of this text is deliberate. The Pharisees are concerned with ritual purity (sacrifice, separation from the unclean). Jesus points them to a prophetic text where God explicitly prioritizes mercy over sacrifice. The word "mercy" (eleos) corresponds to the Hebrew hesed -- covenant faithfulness, loyal love, compassion enacted in relationship. The author will cite Hosea 6:6 again at 12:7, the only other occurrence in the document, in another controversy with the Pharisees over Sabbath observance. The repetition signals that this text is programmatic for the author's understanding of Jesus's mission and for the conflict between Jesus and the Pharisees.

Third, the mission statement: "For I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (ou gar elthon kalesai dikaious alla hamartolous). The verb "call" (kalesai) is the same root as the call to Matthew in verse 9. Jesus defines his mission as calling -- summoning, inviting -- sinners. The word "righteous" (dikaious) may carry irony: are the Pharisees truly righteous, or do they merely consider themselves so? The author does not resolve this explicitly here, but the developing argument (particularly 5:20, where the disciples' righteousness must exceed that of the Pharisees) suggests that the Pharisees' self-assessed righteousness is precisely the problem. Those who believe they are well do not seek a physician, even if they are in fact sick.

## The Question About Fasting and Three Analogies (vv. 14-17)

"Then the disciples of John came to him, saying, 'Why do we and the Pharisees fast, but your disciples do not fast?'" The questioners are identified as John's disciples -- the followers of John the Baptist, who is now imprisoned (4:12). Their question links them with the Pharisees as practitioners of regular fasting (the Pharisees fasted twice weekly, on Mondays and Thursdays, according to the Didache and later rabbinic tradition, though this practice was likely already established). The question is about practice, not doctrine: why is Jesus's community not observing what other pious communities observe?

Jesus answers with three analogies, each making the same point from a different angle.

**The bridegroom:** "Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast." The image of the bridegroom draws on deep scriptural roots. In the prophets, God's relationship to Israel is frequently described as a marriage: Hosea 2:19-20, "I will betroth you to me forever"; Isaiah 54:5, "Your Maker is your husband"; Isaiah 62:5, "As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you." By identifying himself as the bridegroom, Jesus implicitly places himself in the role that the prophets assigned to God in relation to Israel. While the bridegroom is present, mourning (and fasting as an expression of mourning) is inappropriate. The current moment is one of celebration, not lament.

But the saying contains a shadow: "The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them" (apartthe ap' auton). The verb apartthe (taken away, removed) carries a note of violence or abruptness -- this is not a peaceful departure. The author is placing in Jesus's mouth an early anticipation of his removal, which the narrative will develop explicitly beginning at 16:21. When the bridegroom is absent, fasting will resume. The current freedom from fasting is not a permanent abolition of the practice but a recognition that this particular moment -- the presence of the bridegroom -- changes everything.

**The unshrunk cloth on an old garment:** "No one puts a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, for the patch tears away from the garment, and a worse tear is made." The practical wisdom is straightforward: unshrunk (agnaphos, unfulled, not yet processed) cloth, when washed, will shrink and pull away from the old fabric, creating a larger hole than the original. The analogy is about incompatibility. What Jesus is bringing cannot simply be patched onto existing structures of piety. It is not a repair to the old system but something that, if forced into the old framework, will damage both.

**New wine in old wineskins:** "Neither is new wine put into old wineskins. If it is, the skins burst and the wine is spilled and the skins are destroyed. But new wine is put into fresh wineskins, and so both are preserved." New wine (oinos neos) is still fermenting, still expanding. Old wineskins (askois palaiois) have already been stretched to their limit by previous fermentation and have lost their elasticity. The expanding new wine will rupture them. Fresh wineskins (askous kainous) can accommodate the pressure. The conclusion -- "both are preserved" (amphoteroi synterourai) -- indicates that the concern is for preservation of both the wine and the container. The new requires a new vessel.

These three analogies together address the same question: why Jesus's community does not conform to the existing patterns of piety. The answer is not that fasting, or piety more broadly, is wrong. The answer is that the present moment is qualitatively different -- the bridegroom has arrived, something new is fermenting, and the old containers cannot hold what is now happening. The author is not presenting Jesus as anti-Torah (that was explicitly rejected at 5:17-19) but as the fulfillment that changes the shape of practice. The old forms were not wrong in themselves, but they cannot simply absorb the new reality without rupture.

## Interwoven Healings: A Ruler's Daughter and a Woman with Chronic Bleeding (vv. 18-26)

The author now presents two healing accounts nested inside each other -- an intercalation, where one story is interrupted by another and then resumed. This literary technique invites the reader to interpret each story in light of the other.

**The ruler's request (v. 18):** "While he was saying these things to them, behold, a ruler (archon) came in and knelt before him, saying, 'My daughter has just died, but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live.'" The author identifies this man simply as "a ruler" (archon), without specifying what kind of ruler. The term could refer to a synagogue ruler, a civic leader, or any person of authority. His posture -- kneeling (prosekunei, literally "doing obeisance," the same word used for worship) -- is significant. A man of social rank prostrates himself before Jesus. His statement combines desperation and faith: his daughter has died, yet he believes that Jesus's physical touch can restore her life. This is the most extreme request yet in the author's narrative -- not healing of illness but reversal of death.

**The interruption: the woman with a flow of blood (vv. 20-22):** "And behold, a woman who had suffered from a discharge of blood (haimorrosousa) for twelve years came up behind him and touched the fringe of his garment, for she said to herself, 'If I only touch his garment, I will be made well (sothesomai).'"

The woman's condition is described as a twelve-year hemorrhage. Under the purity regulations of Leviticus 15:25-27, a woman with a discharge of blood outside her normal period was ritually unclean for the duration of the condition, and anyone who touched her or anything she sat on became unclean until evening. This woman has lived in a state of perpetual ritual impurity for twelve years -- excluded from Temple worship, separated from normal social contact, unable to participate fully in community life.

She approaches from behind (opisthen) and touches "the fringe" (tou kraspedou) of his garment. The kraspedon refers to the tassels (tsitsit) prescribed in Numbers 15:38-39, which every observant Jewish man wore as a reminder of God's commandments. She touches the most specifically Torah-observant part of his clothing. Her reasoning, expressed internally ("she said to herself"), is that touching the garment alone will suffice -- she does not need Jesus to touch her (which would, under the purity system, render him unclean).

"Jesus turned, and seeing her he said, 'Take heart, daughter; your faith has saved you (he pistis sou sesoken se).'" The verb sozo means both "to save" and "to heal" -- the word carries both physical and comprehensive restoration. The perfect tense (sesoken, "has saved/healed") indicates a completed action with ongoing results. Jesus attributes her healing not to the physical contact with his garment but to her faith (pistis). "Daughter" (thygater) is a term of familial warmth -- Jesus addresses this ritually excluded woman as a member of the family.

The phrase "from that hour" (apo tes horas ekeines) marks the healing as immediate and specific. The author uses this temporal marker at key moments (from this same phrase pattern, compare 15:28, 17:18).

**The return to the ruler's daughter (vv. 23-26):** Jesus arrives at the ruler's house and finds "the flute players and the crowd making a commotion (thorybomenon)." Professional mourners and flute players were customary at funerals in first-century Palestine, even for the poor (the Mishnah, Ketubot 4:4, states that even the poorest man in Israel must provide at least two flute players and one wailing woman for his wife's funeral). Their presence confirms that the girl is truly dead and that formal mourning has begun.

Jesus says, "Go away, for the girl is not dead but sleeping (katheudei)." The mourners laugh at him (kategeleon autou) -- they know death when they see it. The verb katheudo (to sleep) does not mean Jesus is diagnosing a coma; the mourners' laughter and the context make clear the girl is dead. Rather, Jesus redefines death in light of what he is about to do. If he has the power to restore her, then her death is temporary -- functionally equivalent to sleep, from which one can be awakened.

"But when the crowd had been put outside, he went in and took her by the hand, and the girl arose (egerthe)." The verb egeiro (to arise, to raise up) is the same verb that will be used for resurrection throughout the document (it appears at 16:21, 17:9, 17:23, 20:19 for Jesus's own rising, and at 27:52 for the saints who were raised). The author uses it here for the girl's restoration to life. Jesus takes her hand -- physical contact again, as the ruler requested in verse 18. The report of this event "went through all that district" (exelthen he pheme haute eis holen ten gen ekeinen).

The two interwoven stories illuminate each other. Both involve faith that is expressed physically -- the ruler kneels, the woman touches. Both involve conditions that create ritual impurity -- death and chronic bleeding are among the most serious sources of uncleanness in Leviticus. In both cases, rather than Jesus becoming contaminated by contact with the impure, power flows in the opposite direction: the impure is made clean, the sick is healed, the dead is raised. The author is demonstrating that Jesus's authority overrides the contamination that the purity system was designed to contain. Uncleanness does not travel from the afflicted person to Jesus; wholeness travels from Jesus to the afflicted.

## Two Blind Men: The Son of David and the Demand for Faith (vv. 27-31)

"And as Jesus passed on from there, two blind men followed him, crying aloud, 'Have mercy on us, Son of David (huie Dauid).'" This is the first time in the document that anyone addresses Jesus as "Son of David." The title carries specific messianic content. In 2 Samuel 7:12-16, God promised David that his descendant would sit on his throne forever. By the first century, "Son of David" had become a recognized designation for the expected Messiah -- the king from David's line who would restore Israel (Psalms of Solomon 17:21, "Raise up for them their king, the son of David, to reign over your servant Israel"). The author established Jesus's Davidic lineage in the genealogy of chapter 1 ("Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham," 1:1) and has been building toward public recognition of this identity.

The blind men's cry "have mercy on us" (eleeson hemas) uses the same root (eleos/eleeo) that Jesus cited from Hosea 6:6 in verse 13: "I desire mercy." The ones who cry for mercy are the ones Jesus's mission is designed to reach.

"When he entered the house, the blind men came to him, and Jesus said to them, 'Do you believe that I am able to do this (pisteuete hoti dynamai touto poiesai)?' They said to him, 'Yes, Lord (nai, kyrie).'" The private setting (inside a house, away from the crowd) and the direct question about faith are noteworthy. Jesus does not heal immediately; he requires a verbal confession of trust. The address "Lord" (kyrie), on the lips of those who have just called him Son of David, combines the royal-messianic title with the term of personal submission.

"Then he touched their eyes, saying, 'According to your faith (kata ten pistin hymon) be it done to you.' And their eyes were opened." The phrase "according to your faith" makes faith the determining factor. This is consistent with the pattern across chapters 8-9: the centurion's faith brought healing to his servant (8:10, 13), the paralytic's friends' faith preceded forgiveness and healing (9:2), and the hemorrhaging woman's faith was identified as the agent of her restoration (9:22). Faith in the author's usage is not an inner feeling but active trust expressed in concrete action -- coming, kneeling, touching, following, confessing.

"And Jesus sternly warned them (enebrimesato autois), 'See that no one knows about it.'" The verb embrimaomai means to snort, to express strong emotion, to warn sharply. It conveys urgency and perhaps frustration. Jesus commands secrecy. "But they went away and spread his fame through all that district." The command is disobeyed. The author does not comment on whether this disobedience is good or bad, but records it as a fact. The tension between Jesus's desire for secrecy and the inevitable spread of his reputation runs through the narrative (compare 8:4, where the healed leper was told to show himself to the priest and tell no one else). The author may be indicating that public recognition of Jesus as Son of David is premature -- the full meaning of his messiahship has not yet been revealed, and premature acclaim based solely on miraculous power will misunderstand his mission.

## The Mute Demoniac and Two Competing Interpretations (vv. 32-34)

"As they were going away, behold, a demon-oppressed man who was mute (kophon daimonizomenon) was brought to him. And when the demon had been cast out, the mute man spoke. And the crowds marveled, saying, 'Never was anything like this seen in Israel (oudepote ephane houtos en to Israel).'"

The crowds' response is important. They do not compare Jesus to foreign wonder-workers or Greco-Roman miracle accounts. They measure him against Israel's own history -- the patriarchs, Moses, the prophets, the judges -- and find that nothing comparable has appeared. This is a sweeping claim. Israel's history includes Elijah raising a widow's son (1 Kings 17:17-24), Elisha healing Naaman's leprosy (2 Kings 5), and Moses's signs before Pharaoh. Yet the crowds say this surpasses all of it. The cumulative impact of the healings across chapters 8-9 has produced this verdict.

"But the Pharisees said, 'He casts out demons by the prince of demons (en to archonti ton daimonion).'" The Pharisees offer the opposite interpretation of the same evidence. Where the crowds see unprecedented divine action in Israel, the Pharisees see demonic collusion. This is not a minor disagreement -- it is a total inversion. The same acts that produce awe in one group produce accusation in the other. The author juxtaposes these two responses without commentary, allowing the contrast to speak for itself. The accusation will be addressed directly at 12:22-32, where Jesus responds with the argument that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand and warns about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. Here, the author simply records the charge and moves on, letting it hang in the air as the first formal expression of the interpretive division that will deepen throughout the document.

The author has been tracking two parallel responses to Jesus since chapter 8. The crowds consistently respond with amazement, fear, and glorifying God (8:27, 9:8, 9:33). The religious leaders consistently respond with suspicion and accusation (9:3 -- blasphemy; 9:11 -- eating with sinners; 9:34 -- demonic power). This division is structural, not incidental. It will culminate in the Pharisees' formal conspiracy to destroy Jesus at 12:14.

## Sheep Without a Shepherd and the Need for Laborers (vv. 35-38)

"And Jesus went throughout all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction."

This verse is nearly identical to 4:23: "And he went throughout all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction among the people." The repetition creates a literary bracket (inclusio) around the material from 4:23 to 9:35. Everything between these two summary statements -- the mountain discourse (chapters 5-7) and the demonstration of authority through deeds (chapters 8-9) -- is encompassed. The teaching and healing ministry described in summary at 4:23 has now been shown in detail, and the author returns to the summary to close the section and prepare for the next movement.

"When he saw the crowds, he had compassion (esplagchnisthe) for them, because they were harassed (eskulmenoi) and helpless (errimmenoi), like sheep without a shepherd (hosei probata me echonta poimena)."

The verb splagchnizomai (to be moved with compassion) derives from splagchna, the internal organs -- the bowels, the gut. It describes visceral, physical compassion, not a mild sentiment. The author uses this word at decisive moments in the narrative (14:14, 15:32, 20:34). Each time, it describes Jesus's response to human suffering and precedes an act of intervention.

The description of the crowds as "harassed and helpless" uses two participles. Eskulmenoi (from skyllo, to flay, to skin, to trouble, to harass) suggests people being mangled or worn down. Errimmenoi (from rhipto, to throw down, to cast aside) suggests people who have been thrown to the ground and abandoned. The image is of people who have been battered and discarded.

The comparison to "sheep without a shepherd" is a direct allusion to Numbers 27:17, where Moses asks God to appoint a successor "that the congregation of the LORD may not be as sheep that have no shepherd." God's response was to appoint Joshua (whose name, Yehoshua, is the Hebrew form of the name Jesus/Iesous). The connection between the shepherd-less sheep of Numbers and the commissioning that follows in Matthew 10 is structurally parallel: as Moses's concern for leaderless Israel led to Joshua's appointment, so Jesus's compassion for the harassed crowds leads to the commissioning of the twelve.

But the image also evokes Ezekiel 34, where God indicts the shepherds of Israel (the leaders, kings, and priests) who have fed themselves instead of the flock: "You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones, but you do not feed the sheep. The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought" (Ezekiel 34:3-4). God's response in Ezekiel was to declare: "I myself will search for my sheep... I will rescue them... I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall feed them" (Ezekiel 34:11, 12, 23). When Jesus, the Son of David (as the blind men just declared), looks at crowds who are like sheep without a shepherd, the author is activating the Ezekiel 34 promise. The failure of Israel's current leaders (the scribes and Pharisees, whose opposition has been on display throughout the chapter) is precisely the condition Ezekiel described, and Jesus's compassion is the fulfillment of God's promise to shepherd his people through his servant David's heir.

"Then he said to his disciples, 'The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore pray earnestly to the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.'"

The metaphor shifts from shepherding to harvest. The harvest (therismos) is "plentiful" (polys, much, great) -- there are vast numbers of people ready to be gathered. But the laborers (ergatai, workers) are few (oligoi). The disproportion between the need and the available workforce creates the urgency.

The instruction is to pray -- not to recruit, not to organize, but to petition "the Lord of the harvest" (tou kyriou tou therismou) to "send out" (ekbale) laborers. The verb ekballo is strong -- it means to cast out, to thrust out, to drive out. It is the same word used for casting out demons (9:33, 34). The Lord of the harvest does not gently invite laborers; he drives them out into the field. The harvest will not wait, and those who go will not go at their own initiative but because they have been thrust out by God.

The disciples are told to pray for this, and in the very next verse (10:1), Jesus summons them and sends them out. They pray for laborers and immediately become the answer to their own prayer. The author structures this so that the prayer in 9:38 and the commissioning in 10:1 are inseparable -- the prayer for workers and the sending of workers are the same act viewed from the divine and human sides.

## The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **It escalates the demonstration of authority from physical to spiritual.** Chapter 8 showed authority over disease, nature, and demons. Chapter 9 opens with authority over sin (vv. 1-8), which is the most contested claim yet and the one that provokes the first charge of blasphemy. The author is building a cumulative case: Jesus's authority has no boundary. Disease, death, impurity, demons, and sin all yield to it. The question the narrative increasingly presses is not whether Jesus has authority but what his hearers will do with it.

2. **It defines Jesus's mission as directed toward sinners and the excluded.** The calling of Matthew, the meal with tax collectors and sinners, the Hosea 6:6 citation, and the physician metaphor all converge on a single point: Jesus did not come for those who consider themselves righteous but for those who know they are not. This is not an abstract theological principle but a description of Jesus's observable behavior -- where he goes, whom he eats with, whom he calls.

3. **It tracks the growing division between crowds and leaders.** The crowds glorify God (v. 8), marvel at what has never been seen in Israel (v. 33), and are the objects of Jesus's compassion (v. 36). The scribes charge blasphemy (v. 3), the Pharisees question his associations (v. 11) and attribute his power to demons (v. 34). The author is constructing a narrative in which the people who should recognize God's action in Israel are the ones who refuse it, while those on the margins respond with faith.

4. **It establishes faith as the operative condition for receiving what Jesus offers.** The paralytic's friends' faith (v. 2), the hemorrhaging woman's faith (v. 22), and the blind men's faith (v. 29) are all explicitly identified as the basis for healing. Faith in this chapter is consistently enacted -- it takes the form of bringing someone, touching, following, confessing. It is not an internal state but an outward movement toward Jesus, trusting that he has the authority to act.

5. **It presents Jesus through images that the prophets assigned to God.** The bridegroom (v. 15) recalls Hosea 2 and Isaiah 62, where God is Israel's husband. The shepherd (v. 36) recalls Ezekiel 34, where God promises to shepherd his people through his servant David. The physician (v. 12) recalls Exodus 15:26, where God declares "I am the LORD, your healer." The author does not make these identifications explicit in a declarative statement; he embeds them in Jesus's self-descriptions and allows the first-century reader steeped in these texts to draw the conclusion.

6. **It transitions from demonstration to delegation.** The chapter closes with the recognition that the harvest is too great for one laborer. Jesus has been demonstrating what the kingdom looks like in action -- now the work must be multiplied. The prayer for laborers in 9:38 is the hinge that turns the narrative from Jesus acting alone to Jesus sending others to act in his name, which is the subject of chapter 10. The authority demonstrated in chapters 8-9 will now be shared.
