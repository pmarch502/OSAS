# 1 John 3 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (1 John 2):** Chapter 2 opened by addressing the tension between the community's goal of not sinning and the reality that sin occurs: Jesus Christ the righteous serves as advocate (parakletos) before the Father and is himself the atonement (hilasmos) for sins -- not for the community's sins only but for the whole world (2:1-2). The author then developed three tests by which genuine fellowship with God can be recognized. The first test was obedience: the one who claims to know God but does not keep his commandments is a liar (2:3-6). The second test was love: the one who claims to be in the light but hates his brother is in darkness (2:7-11). After reassuring the community of what they already possess -- forgiveness, knowledge, strength, and victory over the evil one (2:12-14) -- the author warned against loving the world and its passing desires (2:15-17). He then addressed the crisis that defines the letter's occasion: people have departed the community, denying that Jesus is the Christ, and the author identifies them as "antichrists" whose departure revealed they were never genuinely part of the fellowship (2:18-23). The community's safeguard is to remain in the original message and in the anointing (the Spirit) they already possess (2:24-27). The chapter concluded with an exhortation to abide in Christ so as to have confidence at his appearing, and with the foundational principle that hands the argument to chapter 3: "If you know that he is righteous, you know that everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him" (2:29). The phrase "born of him" (ex autou gegennetai) opens the door to the exploration of divine parentage that chapter 3 will develop at length.

**What the author argues immediately after (1 John 4):** Chapter 4 opens with a warning to "test the spirits" (4:1) -- not every spirit is from God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world. The criterion for testing is christological: "every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God, and every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not from God; this is the spirit of the antichrist" (4:2-3). This connects directly to the christological denial addressed in 2:22-23 and the "spirit of error" versus "spirit of truth" distinction introduced at 4:6. The author then returns to the love commandment: "Beloved, let us love one another, for love is from God, and everyone who loves has been born of God and knows God. The one who does not love does not know God, because God is love" (4:7-8). The language of divine birth ("born of God") and the ethical test of love continue the argument of chapter 3 without interruption. The author then grounds the command to love in God's initiative: God sent his Son as hilasmos for sins (4:10, echoing 2:2), and if God so loved us, we ought to love one another (4:11). The chapter culminates in the declaration that love is perfected in the community so that they may have confidence in the day of judgment (4:17), and that "perfect love casts out fear" (4:18). The closing test is stated bluntly: "If someone says 'I love God' and hates his brother, he is a liar" (4:20).

**How chapter 3 connects these:** Chapter 2 ended by establishing the principle that practicing righteousness is the evidence of having been born of God (2:29). Chapter 4 will develop the theological basis of love (God is love, God sent his Son) and apply the love test with finality. Chapter 3 is the bridge: it takes the concept of divine birth from 2:29 and expands it into a full exploration of what it means to be "children of God" (tekna theou), contrasting them with "children of the devil" (tekna tou diabolou). The chapter develops both tests -- righteousness and love -- to their sharpest point, using the categories of parentage and family resemblance to explain why conduct reveals identity. The righteous practice of those born of God (3:1-10) and the love that characterizes God's children (3:11-24) are the two pillars of chapter 3, and both flow directly from chapter 2's closing principle while preparing the ground for chapter 4's climactic treatment of love.

## Verses 1-3: The Identity and Hope of God's Children

"See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God -- and so we are! The reason the world does not know us is that it did not know him. Beloved, we are God's children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. And everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure."

**"See what kind of love the Father has given to us" (idete potapen agapen dedoken hemin ho pater).** The exclamatory idete ("see! look! consider!") calls the community to pause and contemplate something remarkable. The adjective potapen ("what kind of, what manner of, how great") expresses astonishment at the quality or nature of the love, not merely its quantity. In the LXX and in first-century Greek usage, potapos conveys wonder: "what sort of thing is this?" (cf. Mark 13:1, where the disciples exclaim, "Look, Teacher, what wonderful stones and what wonderful buildings!" -- potapoi lithoi kai potapai oikodomai). The perfect tense dedoken ("has given") indicates a completed action whose effects remain: the Father's love has been given and continues to be given. Love here is not merely an emotion or attitude but an action -- it has been "given" (dedoken), bestowed as a gift. The love is from "the Father" (ho pater), and its character is defined by what it produces.

**"That we should be called children of God -- and so we are!" (hina tekna theou klethomen, kai esmen).** The hina clause identifies the content or result of the Father's love: the purpose of the love is that the community should be designated "children of God" (tekna theou). The verb klethomen ("should be called") could be merely nominal -- a title given without corresponding reality. But the author immediately adds kai esmen ("and so we are!" or "and we are!"), insisting that the designation is not empty. The community is not merely called children of God; they actually are children of God. The exclamation presses the point: this is not honorary language but a statement about real identity.

The term tekna ("children") is the author's characteristic word for believers in their relationship to God. It differs from huioi ("sons"), which carries connotations of legal standing and inheritance rights. Tekna emphasizes origin, birth, and organic connection to the parent. The word has appeared at 2:29 in the phrase "born of him" (ex autou gegennetai), and the author now develops the birth imagery into a full treatment of parentage and family identity. In the Fourth Gospel, the prologue states: "But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become children of God (tekna theou), to those who believe in his name, who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:12-13). The concept is the same: divine parentage produces children whose origin is God, not human agency.

**"The reason the world does not know us is that it did not know him" (dia touto ho kosmos ou ginoskei hemas, hoti ouk egno auton).** The "world" (kosmos) here carries the sense established in 2:15-17: the system of values and priorities organized against God. The world's failure to recognize the community as God's children is explained by its prior failure to know God ("him," auton -- referring to the Father, or possibly to Christ, since knowing the Father and knowing the Son are inseparable in this author's theology). The Fourth Gospel expressed the same reality: "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you" (John 15:18-19). The community's experience of alienation from the surrounding culture is interpreted not as failure but as confirmation: they are not recognized because they belong to someone the world does not know.

**"Beloved, we are God's children now" (agapetoi, nyn tekna theou esmen).** The address agapetoi ("beloved") signals pastoral warmth and shifts into reassurance. The temporal adverb nyn ("now") is emphatic: the status of being God's children is a present reality, not merely a future hope. Whatever the community will become, they are already children of God.

**"And what we will be has not yet appeared" (kai oupo ephanerosthe ti esometha).** The verb phaneroo ("to appear, to be made manifest") is the same verb used at 2:28 for Christ's future appearing and at 1:2 for the manifestation of the life. The community's ultimate identity has not yet been fully revealed. There is an eschatological dimension to their identity that remains hidden -- a "not yet" that complements the "now" of the previous clause. The tension between "now" and "not yet" is fundamental to this author's understanding of the community's existence: they are already children of God, but the full expression of what that means is still to come.

**"But we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (oidamen hoti ean phanerosthe, homoioi auto esometha, hoti opsometha auton kathos estin).** The "he" whose appearing is anticipated is most naturally Christ, given the reference to his appearing (parousia) at 2:28. The promise is twofold: the community will be "like him" (homoioi auto) and will "see him as he is" (opsometha auton kathos estin). The relationship between likeness and sight is stated as causal: "because we shall see him" (hoti opsometha). Seeing him produces likeness, or alternatively, the capacity to see him as he is requires and entails being like him. In Israel's tradition, seeing God was both a supreme hope and a fearful prospect. Moses was told, "You cannot see my face, for no one may see me and live" (Exodus 33:20), yet he was permitted to see God's "back" (Exodus 33:23). The Psalmist expresses the longing: "As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness" (Psalm 17:15, LXX 16:15). The eschatological hope expressed here is that the barrier will be removed: when Christ appears, the children of God will see him without mediation and will be transformed into his likeness.

**"And everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure" (kai pas ho echon ten elpida tauten ep' auto hagnizei heauton, kathos ekeinos hagnos estin).** The demonstrative pronoun ekeinos ("that one") refers to Christ, as it does consistently in this letter (2:6, 3:5, 7, 16). The eschatological hope produces a present ethical consequence: the one who hopes for the future transformation actively purifies himself now. The verb hagnizo ("to purify") belongs to the cultic vocabulary of the LXX. In Numbers 19:12, the one who has been contaminated by contact with a dead body "shall purify himself" (hagnisthsetai). In Exodus 19:10, God tells Moses, "Go to the people and consecrate them (hagnison autous) today and tomorrow, and let them wash their garments." In 2 Chronicles 29:5, the Levites are commanded: "Consecrate yourselves (hagnisthete), and consecrate the house of the LORD." The term describes the process of making oneself ritually and morally fit for the presence of God. The standard is stated: "as he [ekeinos, Christ] is pure" (kathos ekeinos hagnos estin). Christ's own purity is the measure. The logic is: if the hope is to become like him when he appears, then the present response is to begin the process of becoming like him now. Hope is not passive waiting but active conformity to the one hoped for.

## Verses 4-6: Sin, Lawlessness, and Abiding

"Everyone who practices sin also practices lawlessness; sin is lawlessness. You know that he appeared in order to take away sins, and in him there is no sin. No one who abides in him keeps on sinning; no one who keeps on sinning has either seen him or known him."

**"Everyone who practices sin also practices lawlessness; sin is lawlessness" (pas ho poion ten hamartian kai ten anomian poiei, kai he hamartia estin he anomia).** The verb poion (present participle of poieo, "to do, to practice") indicates habitual action -- a pattern of life, not an isolated act. The author identifies sin (hamartia) with lawlessness (anomia). The word anomia is composed of the negative prefix a- and nomos ("law"), meaning literally "without law" or "against law." In the LXX, anomia is a frequent translation of the Hebrew 'awon ("iniquity") and pesha' ("transgression"): Psalm 51:1 (50:1 LXX), "Blot out my transgressions (anomias)"; Psalm 32:1 (31:1 LXX), "Blessed is the one whose transgression (anomia) is forgiven." Isaiah 53:5-6 uses anomia in parallel with hamartia: "He was wounded for our transgressions (anomias)... the LORD has laid on him the iniquity (anomian) of us all."

The identification of sin with lawlessness -- "sin is lawlessness" (he hamartia estin he anomia) -- is not a casual equation but a definitional statement. For this author, sin is not merely a failure to reach a standard or an accidental misstep; it is active rebellion against God's order, a refusal to live within the boundaries God has established. The "commandments" (entolai) that the author has been discussing since 2:3 are the standard; sin as anomia is the violation of that standard. This definition counters any teaching that would divorce the community's spiritual status from ethical obligation. If someone claims to be born of God while practicing lawlessness, the author's definition exposes the contradiction: sin is not a minor matter that can coexist with fellowship; it is the very thing that defines opposition to God's rule.

**"You know that he appeared in order to take away sins" (kai oidate hoti ekeinos ephanerothe hina tas hamartias are).** The demonstrative ekeinos ("that one") again refers to Christ. The verb phaneroo ("to appear, to be manifested") connects to its uses in 1:2 (the life was manifested), 3:2 (when he appears), and 3:8 (he was manifested to destroy the works of the devil). Christ's appearing -- his incarnation and ministry -- had a specific purpose: "to take away sins" (hina tas hamartias are). The verb airo means "to lift up, to take away, to remove." It is the same verb used by the Baptist in the Fourth Gospel: "Behold, the Lamb of God who takes away (ho airon) the sin of the world" (John 1:29). The image is of removal, of carrying away -- the sins are not merely covered or overlooked but taken away. The Day of Atonement ritual provides the most vivid precedent: the scapegoat upon which the high priest confessed the sins of Israel was sent away into the wilderness, "bearing on itself all their iniquities to a remote area" (Leviticus 16:22, LXX: kai lepsetai ho chimaros eph' heauto tas adikias auton). The sins were carried away, removed from the camp, so that the people could dwell in the presence of a holy God. Christ's manifestation fulfills this function: he appeared to carry sins away.

**"And in him there is no sin" (kai hamartia en auto ouk estin).** This statement about Christ's sinlessness parallels 1:5: "God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all." The construction is identical in form: the negative existential declaration with emphatic denial. As there is no darkness in God, there is no sin in Christ. This is not merely a factual statement about Jesus's moral record but a statement about his nature as it relates to his mission. He is qualified to take away sins because he has none of his own. The sinless advocate (2:1, "Jesus Christ the righteous") is also the sinless sacrifice.

**"No one who abides in him keeps on sinning" (pas ho en auto menon ouch hamartanei).** The present participle menon ("abiding") and the present indicative hamartanei ("sins, keeps on sinning") are both in the present tense, indicating ongoing states. The author's claim is that the person who is continually abiding in Christ does not continually practice sin. The verb meno ("to abide, to remain") carries its full Johannine weight: active, sustained, living connection with Christ, as described in John 15:4-10. The claim is not that a person who abides in Christ will never commit any act of sin (the author has already provided for that in 2:1-2 and 1:8-9). The claim is that habitual, ongoing sin and genuine abiding in Christ are incompatible. The two states -- abiding and continuing in sin -- cannot coexist.

This statement must be read in continuity with the author's prior argument. In 2:1, the author wrote: "My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father." The goal is not sinning; the provision exists for when sin occurs. In 1:8, the author stated that claiming to have no sin is self-deception. The author is not contradicting himself in 3:6. The difference is between the person who sins and confesses (addressed in 1:9 and 2:1) and the person who practices sin as a way of life (addressed here). The first acknowledges sin and turns to the advocate; the second makes sin a habitual pattern, which reveals that the person is not genuinely abiding in Christ.

**"No one who keeps on sinning has either seen him or known him" (pas ho hamartanon ouch heoraken auton oude egnoken auton).** The present participle hamartanon ("sinning, continuing to sin") again indicates habitual practice. The person who lives in a pattern of ongoing sin has neither "seen" (heoraken, perfect of horao) nor "known" (egnoken, perfect of ginosko) Christ. The perfect tenses indicate a past experience with continuing results -- or in this case, the absence of such experience. The person who habitually sins reveals that they never truly saw or knew Christ. This echoes 2:4: "The one who says 'I have come to know him' and does not keep his commandments is a liar." The test is the same: knowledge of Christ is verified by conduct. Ongoing sin falsifies the claim to knowledge.

## Verses 7-10: The Children of God and the Children of the Devil

"Little children, let no one deceive you. Whoever practices righteousness is righteous, as he is righteous. Whoever makes a practice of sinning is of the devil, for the devil has been sinning from the beginning. The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil. No one born of God makes a practice of sinning, for God's seed abides in him; and he cannot keep on sinning, because he has been born of God. By this it is evident who are the children of God, and who are the children of the devil: whoever does not practice righteousness is not of God, nor is the one who does not love his brother."

**"Little children, let no one deceive you" (teknia, medeis planato hymas).** The verb planao ("to deceive, to lead astray") is the same word used in 2:26 for the false teachers' activity ("those who are trying to deceive you"). The warning implies that someone -- presumably the departed teachers or their influence -- is promoting a teaching that divorces spiritual status from ethical conduct. The author counters this deception directly.

**"Whoever practices righteousness is righteous, as he is righteous" (ho poion ten dikaiosynen dikaios estin, kathos ekeinos dikaios estin).** The statement is emphatically simple: the righteous person is identified by the practice of righteousness, and the standard is Christ's own righteousness (ekeinos, "that one," referring to Christ as in 2:6, 3:3, 5). The logic is the same as 2:29: the child bears the family resemblance of the parent. If Christ is righteous, those born of him practice righteousness. The deception the author warns against would apparently claim that one can be "righteous" (in some spiritual or positional sense) without actually practicing righteousness. The author rejects this: righteousness is demonstrated, not merely claimed.

**"Whoever makes a practice of sinning is of the devil" (ho poion ten hamartian ek tou diabolou estin).** The preposition ek ("of, from, out of") indicates origin. The person whose life is characterized by the practice of sin has their origin in the devil -- they belong to the devil's family, not God's. The article ho poion with the present participle again indicates habitual action. The author is not describing someone who occasionally sins (for whom 1:9 and 2:1-2 provide the remedy) but someone whose life-pattern is defined by sin.

**"For the devil has been sinning from the beginning" (hoti ap' arches ho diabolos hamartanei).** The phrase "from the beginning" (ap' arches) here refers to the beginning of the devil's activity -- from the earliest point of human history. The present tense hamartanei ("sins, has been sinning") with ap' arches ("from the beginning") creates a construction indicating continuous action from a starting point. The devil's character has been consistent from the start. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus identified the devil's nature in the same terms: "He was a murderer from the beginning (ap' arches), and does not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he lies, he speaks out of his own character, for he is a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44). The Genesis narrative provides the historical referent: the serpent's deception of Eve (Genesis 3:1-6) is the "beginning" of the devil's sinning. The Wisdom of Solomon (2:24) states: "Through the devil's envy death entered the world." The devil's defining characteristic is sin, and it has been so from the very start.

The logic of the parentage metaphor is now clear. Just as the one who practices righteousness reveals parentage from God (who is righteous), the one who practices sin reveals parentage from the devil (who has been sinning from the beginning). Children bear the marks of their parent. The test is behavioral, not verbal: what a person does reveals whose child they are.

**"The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil" (eis touto ephanerothe ho huios tou theou, hina lyse ta erga tou diabolou).** This is the second purpose statement about Christ's manifestation in this chapter: in verse 5, he appeared "to take away sins"; here, he appeared "to destroy the works of the devil." The verb lyo means "to loose, to dissolve, to destroy, to undo." The "works of the devil" (ta erga tou diabolou) encompass sin, deception, murder, and the entire pattern of rebellion against God that the devil has practiced "from the beginning." Christ's manifestation is not merely remedial (dealing with sins after they occur) but combative (attacking and dismantling the devil's entire enterprise). The Son of God appeared to undo what the devil has done. This gives the ethical exhortations their eschatological weight: the community that practices sin is aligning itself with the very works that Christ came to destroy. To continue in sin is to side with the enemy whose works Christ is demolishing.

**"No one born of God makes a practice of sinning, for God's seed abides in him" (pas ho gegennomenos ek tou theou hamartian ou poiei, hoti sperma autou en auto menei).** The perfect passive participle gegennomenos ("having been born") indicates a completed birth with ongoing effects. The person who has been born of God does not practice sin (present tense, habitual action). The reason given is remarkable: "God's seed abides in him" (sperma autou en auto menei). The word sperma ("seed") is used metaphorically for the divine principle of life that has been implanted in the believer through the new birth. The word sperma in the LXX most commonly refers to physical offspring or descendants (Genesis 3:15, "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed"; Genesis 12:7, "To your seed I will give this land"). Here it describes the divine life-principle itself -- what God has planted in the one who has been born of him. The indwelling of God's seed is what makes habitual sin incompatible with the new birth. The seed of God, abiding in the believer, produces a nature that resists and is contrary to sin.

The verb menei ("abides") connects this to the entire abiding theology of the letter: the word of God abides in the young men (2:14), the anointing abides in the community (2:27), the one who does the will of God abides forever (2:17), and now God's seed abides in the one born of God. The indwelling divine principle is the internal reality that makes the external practice of righteousness possible.

**"And he cannot keep on sinning, because he has been born of God" (kai ou dynatai hamartanein, hoti ek tou theou gegennetai).** The phrase ou dynatai hamartanein ("he is not able to sin" or "he cannot keep on sinning") is the author's strongest statement. The present infinitive hamartanein indicates continuous action: the impossibility is of ongoing, habitual sinning. The reason is the divine birth itself: "because he has been born of God" (hoti ek tou theou gegennetai). The new birth has produced a new nature that is incompatible with a life defined by sin. This does not mean individual acts of sin are impossible (1:8-10 and 2:1-2 have already addressed that reality), but that a settled pattern of sinful living is incompatible with genuine divine parentage. The parent's nature manifests in the child. God's children cannot live as though they are the devil's children.

**"By this it is evident who are the children of God, and who are the children of the devil" (en touto phanera estin ta tekna tou theou kai ta tekna tou diabolou).** The word phanera ("evident, manifest, visible") indicates that the distinction between God's children and the devil's children is observable, not hidden. The criterion that makes it observable is stated next.

**"Whoever does not practice righteousness is not of God, nor is the one who does not love his brother" (pas ho me poion dikaiosynen ouk estin ek tou theou, kai ho me agapon ton adelphon autou).** The two criteria are stated together: the practice of righteousness and love for one's brother. These are the two tests that the author has been developing since chapter 2 (obedience in 2:3-6, love in 2:7-11) and that will continue through chapter 4. They are not separate tests but two aspects of the same reality: the person who is truly born of God will both practice righteousness and love the community. The absence of either one reveals that the person is "not of God" (ouk estin ek tou theou). The preposition ek ("of, from") again indicates origin: the person does not originate from God, does not belong to God's family. This verse serves as a hinge: the first half (practicing righteousness) summarizes verses 4-9; the second half (loving one's brother) introduces the argument of verses 11-18.

## Verses 11-15: The Message from the Beginning -- Love One Another

"For this is the message that you have heard from the beginning, that we should love one another. We should not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous. Do not be surprised, brothers, that the world hates you. We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers. Whoever does not love abides in death. Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer, and you know that no murderer has eternal life abiding in him."

**"For this is the message that you have heard from the beginning, that we should love one another" (hoti haute estin he angelia hen ekousate ap' arches, hina agapomen allelous).** The word angelia ("message") appears only twice in the entire New Testament: here and at 1:5. In 1:5, the message was: "God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all." Here, the message is: "that we should love one another." The author treats both as the message -- the same foundational communication, two expressions of the same reality. God's nature as light produces the demand for love among his children. The "from the beginning" (ap' arches) refers, as in 2:7 and 2:24, to the original proclamation the community received when they first believed. The command to love one another is not new teaching but the original instruction -- echoing Jesus's words at John 13:34: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another."

**"We should not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother" (ou kathos Kain ek tou ponerou en kai esphaxen ton adelphon autou).** Cain is the first concrete example in the letter -- the first named figure from Israel's history. He is introduced as the paradigmatic negative example: he was "of the evil one" (ek tou ponerou, with ek again indicating origin), and he "murdered" (esphaxen, from sphazo, "to slaughter, to slay") his brother. The verb sphazo is a vivid word: it is used in the LXX for the slaughter of sacrificial animals (Leviticus 1:5, 11; 3:2, 8, 13; 4:4, 15, 24, 29, 33) and for violent killing (1 Samuel 22:21 LXX; Judges 12:6 LXX). The choice of this violent, almost butchery-like verb intensifies the horror of Cain's act.

The Genesis narrative (Genesis 4:1-16) provides the background. Cain and Abel both brought offerings to the LORD; God accepted Abel's offering but not Cain's. Cain responded with anger, and despite God's warning ("sin is crouching at the door; its desire is for you, but you must rule over it," Genesis 4:7), Cain rose up against his brother and killed him. The author of 1 John interprets Cain's parentage: he was "of the evil one" (ek tou ponerou). This connects to 2:13-14, where the young men have "overcome the evil one" (ton poneron), and to the Fourth Gospel's identification of the devil as "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44). Cain acted in accordance with the devil's nature -- murder -- and thus showed whose child he was. The parentage test applies retroactively: Cain's murder revealed his true parent.

**"And why did he murder him? Because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous" (kai charin tinos esphaxen auton? hoti ta erga autou ponera en, ta de tou adelphou autou dikaia).** The author provides the motive: the contrast between Cain's evil deeds and Abel's righteous deeds. The righteous conduct of Abel provoked Cain's hostility. This is not merely historical exegesis; it is paradigmatic for the community's situation. The world hates the community for the same reason Cain hated Abel: the righteousness of the one exposes and condemns the evil of the other. The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 4:8 preserves a tradition in which Cain and Abel argued before the murder, with Cain denying divine judgment and Abel affirming it -- the conflict was theological and ethical before it became physical.

**"Do not be surprised, brothers, that the world hates you" (me thaumazete, adelphoi, ei misei hymas ho kosmos).** The transitional me thaumazete ("do not be surprised") connects Cain's hatred to the world's hatred of the community. The logic is: what happened between Cain and Abel is what happens between the world and you. The world's hatred is not surprising but predictable, because the dynamic is the same -- evil hates what is righteous. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus told his disciples to expect this: "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you" (John 15:18). The address adelphoi ("brothers") emphasizes the communal solidarity of those who face the world's hostility together.

**"We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers" (hemeis oidamen hoti metabebekamen ek tou thanatou eis ten zoen, hoti agapomen tous adelphous).** The verb metabaino ("to pass over, to cross from one place to another") appears in the perfect tense (metabebekamen), indicating a completed transition with lasting results. The same verb appears in Jesus's declaration at John 5:24: "Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed (metabebeken) from death to life." The verb describes a decisive crossing from one realm to another -- from the domain of death to the domain of life. The author's assertion is that this transition has already occurred for those who love the brothers.

The evidence of the transition is love: "because we love the brothers" (hoti agapomen tous adelphous). Love for the community is the observable marker that confirms the passage from death to life. This is not a statement that love earns the passage but that love demonstrates it. The child of God loves because they have been born of a loving Father; the presence of love reveals that the birth has occurred and the passage has been made.

**"Whoever does not love abides in death" (ho me agapon menei en to thanato).** The negative is stated with equal force. The verb menei ("abides, remains") -- the same verb that describes the believer's relationship with Christ -- here describes the non-lover's relationship with death. The person who does not love is not passing from death to life; they remain fixed in the domain of death. The verb's present tense indicates an ongoing state. Love is the dividing line between life and death -- not love as sentiment but love as active, practiced commitment to the community of faith.

**"Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer" (pas ho mison ton adelphon autou anthropoktonos estin).** The word anthropoktonos ("murderer," literally "man-killer") appears only twice in the New Testament: here and in John 8:44, where Jesus says of the devil, "He was a murderer (anthropoktonos) from the beginning." The author draws a direct line from hatred to murder: the one who hates is already a murderer in character, even if the physical act has not occurred. This connects back to the Cain narrative: Cain's murder was the outward expression of an inward hatred that already existed. The hatred is the murder in seed form. Jesus taught the same principle in the Sermon on the Mount: "Everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:22). The author of 1 John makes it explicit: hatred is murder in essence, and the one who harbors it shares the devil's defining characteristic.

**"And you know that no murderer has eternal life abiding in him" (kai oidate hoti pas anthropoktonos ouk echei zoen aionion en auto menousan).** Eternal life (zoe aionios) is something that "abides" (menousan, present participle of meno) in the believer. The author has already defined eternal life as the promise connected to abiding in the Son and the Father (2:25). Here the negative is stated: the murderer -- and therefore the one who hates -- does not have eternal life abiding in him. The present participle menousan makes the point that eternal life is not a static possession but an abiding reality that can be present or absent. The person who hates has expelled it. This is a direct application of the parentage test: the murderer belongs to the devil's family (like Cain), and the devil's family does not possess the life that belongs to God's family.

## Verses 16-18: The Definition of Love -- Laying Down One's Life

"By this we know love, that he laid down his life for us, and we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers. But if anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him? Little children, let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth."

**"By this we know love, that he laid down his life for us" (en touto egnokamen ten agapen, hoti ekeinos hyper hemon ten psychen autou etheken).** The demonstrative ekeinos ("that one") once more identifies Christ. The author defines love not by abstract description but by concrete action: Christ "laid down his life" (ten psychen autou etheken). The phrase echoes the Good Shepherd discourse in the Fourth Gospel: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life (ten psychen autou tithsin) for the sheep" (John 10:11). "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18). "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life (ten psychen autou the) for his friends" (John 15:13). Love, for this author, is defined by self-sacrifice. The perfect tense egnokamen ("we have come to know") indicates that knowledge of love arrived through Christ's act and remains as settled knowledge.

The word psyche (typically translated "life" or "soul") here means one's entire existence, one's very self. Christ gave his entire self for the community. The preposition hyper ("for, on behalf of") indicates substitutionary or representative action: Christ's death was for the benefit of the community.

**"And we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers" (kai hemeis opheilomen hyper ton adelphon tas psychas theinai).** The verb opheilomen ("we ought, we are obligated") is the same word used at 2:6: the one who claims to abide in Christ "ought" to walk as Christ walked. The obligation arises from the paradigm: Christ laid down his life for us; therefore we are obligated to do the same for one another. The "brothers" (ton adelphon) are the fellow members of the community. The standard is radical: love is not merely affection or good wishes but willingness to surrender one's life for others. The author is not describing an occasional heroic act but the disposition that should characterize the community -- a readiness to sacrifice self for the other.

**"But if anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him?" (hos d' an eche ton bion tou kosmou kai theore ton adelphon autou chreian echonta kai kleise ta splanchna autou ap' autou, pos he agape tou theou menei en auto?)** The author immediately grounds the radical command in ordinary, practical reality. The move from laying down one's life (v. 16) to sharing material possessions (v. 17) is not a reduction but an application: if you are unwilling to share your goods, how much less would you lay down your life? The word bios ("livelihood, material resources, means of life") was used at 2:16 for "the pride of life" (he alazoneia tou biou) -- the material resources that the world values. The word splanchna ("inward parts, bowels, heart") in the LXX and in first-century usage denoted the seat of compassion and deep emotion (cf. LXX Genesis 43:30, where Joseph's splanchna were moved at seeing Benjamin). To "close one's splanchna" (kleise ta splanchna) is to shut off compassion, to deliberately withhold the natural response of pity.

The rhetorical question "how does God's love abide in him?" (pos he agape tou theou menei en auto?) expects a negative answer: it does not. The love of God -- whether understood as God's love dwelling in the person (subjective genitive) or the love that belongs to God and characterizes his nature (qualitative genitive) -- cannot coexist with the deliberate withholding of material help from a brother in need. The verb menei ("abide") connects to the entire abiding theology: if God's love does not abide in the person, the person is not genuinely abiding in God.

This passage finds a close parallel in James 2:15-16: "If a brother or sister is without clothing and in need of daily food, and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and be filled,' and yet you do not give them what is necessary for their body, what use is that?" Both authors make the same argument: love that does not act is not love. The Deuteronomic tradition also commanded practical generosity: "If there is among you a poor man, one of your brothers, in any of your towns... you shall not harden your heart or shut your hand against your poor brother, but you shall open your hand to him" (Deuteronomy 15:7-8). The author stands in this tradition: love is demonstrated in material action, not in words alone.

**"Little children, let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth" (teknia, me agapomen logo mede glosse alla ergo kai aletheia).** The exhortation distills the argument: love is verified by deeds (ergo, "in work, in action") and truth (aletheia), not by speech (logo, "in word") or tongue (glosse, "in talk"). This echoes the author's consistent method throughout the letter: verbal claims are tested against observable conduct. "The one who says 'I have come to know him' and does not keep his commandments is a liar" (2:4). "If we say that we have fellowship with him and yet walk in the darkness, we lie" (1:6). Now: if we say we love but do not act, the love is empty. Truth and action go together; words without deeds are falsehood.

## Verses 19-22: Confidence Before God

"By this we shall know that we are of the truth and reassure our heart before him; for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything. Beloved, if our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God; and whatever we ask we receive from him, because we keep his commandments and do what pleases him."

**"By this we shall know that we are of the truth" (en touto gnosometha hoti ek tes aletheias esmen).** The phrase "by this" (en touto) refers back to the preceding exhortation: by loving in deed and in truth (v. 18), we shall know that we belong to the truth. The preposition ek ("of, from") indicates origin: "we are of the truth" means we originate from truth, we belong to the truth's domain. The knowledge is experiential: active love produces the settled awareness of one's genuine standing before God.

**"And reassure our heart before him" (kai emprosthen autou peisomen ten kardian hemon).** The verb peitho in the future active (peisomen) means "to persuade, to reassure, to set at ease." The "heart" (kardia) in biblical usage is the center of the whole person -- will, intellect, and emotion. The community's hearts need reassurance because the sharp tests the author has been issuing (practice righteousness or you are not of God; love or you remain in death) could produce anxiety. The author now addresses this: when love is genuine -- practiced in deed and truth -- the heart is reassured before God. The practice of love produces internal confidence.

**"For whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything" (hoti ean kataginoske hemon he kardia, hoti meizn estin ho theos tes kardias hemon kai ginoske panta).** This verse is among the most debated in the letter, not because of textual uncertainty but because of the direction of the argument. The verb kataginosko means "to condemn, to find fault with, to accuse." The heart condemns -- it accuses the believer, producing guilt and doubt. The author's response is: God is greater than the heart, and God knows everything. But in what direction does God's greater knowledge function? Two readings are possible.

If God's greater knowledge serves to intensify the condemnation (God knows even more than the condemning heart), the verse produces terror, not comfort. But this reading contradicts the context: the author is building toward confidence (v. 21), not toward greater fear. The reassurance of verse 19 ("we shall reassure our heart") governs the flow.

If God's greater knowledge serves to override the heart's condemnation (God knows the full reality of the believer's love and faith, even when the heart's self-accusation is partial or excessive), the verse produces comfort. God's omniscience is invoked not as a threat but as a corrective: the believer's heart may condemn on the basis of incomplete self-knowledge, but God sees the full picture and is greater than the heart's limited, self-accusing perspective. This reading fits the context: the author has just said that loving in deed and truth reassures the heart (v. 19). When the heart still condemns despite genuine love, God's greater knowledge provides the deeper assurance.

The logic is pastoral: even when your conscience accuses you, God -- who knows everything, including the reality of your love and your faith -- is greater than your accusing conscience. The community that genuinely loves in deed and truth can rest in God's comprehensive knowledge rather than being paralyzed by the heart's self-condemnation.

**"Beloved, if our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God" (agapetoi, ean he kardia me kataginoske, parrhesian echomen pros ton theon).** The word parrhesia ("confidence, boldness, freedom of speech") appeared at 2:28 in connection with Christ's future appearing. Here it describes the community's present relationship with God in prayer. When the heart does not condemn -- either because love is being practiced genuinely or because God's greater knowledge has overridden the heart's self-accusation -- the community has bold access to God.

**"And whatever we ask we receive from him, because we keep his commandments and do what pleases him" (kai ho ean aitomen lambanomen ap' autou, hoti tas entolas autou teroumen kai ta aresta enopion autou poioumen).** The promise of answered prayer is connected to obedience: "because we keep his commandments and do what pleases him." This is not a mechanical formula (obey and God will give you whatever you want) but a relational reality: those who keep God's commandments and do what pleases him are aligned with God's will, and their requests arise from that alignment. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus made the same connection: "If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7). The abiding relationship produces both obedience and effective prayer. The word aresta ("pleasing things") echoes Jesus's statement in John 8:29: "I always do the things that are pleasing (ta aresta) to him."

## Verses 23-24: The Commandment Summarized -- Believe and Love

"And this is his commandment, that we believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another, just as he gave us commandment. Whoever keeps his commandments abides in God, and God in him. And by this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit whom he has given us."

**"And this is his commandment" (kai haute estin he entole autou).** The singular "commandment" (entole) is striking. Throughout the letter, the author has spoken of "commandments" in the plural (2:3, 4; 3:22; 5:2, 3). Here he consolidates them into one. The one commandment has two inseparable components.

**"That we believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ" (hina pisteusomen to onomati tou huiou autou Iesou Christou).** The first component is faith: believing in the name of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. The "name" (onoma) carries the person's full identity and authority (cf. John 1:12, "to those who believed in his name"; Acts 4:12, "there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved"). This is the christological confession that the departed teachers denied (2:22-23): that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. Faith in this identity is not optional but commanded.

**"And love one another, just as he gave us commandment" (kai agapomen allelous, kathos edoken entolen hemin).** The second component is love for one another. The two components -- right belief about Jesus and mutual love -- are not separable. The author does not present faith and love as two independent requirements but as a single commandment with two dimensions. Correct christological confession without love is empty; love without correct confession is misdirected. The community must hold both together. The phrase "just as he gave us commandment" (kathos edoken entolen) refers back to Jesus's own command at John 13:34: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another."

**"Whoever keeps his commandments abides in God, and God in him" (kai ho teron tas entolas autou en auto menei kai autos en auto).** The return to the plural "commandments" (entolas) subsumes the two-part commandment of verse 23 and all its practical applications. The promise is mutual indwelling: the obedient one abides in God, and God abides in the obedient one. This is the fullest expression of the meno ("abide") theology in the letter to this point: not just the believer in God, but God in the believer. The mutual indwelling recalls Jesus's promise in the Fourth Gospel: "If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him" (John 14:23). Obedience is the condition of mutual indwelling; mutual indwelling is the reality that makes obedience possible. The relationship is not one-directional but reciprocal.

**"And by this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit whom he has given us" (kai en touto ginoskomen hoti menei en hemin, ek tou pneumatos hou hemin edoken).** The "by this" (en touto) is explained by the final clause: "by the Spirit whom he has given us." The Spirit is the evidence of God's indwelling -- the means by which the community knows that God abides in them. The "anointing" (chrisma) of 2:20 and 2:27 is now explicitly identified as the Spirit (pneuma). The Spirit is a gift (edoken, "he has given," aorist of didomi) -- not earned but bestowed. The Spirit's presence is the community's assurance that the mutual indwelling described in verse 24a is real.

This final verse hands the argument to chapter 4, which will open with the command to "test the spirits" (4:1). Having just identified the Spirit as the evidence of God's indwelling, the author must now address a pressing question: not every spirit-claim is genuine. How does the community distinguish the Spirit of God from the spirit of error? Chapter 4 will answer that question with the christological test (4:2-3) and the love test (4:7-21), both of which have been prepared by the arguments of chapter 3.

## Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 3

The chapter develops a single, sustained argument about the relationship between divine parentage and observable conduct:

1. **The identity of God's children is both a present reality and a future hope** (vv. 1-3). The community is already God's children, but the full expression of that identity has not yet been revealed. The eschatological hope of being like Christ when he appears produces a present commitment to purity.

2. **Sin is defined as lawlessness and is incompatible with Christ's mission and with genuine abiding** (vv. 4-6). Christ appeared to take away sins; in him there is no sin. Those who abide in him do not make sin a habitual practice. Those who continue in sin reveal that they have never truly known him.

3. **The parentage test distinguishes God's children from the devil's children** (vv. 7-10). The one who practices righteousness is born of God; the one who practices sin is of the devil. God's seed abides in the one born of God, making habitual sin incompatible with divine parentage. The two criteria that make the distinction visible are the practice of righteousness and love for one's brother.

4. **Love is the original message, and its absence is death** (vv. 11-15). The command to love one another was received from the beginning. Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his righteous brother, is the paradigm of hatred. The community has passed from death to life because they love the brothers; whoever does not love remains in death. Hatred is murder, and no murderer has eternal life abiding in him.

5. **Love is defined by Christ's self-sacrifice and must be expressed in practical action** (vv. 16-18). Christ's laying down of his life defines what love is. The community is obligated to follow his example. Love that withholds material help from a brother in need is not genuine love. Love must be in deed and truth, not merely in word.

6. **Genuine love produces confidence before God, even when the heart accuses** (vv. 19-22). Loving in deed and truth reassures the heart. When the heart still condemns, God's greater knowledge overrides the self-accusation. Confidence before God and effective prayer belong to those who keep his commandments.

7. **The commandment is twofold: believe in Jesus Christ and love one another** (vv. 23-24). Faith and love are a single commandment with two dimensions. Obedience produces mutual indwelling -- the believer in God, and God in the believer. The Spirit, given by God, is the evidence that this indwelling is real.

Throughout, the author's argument is unified by the metaphor of parentage. Children bear the characteristics of their parent. God is righteous; his children practice righteousness. God is love (as chapter 4 will make explicit); his children love one another. The devil has been sinning from the beginning; his children practice sin. The devil was a murderer from the beginning; those who hate are murderers. The test is always the same: observable conduct reveals true parentage. Verbal claims are meaningless unless they are accompanied by the family resemblance that proves the relationship is real.
