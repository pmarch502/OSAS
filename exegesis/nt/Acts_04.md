# Acts 4 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 3):** Peter and John went to the temple at the hour of prayer and healed a man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate, commanding him to walk "in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene" (3:6). The healed man entered the temple walking, leaping, and praising God, and the crowd gathered in Solomon's Portico in amazement. Peter then delivered a second major public speech (the first being the Pentecost sermon in Acts 2), redirecting the crowd's wonder away from himself -- "why do you gaze at us, as if by our own power or piety we had made him walk?" (3:12) -- and toward God, who "has glorified His servant Jesus" (3:13). The speech identified Jesus as "the Holy and Righteous One" and "the Prince of life" whom the crowd "put to death" but whom "God raised from the dead" (3:14-15). The healing was attributed to "faith in His name" (3:16). Peter offered a concession -- "you acted in ignorance, just as your rulers did also" (3:17) -- and called for repentance: "repent and return, so that your sins may be wiped away, in order that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord" (3:19). He then presented Jesus as the fulfillment of Moses's prophecy about "a prophet like me" whom God would raise up, warning that "every soul that does not heed that prophet shall be utterly destroyed from among the people" (3:22-23, citing Deuteronomy 18:15-19). The speech closed by reminding the crowd that they are "the sons of the prophets and of the covenant" and that God sent his servant Jesus "to bless you by turning every one of you from your wicked ways" (3:25-26). Peter was still speaking when the events of chapter 4 begin.

**What the author argues immediately after (Acts 5):** The account of communal sharing that closes chapter 4 sets up the story of Ananias and Sapphira (5:1-11), who sell property but secretly hold back part of the proceeds while pretending to give the full amount. Peter confronts them -- "you have not lied to men but to God" (5:4) -- and both fall dead. The episode establishes that the community's integrity is guarded by the Spirit's presence. Following this, the apostles continue performing signs and wonders in Solomon's Portico (5:12), the community grows further (5:14), and the sick are brought for healing (5:15-16). This prompts a second arrest, this time of all the apostles (5:17-18), their miraculous release from prison by an angel (5:19-20), and a second hearing before the Sanhedrin (5:27-40). The high priest's complaint -- "you have filled Jerusalem with your teaching" (5:28) -- confirms that the order to stop speaking in Jesus's name (given in 4:18) has been completely ineffective. Peter's response in 5:29 -- "we must obey God rather than men" -- echoes and sharpens his response in 4:19. Gamaliel's counsel to let the movement alone (5:34-39) results in a flogging rather than execution, and the apostles leave "rejoicing that they had been considered worthy to suffer shame for His name" (5:41).

**How chapter 4 connects these:** Acts 4 is the hinge between the first public demonstration of apostolic power (the healing in Acts 3) and the full escalation of institutional opposition (the multiple arrests and flogging in Acts 5). The chapter traces a complete cycle: public proclamation produces opposition, opposition produces a hearing, the hearing produces a command to stop, the command produces a prayer for boldness, the prayer produces a fresh filling of the Spirit, and the Spirit-filled community demonstrates the very boldness that was requested. The chapter also transitions from the public square to the courtroom to the prayer room to the community's internal life, showing how external pressure produces internal cohesion. The communal sharing described in 4:32-37 is the material expression of the spiritual unity; the Barnabas example (4:36-37) sets up the Ananias-Sapphira counterexample (5:1-11). Everything in chapter 4 demonstrates that the opposition the authorities mount against the movement accomplishes the opposite of what they intend: the more they press, the more the community grows in numbers, boldness, unity, and generosity.

## Verses 1-4: The Arrest and the Growth of the Community

"As they were speaking to the people, the priests and the captain of the temple guard and the Sadducees came up to them, being greatly disturbed because they were teaching the people and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead" (4:1-2).

The phrase "as they were speaking" (lalounton de auton) connects directly to the preceding scene: Peter has not finished his speech in Solomon's Portico. The arrest interrupts the proclamation. The author wants the reader to understand that the temple authorities did not wait for the speech to conclude; they acted mid-sentence.

Three groups are named. "The priests" (hoi hiereis) -- these are the serving priests, the temple personnel responsible for the sacrificial system. Their presence indicates that the teaching was happening within the temple precincts where they had jurisdiction. "The captain of the temple guard" (ho strategos tou hierou) -- this was the sagan, the second-ranking official in the temple hierarchy after the high priest. He commanded the temple police force and was responsible for maintaining order in the sacred precinct. His involvement indicates that the apostles' teaching was being treated as a matter of temple security, not merely a theological dispute. "The Sadducees" (hoi Saddoukaioi) -- this priestly aristocratic party controlled the temple and the high priesthood. Their theological commitments are directly relevant to what follows.

The author identifies two reasons for their disturbance. First, "they were teaching the people" (didaskein autous ton laon). The apostles were functioning as teachers within the temple, a role that the priestly and scribal establishment considered their own prerogative. Unauthorized teaching in the temple precincts was an assertion of authority that the Sadducean leadership found threatening.

Second, and more specifically, they were "proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead" (katangellein en to Iesou ten anastasin ten ek nekron). The Sadducees denied the resurrection of the dead -- Luke 20:27 states this explicitly: "some of the Sadducees (who say that there is no resurrection)." Their denial was based on their acceptance of only the Torah (the five books of Moses) as fully authoritative and their claim that the Torah does not teach resurrection. Jesus had already confronted this position in Luke 20:37-38, arguing from the Torah itself (the burning bush narrative in Exodus 3:6) that the God who calls himself "the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" is "not the God of the dead but of the living."

The phrase "in Jesus" (en to Iesou) is critical. The apostles were not merely teaching the abstract doctrine of resurrection; they were proclaiming resurrection "in Jesus" -- using Jesus as the concrete evidence, the historical demonstration, that resurrection from the dead is real. If the apostles are right that God raised Jesus, then the Sadducees' entire theological position collapses. The proclamation is not merely annoying; it is existentially threatening to the Sadducean worldview and, by extension, to their institutional authority. They held power in part because of their theological position. If that position is publicly discredited by eyewitness testimony backed by a visible miracle (the healed man was standing right there), their authority is undermined.

"And they laid hands on them and put them in jail until the next day, for it was already evening" (4:3).

The arrest happens in the evening. Jewish legal procedure prohibited trials at night -- a principle later codified in the Mishnah (Sanhedrin 4:1) but reflecting established practice. The apostles must be held overnight for a morning hearing. The word teresis ("custody, holding, jail") indicates a place of detention, not a formal prison sentence. The apostles are being held for trial.

"But many of those who had heard the message believed; and the number of the men came to be about five thousand" (4:4).

The author places the growth notice immediately after the arrest notice. The juxtaposition is deliberate: the authorities seize the speakers, but the message has already done its work. The word "believed" (episteusan) is the aorist of pisteuo -- they came to faith. "The message" (ton logon) is the word they heard -- Peter's speech in chapter 3.

"The number of the men came to be about five thousand" (egeneth ho arithmos ton andron hos chilades pente). The word andron ("men," masculine) may indicate males only, suggesting the total community including women and children is substantially larger. The community that was 120 in Acts 1:15 and grew to about 3,120 after Pentecost (2:41) has now reached approximately five thousand men. Whether this number is cumulative or represents an addition is debated by the grammar, but either way, the movement has grown far beyond the authorities' ability to suppress by arresting two leaders. The message has outrun the messengers. The horse has left the barn.

## Verses 5-7: The Assembly of the Sanhedrin

"On the next day, their rulers and elders and scribes were gathered together in Jerusalem; and Annas the high priest was there, and Caiaphas and John and Alexander, and all who were of high-priestly descent" (4:5-6).

The three groups named -- "rulers and elders and scribes" (tous archontas kai tous presbyterous kai tous grammateis) -- constitute the Sanhedrin, the supreme judicial and legislative body of the Jewish nation. This is the same court that tried Jesus (Luke 22:66: "the council of elders of the people, both chief priests and scribes"). The apostles now stand before the very body that condemned their master to death. What Jesus predicted in Luke 12:11 -- "when they bring you before the synagogues and the rulers and the authorities" -- is happening.

"Annas the high priest" (Hannas ho archiereus) -- Annas (Hanan ben Seth) served as high priest from approximately 6 to 15 CE, when he was deposed by the Roman prefect Valerius Gratus. Despite his removal from the official position, he retained immense influence. Five of his sons and his son-in-law Caiaphas subsequently held the office. The author calls him "the high priest" even though Caiaphas (who served from approximately 18 to 36 CE) was the current officeholder. This reflects the social reality: Annas was the patriarch of the high-priestly family and wielded de facto authority. Luke 3:2 named both: "in the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas," acknowledging the dual reality of official title and actual power. John's account of Jesus's trial also shows Jesus being taken to Annas first, before Caiaphas (John 18:13-24).

"And all who were of high-priestly descent" (hosoi esan ek genous archieratikou) -- the high-priestly family as a whole, the aristocratic priestly clan that dominated the temple establishment. This is not merely a legal proceeding; it is the assembled power structure of second-temple Judaism confronting the apostles.

"When they had placed them in the center, they began to inquire, 'By what power, or in what name, have you done this?'" (4:7).

"Placed them in the center" (en to meso) -- the accused stand in the center of a semicircle of seated judges. The physical arrangement is imposing: two fishermen from Galilee surrounded by the most powerful religious authorities in the nation.

The question is twofold: "by what power" (en poia dynamei) and "in what name" (en poio onomati). The word dynamis ("power") asks about the source of supernatural ability; onoma ("name") asks about the authority under which they act. In Israelite practice, prophets and miracle-workers acted in a name -- that is, under the authorization and in the power of someone greater. Moses acted in the name of YHWH. Elijah acted in the name of YHWH. The court wants to know whose name -- whose authority -- stands behind what the apostles have done. The phrase "have you done this" (epoiesate touto) refers to the healing of the lame man, which is the precipitating event for the entire proceeding.

The question is not innocent. In Deuteronomy 13:1-5, Israel is warned about prophets who perform signs and wonders but lead the people to follow other gods. The judicial inquiry into the source of a miracle-worker's power has a legal basis: if the power comes from an unauthorized or illegitimate source, the miracle-worker is subject to judgment. The court is asking, in effect, whether the apostles' healing power comes from a legitimate divine source or from something that should be condemned.

## Verses 8-12: Peter's Defense

"Then Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, said to them, 'Rulers and elders of the people...'" (4:8).

"Filled with the Holy Spirit" (plestheis pneumatos hagiou) -- the verb pimplemi ("to fill") is the same word used at Pentecost (2:4: "they were all filled with the Holy Spirit") and will be used again in 4:31 when the community prays. Peter was filled at Pentecost; he is filled again here, for this specific moment. The author uses this language to indicate a fresh empowerment for the immediate situation, not a permanent state that one possesses and then never needs renewed. The Spirit fills as the occasion demands.

This filling directly fulfills Jesus's promise in Luke 12:11-12: "When they bring you before the synagogues and the rulers and the authorities, do not worry about how or what you are to speak in your defense, or what you are to say; for the Holy Spirit will teach you in that very hour what you ought to say." And Luke 21:14-15: "So make up your minds not to prepare beforehand to defend yourselves; for I will give you utterance and wisdom which none of your opponents will be able to resist or refute." What was promised has arrived. The Spirit that Jesus said would speak through them is now speaking through Peter before the very court Jesus described.

Peter addresses the court with its proper titles: "rulers and elders of the people" (archontes tou laou kai presbyteroi). He observes the protocol. But his speech is not deferential in content; it is direct, fearless, and accusatory.

"If we are on trial today for a benefit done to a sick man, as to how this man has been made well..." (4:9).

The word "benefit" (euergesia) means a good deed, a benefaction, an act of kindness. The term carries ironic weight: the court is investigating an act of mercy. Peter frames the situation to expose its absurdity -- they are being tried for healing a crippled man. The phrase "a sick man" (anthropou asthenous) uses asthenes, meaning "weak, without strength, sick." The man was powerless, and he was given power. Peter is asking the court to consider what kind of judicial proceeding puts men on trial for restoring a cripple.

"As to how this man has been made well" (en tini houtos sesostai) -- the verb sozo here means both "healed" and "saved." The double meaning is critical for what follows. Peter will move from the particular healing of one man to the universal salvation available through one name. The verb's dual sense allows the transition.

"Let it be known to all of you and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead -- by this name this man stands here before you in good health" (4:10).

Peter's answer to the court's question ("in what name?") is unambiguous: "the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene" (en to onomati Iesou Christou tou Nazoraiou). He provides the name. He does not hedge. And he immediately attaches two defining actions to that name.

First: "whom you crucified" (hon hymeis estaurosate). The pronoun "you" (hymeis) is emphatic. Peter is speaking to the men who bore responsibility for Jesus's execution. These rulers and elders were the ones who handed Jesus over to Pilate, who demanded his death, who chose Barabbas. Peter does not soften this. The verb stauroo ("to crucify") states the fact without euphemism.

Second: "whom God raised from the dead" (hon ho theos egeiren ek nekron). Against the "you" of crucifixion stands the "God" of resurrection. The structure is adversative: you did one thing; God did the opposite. You killed; God raised. The human action and the divine action are placed in direct opposition. This is the same structure Peter used at Pentecost (2:23-24: "you nailed to a cross... but God raised Him up") and in Solomon's Portico (3:15: "but put to death the Prince of life... whom God raised from the dead"). The pattern is consistent: the apostolic proclamation confronts its hearers with their action and then with God's overriding action.

"By this name this man stands here before you in good health" (en touto houtos paresteken enopion hymon hygies). The healed man is present -- the evidence is standing in the room. The word hygies means "whole, healthy, sound." The court can see the result. The man who was lame from birth is standing, whole, in their presence. The name that they are investigating is the name that produced the undeniable evidence before their eyes.

"He is the stone which was rejected by you, the builders, but which became the chief corner stone" (4:11).

Peter quotes Psalm 118:22 (LXX 117:22), but with a pointed adaptation. The psalm reads: "the stone which the builders rejected has become the chief corner stone" (lithon hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes, houtos egenthe eis kephalen gonias). Peter changes "the builders" to "you, the builders" (hyph' hymon ton oikodomounton) -- inserting the pronoun to make the accusation direct. You are the builders. You rejected the stone. God made it the cornerstone anyway.

The image requires unpacking in its Israelite context. The "builders" (oikodomountes) are those responsible for constructing the house -- metaphorically, the leaders responsible for building up Israel. The "stone" (lithos) is a building element that the builders examined and discarded as unsuitable. The "chief corner stone" (kephale gonias, literally "head of the corner") is the stone that determines the alignment and stability of the entire structure -- either the foundation stone at the corner that establishes the building's orientation, or the capstone that completes an arch or wall. Either way, it is the most important stone in the building. What the builders rejected, God placed in the position of greatest structural importance.

Jesus himself had quoted this same psalm in Luke 20:17, speaking directly to the chief priests and scribes in the temple. He added a warning that the author does not repeat here but that the audience would have remembered: "Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces; but on whomever it falls, it will scatter him like dust" (Luke 20:18). The stone that is rejected does not simply go away; it becomes a source of danger to those who rejected it. Peter is standing before the builders and telling them that their rejected stone has been placed by God at the head of the corner.

The psalm's original context is also relevant. Psalm 118 is a processional psalm used at the Feast of Tabernacles and at Passover. Verses 25-26 -- "Save us, we beseech You, O Lord... Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord" -- were the words the crowd shouted at Jesus's entry into Jerusalem (Luke 19:38). The psalm celebrates God's deliverance of the one who was surrounded and pushed down but whom the Lord helped and saved: "I will not die, but live, and tell of the works of the Lord" (Psalm 118:17). The entire psalm is a testimony of deliverance from death. Read christologically, it describes the one who was pushed toward death by his enemies but whom the Lord raised up -- and who now lives to tell of the Lord's works through his witnesses.

"And there is salvation in no one else; for there is no other name under heaven that has been given among men by which we must be saved" (4:12).

Peter moves from the particular to the universal. The verb sozo, which in verse 9 meant "healed/made well" in reference to the lame man, now carries its full weight of "saved" in the broadest sense. The healing was a sign; the salvation is the reality the sign points toward.

"Salvation in no one else" (ouk estin en allo oudeni he soteria) -- the double negative (ouk... oudeni) creates an absolute negation: in no one else, not at all, in no other person. The word soteria ("salvation, deliverance, rescue") in the LXX refers to God's acts of deliverance for Israel -- from Egypt (Exodus 14:13), from enemies (Judges 15:18, 1 Samuel 11:13), from exile (Isaiah 45:17, 46:13). Salvation is what God does when he rescues his people. Peter claims that this divine rescue is now exclusively mediated through one name.

"No other name under heaven" (ou gar onoma estin heteron hypo ton ouranon) -- the phrase "under heaven" (hypo ton ouranon) is a comprehensive expression meaning "in all the world, among all humanity." The scope is universal. "That has been given among men" (to dedomenon en anthropois) -- the passive "has been given" (dedomenon) is a divine passive: God is the one who gives the name. The name was not self-generated or self-proclaimed; it was given from above. "By which we must be saved" (en ho dei sothenai hemas) -- the word dei ("it is necessary, one must") carries the same force of divine necessity that runs throughout Luke-Acts. The dei of salvation through Jesus's name parallels the dei of the Christ's suffering (Luke 24:26: "was it not necessary [edei] for the Christ to suffer?"). The suffering was necessary; the salvation through this name is equally necessary. There is no alternative route.

The claim is breathtaking in its scope, and Peter makes it standing before the supreme court of the nation, as a prisoner on trial. The court asked for a name; Peter has given them the name and declared that it is the only name that saves.

## Verses 13-22: The Council's Response and the Apostles' Release

"Now as they observed the confidence of Peter and John and understood that they were uneducated and untrained men, they were amazed, and began to recognize them as having been with Jesus" (4:13).

The word "confidence" (parrhesia) means bold, frank, fearless speech -- the absence of timidity, the willingness to say everything openly. This word will recur throughout the chapter (4:29, 31) and throughout Acts (2:29, 9:27-28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, 19:8, 26:26, 28:31). It is the defining quality of Spirit-empowered speech. Peter and John speak with the fearlessness of men who have nothing to hide and nothing to lose.

"Uneducated and untrained" (agrammatoi kai idiotai) -- the word agrammatoi means "unlettered," not illiterate in the basic sense but without formal scribal or rabbinic training. They had not studied Torah under a recognized teacher; they had not been through the educational system that produced scribes and Pharisees. The word idiotai means "laymen, private persons, non-professionals" -- people without official religious credentials. These are men from the working class of Galilee, fishermen (Luke 5:1-11), standing before the supreme religious scholars and speaking with an authority and clarity that surpasses what training produces.

"They were amazed" (ethaumazon) -- the imperfect tense indicates ongoing amazement. They kept marveling. How can unschooled men speak like this?

"And began to recognize them as having been with Jesus" (epeginoskon te autous hoti syn to Iesou esan). The verb epiginosko means to recognize, to identify, to perceive. The court recognized that Peter and John had been companions of Jesus -- perhaps remembering their faces from the trial, perhaps recognizing the style and content of their teaching as continuous with Jesus's own. The phrase "with Jesus" (syn to Iesou) is the explanation for their competence. Their education was not in the academies; it was in companionship with Jesus. This is the same criterion Peter himself established for apostleship in Acts 1:21-22: having accompanied Jesus throughout his ministry. Their qualification is experiential, not institutional.

"And seeing the man who had been healed standing with them, they had nothing to say in reply" (4:14).

The healed man is in the room. The evidence is incontestable. The word "standing" (hestota) carries weight: this is a man who could not stand for over forty years (4:22), and now he is standing -- upright, whole, visible -- with the accused. The court cannot deny what their eyes confirm. The phrase "nothing to say in reply" (ouden eichon anteipein) means they had no counter-argument, no rebuttal. The miracle has silenced the court. They cannot argue that the healing did not happen; they cannot argue that it was done by evil power when the man is standing whole in the presence of God's own court. The evidence has overwhelmed the prosecution.

"But when they had ordered them to leave the Council, they began to confer with one another, saying, 'What shall we do with these men? For the fact that a noteworthy miracle has taken place through them is apparent to all who live in Jerusalem, and we cannot deny it'" (4:15-16).

The council removes the apostles from the chamber to deliberate privately. Their admission is remarkable: "a noteworthy miracle" (gneston semeion, "a known sign, a recognizable miracle") has occurred, it is "apparent" (phaneron, "visible, clear, undeniable") to everyone in Jerusalem, and "we cannot deny it" (ou dynametha arneisthai). The supreme court of the nation acknowledges among themselves that a genuine miracle has been performed and that it is public knowledge. Their problem is not the evidence; their problem is the implications of the evidence.

The phrase "what shall we do with these men?" (ti poiesomen tois anthropois toutois) echoes the crowd's question at Pentecost: "what shall we do?" (ti poiesomen, 2:37). But the responses diverge sharply. The crowd at Pentecost asked because they were "cut to the heart" and seeking salvation; the council asks because they are seeking to suppress. Same question, opposite posture.

"But so that it will not spread any further among the people, let us warn them to speak no longer to any man in this name" (4:17).

The council's strategy is containment: prevent the message from spreading further (hina me epi pleion dianemthe eis ton laon). The verb dianemo means "to spread, to distribute, to disseminate." They treat the message like a contagion that must be quarantined. Their tool is a threat: "warn them" (apeilesometha autois) -- the word apeile means a threat, a menacing command. The target of the prohibition is specific: "to speak no longer to any man in this name" (meketi lalein epi to onomati touto medeni anthropon). The name -- Jesus's name -- is what must not be spoken. They cannot undo the miracle; they can only try to prevent the name from being spoken further.

"And when they had summoned them, they commanded them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus" (4:18).

The command is absolute: "not to speak or teach at all" (to katholou me phthengesthia mede didaskein). The verb phthengesthia means "to utter a sound, to speak, to proclaim." Combined with didaskein ("to teach"), the prohibition covers every form of verbal communication about Jesus. They are not merely told to stop preaching in the temple; they are told to stop speaking entirely about this name, to anyone, anywhere.

"But Peter and John answered and said to them, 'Whether it is right in the sight of God to give heed to you rather than to God, you be the judge; for we cannot stop speaking about what we have seen and heard'" (4:19-20).

Peter and John's response places the court in an impossible position. They appeal to a higher authority -- God -- and they do so by asking the court itself to render the judgment: "you be the judge" (krinate). The phrase "in the sight of God" (enopion tou theou) invokes the divine perspective. The question is whether human authority overrides divine authority, and the answer is self-evident to any Israelite: it does not. Obedience to God takes precedence over obedience to human institutions, even the Sanhedrin. This principle has deep roots in Israel's tradition. When Pharaoh commanded the Hebrew midwives to kill male infants, they disobeyed because they "feared God" (Exodus 1:17). When Nebuchadnezzar commanded worship of his image, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refused (Daniel 3:16-18). When Darius prohibited prayer to anyone but the king, Daniel continued praying (Daniel 6:10). The principle is consistent: when human commands contradict divine commands, God's command prevails.

"We cannot stop speaking about what we have seen and heard" (ou dynametha gar hemeis ha eidamen kai ekousamen me lalein). The word "cannot" (ou dynametha) is not rhetorical modesty; it expresses a genuine impossibility. They are compelled by what they have witnessed. The verbs "seen" (eidamen) and "heard" (ekousamen) are the verbs of eyewitness testimony. They have seen the risen Jesus. They have heard his commission. They cannot unsee or unhear. The compulsion to speak arises from the nature of their experience, not from a desire to provoke.

"When they had threatened them further, they let them go (finding no basis on which to punish them) on account of the people, because they were all glorifying God for what had happened; for the man was more than forty years old on whom this miracle of healing had been performed" (4:21-22).

The court releases them under duress. Two factors prevent punishment. First, they have "no basis" (meden... to pos) -- no legal grounds for punishment. The apostles have not violated the Torah; they have healed a man. The court's authority depends on Torah, and the Torah does not criminalize healing. Second, "the people" (ton laon) -- public opinion is entirely on the apostles' side. The people are "glorifying God" (edoxazon ton theon) for the healing. To punish the agents of a miracle that the people attribute to God would be politically disastrous for the council.

The detail that the man was "more than forty years old" (eton gar en pleionon tesserakonta) emphasizes both the duration and the severity of his condition. This was not a recent or mild ailment. The man had been lame from birth (3:2), meaning he had been unable to walk for over forty years. The miracle is proportional to the affliction: a lifelong condition was healed instantly and completely. The number forty also carries its own resonance in Israel's narrative -- forty years in the wilderness, forty days of testing -- though here the primary function is to establish the healing's magnitude and incontestability.

## Verses 23-30: The Community's Prayer

"When they had been released, they went to their own companions and reported all that the chief priests and the elders had said to them" (4:23).

"Their own companions" (tous idious) -- the possessive adjective (idious, "their own") marks the believing community as a distinct body. The apostles return to their people and give a full report. The community functions as a body that processes information together. They do not bear the threat in isolation; they share it.

"And when they heard this, they lifted their voices to God with one accord and said..." (4:24a).

"With one accord" (homothymadon) -- this word appeared in 1:14 ("with one mind were continually devoting themselves to prayer") and 2:46 ("with one mind"). It is a signature word in Acts, describing the community's unified action. The word means "with one passion, with one impulse, unanimously." When the community hears about the threat, their response is not debate, panic, or flight; it is unified prayer. The verb "lifted their voices" (eran phonen) is singular -- "they lifted voice" -- emphasizing the single, collective utterance.

The prayer that follows (4:24b-30) is one of the most theologically structured prayers in Acts. It moves through four elements: an address to God as sovereign creator, a citation of scripture, an application of that scripture to recent events, and specific petitions.

**The Address (v. 24b):**

"O Lord, it is You who made the heaven and the earth and the sea, and all that is in them" (Despota, sy ho poiesas ton ouranon kai ten gen kai ten thalassan kai panta ta en autois).

The word "Lord" here is not kyrios (the usual term) but despotes -- "sovereign master, absolute ruler." The word connotes unrestricted authority, complete ownership, ultimate power. A despotes has authority that no one can override. The community addresses God as the one whose authority surpasses all earthly authorities -- specifically the Sanhedrin that just threatened them.

The description of God as creator -- "who made the heaven and the earth and the sea, and all that is in them" -- draws from multiple scriptural sources. Exodus 20:11: "For in six days the Lord made the heavens and the earth, the sea and all that is in them." Psalm 146:6: "Who made heaven and earth, the sea and all that is in them." Isaiah 42:5: "Thus says God the Lord, who created the heavens and stretched them out, who spread out the earth and its offspring." The prayer establishes God's authority by appealing to creation itself. The God who made everything is greater than the rulers who control a courtroom in one city. The prayer's opening frames the entire situation: the Sanhedrin's threats are placed against the backdrop of cosmic sovereignty. The men who threatened the apostles inhabit a world that the apostles' God created.

**The Scripture Citation (vv. 25-26):**

"Who by the Holy Spirit, through the mouth of our father David Your servant, said, 'Why did the Gentiles rage, and the peoples devise futile things? The kings of the earth took their stand, and the rulers were gathered together against the Lord and against His Christ'" (4:25-26).

The prayer quotes Psalm 2:1-2 (LXX 2:1-2). The attribution is layered: the words are spoken "by the Holy Spirit" (en pneumati hagio), "through the mouth" (dia stomatos) of David, who is identified as "our father" (tou patros hemon) and "Your servant" (paidos sou). The hermeneutic matches Acts 1:16, where Peter said the Holy Spirit "foretold by the mouth of David." Scripture is the Spirit's speech through human agents. David is the instrument; the Spirit is the speaker.

Psalm 2 is a royal psalm, composed for the enthronement of the Davidic king. Its original setting was the coronation ceremony, in which the new king is declared God's "son" (Psalm 2:7) and given authority over the nations. The psalm opens by describing the futile opposition of foreign nations and their rulers against YHWH and his anointed king (mashiach, christos). The nations "rage" (phryasso, to snort, to be insolent); the peoples "devise futile things" (meletao kena, to meditate on empty schemes). The kings and rulers "take their stand" and "gather together" in a coalition against the Lord and his anointed.

The psalm's original force was reassurance: no matter how many nations conspire against the Davidic king, God has installed his king on Zion and will give him the nations as his inheritance (Psalm 2:6-8). The opposition is real but ultimately futile, because God's purpose cannot be overthrown.

**The Application (vv. 27-28):**

"For truly in this city there were gathered together against Your holy servant Jesus, whom You anointed, both Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, to do whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur" (4:27-28).

The community reads Psalm 2 as a prophecy of what happened to Jesus, and they map each element of the psalm onto specific historical actors:

- "The Gentiles" (ethne) of Psalm 2:1 = the Gentile participants in Jesus's execution (the Roman soldiers, the Roman judicial system)
- "The peoples" (laoi) of Psalm 2:1 = "the peoples of Israel" (laois Israel) -- the Jewish crowds who demanded crucifixion
- "The kings of the earth" (basileis tes ges) of Psalm 2:2 = Herod (Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee, before whom Jesus was sent by Pilate, Luke 23:6-12)
- "The rulers" (archontes) of Psalm 2:2 = Pontius Pilate (the Roman governor who authorized the execution)

This mapping is remarkable for its inclusiveness. Both Gentiles and Israelites are identified as participants in the raging against God's anointed. The opposition to Jesus was not the deed of one people against another; it was a human coalition -- Jew and Gentile, king and governor, peoples and nations -- united against God's purpose. The guilt is corporate and universal.

"Your holy servant Jesus" (ton hagion paida sou Iesoun) -- the word pais ("servant" or "child") is the same word used in Acts 3:13 and 3:26 for Jesus, and it echoes the Servant (pais) of the LXX Isaiah: "Behold, My servant" (idou ho pais mou, Isaiah 42:1 LXX). In the prayer, Jesus is identified simultaneously as the "anointed" (christos) of Psalm 2 and the "servant" (pais) of Isaiah -- the one whom God has set apart and empowered for his mission.

"Whom You anointed" (hon echricas) -- the verb chrio ("to anoint") is the root of christos ("anointed one, messiah"). The anointing refers to God's empowerment of Jesus for his mission. In Luke 4:18, Jesus read Isaiah 61:1 in the Nazareth synagogue: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me, because He anointed Me" (echrisen me). In Acts 10:38, Peter will later summarize: "God anointed Him with the Holy Spirit and with power." The anointing is the act by which God designated and equipped Jesus as his messianic servant.

The theological climax of this section comes in verse 28: "to do whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur" (poiesai hosa he cheir sou kai he boule sou proorisen genesthai). The verb proorizo means "to determine beforehand, to mark out in advance, to predestine." The subject of the predetermining is God -- specifically "Your hand" (he cheir sou, symbolizing God's power and action) and "Your purpose" (he boule sou, meaning God's counsel, plan, deliberate intention).

The claim is that Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and the peoples of Israel -- all of them acting with hostile intent against Jesus -- accomplished exactly what God had already determined would happen. Their conspiracy was real; their guilt is not diminished. But the result of their conspiracy was the fulfillment of God's plan, not the defeat of it. This is the same dual affirmation Peter made at Pentecost: "this Man, delivered over by the predetermined plan and foreknowledge of God, you nailed to a cross by the hands of godless men and put Him to death" (2:23). Human agency and divine sovereignty operate simultaneously. The actors are responsible for their choices; God is sovereign over the outcome.

This is not a new theological idea in Israel's tradition. Joseph said to his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20). The same action was simultaneously human wickedness and divine purpose. The community's prayer applies this principle to the crucifixion: the most wicked act in human history -- the murder of God's anointed servant -- was the predetermined means by which God accomplished his saving purpose. The raging of the nations described in Psalm 2 was not a failure of God's plan; it was the mechanism of God's plan.

**The Petitions (vv. 29-30):**

"And now, Lord, take note of their threats, and grant that Your bond-servants may speak Your word with all confidence, while You extend Your hand to heal, and signs and wonders take place through the name of Your holy servant Jesus" (4:29-30).

The petitions are as revealing for what they do not request as for what they do. The community does not ask for protection. They do not ask for the threats to be removed. They do not ask for the Sanhedrin to be punished. They do not ask to be hidden or kept safe. They ask for two things.

First, boldness: "grant that Your bond-servants may speak Your word with all confidence" (dos tois doulois sou meta parrhesias pases lalein ton logon sou). The word "bond-servants" (doulois, "slaves, servants") describes their self-understanding: they belong to God; they serve at his command. The word parrhesia ("confidence, boldness, fearless speech") is what the council has just tried to suppress. The community asks God for the very quality the authorities are trying to destroy. The threat was "do not speak"; the prayer is "let us speak with all boldness." The community's response to intimidation is not to pray for the intimidation to stop but to pray for the courage to speak through it.

Second, continued signs: "while You extend Your hand to heal, and signs and wonders take place through the name of Your holy servant Jesus" (en to ten cheira sou ekteinein se eis iasin kai semeia kai terata ginesthai dia tou onomatos tou hagiou paidos sou Iesou). They ask God to keep doing what provoked the opposition in the first place. The healing of the lame man triggered the arrest; the community asks for more healings. The "name" that the Sanhedrin prohibited is the name through which the community asks God to act. The "hand" (cheir) of God, which in verse 28 predestined the crucifixion, is now asked to extend for healing. The same sovereign hand that accomplished God's purpose through suffering now accomplishes God's purpose through restoration.

The prayer's logic is coherent: the God who made heaven and earth, who spoke through David about the futile raging of nations, who predestined even the crucifixion to serve his plan -- this God can certainly grant boldness to his servants and continue to work miracles through Jesus's name. The threats of the Sanhedrin are placed within a framework of divine sovereignty that renders them significant (they are noted and acknowledged) but not ultimate (they cannot override God's purpose).

## Verse 31: The Answer to the Prayer

"And when they had prayed, the place where they had gathered together was shaken, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak the word of God with boldness" (4:31).

The answer is immediate and physical. "The place was shaken" (esaleuthe ho topos) -- the verb saleuo means to shake, to agitate, to cause to tremble. Physical shaking accompanied divine appearances throughout Israel's history. Sinai "quaked violently" when God descended upon it (Exodus 19:18). The foundations of the thresholds trembled when the seraphim called out in Isaiah's temple vision (Isaiah 6:4). The earth quaked when God manifested his power (Judges 5:4, 2 Samuel 22:8, Psalm 68:8). The shaking is a theophanic sign -- it signals God's arrival, God's response, God's presence. The community asked God to take note; God answers with a physical manifestation that shakes the building.

"They were all filled with the Holy Spirit" (eplesthesan hapantes tou hagiou pneumatos). This is the same verb (pimplemi) and the same construction used at Pentecost (2:4). Many of these people were already filled at Pentecost. The second filling demonstrates that the Spirit's empowerment is not a one-time endowment but a renewable resource that God provides as situations demand. The community needed fresh courage for a new threat; God provides fresh filling for the new moment. The pattern will continue: Acts 13:9 describes Paul as "filled with the Holy Spirit" for a specific confrontation, and 13:52 describes the disciples as "continually filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit."

"And began to speak the word of God with boldness" (elaloun ton logon tou theou meta parrhesias). The prayer in verse 29 asked for parrhesia ("boldness, confidence") in speaking God's word. Verse 31 reports that they received exactly what they asked for. The prayer was answered completely and immediately. The sequence is: threat, prayer for boldness, filling with the Spirit, bold speech. This sequence -- opposition prompting prayer, prayer prompting divine empowerment, divine empowerment producing the very quality the opposition tried to suppress -- is the paradigmatic cycle of the early community's life.

## Verses 32-35: The Life of the Believing Community

"And the congregation of those who believed were of one heart and soul; and not one of them claimed that anything belonging to him was his own, but all things were common property to them" (4:32).

The description shifts from the community's external situation (trial, threat, prayer) to its internal character. This parallels the earlier community summary in 2:42-47, but with expanded emphasis on the economic dimension of their common life.

"The congregation of those who believed" (tou plethous ton pisteusanton) -- the word plethos means "multitude, full number, congregation." These are not a few ascetics; they are a large and growing body. "Of one heart and soul" (kardia kai psyche mia) -- the phrase echoes Deuteronomy 6:5 ("love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul") and describes an interior unity that goes beyond organizational agreement. The "heart" (kardia) in Hebrew and Greek thought is the seat of will, intention, and moral decision. The "soul" (psyche) is the whole person, the life-force, the self. To be "of one heart and soul" is to share a single orientation, a unified purpose, a common life at the deepest level.

"Not one of them claimed that anything belonging to him was his own" (oude heis ti ton hyparchonton auto elegen idion einai). The verb elegen ("claimed, said") is important: the statement is about how they spoke about their possessions, which reflects how they thought about them. No one said "this is mine." The possessions were not confiscated or legislated away; the community's members voluntarily stopped asserting individual ownership. The word idion ("one's own, private") is the opposite of koinon ("common, shared"). They moved from idion to koinon -- from private claim to shared resource.

"But all things were common property to them" (alla en autois hapanta koina). The word koina ("common, shared") describes the fundamental economic orientation: resources belonged to the community, not to individuals. This is not an abstract ideal but a practical reality: the following verses describe the mechanism by which it was implemented.

"And with great power the apostles were giving testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and abundant grace was upon them all" (4:33).

Two parallel statements define the community's public and internal character. Publicly: "with great power" (dynamei megale) the apostles testified to "the resurrection" (te anastasei). The resurrection remains the center of the apostolic message -- the specific content of their witness. The word dynamis ("power") links to 1:8 ("you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you") and to the fresh filling of 4:31. The power is for testimony.

Internally: "abundant grace was upon them all" (charis te megale en epi pantas autous). The word charis ("grace, favor, generous gift") describes the quality of life within the community. Grace is "upon them all" (epi pantas autous) -- it rests on the entire community, not just the leaders. The possessive sharing described in the surrounding verses is presented as an expression of this grace. Generosity is not a rule they follow; it is the overflow of a grace that rests on them.

"For there was not a needy person among them, for all who were owners of land or houses would sell them and bring the proceeds of the sales and lay them at the apostles' feet, and they would be distributed to each as any had need" (4:34-35).

"There was not a needy person among them" (oude gar endees tis en en autois) -- this sentence echoes Deuteronomy 15:4 (LXX): "there will be no one in need among you" (ouk estai en soi endees). The Deuteronomic text describes the ideal condition of Israel in the promised land, conditional on obedience: "if only you listen obediently to the voice of the Lord your God" (Deuteronomy 15:5). The promise was that if Israel obeyed God's commandments -- particularly the laws of debt release and generosity toward the poor (Deuteronomy 15:7-11) -- there would be no poverty within the community.

The author's language is deliberate. The early community is fulfilling what Moses envisioned for Israel. The Deuteronomic ideal -- a people so obedient to God and so generous toward one another that no one among them lacks what they need -- has been realized among those who believe in Jesus. The community of the Spirit is becoming what Israel was called to be. The eschatological fulfillment is not merely spiritual; it has concrete economic expression. The absence of poverty within the community is evidence that God's intention for his people is being achieved.

The mechanism is described: owners of property "would sell them" (polountes epheron) and "bring the proceeds" (tas timas ton pipraskomenon) and "lay them at the apostles' feet" (etithoun para tous podas ton apostolon). The phrase "at the apostles' feet" indicates that the apostles functioned as administrators of the common fund. The proceeds were not hoarded or randomly distributed; they were placed under apostolic oversight for systematic distribution "to each as any had need" (diedieto de hekasto kathoti an tis chreian eichen). The distribution was based on need, not equality or entitlement. Each person received what they needed.

The verb tenses are significant. The imperfect tenses -- "would sell" (polountes), "would bring" (epheron), "would be distributed" (diedieto) -- indicate ongoing, repeated action, not a single event. This was the community's sustained practice over time, not a one-time liquidation.

## Verses 36-37: The Example of Barnabas

"Now Joseph, a Levite of Cyprian birth, who was also called Barnabas by the apostles (which translated means Son of Encouragement), and who owned a tract of land, sold it and brought the money and laid it at the apostles' feet" (4:36-37).

The general description of communal sharing (vv. 34-35) is now illustrated with a specific example. Joseph, called Barnabas, is a concrete instance of the pattern: he owned land, he sold it, he brought the money, he laid it at the apostles' feet.

The man has three identifiers. His given name is Joseph (Ioseph). He is a Levite (Leuites) -- a member of the tribe set apart for temple service. He is "of Cyprian birth" (Kyprios to genei) -- born on the island of Cyprus, in the Jewish diaspora, not in the land of Israel. The apostles have given him the name Barnabas (Barnabas), which the author translates as "Son of Encouragement" (huios parakleseos). The word paraklesis means "encouragement, consolation, exhortation, comfort." It is related to parakletos ("advocate, helper") and to the verb parakaleo ("to encourage, to call alongside, to comfort"). Barnabas is named for his character: he is a man who strengthens and encourages others. The apostles, not his parents, gave him this name -- the community recognized and named the quality they saw in him.

That Barnabas is a Levite who owns land is noteworthy. In the Torah, the Levites received no territorial inheritance in the promised land: "The Lord is their inheritance" (Deuteronomy 18:2; Numbers 18:20; Joshua 13:14, 33). They were given cities to live in (Numbers 35:1-8, Joshua 21) but no tribal territory. The Levitical principle was that those set apart for God's service find their portion in God, not in land. That a Levite in the diaspora (Cyprus) owns land reflects the historical reality that the strict Levitical land laws had been adapted in the centuries since the exile. But the symbolic resonance remains: a man from the tribe whose inheritance is the Lord gives up his land and places the proceeds at the apostles' feet. He enacts the Levitical principle in a new context -- his portion is now the community of God's servant Jesus, not a tract of earth.

Barnabas's introduction here serves a narrative function beyond the immediate example. He will become one of the most important figures in Acts: a bridge between the Jerusalem community and the wider mission. He will vouch for Paul when the Jerusalem community is afraid of him (9:27). He will be sent to Antioch when the gospel reaches Gentiles there (11:22). He will accompany Paul on the first missionary journey (13:1-3). He is "Son of Encouragement" in action throughout the narrative. His introduction here, as a generous Levite from the diaspora, foreshadows his role as a connector between Jerusalem and the nations.

The Barnabas example also creates a literary setup. The author has just described the ideal -- communal sharing, laying proceeds at the apostles' feet, no needy among them -- and has given a positive example. What follows immediately in Acts 5:1-11 is the negative counterexample: Ananias and Sapphira, who sell property and bring only a portion while claiming it is the whole. The Barnabas story is the light against which the Ananias story will be seen as darkness. The contrast is between a man who gives everything openly and a couple who withholds secretly while pretending to give everything.

## Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **The chapter demonstrates that opposition to the apostolic message is futile and counterproductive.** The authorities arrest the apostles to stop the spread of the message, but the message has already produced five thousand believers (v. 4). They command silence, but the community prays for boldness and receives it (vv. 29-31). They threaten, but the community grows in unity and generosity (vv. 32-37). The author's narrative logic is consistent: institutional power cannot suppress what God's Spirit is driving forward. Every attempt to contain the movement produces the opposite of the intended effect.

2. **The Sadducees' specific objection -- the proclamation of resurrection -- reveals the real stakes.** The arrest is not about public order or unauthorized teaching; it is about a theological claim that undermines the Sadducean position. If Jesus has been raised, then the Sadducees are wrong about resurrection, wrong about the scope of scriptural authority, and wrong in their treatment of Jesus. The apostles' proclamation is not merely informational; it is a direct challenge to the theological legitimacy of the temple establishment.

3. **Peter's defense before the Sanhedrin fulfills Jesus's promises about the Spirit's guidance under persecution.** Luke 12:11-12 and 21:14-15 predicted that the Holy Spirit would give the disciples words to speak when brought before authorities. Acts 4:8 narrates the fulfillment: Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, speaks with a boldness and clarity that astonishes the educated court. The promise-fulfillment pattern that structures the entire Luke-Acts narrative is operative here.

4. **The rejected stone metaphor (Psalm 118:22) places the court in a position of judgment on itself.** Peter's citation identifies the Sanhedrin members as the "builders" who rejected the stone that God made the cornerstone. The accusation is that the nation's leaders have failed in their fundamental responsibility -- building up the house of Israel -- by rejecting the very stone on which the structure depends. The court that judges the apostles is itself being judged by the scripture that Peter cites.

5. **The claim that "there is no other name" (v. 12) is rooted in the specific evidence of the healing.** Peter moves from the particular (this man was healed through Jesus's name) to the universal (all salvation comes through this name alone). The universal claim is anchored in the particular demonstration. The lame man standing whole in the courtroom is the tangible proof that gives the universal claim its force.

6. **The community prayer (vv. 24-30) provides the theological framework for understanding persecution.** The prayer reads Psalm 2 as a prophecy of the crucifixion and identifies the conspiracy of Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, and Israelites as the fulfillment of the psalm's description of nations raging against God's anointed. But the prayer also affirms that this conspiracy accomplished "whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur" (v. 28). The community does not interpret opposition as evidence that God's plan has failed; they interpret it as the very mechanism through which God's plan operates. This reading allows them to face threats not with despair but with confidence, asking not for safety but for boldness.

7. **The prayer's petitions reveal the community's priorities under pressure.** They do not ask for the threat to be removed. They do not ask for vengeance on their opponents. They ask for boldness to keep speaking and for God to keep performing signs through Jesus's name. Their response to the command "stop speaking in this name" is to ask God for more power to speak in this name and for more demonstrations of this name's authority. The community's orientation is offensive, not defensive: they ask for greater capacity to do exactly what got them arrested.

8. **The shaking and second filling (v. 31) establishes that the Spirit's empowerment is renewable and situation-specific.** People who were filled at Pentecost are filled again. The filling is not a permanent possession but a divine provision matched to the need. When the situation demands fresh courage, God provides fresh power. This understanding of the Spirit as continually given, not once deposited, shapes the community's dependence on God: they must keep praying because they keep needing to be filled.

9. **The communal life described in vv. 32-35 fulfills the Deuteronomic vision for Israel.** The statement "there was not a needy person among them" (v. 34) echoes Deuteronomy 15:4: "there will be no poor among you." The Deuteronomic promise was conditional on obedience; the early community, living under the Spirit and in obedience to the apostolic teaching, achieves what Moses envisioned. The community of the Spirit is the community that Israel was called to be -- a people so devoted to God and one another that poverty is eliminated within their fellowship.

10. **The introduction of Barnabas (vv. 36-37) accomplishes multiple narrative purposes.** He is a concrete example of the communal ideal. He is a Levite -- from the tribe whose inheritance is the Lord, not land -- who gives up his land for the community. He is from Cyprus -- the first named individual from outside Israel to be highlighted, hinting at the coming geographic expansion. And his introduction as the positive example of generosity sets up the negative counterexample of Ananias and Sapphira that immediately follows in chapter 5. The author ends the chapter with an image of openness and integrity that will be violated in the opening of the next chapter, creating a narrative contrast between genuine and counterfeit participation in the community's life.

11. **The name of Jesus is the central contested reality of the chapter.** The court asks "in what name" (v. 7); Peter answers with the name (v. 10); there is no other name for salvation (v. 12); the court commands silence "in this name" (v. 18); the community prays for signs "through the name" (v. 30). The name is simultaneously the source of power, the content of proclamation, and the target of suppression. The chapter's conflict is, at bottom, a conflict over whether this name will be spoken or silenced -- and the chapter's answer is that it cannot be silenced, because the God who gave the name is the God who made heaven and earth.
