# Galatians 4 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before this chapter:** Chapter 3 provided the scriptural and experiential demonstration of the thesis stated in 2:15-21 -- that no one is justified by erga nomou (circumcision and proselyte conversion) but through faith in Christ Jesus. The demonstration proceeded through six interlocking arguments: the Galatians' own experience of receiving the Spirit through faith-hearing without circumcision (3:1-5); Abraham's justification by faith before circumcision existed (3:6-9); the law's curse on those who fail to keep all its provisions and Christ's exhaustion of that curse on the cross (3:10-14); the priority of the Abrahamic promise over the Sinai law, which came 430 years later and cannot alter the prior covenant (3:15-18); the law's addition for transgressions with a defined jurisdictional role, unable to give life (3:19-22); and the law's function as a paidagogos -- a household slave who escorts a child to school -- whose supervisory role over "us" (Jewish believers under the law's covenant jurisdiction) has ended with the arrival of Christ/faith (3:23-25). The chapter culminated in the declaration that addresses the Galatians directly: "In Christ Jesus you are all sons of God, through faith" (3:26). Baptism into Christ constitutes putting on Christ (3:27); in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male and female (3:28); and "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed, heirs according to promise" (3:29). The Galatians are already what proselyte conversion claims to make them.

**What the author argues immediately after (chapter 5):** The final verse of chapter 4 (4:31, "we are not children of the slave but of the free woman") flows directly into 5:1: "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery." Paul then states the consequences of accepting circumcision as a basis of justification without qualification: Christ will be of no advantage (5:2); the circumcised person is obligated to keep the whole law (5:3); those who seek justification through the law are severed from Christ and have fallen from grace (5:4). The positive alternative is "faith working through love" (5:6). The agitators are denounced (5:7-12), and freedom is defined not as license but as the arena for love-service (5:13-15), with the Spirit producing the character that the law aimed at (5:16-26). The ethical exhortation of chapters 5-6 answers the implicit question that the argument of chapters 3-4 has raised: if the law's covenant jurisdiction has ended and proselyte conversion is unnecessary, what governs conduct? The answer is the Spirit, who produces love that fulfills the law's ethical content (5:14, citing Leviticus 19:18) without requiring covenant entry through circumcision (5:18).

**How chapter 4 connects to both and advances the argument:** Chapter 4 extends, intensifies, and completes the theological argument of chapters 3-4 in four movements. First, the heir-guardian metaphor (4:1-7) continues the paidagogos metaphor of 3:24-25, now from the perspective of the heir's status rather than the custodian's function. The minor heir who was no different from a slave has come of age through God's sending of the Son, who entered the Sinai covenant's jurisdiction ("born under the law") to redeem those held there. The Spirit of the Son in believers' hearts confirms their adoption. Second, the stoicheia equation (4:8-11) makes the letter's most radical move: it equates the Galatians' proposed adoption of Torah-covenant membership with a return to their former pagan bondage, on the ground that both systems function identically as slavery to elemental principles. Third, the personal appeal (4:12-20) exposes the rival teachers' motives as manipulative flattery and recalls the Galatians' initial reception of Paul. Fourth, the Hagar-Sarah allegory (4:21-31) reads the Torah against those who claim to champion it, demonstrating from scripture that the flesh-method of becoming Abraham's child (proselyte conversion through circumcision) produces slavery, while the promise-method (faith) produces freedom -- and that the Torah itself commands the expulsion of the slave-woman's son. The chapter thus moves from the christological event that ended minority (4:1-7), through the practical consequences of ignoring that event (4:8-11), through the personal relationship at stake (4:12-20), to the scriptural demonstration that proselyte conversion is self-defeating by the Torah's own testimony (4:21-31).

## Verses 1-7: The Heir Under Guardians -- From Minority to Sonship

> I mean that the heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave, though he is the owner of everything, but he is under guardians and managers until the date set by his father. In the same way we also, when we were children, were enslaved under the elementary principles of the world. But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, "Abba! Father!" So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God.

This section continues directly from 3:23-29. The paidagogos metaphor (3:24-25) described the law's function -- a custodian-escort whose supervisory role has ended. The heir-guardian metaphor (4:1-2) now describes the heir's experience -- a minor who, despite being the owner of everything, lived under restrictions indistinguishable from slavery. The two metaphors are complementary perspectives on the same reality: 3:24-25 looked at the custodian; 4:1-7 looks at the child. Together they establish both that the law's custodial role was real and temporary, and that the heir's minority was real and has ended.

**Verse 1: "I mean that the heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave, though he is the owner of everything."**

"I mean" (lego de) -- the conjunction de marks continuation, not contrast. Paul is elaborating what he has just said in 3:23-29, not changing subjects. The heir-guardian metaphor is the next step in the same argument.

"The heir, as long as he is a child" (ho kleronomos... eph' hoson chronon nepios estin) -- the word kleronomos ("heir") returns from 3:29, where the Galatians were declared "heirs according to promise." The word nepios ("infant, minor, one under age") designates a child who has not yet reached the legal age at which he assumes control of his inheritance. In Roman law, the impubes (minor under the age of puberty, typically fourteen for males) was subject to a tutor (guardian) who managed both his person and his property. In Greek practice, the age of majority varied but the principle was the same: a minor heir had no independent authority despite his legal ownership. The temporal clause eph' hoson chronon ("for as long a time as, during whatever period") emphasizes that the heir's minority is measured by time -- it has a duration, and that duration ends.

"Is no different from a slave" (ouden diapherei doulou) -- the verb diaphero ("to differ, to be distinct from") with the genitive doulou ("from a slave") draws a stark comparison. The heir who is still a minor has no more practical freedom than a slave. Both are under the authority of others. Both obey instructions. Both lack independent agency. The comparison is deliberately provocative: the word doulos ("slave") has been the letter's primary term for the condition from which Christ liberates (2:4; the false brothers sought to "bring us into slavery"; the entire slavery/freedom contrast runs through the letter). The heir is technically an owner but functionally a slave. Status and experience are disconnected: he possesses the title without the reality.

"Though he is the owner of everything" (kyrios panton on) -- the concessive participle on ("being, though he is") preserves the paradox. The word kyrios ("lord, owner, master") placed alongside panton ("of all things, of everything") identifies the heir's legal status: he is the master of the entire estate. But the concession means nothing in practice until the appointed time. Ownership without access is indistinguishable from dispossession. This paradox maps onto the argument of 3:23-29: those under the law's custody (Jewish believers) were heirs of the Abrahamic promise, but their experience under the law's jurisdiction was functionally equivalent to slavery -- they possessed the inheritance in principle but could not access it directly until the appointed time arrived.

**Verse 2: "But he is under guardians and managers until the date set by his father."**

"Under guardians and managers" (hypo epitropous estin kai oikonomous) -- two categories of supervisory authority are named. The word epitropos ("guardian, trustee, steward") in Roman law was the tutor -- the person appointed to manage the minor's person and legal affairs. The word oikonomos ("household manager, steward, estate administrator") managed the minor's property and financial affairs. The preposition hypo ("under") with the accusative indicates subjection to authority -- the same preposition used throughout the letter for being "under the law" (hypo nomon, 3:23; 4:4, 5, 21; 5:18). The parallel is deliberate: being under guardians and managers in the domestic analogy corresponds to being under the law in the theological argument.

"Until the date set by his father" (achri tes prothesmias tou patros) -- the word prothesmia ("appointed date, fixed time, predetermined term") is a legal term for a deadline or a date fixed in advance. In Greek law, it could refer to the specific date set by a father's will or by legal convention for the heir's assumption of control. The genitive tou patros ("of the father") identifies who sets the date: the father, not the guardians, not the heir, and not external circumstances. The father's authority is supreme over the entire arrangement -- he appointed the guardians, he set the date, and when the date arrives, his decision terminates their authority. This maps onto the theological argument with precision: God (the Father) appointed the law (the guardians) and set the date (the fullness of time, v. 4) at which the law's supervisory role would end. The law did not determine its own duration; God did.

The temporal marker achri ("until") echoes 3:19 (achris hou elthe to sperma, "until the offspring should come"), reinforcing the parallel between the two metaphors: the paidagogos served until Christ came; the guardians serve until the father's appointed date. Both express the same truth: the law's custodial authority was real, divinely appointed, and temporary.

**Verse 3: "In the same way we also, when we were children, were enslaved under the elementary principles of the world."**

"In the same way we also" (houtos kai hemeis) -- the adverb houtos ("in the same way, likewise, so also") connects the analogy to the reality it illustrates. The pronoun hemeis ("we") is critical. The whole-book analysis identifies this "we" as Jewish believers -- those who were under the law's covenant jurisdiction. The "we" of 4:3 corresponds to the "we" of 3:23-25 ("we were held captive under the law," "the law was our paidagogos"). These are not the Galatian Gentile believers, who were never under the law's custody. They are Jewish believers describing their pre-Christ experience within the Sinai covenant.

"When we were children" (hote emen nepioi) -- the verb emen (imperfect of eimi, "to be") with the plural subject and the predicate nepioi ("minors, children under age") describes the pre-Christ era of Jewish experience. The word nepioi picks up from verse 1: during the period of minority, the heir was under guardians. The "we" -- Jewish believers -- were in the position of minor heirs: possessing the promises in principle but living under custodial authority in practice.

"Were enslaved under the elementary principles of the world" (hypo ta stoicheia tou kosmou emetha dedoulomenoi) -- the periphrastic construction emetha dedoulomenoi (imperfect of eimi + perfect passive participle of douloo, "to enslave") describes a sustained state of enslavement. The phrase hypo ta stoicheia tou kosmou ("under the elementary principles of the world") introduces one of the letter's most important and contested terms.

The word stoicheia (plural of stoicheion) has a range of meanings in first-century usage. Its root sense is "element" -- something that stands in a row or sequence. In ancient usage it could refer to: (a) the letters of the alphabet as the basic elements of language; (b) the basic elements or components of the physical world (earth, water, air, fire -- so Empedocles, Plato's Timaeus, and later Stoic physics); (c) rudimentary principles or foundational teachings (as in Hebrews 5:12, ta stoicheia tes arches ton logion tou theou, "the elementary principles of the oracles of God"); (d) cosmic or astral powers, elemental spirits, or forces associated with the regulation of cosmic order (as in later Jewish and early Christian usage). In the Wisdom of Solomon 7:17, stoicheia appears in connection with cosmological knowledge: "the structure of the world and the operation of the elements (energeian stoicheion)." In 2 Peter 3:10, 12, stoicheia refers to the elements of the physical cosmos that will be dissolved by fire.

Paul's usage in Galatians must be determined by context, and the context is decisive. In 4:3, the stoicheia are what "we" (Jewish believers) were enslaved under before Christ -- this points to the regulatory system of the Sinai covenant as experienced by those under its jurisdiction. In 4:8-9, Paul will equate the Galatians' former pagan bondage with the stoicheia and call their proposed adoption of Torah-covenant membership a "return" (palin, "again") to the same stoicheia. The equation requires that stoicheia function as a category encompassing both the Torah's prescriptions (as a covenant system conditioning access to God) and pagan worship practices. The whole-book analysis defines stoicheia tou kosmou as "the regulatory system under which both Jews and Gentiles lived before Christ" -- for Jews, these were the Torah's prescriptions about times, seasons, food, and ritual as a covenant system; for Gentiles, these were the worship practices tied to cosmic cycles and non-gods. Both systems, despite different content, functioned identically: both conditioned access to God on compliance with external regulations.

The genitive tou kosmou ("of the world") locates the stoicheia within the world-order -- the present evil age (1:4) from which Christ has delivered believers. The stoicheia belong to the kosmos, the world-system that operates according to principles other than grace and faith. The phrase does not say the stoicheia are evil in themselves (the Torah is from God; Paul has already denied that the law is contrary to God's promises, 3:21); it says they belong to the world-order whose regulatory function over believers has ended.

The verse establishes that Jewish believers, before Christ, were in the same structural position as the minor heir of verses 1-2: they were heirs who lived as slaves, under the custodial authority of the stoicheia. The law's covenant system -- its calendar regulations, food laws, circumcision requirements, and ritual prescriptions as a comprehensive system of external compliance -- constituted a form of bondage for those under its jurisdiction. This is not a judgment on the law's moral content (which Paul will continue to teach, 5:14; 6:2) but on the structural position of being under its covenant jurisdiction as a condition of standing before God.

**Verse 4: "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law."**

"When the fullness of time had come" (hote de elthen to pleroma tou chronou) -- the conjunction hote de ("but when") marks the decisive transition from the era of minority to the era of maturity. The phrase to pleroma tou chronou ("the fullness of time") designates the moment when the father's appointed date (prothesmia, v. 2) arrived. The word pleroma ("fullness, completion, that which fills up") indicates that the time had reached its intended measure -- it was not premature, not delayed, but precisely on schedule. The word chronos ("time") refers to chronological time, the measured duration that the father determined. The theological claim is that the Christ-event was not an accident of history but the arrival of the date God had set from the beginning. The law's custodial function did not expire by its own logic; it expired because the father's clock reached the appointed hour.

"God sent forth his Son" (exapesteilen ho theos ton huion autou) -- the verb exapostello ("to send out, to send forth, to dispatch") with the prefix ex- ("out of, forth from") suggests sending from one location to another -- from God's presence into the world. The subject is ho theos ("God") and the object is ton huion autou ("his Son") -- the fullest christological designation in the passage. The Son is God's own Son, not an envoy or a prophet but one whose relationship to God is that of son to father. The language echoes the prophetic pattern of divine sending: in the LXX, God "sends" (apostello/exapostello) his servants, his word, his spirit, and his angel. But here God sends his Son -- the one who is uniquely positioned to accomplish what prophets and angels could not.

The whole-book analysis notes that this sending fulfills the purpose stated in 1:4: Christ "gave himself for our sins so that he might deliver us from the present evil age." The sending of the Son is the mechanism of that deliverance.

"Born of woman" (genomenon ek gynaikos) -- the participle genomenon (from ginomai, "to become, to be born") describes the Son's entrance into human existence. The phrase ek gynaikos ("of a woman, from a woman") is a Semitism for ordinary human birth. In Job 14:1 (LXX): brotos gar gennetos gynaikos ("for a mortal born of a woman"); Job 15:14: tis gar estin brotos hoti estai amemptos, e hos esomenos dikaios gennetos gynaikos ("what is a mortal that he should be blameless, or one born of a woman that he should be righteous?"); Job 25:4: pos gar estai dikaios brotos enanti kyriou, e tis an apokatharisei heauton gennetos gynaikos ("how then can a mortal be righteous before God, or how can one born of a woman make himself clean?"). The phrase identifies Jesus as fully human -- born in the ordinary way, entering the human condition without reservation.

"Born under the law" (genomenon hypo nomon) -- the second participle genomenon, parallel to the first, places the Son not merely within the human condition but specifically within the Sinai covenant's jurisdiction. The phrase hypo nomon ("under the law") has been established by the whole-book analysis as referring to being under the law's covenant jurisdiction (3:23; 4:5, 21; 5:18). The Son was born a Jew, within the boundaries of the Sinai covenant, subject to its requirements and obligations. He entered the very system that held its members in custodial bondage (4:3). The purpose of this entry is stated in the next verse: he entered the system in order to redeem those held within it.

The two participial clauses trace a double descent: from divine preexistence into humanity (born of woman), and from humanity in general into the specific covenant jurisdiction of Israel (born under the law). Each descent is purposeful -- the Son entered the condition he came to transform.

**Verse 5: "To redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons."**

"To redeem those who were under the law" (hina tous hypo nomon exagorase) -- the purpose clause (hina + subjunctive) states the first goal of the Son's mission. The verb exagorazo ("to buy out, to redeem, to purchase from") is the same commercial term used in 3:13 ("Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law"). The prefix ex- ("out of") indicates removal from a prior condition: those under the law's jurisdiction are bought out of that jurisdiction. The recipients -- tous hypo nomon ("those under the law") -- are those who were within the Sinai covenant's domain, the same "we" of verse 3 (Jewish believers). The Son entered the law's jurisdiction (v. 4) in order to extract those held within it.

The verb exagorazo carries echoes of Israel's foundational redemptive narrative: the Exodus. In the LXX, the vocabulary of redemption -- lytroo ("to ransom"), exaireo ("to pluck out, to deliver"), and related terms -- describes God's act of buying Israel out of slavery in Egypt (Exodus 6:6; 15:13; Deuteronomy 7:8; 9:26; 13:5; 15:15; 21:8; 24:18). Paul's use of commercial-redemption language places Christ's work in the same category as the Exodus: a liberating purchase that removes the enslaved from the jurisdiction of their master. The parallel is striking: the jurisdiction from which Christ redeems is the Sinai covenant's domain -- the covenant given to the people after the Exodus. The law's covenant-jurisdictional function, which was always intended to operate until the seed arrived (3:19), has reached the point where the seed has come and a new act of divine redemption liberates those held within it.

"So that we might receive adoption as sons" (hina ten huiothesian apolabomen) -- the second purpose clause identifies the positive goal. The word huiothesia ("adoption as sons, sonship") is a compound of huios ("son") and thesis ("placement, setting") -- the placement into the position of a son. In Roman legal practice, adoption (adoptio) was the formal, legal act by which a person was placed into a new family with full rights of sonship, including inheritance rights. The adopted son ceased to belong legally to his former family and became, in the eyes of the law, a full son of the adopting father. The word carries this legal precision: believers receive the formal, recognized, publicly ratified status of sons.

The pronoun shifts at this clause. The whole-book analysis identifies the "we" (apolabomen, first person plural subjunctive) as broadening from the narrow "we" of verse 3 (Jewish believers under the law) to include all believers -- Jewish and Gentile. The same broadening occurred at 3:14b ("so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith"). The redemption of Jewish believers from the law's jurisdiction was not for their benefit alone; it was so that "we" -- all who believe, Jew and Gentile together -- might receive adoption as sons. The liberation of the heirs from their guardians opens sonship to everyone who belongs to Christ, regardless of whether they were ever under the guardians' authority.

The verb apolambano ("to receive back, to recover, to receive what is due") with the prefix apo- ("back, in return") may carry the nuance of receiving what was already owed -- recovering what was promised. The inheritance was always intended for the heirs; the guardians' authority deferred its reception. Now the appointed time has come, and the heirs receive what was promised to them.

**Verse 6: "And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, 'Abba! Father!'"**

"And because you are sons" (hoti de este huioi) -- the conjunction hoti is causal: "because." The pronoun shifts decisively. The "you" (este, second person plural) addresses the Galatians directly -- Gentile believers. Paul's declaration is not that the Galatians may become sons if they accept circumcision; it is that they are sons already. Their sonship is a present reality, the established basis for what follows. The causal connection is important: the sending of the Spirit is a consequence of their sonship, not the cause of it. God did not send the Spirit to make them sons; God sent the Spirit because they are sons. Their status (sons) precedes and grounds the experience (receiving the Spirit).

"God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts" (exapesteilen ho theos to pneuma tou huiou autou eis tas kardias hemon) -- the verb exapostello ("to send forth") is the same verb used in verse 4 for the sending of the Son. Two sendings define the passage: God sent forth the Son (v. 4) and God sent forth the Spirit of the Son (v. 6). The parallel is structural: the first sending accomplished redemption and adoption; the second sending confirms and makes experiential the adoption already accomplished.

The Spirit is identified as "the Spirit of his Son" (to pneuma tou huiou autou) -- not merely the Spirit of God in general but the Spirit belonging to and proceeding from the Son. This identification links the Spirit's activity to the Son's identity: the Spirit who enters believers' hearts is the Spirit of the same Son who was born of woman and born under the law (v. 4). The connection is tight: the Son entered the law's jurisdiction to redeem; the Spirit of the Son enters believers' hearts to confirm.

"Into our hearts" (eis tas kardias hemon) -- the destination of the Spirit's sending is internal: the heart, the center of will, identity, and orientation in Jewish anthropology. The LXX uses kardia to translate the Hebrew lev/levav throughout the scriptures; Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart (ex holes tes kardias sou)." The Spirit's entry into the heart is the inner counterpart to the outward declaration of sonship.

The pronoun hemon ("our") in some manuscripts reads hymon ("your"). The ambiguity is productive: whether "our hearts" or "your hearts," the point is that the Spirit enters the hearts of believers. If hemon is original, the broadened "we" includes Paul alongside the Galatians -- all believers, Jewish and Gentile, have received the Spirit of the Son.

"Crying, 'Abba! Father!'" (krazon: Abba ho pater) -- the participle krazon ("crying out") describes the Spirit's activity within the heart. The verb krazo ("to cry out, to call aloud, to shout") conveys intensity and urgency -- this is not a whisper but a cry. The grammatical subject of krazon is the Spirit (to pneuma), not the believer: it is the Spirit who cries out. The Spirit's cry within the believer constitutes the experiential evidence of sonship. Romans 8:15-16 provides the parallel: "You have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!' The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God."

The double address Abba ho pater uses both the Aramaic abba (the familiar term for "father" used in Jewish family life, attested in the Targums and later rabbinic literature) and the Greek ho pater ("Father") as a translation. The combination preserves the original Aramaic term used by Jesus himself (Mark 14:36: abba ho pater, "Abba, Father") while making it accessible to Greek-speaking readers. The term is intimate: it addresses God as a child addresses a father, presupposing the relationship of sonship that the passage has been establishing.

The theological significance for the letter's argument is direct: the Galatians already have the Spirit of the Son in their hearts. The Spirit is already crying "Abba, Father." Their sonship is already confirmed by the most intimate possible evidence -- the Spirit's own testimony within them. Circumcision cannot add to this. Proselyte conversion cannot intensify what the Spirit of God's own Son is already doing in their hearts. The rival teachers' demand is not merely unnecessary; it is an attempt to supplement the unsupplementable.

**Verse 7: "So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God."**

"So you are no longer a slave, but a son" (hoste ouketi ei doulos alla huios) -- the inferential hoste ("so, therefore, consequently") draws the conclusion from everything that has preceded. The adverb ouketi ("no longer") marks the change of status: what was formerly the case is no longer the case. The address shifts to the second person singular (ei, "you are") -- Paul individualizes the declaration, speaking to each Galatian believer personally. The contrast is absolute: doulos ("slave") versus huios ("son"). The minor heir who was "no different from a slave" (v. 1) is now recognized as a son. The transition from slavery to sonship is the transition from the era of the stoicheia (v. 3) to the era of the Spirit (v. 6), from minority under guardians to maturity under the Father.

"And if a son, then an heir through God" (ei de huios, kai kleronomos dia theou) -- the conditional is logical, not uncertain: if you are a son (and you are, v. 6), then you are an heir. The word kleronomos ("heir") returns from verse 1 and from 3:29, closing the circle: the chapter began with the minor heir who was no different from a slave; it concludes with the heir who has come of age through God. The phrase dia theou ("through God") identifies the agent: sonship and inheritance come through God's action -- the sending of the Son (v. 4), the sending of the Spirit (v. 6), the adoption (v. 5). The entire process is God's initiative. The Galatians did not earn their inheritance; God gave it to them through the double sending.

The section 4:1-7 completes the argument begun in 3:23. The law's custodial function has been presented from two angles: 3:23-25 described it as a paidagogos whose term of service has ended; 4:1-7 describes it as a guardian-and-manager system over a minor heir whose appointed time of maturity has arrived. The Son entered the system to buy out its members. The Spirit confirms the new status. The Galatians are sons and heirs. Everything the rival teachers promise through circumcision -- Abrahamic sonship, inheritance, standing before God -- the Galatians already possess through faith, incorporation into Christ, and the Spirit's indwelling presence. The agitators' program would take the heir who has come of age and place him back under the guardians whose commission has expired.

## Verses 8-11: The Stoicheia Equation -- Proselyte Conversion as Return to Pagan Bondage

> Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods. But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God, how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles? Do you wish to be enslaved by them all over again? You observe days and months and seasons and years! I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain.

This section contains the letter's most radical argumentative move. Paul equates the Galatians' proposed adoption of Torah-covenant membership -- the very program the rival teachers present as an advance in religious sophistication -- with a return to the pagan bondage they had left behind. The equation depends on the word palin ("again") in verse 9, which forces the reader to recognize that both systems, despite radically different content, occupy the same structural position: bondage to stoicheia.

**Verse 8: "Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods."**

"Formerly" (alla tote men) -- the adversative alla ("but") marks a shift from the "we" (Jewish believers) of verses 3-5 to the "you" (Galatian Gentile believers) of verses 8-11. The temporal adverb tote ("then, at that time, formerly") with the particle men (setting up a men... de contrast with verse 9) establishes the before-and-after structure: "then" (before conversion) versus "now" (after conversion).

"When you did not know God" (ouk eidotes theon) -- the participle eidotes (from oida, "to know") in the negative describes the Galatians' pre-conversion state: they did not know God. The verb oida implies intimate, settled knowledge -- not mere awareness but genuine recognition. The Galatians lived without knowledge of the true God.

"You were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods" (edouleusate tois physei me ousin theois) -- the verb douleo ("to serve as a slave, to be enslaved") in the aorist describes the Galatians' former condition as one of slavery. The objects of this slavery are identified as tois physei me ousin theois ("those that by nature are not gods"). The dative phrase physei ("by nature") modifies the participial clause me ousin theois ("not being gods"): whatever these entities are, they are not gods by nature -- they are not genuinely divine. The language echoes Isaiah 37:19 (LXX, = 2 Kings 19:18): "and cast their gods into the fire, for they were not gods (hoti ouk eisin theoi) but the works of human hands." Jeremiah 2:11: "Has a nation changed its gods, even though they are no gods (autoi de ouk eisin theoi)?" The Galatians' former worship was directed at entities that are not genuinely gods -- the idols, cosmic forces, and pagan deities of their pre-conversion religious practice.

This verse establishes the baseline against which the stoicheia equation will work. The Galatians' pre-conversion state was slavery to non-gods. This is the condition from which the gospel delivered them. The rival teachers are not proposing a return to pagan idols; they are proposing something entirely different -- circumcision, Torah-observance, covenant entry. What Paul will argue in verse 9 is that despite the radical difference in content, the structural position is identical: both constitute enslavement to stoicheia.

**Verse 9: "But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God, how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles? Do you wish to be enslaved by them all over again?"**

"But now that you have come to know God" (nyn de gnontes theon) -- the temporal nyn de ("but now") marks the contrast with the former state. The aorist participle gnontes (from ginosko, "to come to know, to recognize") describes the Galatians' conversion: they came to know God. Their knowledge of God replaced their ignorance (v. 8). This is not theoretical knowledge but relational recognition -- they came into relationship with the living God.

"Or rather to be known by God" (mallon de gnosthentes hypo theou) -- Paul corrects himself mid-sentence. The adverb mallon ("rather, more accurately") introduces a more precise formulation. The aorist passive participle gnosthentes ("having been known") reverses the direction: the Galatians did not discover God; God knew them. The divine passive hypo theou ("by God") identifies God as the subject. God's knowledge of them is prior to and the ground of their knowledge of him. This correction is theologically important: if God's knowing them is the foundation of the relationship, then the relationship rests on God's initiative, not on the Galatians' religious performance. Any system that conditions standing before God on human compliance -- whether pagan ritual or proselyte conversion -- misunderstands who initiated the relationship.

The correction also echoes the prophetic language of divine election. In Amos 3:2 (LXX): hymas egnon ek pason ton phylon tes ges ("you I have known from all the families of the earth") -- where "know" (ginosko/yada) means to choose, to single out, to enter into covenant relationship with. God has known the Galatians -- chosen them, entered into relationship with them. This is not something their religious performance produced; it is something God did.

"How can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles?" (pos epistrephete palin epi ta asthene kai ptocha stoicheia) -- the verb epistrepho ("to turn, to turn back, to return") describes a reversal of direction. The present tense epistrephete indicates that the turning is in progress -- the Galatians are in the act of turning back. The adverb palin ("again, once more") is the crux of the entire equation.

The word palin creates an apparently insoluble problem: how can the Galatians be "turning back again" to the stoicheia by adopting Torah-covenant membership, when they were never under the Torah before? They were under pagan stoicheia, not Torah-stoicheia. Paul's answer, implicit in the very use of palin, is that both systems are stoicheia. The Galatians are not returning to the identical stoicheia they left; they are returning to the category of stoicheia-bondage. Becoming a proselyte is a "return" because it places them back into the structural position they occupied before conversion: subjection to an external regulatory system as the condition of access to God. The content differs (Torah is from God; pagan worship is not), but the structural function is identical (both condition access to the divine on human compliance with a system of external regulations).

The adjectives asthene ("weak, powerless, lacking strength") and ptocha ("impoverished, beggarly, destitute") characterize the stoicheia with contempt. The word asthenes denotes incapacity -- the stoicheia lack the power to accomplish what they promise. The word ptochos denotes poverty -- the stoicheia have nothing to give. In the LXX, ptochos describes those who are destitute and dependent (Psalm 40:17 LXX = 41:1; Psalm 69:29 LXX = 70:5). The stoicheia are bankrupt regulatory systems: they demand compliance but cannot deliver what they promise. The Torah's covenant system cannot give life (3:21); pagan worship serves non-gods (4:8). Both are weak and impoverished -- incapable of producing the righteousness, life, or standing before God that their adherents seek through them.

"Do you wish to be enslaved by them all over again?" (hois palin anothen douleuein thelete?) -- the word anothen ("again, anew, from the beginning, from above") combined with palin ("again") creates a double emphasis on repetition: "again anew," "once more from the start." The verb douleo ("to be enslaved, to serve as a slave") returns from verse 8, where the Galatians "were enslaved" to non-gods. The verb thelo ("to wish, to desire, to want") in the present tense suggests active volition: this is not something being forced on them but something they are choosing. Paul asks: do you actually want this? The question is incredulous -- the Galatians, who were delivered from slavery, are voluntarily choosing to re-enter slavery. The form has changed (circumcision instead of idol worship); the substance is the same (bondage to an external system as the condition of divine access).

The whole-book analysis identifies this equation as the letter's most radical move. It is radical because it places the Sinai covenant's regulatory system in the same structural category as pagan idol worship. This is not a judgment on the Torah's content (Paul will continue to teach Torah's ethical instruction, 5:14; 6:2) or on the Torah's origin (the Torah is from God, delivered through angels and a mediator, 3:19). It is a judgment on what happens when the Torah's covenant system is adopted as the basis of standing before God by those who were never under its jurisdiction. For Gentiles to enter the Sinai covenant through circumcision is not an upgrade from paganism but a lateral move into the same category: bondage to stoicheia.

**Verse 10: "You observe days and months and seasons and years!"**

"You observe" (hemeras paratereisthe kai menas kai kairous kai eniautous) -- the verb paratereo ("to watch closely, to observe scrupulously, to keep with attention") describes meticulous observance. The present tense indicates ongoing practice: the Galatians are already doing this. The objects are four categories of time-observance:

- hemeras ("days") -- likely the weekly Sabbath and other designated days in the Jewish calendar.
- menas ("months") -- the new moon observances; the first day of each lunar month was marked by special offerings and celebrations (Numbers 28:11-15; 1 Chronicles 23:31; Isaiah 1:13-14).
- kairous ("seasons, appointed times") -- the annual festivals: Passover, Weeks (Pentecost), Tabernacles, and other appointed gatherings (Leviticus 23; Numbers 28-29).
- eniautous ("years") -- likely the sabbatical year (every seventh year, Leviticus 25:1-7) or the jubilee (every fiftieth year, Leviticus 25:8-12), though the reference may be more general.

The four categories encompass the entire Jewish liturgical calendar. The Galatians have adopted the calendar before completing the process of proselyte conversion (circumcision has not yet occurred, 5:2). Calendar observance is one of the boundary markers that define Jewish covenant identity, and the Galatians' adoption of it represents the beginning of their move toward full proselyte conversion. This concrete evidence of their regression -- the observable fact that they are already keeping the Jewish calendar -- grounds Paul's abstract argument about stoicheia in lived reality. The stoicheia are not merely theoretical; they are hemeras kai menas kai kairous kai eniautous -- the calendar system that regulates life within the covenant.

The parallel with Colossians 2:16 is notable: "Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival (heortes) or a new moon (neomenias) or a Sabbath (sabbaton)." The shared concern with calendrical observance as a marker of covenant membership (or submission to regulatory systems) suggests a common pattern in Paul's engagement with the issue of Gentile believers adopting Jewish practice.

**Verse 11: "I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain."**

"I am afraid" (phoboumai hymas) -- the verb phobeo ("to fear, to be afraid, to be apprehensive") in the present tense describes Paul's current emotional state. The accusative hymas ("you, on your account") identifies the object of his fear: he is afraid for them, not of them.

"I may have labored over you in vain" (me pos eike kekopiaka eis hymas) -- the particles me pos ("lest somehow, that perhaps") introduce an apprehension. The adverb eike ("in vain, to no purpose") echoes 3:4 (epathete... eike, "did you suffer... in vain?"). The verb kopiao ("to labor, to toil, to work hard") in the perfect tense kekopiaka describes completed labor with continuing relevance: Paul's missionary work among the Galatians was real, costly effort, and its effects persist -- but they are at risk of being rendered pointless. The preposition eis hymas ("for you, toward you, with reference to you") identifies the Galatians as the objects of his labor.

The fear is not that the gospel is wrong but that the Galatians' response may undo the gospel's effects. If they complete their turn to proselyte conversion, Paul's labor will have been for nothing -- not because the gospel he preached was deficient but because the Galatians will have abandoned it for a different operating system. The verse expresses pastoral anguish, not theological uncertainty.

## Verses 12-20: The Personal Appeal -- From Warmth to Estrangement

> Brothers, I entreat you, become as I am, for I also became as you are. You did me no wrong. You know it was because of a bodily ailment that I preached the gospel to you at first, and though my condition was a trial to you, you did not scorn or despise me, but received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus. What then has become of your blessedness? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me. Have I then become your enemy by telling you the truth? They make much of you, but for no good purpose. They want to shut you out, that you may make much of them. It is always good to be made much of for a good purpose, and not only when I am present with you, my little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until Christ is formed in you! I wish I could be present with you now and change my tone, for I am perplexed about you.

The argument shifts from theological demonstration to personal appeal. Paul recalls the history of his relationship with the Galatians, contrasting their initial extraordinary warmth with their present estrangement, exposing the rival teachers' manipulative tactics, and expressing his anguish with the metaphor of childbirth. The section does not advance the theological argument per se, but it serves a rhetorical function: it reminds the Galatians that Paul is not an adversary delivering a lecture but a father in anguish over his children.

**Verse 12a: "Brothers, I entreat you, become as I am, for I also became as you are."**

"Brothers, I entreat you" (deomai hymon, adelphoi) -- the verb deomai ("to beg, to entreat, to implore") expresses earnest request. The address adelphoi ("brothers") maintains the familial bond. After the sharp rebuke of 3:1 ("O foolish Galatians!") and the alarming warning of 4:8-11, Paul returns to the register of appeal.

"Become as I am, for I also became as you are" (ginesthe hos ego, hoti kago hos hymeis) -- the exhortation is compressed and depends on context for its meaning. Paul asks the Galatians to become like him. In what respect? Given the letter's argument, the answer is: free from the law's covenant jurisdiction. Paul, a Jew by birth (2:15) and a Torah-keeping Jew throughout his life, died to the law through the law (2:19) in the sense that the law's covenant-jurisdictional claim -- its demand to serve as the basis of standing before God -- was exhausted at the cross. He became "as you are" not by abandoning his own Jewish practice but by accepting Gentile believers as full members of God's people without requiring them to become proselytes -- the same table fellowship Cephas initially practiced at Antioch and then abandoned under pressure (2:12, 14). Paul, a Jew, became as a Gentile in the sense of not imposing the Sinai covenant's boundary markers as conditions of community membership. He now asks the Galatians to become as he is -- to live in the freedom of faith rather than entering the law's covenant jurisdiction through proselyte conversion.

**Verse 12b: "You did me no wrong."**

"You did me no wrong" (ouden me edikesate) -- the verb adikeo ("to wrong, to injure, to treat unjustly") in the aorist describes a past event or, more precisely, the absence of one: the Galatians did not wrong Paul during his initial visit. This statement seems abrupt, and its precise referent is unclear. It may preemptively dismiss any suggestion that Paul has personal grievances against the Galatians, or it may recall that their original relationship was without conflict -- the current tension is new and entirely the product of the rival teachers' intervention.

**Verses 13-14: "You know it was because of a bodily ailment that I preached the gospel to you at first, and though my condition was a trial to you, you did not scorn or despise me, but received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus."**

"Because of a bodily ailment" (di' astheneian tes sarkos) -- the phrase identifies the occasion of Paul's initial preaching in Galatia: a physical weakness or illness. The word astheneia ("weakness, sickness, infirmity") combined with tes sarkos ("of the flesh") -- here non-polemical, meaning "of the body," the physical condition, as in 2:20 -- describes a bodily ailment that either compelled Paul to stop in Galatia or provided the context within which he preached. The preposition dia with the accusative indicates cause: "because of" bodily weakness. Paul's visit was unplanned; his illness forced a stay during which he preached.

"At first" (to proteron) -- the adverb indicates an initial visit, implying at least one subsequent one. Paul visited Galatia at least twice.

"Though my condition was a trial to you" (kai ton peirasmon hymon en te sarki mou) -- the word peirasmos ("trial, test, temptation") identifies Paul's physical condition as something that tested the Galatians' reception of him. Whatever the ailment was -- and speculation has ranged from ophthalmia (suggested by v. 15) to epilepsy to malaria to the effects of physical persecution -- it presented the Galatians with a reason to reject Paul. In the ancient world, physical affliction could be read as a sign of divine disfavor; a suffering, weakened teacher would have seemed an unlikely bearer of divine truth.

"You did not scorn or despise me" (ouk exouthenasate oude exeptysate) -- two strong verbs in the negative. The verb exoutheneo ("to despise, to treat with contempt, to regard as nothing") appears in the LXX for contempt of persons or things considered worthless (1 Samuel 8:7; Psalm 22:24 LXX). The verb ekptyo ("to spit out, to reject with disgust") is more visceral: literally "to spit out," expressing physical revulsion. In some Greco-Roman contexts, spitting was an apotropaic gesture against illness -- to ward off contamination from the sick. Paul is saying: you did not reject me as contaminated or despicable despite my bodily weakness.

"But received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus" (alla hos angelon theou edexasthe me, hos Christon Iesoun) -- the verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to accept") describes the warmth of the Galatians' initial reception. The double comparison -- "as an angel of God" (hos angelon theou) and "as Christ Jesus" (hos Christon Iesoun) -- expresses the highest possible honor. They received Paul as though he were a divine messenger (angelos, "angel, messenger") and even as though he were Christ Jesus himself. The irony in the letter's context is sharp: Paul pronounced anathema on anyone, even "an angel from heaven" (angelos ex ouranou, 1:8), who preaches a different gospel. The Galatians received Paul as an angel and now are turning to those who preach the different gospel Paul's anathema forbids.

**Verse 15: "What then has become of your blessedness? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me."**

"What then has become of your blessedness?" (pou oun ho makarismos hymon?) -- the word makarismos ("blessedness, felicitation, the state of being pronounced happy") asks what happened to the Galatians' sense of joy and privilege. The word pou ("where?") suggests that the blessedness has gone missing -- it was present once and is absent now.

"If possible, you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me" (ei dynaton tous ophthalmous hymon exoryxantes edokate moi) -- the verb exorysso ("to dig out, to gouge out, to tear out") describes an extreme act of physical sacrifice. The reference to eyes (ophthalmous) may suggest that Paul's ailment affected his eyes, making this particular sacrifice the most relevant; or it may be proverbial, expressing the highest conceivable act of self-sacrifice (one's eyes being among the most precious possessions). Either way, the point is the contrast: the Galatians who would once have sacrificed their own eyes for Paul are now treating him as an adversary (v. 16).

**Verse 16: "Have I then become your enemy by telling you the truth?"**

"Have I then become your enemy" (hoste echthros hymon gegona) -- the inferential hoste ("so, therefore") draws the painful conclusion. The word echthros ("enemy, adversary, hostile person") is a strong term. The verb gegona (perfect of ginomai, "to become") describes a change of status: Paul has become their enemy. The perfect tense indicates that this new state persists.

"By telling you the truth" (aletheuon hymin) -- the participle aletheuo ("to speak truth, to deal truthfully, to be truthful") identifies the cause of the estrangement: Paul told them the truth. The truth he tells is the letter's argument: proselyte conversion is unnecessary, the law cannot justify, the Spirit came through faith and not through circumcision. The rival teachers' message is more flattering; Paul's message is true. The Galatians prefer the flattery.

**Verses 17-18: "They make much of you, but for no good purpose. They want to shut you out, that you may make much of them. It is always good to be made much of for a good purpose, and not only when I am present with you."**

"They make much of you" (zelousin hymas) -- the verb zeloo ("to be zealous for, to court, to make much of, to pay assiduous attention to") describes the rival teachers' behavior toward the Galatians. The word carries the sense of courting or wooing -- the agitators are flattering the Galatians, showing them attention and honor.

"But for no good purpose" (ou kalos) -- the adverb kalos ("well, rightly, nobly") in the negative identifies the flattery as insincere: they do not court you with good intentions.

"They want to shut you out" (alla ekkleisai hymas thelousin) -- the verb ekkleio ("to shut out, to exclude, to lock out") reveals the real agenda. The rival teachers want to exclude the Galatians -- not from the community entirely but from direct access to God through faith alone. By making circumcision the condition of full standing, the agitators create a gatekeeping function for themselves: they become the necessary intermediaries through whom the Galatians must pass in order to achieve full acceptance. The exclusion is functional: the Galatians are told they are incomplete, insufficient, lacking -- and the rival teachers position themselves as the ones who can supply what is missing.

"That you may make much of them" (hina autous zeloute) -- the purpose clause reveals the endgame. The verb zeloo returns with the subject and object reversed: the rival teachers court the Galatians so that the Galatians will court them in return. The agitators want dependence, loyalty, and flattery directed at themselves. Their program is self-serving: by creating a need (you must be circumcised) that only they can meet (we will guide your conversion), they make themselves indispensable.

"It is always good to be made much of for a good purpose, and not only when I am present with you" (kalon de zeleousthai en kalo pantote, kai me monon en to pareinai me pros hymas) -- Paul does not oppose being courted per se. Genuine attention for good purposes is always good (pantote, "always"), whether Paul is present or absent. The implicit contrast is with the rival teachers, whose attention is manipulative, and with Paul himself, whose absence has left the Galatians vulnerable to manipulation. The rival teachers work best when Paul is absent; genuine spiritual care operates regardless of the teacher's presence.

**Verse 19: "My little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until Christ is formed in you!"**

"My little children" (tekna mou) -- the word teknon ("child, offspring") combined with the possessive mou ("my") is intimate and parental. Paul addresses the Galatians as a parent addresses young children. The relationship is not that of a teacher to students or an apostle to communities but of a parent to offspring.

"For whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth" (hous palin odino) -- the verb odino ("to be in labor, to experience birth pangs, to suffer the pains of childbirth") is a maternal metaphor: Paul describes himself as a mother in labor. The adverb palin ("again") indicates that this is not the first time: Paul already went through the labor of bringing the Galatians to birth in Christ during his initial mission. Now he is going through it again -- the agitators' intervention has undone the previous birth, and Paul must labor to bring them to birth once more. The image is anguished: childbirth is painful, exhausting, and uncertain in outcome.

The metaphor of labor pains has deep roots in Israel's prophetic tradition. Isaiah 26:17-18: "Like a pregnant woman who writhes and cries out in her pangs when she is near to giving birth, so were we because of you, O LORD." Isaiah 66:7-9: "Before she was in labor she gave birth; before her pain came upon her she delivered a son." The image of labor pains is also associated with the messianic age: the "birth pangs of the Messiah" (chevle mashiach) in later Jewish literature describe the suffering that precedes the new age. Paul's use of the image is personal and pastoral: he is the one in labor, and the Galatians are the ones being born.

"Until Christ is formed in you" (mechris hou morphothe Christos en hymin) -- the verb morphoo ("to form, to shape, to give shape to") describes the process by which Christ takes shape within the Galatians. The word morphe and its cognates carry the sense of essential form, not merely outward appearance. The preposition en hymin ("in you") can be read as distributive ("in each of you") or corporate ("among you"). The goal of Paul's labor is not merely that the Galatians would believe certain propositions but that Christ would be formed in them -- that their inner reality, their communal identity, their way of life would take the shape of Christ. This echoes 2:20: "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me." What Paul said of himself individually, he now desires for the Galatians corporately: Christ formed within them.

The verse is theologically rich: the rival teachers want to impose an external form (circumcision, calendar observance, proselyte status). Paul wants an internal formation (Christ taking shape within them). The contrast between external regulation and internal transformation is the same contrast that has structured the entire letter: flesh versus Spirit, works of the law versus faith, bondage to stoicheia versus the Spirit of the Son crying "Abba."

**Verse 20: "I wish I could be present with you now and change my tone, for I am perplexed about you."**

"I wish I could be present with you now" (ethelon de pareinai pros hymas arti) -- the verb thelo ("to wish, to desire") with the infinitive pareinai ("to be present") expresses a wish that cannot currently be fulfilled: Paul is distant from the Galatians. The temporal adverb arti ("now, at this present moment") emphasizes the immediacy of the desire.

"And change my tone" (kai allaxai ten phonen mou) -- the verb allasso ("to change, to alter, to exchange") with phone ("voice, tone") expresses Paul's frustration with the medium of a letter. A letter has a fixed tone; a personal presence allows flexibility, responsiveness, adaptation to the audience's reactions. Paul wishes he could modulate his approach -- sometimes sharp, sometimes tender, sometimes interrogative -- based on what he sees in their faces. The letter forces him to use a single register, and no single register is adequate to the complexity of the situation.

"For I am perplexed about you" (hoti aporoumai en hymin) -- the verb aporeo ("to be at a loss, to be perplexed, to be in difficulty") describes Paul's emotional and intellectual state. He does not know what will reach them. His arguments are strong -- the experiential evidence (3:1-5), the scriptural demonstrations (3:6-29), the theological logic (4:1-11) -- but the Galatians' response is uncertain. The perplexity is not about the truth of the gospel (Paul is certain of that) but about the Galatians themselves: will they hear? Will they turn back? Are they already lost? The uncertainty drives the anguish of verse 19 and motivates the final, climactic argument that follows in verses 21-31.

## Verses 21-31: The Hagar-Sarah Allegory -- The Torah Against the Proselyte Program

> Tell me, you who desire to be under the law, do you not listen to the law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by a slave woman and one by a free woman. But the son of the slave was born according to the flesh, while the son of the free woman was born through promise. Now this may be interpreted allegorically: these women are two covenants. One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children for slavery; she is Hagar. Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia; she corresponds to the present Jerusalem, for she is in slavery with her children. But the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother. For it is written, "Rejoice, O barren one who does not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor! For the children of the desolate one will be more than those of the one who has a husband." Now you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise. But just as at that time he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, so also it is now. But what does the Scripture say? "Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman." So, brothers, we are not children of the slave but of the free woman.

This section is the climactic argument of the theological section (2:15-4:31). Paul turns the Torah against those who claim to champion it. He asks those who want to place themselves under the law whether they have actually listened to what the law says. The answer, drawn from the Torah's own narrative, is that the flesh-method of becoming Abraham's child produces slavery, while the promise-method produces freedom -- and that the Torah itself commands the expulsion of the flesh-method's products.

**Verse 21: "Tell me, you who desire to be under the law, do you not listen to the law?"**

"Tell me" (legete moi) -- the imperative legete ("tell me, say to me") is direct and challenging. Paul demands an answer.

"You who desire to be under the law" (hoi hypo nomon thelontes einai) -- the present participle thelontes ("desiring, wanting, wishing") from thelo describes the Galatians' active volition: they want to be under the law. The phrase hypo nomon ("under the law") carries the same meaning it has had throughout the letter: under the law's covenant jurisdiction, entered through circumcision (3:23; 4:4, 5; 5:18). The Galatians have not yet completed this entry (circumcision has not occurred, 5:2) but they are actively pursuing it. Paul addresses their desire, not their accomplished status.

"Do you not listen to the law?" (ton nomon ouk akouete?) -- the verb akouo ("to hear, to listen to, to understand") with the negative ouk forms a rhetorical question that expects the answer "yes, we listen." But Paul's point is that they do not truly hear -- they are using the law's authority to justify their program without listening to what the law actually says. The word nomos ("law") appears twice in the verse with slightly different referents: the first "under the law" refers to the law as covenant jurisdiction; the second "listen to the law" refers to the Torah as written text, the Pentateuch that contains the Abraham narrative. Paul will now read the Torah's narrative against the proselyte program that claims the Torah's authority.

**Verses 22-23: "For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by a slave woman and one by a free woman. But the son of the slave was born according to the flesh, while the son of the free woman was born through promise."**

"For it is written" (gegraptai gar) -- the standard citation formula introduces the scriptural basis. The narrative is drawn from Genesis 16-21: the stories of Hagar and Sarah, Ishmael and Isaac.

"Abraham had two sons" (Abraam duo huious eschen) -- the statement is a simplification of the Genesis narrative, which eventually records additional sons through Keturah (Genesis 25:1-4). But the relevant pair for Paul's argument is the pair that illustrates the flesh/promise contrast: Ishmael and Isaac.

"One by a slave woman and one by a free woman" (hena ek tes paidiskes kai hena ek tes eleutheras) -- the word paidiske ("slave girl, female servant, maidservant") designates Hagar. In Genesis 16:1 (LXX), Hagar is identified as paidiske Aigyptia ("an Egyptian slave girl"). The word eleuthera ("free woman") designates Sarah, who was Abraham's wife, not a slave. The contrast slave/free is the fundamental binary of the allegory and of the entire letter: bondage versus freedom (2:4; 4:7; 5:1, 13).

"The son of the slave was born according to the flesh" (all' ho men ek tes paidiskes kata sarka gegennentai) -- the phrase kata sarka ("according to the flesh") describes the mode of Ishmael's birth. The word sarx here carries the polemical sense the whole-book analysis has traced throughout the letter. "According to the flesh" does not merely mean "by natural biological processes" (Isaac was also born biologically); it means "by the flesh-method" -- through human initiative, effort, and planning apart from divine promise. In Genesis 16, Hagar's pregnancy was the product of Abraham and Sarah's own plan to produce an heir through a surrogate when the promise seemed to have failed. The "flesh" element is the human attempt to achieve by their own mechanism what God had promised to accomplish by his.

The whole-book analysis is explicit: "Born according to the flesh" (kata sarka) in 4:23, 29 = the flesh-method of becoming Abraham's child (circumcision/proselyte conversion). Ishmael was produced through human reproductive effort as a substitute for divine promise; proselyte conversion produces Abraham's children through circumcision (a literal act on the flesh) as a substitute for faith. The parallel is structural: both attempt to achieve by the flesh-method what God accomplishes by the promise-method.

"While the son of the free woman was born through promise" (ho de ek tes eleutheras di' epangelias) -- the preposition dia with the genitive epangelias ("through promise") identifies the mode of Isaac's birth. Isaac was born because God promised Abraham and Sarah a son (Genesis 17:16, 19; 18:10, 14) and then fulfilled that promise. Isaac's birth was not the product of human planning or effort; it was the product of divine promise and divine power. Sarah was barren and old (Genesis 18:11-12); Isaac's birth required divine intervention. The promise-method does not depend on human capacity but on divine commitment.

The flesh/promise contrast is the heart of the allegory, and it maps directly onto the letter's central argument. The rival teachers' program is the flesh-method: produce Abraham's children through circumcision. Paul's gospel is the promise-method: Abraham's children are produced through faith in the promise, confirmed by the Spirit. The two methods are not complementary but competitive -- as the rest of the allegory will demonstrate.

**Verse 24: "Now this may be interpreted allegorically: these women are two covenants."**

"This may be interpreted allegorically" (hatina estin allegoroumena) -- the verb allegoreo ("to speak allegorically, to say one thing and mean another") is the only occurrence of this word in the New Testament. The neuter plural relative pronoun hatina ("which things") refers to the preceding narrative. Paul explicitly signals that he is reading the Genesis account as pointing beyond itself to a deeper significance. The present passive participle allegoroumena ("being allegorized, which are spoken allegorically") identifies the narrative as one that contains a deeper meaning alongside its literal sense.

Paul is not denying the historicity of the Genesis narrative. The verb allegoreo does not mean "this is fictional" but "this has a meaning beyond the surface." This interpretive method was well established in first-century Jewish and Greek practice. Philo of Alexandria regularly read the patriarchal narratives allegorically (e.g., De Congressu, where Hagar represents preliminary education and Sarah represents wisdom). Paul uses the same technique but applies it to a different theological question: not philosophy and education but covenant and standing before God.

"These women are two covenants" (hautai gar eisin duo diathekai) -- the interpretive key is stated directly. The two women, Hagar and Sarah, represent two covenants. The word diatheke ("covenant") is the LXX's standard translation of berit. The two covenants are the Sinai covenant and the promise-covenant, as the following verses will specify.

**Verse 24b-25: "One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children for slavery; she is Hagar. Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia; she corresponds to the present Jerusalem, for she is in slavery with her children."**

"One is from Mount Sinai" (mia men apo orous Sina) -- the first covenant originates from Mount Sinai, the location where the Torah was given to Israel through Moses (Exodus 19-24). The preposition apo ("from") identifies the covenant's origin.

"Bearing children for slavery" (eis douleian gennousa) -- the participle gennousa ("bearing, giving birth to") describes what this covenant produces: children born into slavery (eis douleian, "for slavery, into slavery"). The covenant originating from Sinai, when used as the mechanism for producing Abraham's children (i.e., when Gentiles enter it through proselyte conversion to become Abraham's heirs), produces slaves, not free children. This is the letter's thesis in narrative form: the flesh-method of becoming Abraham's child -- circumcision, proselyte conversion, formal entry into the Sinai covenant -- does not produce the free sonship it promises. It produces bondage.

"She is Hagar" (hetis estin Hagar) -- the identification is direct: the Sinai covenant corresponds to Hagar, the slave woman. The designation is deliberately provocative: the Sinai covenant, the Torah itself, is mapped onto the slave woman whose son was born kata sarka.

"Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia" (to de Hagar Sina oros estin en te Arabia) -- this verse has textual and interpretive difficulties, but its function in the argument is clear. Paul connects Hagar geographically to Mount Sinai (both located in Arabia) and theologically to the covenant given there. The geographic connection may exploit an association between the name Hagar and a local designation for Mount Sinai in Nabatean Arabic -- some scholars have noted that the Arabic word hajar means "rock" and was applied to the Sinai region. Whether or not this etymological connection is operative, the theological point stands: Hagar and Sinai are linked as representatives of the same covenant system.

"She corresponds to the present Jerusalem" (systoichei de te nyn Ierousalem) -- the verb systoicheo ("to stand in the same column, to correspond to, to be in the same category as") is a mathematical term: elements that stand in the same column of a list correspond to each other. The present Jerusalem (te nyn Ierousalem, "the now Jerusalem") -- the actual, existing city and its temple-centered religious establishment -- corresponds to Hagar. The present Jerusalem, as the center of the Torah-covenant system and the base of the agitators' program, occupies the Hagar position in the allegory: it produces children through the flesh-method (proselyte conversion) and those children are born into slavery.

"For she is in slavery with her children" (douleuei gar meta ton teknon autes) -- the verb douleo ("to be enslaved, to serve as a slave") describes the present Jerusalem's condition: she and her children are in slavery. This does not mean that all Jews are slaves in an absolute sense; it means that the system of proselyte conversion that the present Jerusalem represents -- the system the agitators are importing into Galatia -- produces slavery rather than freedom. The children of the present Jerusalem, insofar as they are produced by the flesh-method (circumcision as the basis of standing before God), are born into bondage to the stoicheia (4:3, 9).

**Verse 26: "But the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother."**

"The Jerusalem above" (he de ano Ierousalem) -- the adjective ano ("above, upward, heavenly") identifies a second Jerusalem that exists above the present, earthly one. The concept of a heavenly Jerusalem appears in Jewish apocalyptic and Second Temple literature. In 2 Baruch 4:2-6, God shows Adam the heavenly city before the fall. In 4 Ezra 7:26, the city that is "now not seen" will appear. The heavenly Jerusalem represents God's ultimate purpose, the reality toward which history moves -- in contrast to the present Jerusalem, which represents the current, incomplete, enslaved system.

"Is free" (eleuthera estin) -- the adjective eleuthera ("free") places the heavenly Jerusalem in the Sarah column: Sarah was the free woman (v. 22), and the Jerusalem above is free. The contrast is absolute: the present Jerusalem is in slavery (v. 25); the Jerusalem above is free.

"And she is our mother" (hetis estin meter hemon) -- the pronoun hemon ("our") here is inclusive: the Jerusalem above is the mother of all believers, Jewish and Gentile. The imagery of the heavenly Jerusalem as mother is drawn from the prophetic tradition, as the next verse will demonstrate with a citation from Isaiah. Believers are children of the free woman, children of the Jerusalem above, children of promise -- not because of circumcision but because of God's promise and the Spirit's confirmation.

**Verse 27: "For it is written, 'Rejoice, O barren one who does not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor! For the children of the desolate one will be more than those of the one who has a husband.'"**

The citation is from Isaiah 54:1 (LXX: euphrantheti, steira he ou tiktousa; rexon kai boeson, he ouk odinousa; hoti polla ta tekna tes eremou mallon e tes echouses ton andra). The text in its original context addresses Jerusalem after the exile. Zion, personified as a barren, desolate woman, is told to rejoice because she will bear more children than the married woman. The "barren one" is Jerusalem, devastated and childless after the Babylonian destruction; the "one who has a husband" is Jerusalem before the exile, when she was populated and prosperous.

Paul's application maps Sarah onto the barren woman and Hagar onto the married woman. Sarah was barren (Genesis 11:30; 16:1-2); Hagar bore a child first. Yet the barren woman's children will outnumber the married woman's. In the allegory, the barren one is the promise-method (seemingly producing no children, since it does not rely on human effort), and the married one is the flesh-method (apparently productive, since circumcision visibly produces converts). Paul's claim is that the promise-method, despite appearing barren, will ultimately produce far more children than the flesh-method. The Gentile mission, which does not produce children through circumcision, will outnumber the proselyte-conversion program. Isaiah's prophecy about the barren woman's abundant offspring is fulfilled in the Gentile believers who become Abraham's children through faith.

The verb odino ("to be in labor") in the Isaiah citation echoes verse 19, where Paul used the same word for his own labor pains over the Galatians. The linguistic connection is suggestive: Paul's labor pains (4:19) are the labor pains of the Jerusalem above (4:26-27), the barren one who is now producing children through promise rather than through flesh.

**Verse 28: "Now you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise."**

"Now you, brothers" (hymeis de, adelphoi) -- the pronoun hymeis ("you") addresses the Galatians directly. The address adelphoi ("brothers") maintains the familial tone.

"Like Isaac, are children of promise" (kata Isaak epangelias tekna este) -- the phrase kata Isaak ("according to Isaac, like Isaac, in the manner of Isaac") identifies the Galatians' mode of entry into Abraham's family: they are children in the same way Isaac was a child -- through promise, not through flesh. The word epangelias ("of promise") is a genitive of quality: they are promise-children, children defined by the promise. The verb este ("you are") is present tense: this is their current, actual status. They are already children of promise. Circumcision would not make them children of promise; it would make them children of flesh -- Ishmael-children, Hagar-children, slaves.

The identification is the allegory's positive claim: the Galatians occupy the Isaac position. They are the children of the free woman, the children of the Jerusalem above, the children born through divine promise rather than human effort. Their status as Abraham's children is not prospective (waiting for circumcision to complete it) but actual and present (established by faith and confirmed by the Spirit).

**Verse 29: "But just as at that time he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, so also it is now."**

"He who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit" (ho kata sarka gennetheis edioken ton kata pneuma) -- Paul introduces a detail not explicitly found in Genesis but drawn from interpretive tradition. In Genesis 21:9, the LXX reads: "Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham, playing with her son Isaac" (paidzonta meta Isaak tou huiou autes). The verb paizo ("to play") in some readings was understood as hostile: mocking, mistreating, or persecuting. The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 21:9 interprets Ishmael's behavior as involving idolatry and violence. In Genesis Rabbah 53:11, the verb metsacheq ("playing/laughing," the Hebrew equivalent of LXX's paidzonta) is interpreted as including sexual immorality, idolatry, and murder. Paul draws on this interpretive tradition to identify Ishmael's behavior toward Isaac as persecution.

The phrase kata sarka ("according to the flesh") describes Ishmael's birth-mode, as in verse 23. But Paul substitutes kata pneuma ("according to the Spirit") for the expected di' epangelias ("through promise") in describing Isaac's birth-mode. This substitution is significant: it links the promise-method to the Spirit and integrates the allegory with the letter's broader flesh/Spirit opposition (3:3; 5:16-25). Isaac was born through promise, and the promise is the sphere of the Spirit's operation (3:14, "the promise of the Spirit"). The flesh-method and the Spirit-method are the two contrasting principles that run through the entire letter.

"So also it is now" (houtos kai nyn) -- Paul applies the Genesis pattern to the present situation. The Galatian believers (born kata pneuma, through promise) are being persecuted by those who champion the flesh-method (the rival teachers who promote circumcision). The agitators' program is not a neutral addition to the Galatians' faith; it is an act of hostility from the Ishmael-position against the Isaac-position. The persecution may be social pressure, theological coercion, or the exclusion described in verse 17 (the agitators "want to shut you out"). Whatever form it takes, it follows the Genesis pattern: the flesh-born persecutes the Spirit-born.

**Verse 30: "But what does the Scripture say? 'Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman.'"**

"But what does the Scripture say?" (alla ti legei he graphe?) -- Paul returns to the Torah's own text for the decisive verdict. The question he graphe ("the Scripture") again personifies Scripture as a speaking authority (as in 3:8, 22). The answer comes not from Paul's theological argument but from the Torah's own narrative.

The citation is from Genesis 21:10 (LXX: ekbale ten paidisken tauten kai ton huion autes; ou gar kleronomsei ho huios tes paidiskes tautes meta tou huiou mou Isaak). In the Genesis narrative, these are Sarah's words to Abraham after she sees Ishmael playing with Isaac. Paul presents them as the words of Scripture itself -- the Torah's own command.

"Cast out the slave woman and her son" (ekbale ten paidisken kai ton huion autes) -- the verb ekballo ("to cast out, to drive out, to expel") is forceful: removal, expulsion, ejection. The slave woman (Hagar = the Sinai covenant as mechanism for producing Abraham's children through circumcision) and her son (those produced by the flesh-method) are to be expelled.

"For the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman" (ou gar me kleronomese ho huios tes paidiskes meta tou huiou tes eleutheras) -- the double negative ou... me with the future tense kleronomese creates an emphatic denial: the slave woman's son will absolutely not inherit alongside the free woman's son. The word kleronomeo ("to inherit, to receive an inheritance") returns to the inheritance language that has been central to the argument since 3:18 (inheritance by promise, not by law) and 3:29 (heirs according to promise) and 4:1-7 (the minor heir who has come of age). The inheritance cannot be shared between the flesh-method's products and the promise-method's products. The two are mutually exclusive. The Torah itself -- the very text the agitators claim to champion -- commands the expulsion of the flesh-method and its children.

The force of this citation in the letter's argument is devastating. The rival teachers are urging the Galatians to become Hagar's children -- to enter Abraham's family through the flesh-method of circumcision. The Torah they appeal to says: cast out the slave woman and her son. The Torah itself condemns the proselyte-conversion program. The agitators are undone by their own authority.

The whole-book analysis states the point precisely: "The allegory is not a wholesale rejection of the Sinai covenant as inherently evil. It is a claim about what happens when the Sinai covenant is treated as the mechanism for Gentile entry into Abraham's family." The Sinai covenant, in its proper function within God's purposes, served a legitimate role as a paidagogos (3:24) and as the framework for Israel's life with God. But when it is repurposed as the mechanism by which Gentiles become Abraham's children -- when circumcision replaces faith as the entry point into the Abrahamic inheritance -- it becomes the Hagar-method, and the Torah itself says to cast it out.

**Verse 31: "So, brothers, we are not children of the slave but of the free woman."**

"So, brothers" (dio, adelphoi) -- the inferential conjunction dio ("therefore, for this reason, so") draws the conclusion from the entire allegory. The address adelphoi ("brothers") maintains the tone of family relationship.

"We are not children of the slave but of the free woman" (ouk esmen paidiskes tekna alla tes eleutheras) -- the pronoun hemeis ("we") is now fully inclusive: all believers, Jewish and Gentile, including Paul and the Galatians together. The declaration is definitive: we belong to the Sarah line, not the Hagar line. We are children of the free woman, not children of the slave. Our entry into Abraham's family was through promise and faith, not through circumcision and proselyte conversion. Our mother is the Jerusalem above, not the present Jerusalem. Our inheritance is secured by God's promise, not by human effort.

The verse functions as the conclusion to the entire theological argument that began in 2:15. Everything from the thesis of 2:15-21 (no one is justified by erga nomou but through faith), through the scriptural demonstrations of chapter 3 (Abraham, the law's curse, the priority of promise, the paidagogos, identity in Christ), through the heir-guardian metaphor and the stoicheia equation of 4:1-11, through the personal appeal of 4:12-20, has led to this conclusion: we are children of the free woman. The theological argument is complete. What follows in 5:1 -- "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery" -- is the practical application of this conclusion.

## Chapter Summary

Galatians 4 is the culmination of the theological argument that has been building since 2:15. It extends, intensifies, and completes the case against proselyte conversion through four movements, each advancing the argument to a new level of force.

**The heir-guardian metaphor (4:1-7)** continues the paidagogos metaphor of 3:23-25 from the heir's perspective. The minor heir, though owning everything, lived no differently from a slave under guardians and managers until the father's appointed time. Paul identifies this minor-heir condition with the pre-Christ experience of Jewish believers under the stoicheia tou kosmou (4:3) -- the elemental regulatory system of the Sinai covenant. The father's appointed time arrived when God sent the Son, who was born into the human condition (born of woman) and into the Sinai covenant's jurisdiction (born under the law) in order to redeem those held under the law so that all believers might receive adoption as sons (4:4-5). The Spirit of the Son now cries "Abba, Father" in believers' hearts (4:6), confirming the sonship that God's double sending -- of the Son and of the Spirit -- accomplished. The Galatians are sons and heirs (4:7). Everything proselyte conversion promises, they already possess.

**The stoicheia equation (4:8-11)** makes the letter's most radical argumentative move. The Galatians' former state was slavery to non-gods (4:8). Their proposed adoption of Torah-covenant membership is classified as a "return" (palin, 4:9) to the same structural position: bondage to stoicheia. The word "again" is the key: the Galatians were never under Torah before, so adopting it can only be a "return" if both systems -- pagan worship and Torah-as-basis-of-standing-before-God -- function identically as bondage to elemental principles. The content differs (Torah is from God; paganism is not), but the structure is the same (external regulation as the condition of access to God). The Galatians are already observing the Jewish calendar (4:10), and Paul fears his labor may have been in vain (4:11). The equation does not say Torah is paganism; it says that the function of both -- conditioning access to God on compliance with an external regulatory system -- is identical.

**The personal appeal (4:12-20)** shifts from theological argument to pastoral anguish. Paul recalls the Galatians' extraordinary initial reception of him -- they received him as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus himself (4:14) -- and contrasts it with their present estrangement, in which telling them the truth has made him their enemy (4:16). He exposes the rival teachers' motives: they court the Galatians not for their benefit but to create dependence, shutting them out so that the Galatians will be forced to seek the agitators' approval (4:17). Paul describes his own anguish with the metaphor of childbirth: he is again in labor until Christ is formed in them (4:19). The section does not advance the theological argument directly but grounds it in relationship: Paul is not a detached theologian but a parent in pain.

**The Hagar-Sarah allegory (4:21-31)** is the theological argument's climax. Paul turns the Torah against those who claim to champion it, reading the Genesis narrative of Abraham's two sons as an allegory of two methods of becoming Abraham's child. The flesh-method (Ishmael, born kata sarka through Hagar the slave woman = the Sinai covenant, the present Jerusalem, proselyte conversion through circumcision) produces children born into slavery. The promise-method (Isaac, born di' epangelias through Sarah the free woman = the promise covenant, the Jerusalem above, faith and the Spirit) produces children born into freedom. The barren woman's children will outnumber the married woman's (Isaiah 54:1, cited in 4:27) -- the promise-method will be more fruitful than the flesh-method. The flesh-born persecutes the Spirit-born, then as now (4:29). And the Torah itself commands: "Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman" (4:30, citing Genesis 21:10). The agitators' own authority -- the Torah -- condemns their program. The chapter concludes with the declaration that summarizes the entire theological argument: "We are not children of the slave but of the free woman" (4:31).

**What the chapter depends on from what preceded:** Every argument in chapter 4 builds on foundations laid in chapters 2-3. The heir-guardian metaphor (4:1-7) continues the paidagogos metaphor of 3:23-25. The stoicheia equation (4:8-11) extends the argument that the law's covenant-jurisdictional function was temporary (3:19, 23-25) to its radical conclusion: entering the law's jurisdiction now is not progress but regression to the same category of bondage the Galatians left behind. The Hagar-Sarah allegory (4:21-31) builds on the seed argument of 3:16 and 3:29 (Abraham's children are determined by incorporation into Christ, the seed, not by circumcision), the flesh/Spirit contrast of 3:3 (begun by the Spirit, completed by the flesh?), and the mutual exclusivity of law and promise established in 3:18 (if the inheritance comes by law, it no longer comes by promise). The chapter also presupposes the thesis of 2:15-21 (no one is justified by erga nomou), the autobiographical defense of chapters 1-2 (Paul's gospel is divinely sourced and independently received), and the experiential argument of 3:1-5 (the Galatians received the Spirit without circumcision).

**What it sets up for what follows:** The declaration of 4:31 -- "we are not children of the slave but of the free woman" -- flows directly into 5:1: "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery." The theological argument is complete; what follows is its application. Chapter 5 will state the consequences of circumcision without qualification (severance from Christ, falling from grace, 5:4), define the positive alternative (faith working through love, 5:6), expose the agitators one final time (5:7-12), and then demonstrate that the Spirit produces the character the law aimed at (5:16-26) -- answering the implicit objection that freedom from the law's covenant jurisdiction leads to moral chaos. The "fruit of the Spirit" (5:22-23) is the content of what the Jerusalem above produces in her children: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. "Against such things there is no law" (5:23). The ethical exhortation of chapters 5-6, grounded in "the law of Christ" (6:2) and the love-command of Leviticus 19:18 (5:14), demonstrates that the children of the free woman fulfill Torah's ethical content through the Spirit, without requiring the flesh-method of proselyte conversion that the Hagar-Sarah allegory has condemned.
