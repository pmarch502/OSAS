# Galatians 4 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**Where this stands in the letter:** Chapter 4 continues without a break from 3:29, and the two chapters should be read as one movement. The connections are verbal and tight: the paidagogos of 3:24-25 becomes the guardians and stewards of 4:2; the release from the paidagogos becomes the heir's coming of age; the "sons of God" of 3:26 becomes the adoption of 4:5 and the Abba cry of 4:6; and the "heirs according to promise" of 3:29 becomes "no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir" at 4:7.

The chapter has four parts and they are of markedly different kinds, which is itself worth noticing. Verses 1-7 complete the argument begun at 3:23 with a household metaphor. Verses 8-11 apply it to the Galatians' present situation with the sharpest warning in the letter. Verses 12-20 abandon argument altogether for personal appeal, and are the most emotional passage Paul wrote to these congregations. Verses 21-31 return to scripture with an allegory of Hagar and Sarah that restates the whole case in narrative form.

**The Genesis material behind the last section, since the allegory assumes it.** Abraham was promised offspring, but Sarah was barren. In Genesis 16, Sarah proposes that Abraham father a child by her Egyptian slave Hagar; Hagar conceives, the two women fall out, and Hagar flees into the wilderness. There the angel of the LORD finds her, tells her to return, and promises that her offspring will be multiplied beyond counting -- and Hagar names God, calling him El Roi, "the God who sees me." She is the only person in Genesis to give God a name. Ishmael is born.

In Genesis 17 and 18 the promise is renewed and specified: the son will be Sarah's. Isaac is born in Genesis 21. At the weaning feast Sarah sees Ishmael doing something -- the Hebrew word is ambiguous -- and demands that Hagar and her son be sent away. The matter distresses Abraham; God tells him to do as Sarah says, and adds a promise to make a nation of Ishmael as well. Hagar and the boy go into the wilderness, run out of water, and God hears the boy crying, opens Hagar's eyes to a well, and promises again to make him a great nation.

Paul builds his allegory on this narrative and assumes his readers know it.

**What the author argues immediately after (Galatians 5):** Chapter 5 opens with the imperative that the Hagar allegory has prepared: "for freedom Christ set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery" (5:1). It then states in the plainest terms what circumcision would cost (5:2-6), attacks the other teachers directly (5:7-12), and turns to the positive content of freedom -- service in love (5:13-15) and life in the Spirit set against the works of the flesh (5:16-26).

**How chapter 4 connects to what follows:** The slavery and freedom pairing, introduced at 2:4 and developed through this chapter, reaches its imperative at 5:1. The Spirit, named at 4:6 as the evidence of sonship, becomes the governing category of 5:16-26. And 4:29's observation that "the one born according to flesh persecuted the one born according to Spirit, so also now" identifies the other teachers as a present force in Galatia, which 5:7-12 and 6:12-13 then address directly.

## Verses 1-7: The Heir Comes of Age

> I mean that the heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave, though he is owner of everything; but he is under guardians and stewards until the date set by the father. So also we, when we were children, were enslaved under the elements of the world. But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of a woman, born under law, so that he might redeem those under law, so that we might receive the adoption as sons. And because you are sons, God sent forth the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying "Abba! Father!" So you are no longer a slave, but a son; and if a son, then an heir through God.

**Verses 1-2: "I mean that the heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave, though he is owner of everything; but he is under guardians and stewards until the date set by the father."**

The metaphor is the household, and it picks up the paidagōgos figure of 3:24-25 from the other side. There the focus was on the escort; here it is on the boy.

**Nēpios** is often translated "child," but the sense is legal rather than descriptive. The word means a minor -- someone not yet of age and therefore not competent to act for himself in law, hold property in his own hand, or give a binding undertaking. It is a status, and it says nothing about maturity or character. A capable, intelligent, well-grown boy of fourteen is a nēpios; a foolish man of thirty is not.

The comparison is stated precisely: the minor heir **ouden diapherei doulou** ("differs in nothing from a slave"). In his day-to-day existence he is under orders, his movements supervised, his property managed by others, his instructions given him by household staff -- functionally indistinguishable from a slave in that same household.

And then the qualification the whole metaphor turns on: **kyrios pantōn ōn**, "though being lord of all."

**The estate is already his.** Nothing whatever about his ownership is in question, not for a moment, not at any point in the minority. What is deferred is only his exercise of it.

This is the point of the figure and it should be held in view through everything that follows: **the minority is a phase in the life of an owner, not the condition of an outsider.** Whatever the argument goes on to say about slavery and elements and coming of age, it is being said about someone who was the heir the whole time.

**Epitropous kai oikonomous** ("guardians and stewards") name two household offices. The **epitropos** had charge of the minor's person -- his upbringing, his welfare, decisions made on his behalf. The **oikonomos** (from oikos, "house," and nemō, "to manage") had charge of the property: the estate's administration, its accounts, its staff.

Both nouns are plural, and **neither is identified with the law**. This becomes important at verse 3.

**Achri tēs prothesmias tou patros** ("until the date appointed by the father") uses **prothesmia**, a legal term for a fixed and predetermined day -- the deadline named in a contract, the date on which a term expires or an action must be brought.

Arrangements for the age of majority varied across Roman, Greek and Jewish practice, and Paul is not describing any single legal system. What he takes from all of them is the feature his argument needs: **the end of the minority is a date, not an achievement.** It does not arrive when the boy proves ready. It does not depend on his conduct. The father set it in advance, and it comes.

**Verse 3: "So also we, when we were children, were enslaved under the elements of the world."**

**Houtōs kai hēmeis** ("so also we") applies the figure, and the first person plural continues from 3:23-25 -- the same "we" that was kept in custody and released from the paidagōgos.

**Ta stoicheia tou kosmou is the chapter's most contested phrase, and its range needs setting out honestly.**

The noun **stoicheion** means "one of a row, a member of a series, a basic constituent" -- something that stands in line with others of its kind. From that root sense it developed several established uses, and all of them were live in the first century:

- **The letters of the alphabet**, which are the elements of written language, and from there **the rudiments or first principles** of any subject. Hebrews 5:12 uses it exactly this way: "you need someone to teach you again the basic principles (stoicheia) of the oracles of God."
- **The four physical elements** of Greek natural philosophy -- earth, water, air, fire -- from which the world was thought to be composed. 2 Peter 3:10 and 3:12 use the word in this sense, of the elements dissolving in fire.
- **The heavenly bodies**, which are the visible elements of the cosmos and which govern the calendar by their motions.
- **Elemental spirits or powers.** This grows out of the previous sense: in much ancient thought, including ordinary popular religion, sun, moon, planets and stars were not inert matter but animate powers with influence over human affairs, and the times they governed were their domain.

The phrase recurs at 4:9, and Colossians uses it twice (2:8; 2:20). Which sense Paul intends is not settled by the word itself, and readers should be honest about that rather than choosing one and proceeding as though it were given.

What the verse *does* establish is the **relation**: the "we" of the minority period were **dedoulōmenoi**, "having been enslaved," under them. The perfect passive participle describes a condition they were held in rather than an act they performed.

**A qualification that matters, and that is easy to slide past.** That is a strong statement about the pre-Messianic condition of the group Paul places himself in, and it should be read alongside verse 2. The overseers of the minority were named there as "guardians and stewards" -- plural, unspecified, and **not** identified with the law. On the terms of the metaphor, the Torah is what the father gave *during* the minority; the stoicheia name the condition of *being* a minor.

The text does not equate the two. A reader should notice if an interpretation quietly does, since a great deal follows from it.

**Verses 4-5: "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of a woman, born under law, so that he might redeem those under law, so that we might receive the adoption as sons."**

**To plērōma tou chronou** ("the fullness of the time") answers the prothesmia of verse 2: the appointed date arrived. The image is of a container filling until it is full -- time running its course until the measure is complete and the term expires.

**Exapesteilen** ("he sent forth," from ex-apostellō, "to send out from") occurs twice in this short paragraph -- of the Son here, and of the Spirit in verse 6. The parallel is structural and deliberate: two sendings from the same source, with one result.

Two participles describe the Son, matched in form and set side by side.

**Genomenon ek gynaikos** ("born of a woman") states full human birth. The phrase is a Semitic idiom for a human being as such -- "man born of woman is of few days and full of trouble" (Job 14:1); "among those born of women there has arisen no one greater than John the Baptist" (Matthew 11:11). It asserts ordinary human origin and nothing more; nothing in the phrase points to the circumstances of the birth.

**Genomenon hypo nomon** ("born under law") states the condition into which he was born.

**This second phrase is the control on the letter's use of hypo nomon, and it deserves careful statement because it settles a question that recurs throughout.**

The expression hypo nomon occurs at 3:23, 4:4, 4:5, 4:21 and 5:18. Here it is predicated of Christ himself, and the purpose clause immediately following is **hina tous hypo nomon exagorasē** -- "so that he might redeem those under law." Christ was born into the very condition from which he redeems.

Now: Christ observed the Torah. Nothing in the New Testament suggests otherwise, and this letter's argument would collapse if he had not, since a Messiah who disregarded the covenant could hardly be the seed to whom its promises ran.

So "under law" cannot be a term of reproach for Torah observance. If it were, verse 5 would say that he needed rescuing from his own faithfulness -- which is incoherent, and which no reader would propose if the phrase were not already carrying freight from elsewhere.

The phrase must therefore denote something else: a **standing**, a jurisdiction, a position under the law's authority and subject to its sanctions. That is a condition one can be born into and redeemed from without the practice of the commandments being what is at issue.

This bears directly on 2:19 ("through law I died to law"), on 4:21 ("you who want to be under law"), and on 5:18 ("if you are led by the Spirit you are not under law"). In each case the question the reader should be asking is what standing is in view -- not whether commandments are being kept.

**Exagorasē** ("might redeem, might buy out") is the verb of 3:13 -- purchase out of a condition, as one buys a slave out of slavery.

**Huiothesian ("adoption as sons") is a legal term whose force depends on knowing what the institution was.**

Adoption in Roman law was a formal act by which a person was transferred out of one family and into another, and its consequences were total. The adopted son took a new father, a new name, and a new family identity. His previous debts and obligations were extinguished -- he was, in law, a different person, and his old liabilities did not follow him. He received full inheritance rights, identical in every respect to those of a son born into the family. The act was witnessed, formal, and in principle irrevocable.

Paul also uses the word with a Jewish resonance. At Romans 9:4 he lists it first among Israel's possessions: "they are Israelites, and to them belong the **adoption**, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises." Sonship is Israel's own designation in the scriptures -- "Israel is my firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22); "you are the sons of the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 14:1); "when Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1).

And the verb Paul uses, **apolabōmen**, is not the simple "receive" but a compound that can carry the sense of receiving back, obtaining what is due, getting what belongs to one.

**Verse 6: "And because you are sons, God sent forth the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying 'Abba! Father!'"**

The pronouns shift within the single verse: "because **you** are sons... into **our** hearts." Paul states the status of the Galatians and then includes himself in the consequence.

**The logic of the causal clause deserves to be stated plainly, because it is very easy to reverse and is frequently reversed.**

Paul does not say that God sent the Spirit *so that* they might become sons. He says that **because** (hoti) they are sons, God sent the Spirit. The status comes first; the Spirit follows from it. Which means the Spirit's presence is the **consequence** of sonship and therefore the **evidence** of it -- not the mechanism that produces it.

**Abba** is Aramaic, the everyday word by which a child addressed a father. Paul leaves it untranslated and then supplies the Greek gloss immediately (ho patēr). The same doubled form appears at Romans 8:15, in a passage closely parallel to this one, and in the mouth of Jesus at Gethsemane in Mark 14:36.

**The preservation of the Aramaic in Greek-speaking Gentile congregations is itself a datum.** These were people who did not speak Aramaic, in a region far from Judea, and the word had nonetheless become a fixed part of their prayer -- carried across the language boundary intact, as a handful of words were (amen, maranatha, hosanna). Something about it was not to be translated away.

**Krazon** ("crying out") denotes a loud cry, not a murmur. It is used of shouting, of a raven's call, of the cry of a crowd. The Spirit does not whisper this.

**Set beside 3:2-5, this is the second appeal to the readers' own experience, and it is the letter's most concrete practical argument.** They received the Spirit; the Spirit in them cries out the address that only a son uses. Men who are already saying "Father" are being offered a procedure for entering the family.

**Verse 7: "So you are no longer a slave, but a son; and if a son, then an heir through God."**

The address becomes **singular** -- ouketi ei doulos, "**you** (one person) are no longer a slave." After a chapter of plurals, the sentence turns to the individual reader and puts it to him directly.

The chain completes the metaphor in three steps: slave, son, heir. **Klēronomos** returns to the inheritance language of 3:18 and 3:29, and **dia theou** ("through God") names the source of the standing -- not through any procedure, and not through descent.

## Verses 8-11: Turning Back

> Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods. But now that you have come to know God -- or rather to be known by God -- how can you turn back again to the weak and beggarly elements, to which you want to be enslaved all over again? You observe days and months and seasons and years. I am afraid for you, that perhaps I have labored over you in vain.

**Verse 8: "Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods."**

The description of the readers' past is unambiguous, and it constrains everything that follows in the paragraph.

**Ouk eidotes theon** ("not knowing God") and **edouleusate tois physei mē ousin theois** ("you were enslaved to those which by nature are not gods") is the standard Jewish description of Gentile idolatry.

The formulation -- that the gods of the nations are not gods at all -- runs through the scriptures: "has a nation changed its gods, even though they are no gods?" (Jeremiah 2:11); "they were no gods, but the work of men's hands, wood and stone" (Isaiah 37:19); "you have made for yourselves priests like the peoples of other lands, so that whoever comes to consecrate himself becomes a priest of what are no gods" (2 Chronicles 13:9). The Wisdom of Solomon 13:1-2 gives the fuller Hellenistic-Jewish version, in which those ignorant of God supposed "fire or wind or swift air, or the circle of the stars, or turbulent water, or the luminaries of heaven" to be the gods that rule the world -- a list that touches the stoicheia directly.

**The verse establishes that the Galatian recipients were pagans, not adherents of a synagogue, and this is not a small point.** A Gentile "God-fearer" attached to a Jewish community -- of whom there were many, and who appear repeatedly in Acts -- would already have been keeping some Jewish observance and would have been familiar with the scriptures. Paul's description rules that out. They did not know God, and what they served were not gods.

Whatever they are now moving toward, therefore, they cannot be *returning* to a Jewish practice they held before, because they held none.

**Verse 9: "But now that you have come to know God -- or rather to be known by God -- how can you turn back again to the weak and beggarly elements, to which you want to be enslaved all over again?"**

Paul corrects himself mid-sentence: **gnontes theon, mallon de gnōsthentes hypo theou** -- "having come to know God -- or rather, having been known by God."

The correction relocates the initiative. What matters is not their achievement of knowledge but God's knowing of them. The idiom is scriptural and is used of God's regard for his own: "the LORD knows the way of the righteous" (Psalm 1:6); "before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (Jeremiah 1:5); "you only have I known of all the families of the earth" (Amos 3:2).

The stoicheia of verse 3 return with two adjectives, and both are contemptuous. **Asthenē** is "without strength, unable to accomplish, impotent." **Ptōcha** is destitute -- the word for a beggar, someone who has nothing to give. Together: powerless and bankrupt. Whatever the Galatians expect from that quarter, it has not got it.

**The temporal language is emphatic and doubled.** Epistrephete **palin** ("you are turning back **again**"); hois **palin anōthen** douleuein thelete ("to which you want to be enslaved **again, over from the start**"). Palin anōthen is redundant by design -- "again anew," "all over again from the beginning."

The repetition matters for the verse that follows, because a return requires something previously departed from.

**Verse 10: "You observe days and months and seasons and years."**

The list is **hēmeras... kai mēnas kai kairous kai eniautous**. The verb, paratēreisthe, means to observe scrupulously, to watch closely -- it suggests careful attention to a schedule rather than casual participation.

**Two readings are current. They are not evenly balanced, and this comment sets out why -- the weight of the evidence falls on the second, though the first has real support that has to be answered rather than dismissed.**

***The reading the evidence favours: a cosmic or celestial calendar, not the Jewish one.***

- **The list has no weekly unit**, and this is the decisive observation. Any Jewish calendar list would be expected to include the sabbath -- the sabbath is by far the most conspicuous and most frequently observed of all Jewish times, and the parallel list at Colossians 2:16 duly includes it. Paul's list here runs days, months, seasons, years, with nothing whatever between the day and the month. A Jewish calendar described without its weekly centre is not a Jewish calendar.
- **The sequence is the sequence of Genesis 1:14**, where the luminaries are set "for signs and for **seasons** and for **days** and **years**." That is celestial timekeeping -- the calendar as governed by the heavenly bodies -- which sits naturally under "the elements of the world" on the astral reading of stoicheia. Note that this also blunts the strongest lexical argument on the other side, since Genesis 1:14 is itself a use of kairos for astronomical rather than festal time.
- **Verse 8 has just identified the readers as former idolaters** rather than synagogue adherents, and verse 9's palin anōthen requires a genuine return to something once left. Gentiles who never kept a Jewish calendar cannot return to one. This is not a subtlety; it is a plain requirement of the sentence Paul wrote.

***What has to be answered on the other side: the Jewish calendar.***

- **Kairos** is the standard Greek rendering of the Hebrew mo'ed, the "appointed time," which is the technical term for the festivals. Leviticus 23:4: "These are the appointed feasts of the LORD... which you shall proclaim at the time appointed for them (en tois kairois autōn)." The word does carry festal weight in the Greek scriptures. (Though see the Genesis 1:14 point above, which shows the word doing astronomical duty as well.)
- **Colossians 2:16** supplies a genuinely close parallel -- "a **festival or a new moon or a sabbath**" -- and it stands in a chapter that twice uses the phrase stoicheia tou kosmou (2:8; 2:20), the identical phrase to the one here. This is the strongest argument on this side.
- The letter's crisis is a proselyte-conversion crisis, and calendar observance is part of the package a proselyte takes on, so a reference to it would be contextually natural.

On this reading, the shock of the verse is that Paul classes the adoption of the Jewish calendar with the readers' pagan past. That is a startling thing to say, and those who take this reading argue it is meant to be.

**Where this lands.** The Colossians parallel is real and the festal sense of kairos is real, but neither can account for a list that omits the sabbath while reproducing the Genesis 1:14 sequence, addressed to people described three verses earlier as former idolaters and said to be turning *back*. A reader should hold the counter-evidence, and should also notice that the case is not symmetrical.

***A complication that bears on both readings and is worth carrying forward.*** Verse 3 has already placed "we" -- on the most natural reading, Jews in their minority -- under the same stoicheia. So the term is not reserved for paganism in Paul's usage, and the comparison he is drawing may be between two conditions of servitude rather than between two calendars.

If so, the force of the paragraph does not depend on identifying which calendar is meant. What is being said would be that submitting to an externally imposed schedule as the condition of one's standing puts a person back in the posture of a minor, whatever the content of the schedule.

**Verse 11: "I am afraid for you, that perhaps I have labored over you in vain."**

**Kekopiaka** is the perfect of kopiaō, "to labour to the point of exhaustion" -- the word for hard physical work that leaves a person spent. Paul uses it of his own manual labour and of his apostolic work interchangeably. The perfect tense makes it labour whose outcome now stands in question.

**Eikē** ("in vain, to no purpose") is the word from 3:4, where Paul asked whether the Galatians had experienced so much for nothing. There he raised the possibility and immediately withdrew it ("if indeed it was in vain"). Here he states it as a fear and does not withdraw it -- which is a measure of how the letter's temperature has risen between chapter 3 and chapter 4.

## Verses 12-20: The Personal Appeal

> Become as I am, for I also have become as you are, brothers, I beg you. You did me no wrong. You know that it was because of a weakness of the flesh that I preached the gospel to you at the first; and though my condition was a trial to you, you did not despise or spit at me, but received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus. What then has become of your blessing? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me. So have I become your enemy by telling you the truth? They make much of you, but for no good purpose; they want to shut you out, so that you may make much of them. It is always good to be made much of for a good purpose, and not only when I am present with you. My little children, for whom I am again in labor until Christ is formed in you -- I wish I could be present with you now and change my tone, for I am perplexed about you.

This section stops arguing. There is no scripture in it, no logical structure, no appeal to precedent, no citation. It is nine verses of personal address, and it sits between the letter's harshest warning and its hardest allegory.

**Verse 12: "Become as I am, for I also have become as you are, brothers, I beg you. You did me no wrong."**

**Ginesthe hōs egō, hoti kagō hōs hymeis.** The Greek is compressed to the edge of obscurity: the second clause has no verb at all, and neither clause has an object. What the two halves are *about* has to be supplied from context, and any supply is an interpretation.

The most economical reading takes them as reciprocal in a specific respect. *Be as I am* -- not seeking standing through the law -- *because I became as you are* when I lived among you, setting aside the prerogatives of my birth in order to live as a Gentile among Gentiles.

That reading is supported from two directions. At 1 Corinthians 9:19-23 Paul describes exactly this practice: "to the Jews I became as a Jew... to those outside the law I became as one outside the law... I have become all things to all people." And at Galatians 2:14 he states Peter's Gentile-style living as the *premise* of a rebuke rather than as the offence, which indicates that he regarded such accommodation as legitimate.

The verb in the first clause is a present imperative, **ginesthe** -- "keep becoming," or "be in the process of becoming" -- which suits a call to return to a position previously held rather than to adopt a new one.

**Deomai hymōn** ("I beg you") is entreaty. The verb is used of petitioning a superior or of pleading. It sets the register for the whole section: not command, not argument, but request from a man with no leverage.

"You did me no wrong" (ouden me ēdikēsate) turns from the appeal to the memory that grounds it. Whatever has gone wrong between them, it did not begin with an injury on their side.

**Verses 13-14: "You know that it was because of a weakness of the flesh that I preached the gospel to you at the first; and though my condition was a trial to you, you did not despise or spit at me, but received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus."**

**Di' astheneian tēs sarkos** ("because of a weakness of the flesh") indicates that illness was the *occasion* of Paul's original visit. He did not plan to evangelize Galatia; he was unwell, and he stayed, and he preached. Nothing is said about the nature of the illness, and the many proposals -- malaria, ophthalmia, epilepsy, the "thorn in the flesh" of 2 Corinthians 12:7 -- are all reconstructions from silence.

Note the sense of sarx here: plain bodily existence, with none of the polemical charge the word carries at 3:3 or 6:12-13. The letter's key words shift sense with context, and this is one of the clearest instances of it within a single chapter -- sarx is neutral at 4:13-14 and loaded at 4:23 and 4:29.

**Ton peirasmon hymōn en tē sarki mou** ("your trial in my flesh"). Peirasmos is a test. Paul's physical state put the Galatians in a position where they had to decide how to treat him, and the decision was a test of them rather than of him.

**Ouk exouthenēsate oude exeptysate** ("you did not despise, nor did you spit"). The second verb is worth explaining, because the gesture behind it is unfamiliar and is the point of the sentence.

**Ekptyō** means literally "to spit out." Spitting was a recognized apotropaic gesture across the ancient Mediterranean -- a way of averting evil, warding off contamination, or turning aside an ill omen. It was used specifically at the sight of sickness, deformity, or a person thought to bring bad luck, and it belongs to the same complex of belief as the evil eye that Paul invoked at 3:1. Pliny records the practice as a commonplace.

Whatever Paul's condition looked like, in other words, it was the kind of thing at which people spat.

They did not. Instead they received him **hōs angelon theou, hōs Christon Iēsoun** -- "as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus." The two comparisons escalate: as a divine messenger, and then as the one whose message he brought.

**Verse 15: "What then has become of your blessing? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me."**

**Ho makarismos hymōn** is "your blessedness" or "your congratulating of yourselves" -- the sense of good fortune they had in that first encounter. The question asks where it has gone.

**Tous ophthalmous hymōn exoryxantes** ("having dug out your eyes") is vivid and violent. Two readings have been proposed: that it is a figure for the costliest imaginable gift -- one gives what one can least spare, and sight is the standing example of that -- or that it points to an eye ailment as the "weakness of the flesh" of verse 13. The second has been connected to 6:11's "large letters," though that has other explanations. The text does not decide, and the figure works either way.

**Verse 16: "So have I become your enemy by telling you the truth?"**

**Alētheuōn hymin** ("speaking truth to you"). The verb alētheuō means to tell the truth, to deal truthfully.

The question sets verses 13-15 against the present. Nothing in the relationship has changed except that Paul has said something they did not want to hear. And the word "truth" here connects to "the truth of the gospel" at 2:5 and 2:14 -- what is at stake is not Paul's candour in general but this specific message.

**Verse 17: "They make much of you, but for no good purpose; they want to shut you out, so that you may make much of them."**

**Zēlousin hymas** ("they are zealous for you") uses a verb with a wide range -- to be zealous for, to court, to be devoted to, to seek someone's favour, and also to envy. In this context it describes eager attention paid to the Galatians. The other teachers are being solicitous, and Paul does not deny it.

**Ekkleisai hymas thelousin** ("they want to shut you out") uses ekkleiō, "to lock out, to exclude, to shut off." **The object of the exclusion is not stated**, and two readings are available:

- They want to shut the Galatians out *from Paul*, isolating them from their founder so that the attachment transfers.
- They want to shut them out *from the community of the saved* unless they submit -- which would be the mechanism of the pressure the letter keeps naming with anankazō ("compel," 2:3, 2:14, 6:12).

The purpose clause exposes the transaction either way: **hina autous zēloute** -- "so that you may be zealous for **them**." Attention paid in order to secure attention returned.

**Verse 18: "It is always good to be made much of for a good purpose, and not only when I am present with you."**

Paul concedes the practice while questioning the motive: being courted is not itself a bad thing, and he is not claiming a monopoly on their affection.

The closing clause -- "and not only when I am present" -- indicates that the Galatians' devotion had been a function of Paul's physical presence, and that his absence is part of what has gone wrong. It is a piece of self-knowledge about the limits of what a founder can do from a distance, and it leads directly to verse 20.

**Verse 19: "My little children, for whom I am again in labor until Christ is formed in you."**

**Teknia mou** ("my little children") is a diminutive of affection and occurs only here in Paul. It is characteristic of 1 John, where it appears repeatedly. In the space of one verse the letter's most severe author becomes its most tender.

**Palin ōdinō** ("I am again in labour"). Ōdinō is the verb for a woman's birth pangs -- not pregnancy but the pains of delivery.

**The metaphor breaks on purpose, and its breaking is the point.** Paul, a man, describes himself in labour; and he describes going into labour a second time with children he has already borne. No process in the world corresponds to that. The figure fails, and the failure is what conveys the distress -- it is the language of a person reaching for an image and finding that none of them fit.

**Mechris hou morphōthē Christos en hymin** ("until Christ be formed in you"). **Morphoō** -- "to form, to shape, to give form to" -- is related to morphē, "form," and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament.

**This is the letter's only statement of what Paul's labour is *for*, and it is worth registering what he does not say.** Not until you are properly admitted. Not until your status is secure. Not until you have completed something, or joined something, or received something. Until Christ takes shape in you.

Set against verse 17, two things are being offered to these congregations. The other teachers offer a mark that can be pointed to and counted. Paul is holding out for a shape that has to be formed, which cannot be pointed to and takes as long as it takes.

**Verse 20: "I wish I could be present with you now and change my tone, for I am perplexed about you."**

**Allaxai tēn phōnēn mou** ("to change my voice"). A letter carries one tone and cannot modulate. The writer cannot see the face of the person reading, cannot tell when he has gone too far, cannot soften a sentence that has landed wrong. Paul says he would use a different voice if he could be there -- which is an admission that the letter is a blunt instrument for what he is trying to do, made in the middle of the letter.

**Aporoumai** ("I am at a loss") is from aporeō -- literally to be without a way through, to have no road, to be at a standstill. It is a rare admission in Paul's letters, and it closes the section.

## Verses 21-31: Hagar and Sarah

> Tell me, you who want to be under law, do you not hear the law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave woman and one by the free woman. But the son of the slave was born according to flesh, while the son of the free was born through promise. Now this may be interpreted allegorically: these women are two covenants. One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children for slavery; she is Hagar. Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia; she corresponds to the present Jerusalem, for she is in slavery with her children. But the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother. For it is written: "Rejoice, O barren one who does not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor; for the children of the desolate one will be more than those of the one who has a husband." Now you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise. But just as at that time the one born according to flesh persecuted the one born according to Spirit, so also it is now. But what does the scripture say? "Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman." So then, brothers, we are not children of the slave woman but of the free.

**Verse 21: "Tell me, you who want to be under law, do you not hear the law?"**

The sentence uses nomos twice in two different senses, and the wordplay is deliberate.

**Hoi hypo nomon thelontes einai** ("you who wish to be under law") uses the phrase whose meaning was fixed at 4:4-5: a standing, a jurisdiction, a position under the law's authority.

**Ton nomon ouk akouete** ("do you not hear the law?") uses nomos for the Pentateuch as scripture -- since what follows is a narrative from Genesis, not a commandment. This is standard Jewish usage: "the law" can name the five books as a whole, as in "the law and the prophets."

The pun marks the transition and makes a point in passing: people who want to place themselves under the law's jurisdiction are about to be shown what the law itself narrates.

**Verses 22-23: "For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave woman and one by the free woman. But the son of the slave was born according to flesh, while the son of the free was born through promise."**

Note that Paul names Hagar (at 4:24-25) but **never names Sarah**, referring to her only as "the free woman." This keeps the contrast on status rather than on personality, which is what an allegory of this kind requires: the women are being used as positions in a scheme.

The two births are distinguished by their **manner**.

**Kata sarka gegennētai** ("has been born according to flesh") describes Ishmael. In Genesis the circumstances are precise and are not discreditable to anyone in an obvious way: Sarah is barren, the promise appears blocked, and she proposes a solution using the resources available to her -- her Egyptian slave. Ishmael is a real son of Abraham, and he is produced by human arrangement to secure a promise that seemed not to be arriving on its own.

**Di' epangelias** ("through promise") describes Isaac -- a son who existed only because God said he would, born to a woman past childbearing who laughed when she heard it.

**Kata sarka is the letter's own phrase**, and by this point it has accumulated its senses. It has meant human effort as against the Spirit (3:3); it will mean circumcision explicitly at 6:12-13. Applied here to a son produced by human contrivance, in a letter about a procedure performed on the flesh, it carries both at once.

**Verse 24: "Now this may be interpreted allegorically: these women are two covenants. One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children for slavery; she is Hagar."**

**Hatina estin allēgoroumena** ("which things are being allegorized"). Paul flags his own method, and he does this almost nowhere else in his letters. The participle is present passive: these things are *being taken figuratively*.

**Allegorical reading was an established practice, and knowing that changes how the announcement should be heard.** Greek readers had long allegorized Homer, extracting philosophical meaning from myths that were morally awkward if taken at face value. And Jewish readers did the same with the scriptures: **Philo of Alexandria**, a contemporary of Paul, allegorized the Pentateuch systematically and at length -- including, as it happens, **these same two women**. In Philo's reading Hagar represents the preliminary studies (grammar, geometry, rhetoric) and Sarah represents virtue or wisdom, so that a student passes through Hagar to reach Sarah.

Paul's allegory is not Philo's, and there is no reason to think he took it from him. But the practice was familiar, and a reader who has been told "this is being allegorized" would know what kind of operation was about to be performed.

**The announcement limits the claim, and the limitation should be respected.** Paul is not offering an interpretation of what Genesis means. He is declaring what he is doing with Genesis. A figure is being constructed for a purpose, and the reader is being told so before it is built.

**Mia men apo orous Sina eis douleian gennōsa** ("one from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery"). The description is of what this covenant, so used, *produces*: eis douleian gennōsa, giving birth into slavery. The participle describes an effect, not an essence -- what comes of it, not what it is in itself.

**Verse 25: "Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia; she corresponds to the present Jerusalem, for she is in slavery with her children."**

The text of this verse is uncertain. Some manuscripts read "for Sinai is a mountain in Arabia," omitting Hagar from the equation; others have the fuller form. The variant does not change the argument's shape.

**Systoichei** ("she stands in the same row, corresponds to") is from the same root as stoicheia (4:3, 9), and it will recur in related forms at 5:25 (stoichōmen, "let us walk in line") and 6:16 (stoichēsousin, "as many as walk by this rule"). The verb is used of items standing in the same column of a list, or of soldiers in the same file. Paul is building two columns and assigning things to them.

**What stands in Hagar's column is stated with more precision than it is usually read with.** It is **tē nyn Ierousalēm** -- "the **present** Jerusalem." The adjective is temporal and the referent political: the city as it now is, which **douleuei meta tōn teknōn autēs** ("is in slavery with her children"), present tense.

Judea in this period was under Roman rule. The statement is, on its face, a description of a city's actual condition.

Paul does not write "the Sinai covenant is in slavery." He writes that the present city is in slavery, and that it lines up in the same column as Hagar.

**Verse 26: "But the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother."**

**Hē anō Ierousalēm** ("the Jerusalem above"). The expectation of a heavenly or restored Jerusalem is well established in Jewish literature of this period and is not Paul's invention. 4 Ezra 7:26 speaks of a city that shall appear; 4 Ezra 10:25-28 has Zion revealed as a built city in a vision. 2 Baruch 4:2-6 says the city was shown to Adam before he sinned, and to Abraham, and to Moses on the mountain, and that it is preserved with God. Within the New Testament, Hebrews 12:22 speaks of "the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem," and Revelation 21:2 of "the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God."

The image of a city as **mother** is likewise scriptural: Isaiah 50:1 addresses Zion as a mother; Isaiah 54, which Paul is about to quote, runs the figure at length; and Psalm 87:5 says "of Zion it shall be said, 'This one and that one were born in her.'"

**Mētēr hēmōn** ("our mother") -- and the pronoun is inclusive. Paul places himself in the free column, which will matter at verse 31.

**Verse 27: "For it is written: 'Rejoice, O barren one who does not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor; for the children of the desolate one will be more than those of the one who has a husband.'"**

The citation is **Isaiah 54:1**, quoted exactly from the Greek version. It follows immediately after the fourth servant song (Isaiah 52:13-53:12).

**The original setting of this verse bears heavily on how the passage should be read, and it cuts in a direction that is easy to miss.**

In Isaiah, the barren and desolate woman is **Zion herself**, in exile, addressed with a promise that her children will outnumber what she had before. The chapter continues in the same voice: "For your Maker is your husband, the LORD of hosts is his name... For the LORD has called you like a wife deserted and grieved in spirit, like a wife of youth when she is cast off... For a brief moment I deserted you, but with great compassion I will gather you" (54:5-7).

It is a promise **to** Jerusalem of restoration. It is not a promise that someone else will replace her.

Two readings therefore follow, and they have very different implications for the passage as a whole.

*Paul is invoking Zion's own promised restoration.* The desolate city was told her children would exceed her former numbers; that is now happening, and the Gentile mission is how. On this reading the citation is a promise being kept to Jerusalem, and the passage is not writing her off -- indeed it is the opposite.

*Paul is transferring Zion's promises to "the Jerusalem above."* On this reading the present city is left in Hagar's column and the promises made to her have been relocated to a different Jerusalem.

The passage does not say which. **And this is arguably a more consequential unresolved question than the Hagar equation itself**, because the second reading implies considerably more than the allegory's columns do on their own -- and because most treatments of the passage decide it silently.

**Verses 28-29: "Now you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise. But just as at that time the one born according to flesh persecuted the one born according to Spirit, so also it is now."**

**On the claim that Ishmael persecuted Isaac.** Genesis 21:9 says only that Sarah saw Ishmael doing something. The Hebrew participle is **metsacheq**, from the root tsachaq -- which is the root of Isaac's own name (Yitschaq, "he laughs"), and which means to laugh, to play, to sport, and in some forms to mock or make sport of. The Greek version renders it **paizonta**, "playing." What exactly Sarah saw, and why it provoked her to demand expulsion, the text does not say.

Reading this as persecution is therefore an interpretive tradition rather than a plain sense of the Genesis narrative. The tradition is attested in Jewish sources, and Paul is drawing on it rather than inventing it -- but a reader should know that Genesis does not say what Paul's sentence assumes.

**Houtōs kai nyn** ("so also now") makes the application explicit and present. Someone in Galatia is doing to the Galatians what the tradition says Ishmael did to Isaac. The letter names that activity elsewhere: they "trouble" (1:7; 5:10), they "want to shut you out" (4:17), they "compel" (6:12), they "unsettle" (5:12).

**Verse 30: "But what does the scripture say? 'Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman.'"**

The citation is Genesis 21:10, and **Paul has modified it**. The Greek version reads: "...shall not inherit with **my son Isaac**" (meta tou huiou mou Isaak). Paul writes meta tou huiou tēs eleutheras -- "with **the son of the free woman**." The proper name is removed so that the sentence speaks in the terms of his two columns rather than in the terms of the original scene.

This is the fourth adapted citation in the letter, after 2:16, 3:10-11 and 3:13. The pattern is consistent enough to be a habit.

**Two features of the Genesis source should be known, because both bear on how much weight the citation can carry.**

*First, the words are Sarah's, not God's.* Genesis 21:10 is a demand made by an angry woman at a weaning feast. It is not a divine pronouncement, and Paul is quoting a character's speech within a narrative.

*Second, Genesis immediately qualifies it.* The very next verse says the matter "was very displeasing to Abraham on account of his son" (21:11). God's reply then both endorses the separation and adds something Paul does not quote: "and I will make a nation of the son of the slave woman also, because he is your offspring" (21:13). And the chapter goes on to narrate God hearing the boy crying in the wilderness, opening Hagar's eyes to a well, and promising again to make him a great nation (21:17-20).

The Genesis narrative does not write Ishmael off. It provides for him, twice, in the same chapter. And Hagar herself is met by God in the wilderness on both occasions she is driven there, and in the first of them she becomes the only person in the book of Genesis to give God a name (16:13).

**The imperative and its referent.** Ekbale ("cast out") is a command, and this letter is addressed to Gentile congregations in Galatia.

Whatever they are being told to expel must therefore be something within their reach. They cannot expel a city, a covenant, or a people. And verse 29 has just identified a party operating among them now.

The letter's other imperatives concerning that party point the same way: "let him be accursed" (1:8-9); "the one troubling you will bear the judgment" (5:10); "I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves" (5:12). The most natural referent of "cast out" is the group in Galatia running the circumcision programme.

**Verse 31: "So then, brothers, we are not children of the slave woman but of the free."**

**Ouk esmen** ("we are not") -- inclusive again, as at 4:26.

Paul is a Jew by birth and says so plainly at 2:15. He places himself among the free. Whatever occupies Hagar's column, therefore, it cannot be Jews as such -- and the sentence that closes the allegory is the one that most clearly limits what the allegory can be made to mean.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. **The coming-of-age figure completes the paidagogos argument and governs how it should be read.** The minor heir "is owner of everything" the whole time he is under supervision, and his majority arrives on a date the father fixed in advance -- not on his earning it or proving ready for it. What ends is not his relation to the household but his subjection within it. This is the letter's own gloss on 3:25, and it tells against readings in which the arrival of faith terminates the law rather than a standing under it.

2. **4:4 is the control on hypo nomon, and it settles a question that recurs throughout the letter.** Christ was "born under law" in order to redeem those "under law." Since Christ observed Torah, the phrase cannot describe Torah practice; it denotes a jurisdiction -- a position under the law's authority and sanctions. That governs 3:23, 4:5, 4:21 and 5:18, and it bears directly on what 2:19's "died to law" can mean.

3. **The stoicheia are not identified with the law, and 4:2 is why.** The overseers of the minority are named as "guardians and stewards" -- plural, unspecified, and distinct from the Torah, which on the terms of the metaphor is what the father gave *during* the minority. Whatever the stoicheia are, the text does not equate them with the instruction given under them, and a reader should notice when an interpretation does.

4. **The Spirit is presented as the consequence of sonship, not its cause.** "*Because* you are sons, God sent forth the Spirit" (4:6). Together with 3:2-5 this is the second appeal to the readers' own experience, and it is the letter's most concrete practical argument: the Abba cry is already in their mouths -- preserved in Aramaic in Greek-speaking Gentile congregations -- and they are being sold an entry procedure.

5. **4:10 is contested but the evidence is not evenly split, and treating it as evenly split is itself a distortion.** Three things weigh against the Jewish-calendar reading: the list has no weekly unit at all, though the sabbath is the most conspicuous of Jewish times and the Colossians parallel duly has it; the sequence reproduces Genesis 1:14's luminaries, which is celestial rather than festal timekeeping and which shows kairos doing astronomical duty; and verse 8 identifies the readers as former idolaters while verse 9 requires a genuine return to something once left, which Gentiles cannot have. What has to be answered on the other side is real -- kairos does render mo'ed, and Colossians 2:16 is a close parallel beside the same stoicheia phrase -- but it does not account for a sabbath-less list addressed to former pagans who are said to be turning back. And complicating both: 4:3 has already placed "we" under the same stoicheia, so the word is not pagan-only in Paul's usage.

6. **Verses 12-20 are appeal rather than argument, and the letter needs them.** They received him when he was ill -- when the customary response would have been to spit -- and would have given him their eyes; now he is their enemy for telling them the truth. Set against 4:17, where the other teachers "make much of you" so that "you may make much of them," the section frames the crisis as a contest for these congregations' affection and not only for their theology.

7. **4:19 states the goal of Paul's labour, and it is formation rather than status.** "Until Christ be formed in you" uses a verb found nowhere else in the New Testament. And the metaphor is deliberately impossible -- a man in labour, and in labour a second time with children already born. Whatever is being sought is not a rite completed but a shape taken, which is not a thing that can be counted or displayed.

8. **Paul announces the Hagar passage as allegory, and the announcement limits what it claims.** He is not saying what Genesis means; he is saying what he is doing with it. Allegorical reading was an established practice -- Philo allegorized these same two women in the same period, to entirely different ends -- so the announcement told a first-century reader what kind of operation was coming. And the figure is built against expectation: Hagar the Egyptian slave and Ishmael the excluded son were, in every Jewish telling, the *Gentile* position. Assigning Sinai and the present Jerusalem to that column is the rhetorical shock the passage runs on, and it is aimed at men who have been told that conversion will make them Isaac.

9. **What stands in Hagar's column is described with care and should be quoted precisely.** A covenant "bearing children into slavery" -- named by what it produces when used this way. "The **present** Jerusalem" -- a temporal and political designation, a city in slavery with her children under Rome. And at 4:26 and 4:31 Paul places himself, a Jew by birth, in the free column. Whatever occupies Hagar's side, it cannot be Jews, and it is not stated to be the Sinai covenant considered in itself.

10. **The unresolved question in the passage is Isaiah 54:1, not Hagar.** The verse Paul quotes is a promise of restoration made to Zion in exile, and the chapter it comes from is about God gathering back a wife he had briefly deserted. Whether Paul invokes it as Zion's own promise now being kept, or transfers it to the Jerusalem above and leaves the present city behind, decides far more about the passage's implications than the columns do -- and the text does not settle it, while most treatments settle it without saying so.

11. **The command at 4:30 must have a referent the readers can act on, and the Genesis source is far more qualified than the citation shows.** Verse 29 identifies a persecuting party operating in Galatia now, and the letter commands action against that party and no other. Meanwhile the words Paul quotes are Sarah's rather than God's; God's own reply in the same passage promises to make a nation of the expelled son; and Hagar is twice met in the wilderness by God, once naming him. The harshest sentence in the chapter falls on a faction in Galatia, and its scriptural warrant is considerably more complicated than the quotation alone suggests.
