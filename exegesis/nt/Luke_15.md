# Luke 15 -- First-Century Reading

## Context: The Argument Before and After

Luke 14 closes with Jesus addressing great crowds about the cost of following him. He warns that anyone who does not renounce all that he has cannot be his disciple (14:33), then compares useless discipleship to salt that has lost its taste and is "thrown away" (14:34-35). He concludes with the pointed exhortation: "He who has ears to hear, let him hear." This is a challenge directed outward -- to the crowd, but implicitly also to the religious leaders standing among them. The question is who will truly hear.

Luke 16 opens with Jesus turning to his disciples and telling a parable about a dishonest steward who shrewdly prepares for his coming displacement, followed by direct teaching about faithfulness and mammon, which Luke then notes the Pharisees heard and "scoffed at" because they were "lovers of money" (16:14). The audience thus shifts between chapters: in 15 Jesus addresses the murmuring Pharisees and scribes, in 16 he speaks to his disciples while the Pharisees eavesdrop and react.

Luke 15 fits between these two movements. The end of chapter 14 poses the question of hearing and responding. Chapter 15 answers it by dramatizing who is actually hearing (the tax collectors and sinners) and who refuses to hear (the Pharisees and scribes). Chapter 16 then extends the argument by asking what people do with what has been entrusted to them. The thread that runs through all three chapters is the question of who truly belongs in God's restored people, who recognizes it, and who refuses it.

---

## The Narrative Frame (15:1-3)

The chapter opens with a scene, not a parable. Tax collectors and sinners were drawing near to Jesus to hear him. The Pharisees and scribes murmured, saying, "This man receives sinners and eats with them." Jesus then spoke "this parable unto them" -- the singular "parable" is significant. Luke presents the three stories that follow not as three separate parables but as one composite argument directed at the murmuring leaders.

This complaint is not new in Luke. In 5:29-32, after calling Levi the tax collector, Jesus ate at Levi's house with "a large crowd of tax collectors and other people." The Pharisees and their scribes grumbled to the disciples: "Why do you eat and drink with the tax collectors and sinners?" Jesus answered: "It is not those who are healthy who need a physician, but those who are sick. I have not come to call the righteous to repentance, but sinners." The scene in chapter 15 repeats this pattern -- the same groups, the same complaint -- but now Jesus answers not with a brief proverb but with an extended, three-part argument.

The complaint concerns table fellowship. In first-century Jewish practice, sharing a meal was not a casual social act. To eat with someone was to accept them, to declare solidarity with them. The Pharisees' objection is not merely that Jesus talks to sinners but that he sits at table with them as though they belong. The entire chapter is Jesus' defense of this practice.

## The Lost Sheep (15:4-7)

### The Old Testament Background

The image of a shepherd searching for lost sheep has deep roots in Israel's scriptures. Ezekiel 34 is the primary background. There God indicts the shepherds of Israel -- the leaders -- who fed themselves but did not feed the flock, who did not seek the lost or bring back the scattered (Ezek 34:2-6). Because the shepherds failed, God declares: "Behold, I Myself will search for My sheep and seek them out... I will seek the lost, bring back the scattered, bind up the broken, and strengthen the sick" (Ezek 34:11, 16). Jeremiah uses the same imagery: "My people have become lost sheep; their shepherds have led them astray" (Jer 50:6). Isaiah depicts God tenderly gathering lambs in his arms (Isa 40:11). Micah promises that God will "assemble those who limp and gather the scattered" (Mic 4:6).

When a first-century hearer trained in Israel's scriptures heard a story about a shepherd going out to find a lost sheep, they would not hear a generic moral tale. They would hear Ezekiel 34 being enacted. And the implication would be devastating: if God must come himself to seek the lost sheep, it is because the appointed shepherds -- the leaders -- have failed.

### The Parable

Jesus begins with a rhetorical question: "What man of you, having a hundred sheep, if he lose one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness and go after that which is lost, until he find it?" The phrase "what man of you" draws the hearers in -- including the Pharisees. Jesus asks them to imagine themselves as the shepherd. The expected answer is: of course you would go. Any responsible shepherd would.

Several details are important:

**The shepherd goes "until he find it."** The search is not half-hearted or time-limited. It continues until the lost one is recovered. This mirrors Ezekiel 34's language where God says he will seek and search for his sheep.

**The shepherd lays the sheep on his shoulders.** This echoes Isaiah 40:11, where God gathers lambs in his arms. It is an image of tenderness and physical effort -- the shepherd bears the weight of the lost one home.

**The celebration.** Upon returning, the shepherd gathers friends and neighbors and says, "Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep which was lost." The recovery of what was lost requires communal celebration. The shepherd does not simply put the sheep back in the pen and move on. He calls others to share in the joy.

**The application (v. 7).** Jesus declares: "I say unto you, that likewise joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repenteth, more than over ninety-nine just persons which need no repentance."

This statement is carefully phrased. The "ninety-nine just persons who need no repentance" can be read in two ways. It could be ironic -- implying that the Pharisees consider themselves righteous but are not. Or it could be taken at face value -- the ninety-nine are genuinely within the fold and are not the focus of this story. Either way, the point is the same: the recovery of one who was outside generates extraordinary joy. The emphasis is not on the status of the ninety-nine but on the proper response to finding the lost. And Jesus places this joy "in heaven" -- this is God's response, not merely a human sentiment.

Note carefully what the sheep does in this parable: nothing. The sheep does not find its own way home. The sheep does not earn its recovery. The shepherd does all the work -- seeking, finding, carrying, celebrating. The initiative belongs entirely to the one who searches.

## The Lost Coin (15:8-10)

Jesus immediately continues with a second image. A woman who has ten silver coins (drachmas) loses one. She lights a lamp, sweeps the house, and seeks diligently until she finds it. Then she gathers her friends and neighbors: "Rejoice with me, for I have found the piece which I had lost."

The structure precisely mirrors the first parable: something of value is lost, the owner searches with effort and determination, the lost thing is found, celebration follows, and Jesus provides the theological application.

### Details

**The drachma.** A Greek silver coin, roughly equivalent to a denarius -- approximately one day's wage for a laborer. Ten drachmas was not a fortune, but for a woman of modest means it was significant. Some scholars have suggested these coins were part of a headdress worn as a marriage token or dowry ornament, which would explain why losing one of ten was so distressing -- it would be like losing a piece of one's wedding jewelry. Whether or not this specific cultural practice is in view, the proportional loss (one-tenth) is larger than the sheep (one-hundredth), and the value is more personal.

**The lamp and the sweeping.** Palestinian houses of this period were often small, with few windows and packed-earth floors. A lost coin could easily become lodged in a crack or buried in debris. The woman's search is thorough and physical -- she changes the environment (light, sweeping) to locate what is hidden. This is not a passive waiting for the coin to turn up.

**The application (v. 10).** "Likewise, I say unto you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repenteth." The phrasing is slightly different from verse 7. There it was "joy in heaven"; here it is "joy in the presence of the angels." The joy is not attributed to the angels themselves but occurs before them -- it is God's own joy, witnessed by the heavenly court.

Again, the coin does nothing. A coin cannot repent. A coin cannot seek its owner. The entire initiative belongs to the woman who searches. And yet Jesus applies both parables to "one sinner who repents." This tells us something about what Jesus means by repentance in this context: it is not a self-generated moral achievement by the sinner but the end result of being found by the one who was searching. The repentance is real, but it follows the seeking, not the other way around.

## The Two Sons and Their Father (15:11-32)

The third and longest story shifts the imagery from possessions (a sheep, a coin) to persons (sons). The ratio of lost-to-whole escalates: one of a hundred, one of ten, now one of two. With each parable, the relational stakes increase. But this story does something the first two did not: it introduces a second figure who also needs to respond -- the older son. This is where Jesus' argument reaches the Pharisees most directly.

### The Younger Son's Departure (15:12-16)

The younger son says to his father: "Father, give me the portion of goods that falls to me." Under Torah (Deut 21:17), the firstborn received a double portion. In a two-son family, this meant the elder received two-thirds and the younger one-third. But the normal practice was for inheritance to be distributed at the father's death, or at the father's discretion late in life. For a son to demand his share while the father was still alive and well was, in the culture of the time, an act of breathtaking disrespect. It effectively treated the father as though he were already dead. The father, remarkably, complies. He "divided unto them his living" -- note the plural "them," meaning both sons received their portions.

The younger son then gathers everything and travels to "a far country." In Israel's scriptural memory, departure to a far country carries the resonance of exile. The people were sent away from the land because of unfaithfulness. The son's journey recapitulates the pattern: he leaves the father's house, the father's land, the father's people.

In the far country, he "wasted his substance with riotous living." When his resources are exhausted, a famine strikes, and he "began to be in want." He attaches himself to a citizen of that country, who sends him to feed swine. For a Jewish audience, this detail is pointed. Swine are unclean animals under Torah. To feed them is to be immersed in uncleanness. To desire to eat "the husks that the swine did eat" is to be reduced below the animals themselves. The son has reached the furthest possible distance from his father's house and from covenant life.

"And no man gave unto him." He is utterly alone, without community, without provision, without dignity.

### The Turning Point (15:17-20a)

"And when he came to himself." This phrase is significant. It implies that his previous state was a kind of self-forgetfulness or self-alienation. He was not in his right mind -- not in the sense of madness, but in the sense that he had lost awareness of who he was and where he belonged. Coming to himself, he remembers his father's house: "How many hired servants of my father have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger!"

He rehearses a speech: "I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before you, and am no more worthy to be called your son: make me as one of your hired servants." The speech has three parts: (1) an acknowledgment of sin against God ("heaven") and against the father; (2) a renunciation of his status as son; (3) a request to be demoted to hired servant. This is not merely pragmatic hunger management. The language "I have sinned against heaven and before you" echoes formal penitential language in Israel's tradition.

"And he arose, and came to his father." The return is a physical act -- he gets up, he walks back. The journey to the far country is reversed.

### The Father's Response (15:20b-24)

"But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him." This detail is crucial. The father was watching. The father was looking toward the road. The father saw him while he was still far away. This implies that the father had been watching, perhaps daily, for the son's return.

"And had compassion, and ran." In the culture of the ancient Near East, a patriarch did not run. Running required gathering up one's robes, exposing the legs -- it was considered undignified for an elder. The father abandons social propriety. His compassion overrides protocol.

"And fell on his neck, and kissed him." The father initiates the physical reconciliation before the son has spoken a word.

The son begins his rehearsed speech: "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in your sight, and am no more worthy to be called your son." But he never finishes. The third element of his prepared statement -- "make me as one of your hired servants" -- is cut off. The father does not let him complete the demotion request.

Instead, the father commands his servants: "Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet." Each item signifies restoration:

- **The best robe** (literally "the first robe," *ten stolen ten proten*): the finest garment in the house, signifying honor and status restored. This may be the father's own robe, a sign of the father clothing the son in his own dignity.
- **A ring on his hand**: in the ancient world, a signet ring carried the authority of the household. This is not decorative jewelry; it restores the son's standing to act on the family's behalf.
- **Sandals on his feet**: servants and slaves went barefoot. Sandals are the mark of a free person, a son of the house.

The father then commands the fatted calf to be killed -- an animal kept in reserve for a significant occasion. The celebration that follows mirrors the celebrations in the first two parables: the recovery of the lost demands feasting and rejoicing.

The father's declaration summarizes the point: "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found." The language of death and life, lost and found, echoes the first two parables. The son was in a state of death -- cut off from family, from community, from covenant identity -- and has been restored to life.

### The Older Son's Refusal (15:25-32)

Here the parable takes its decisive turn. The older son, who was "in the field" (working, fulfilling his duties), approaches the house and hears music and dancing. He calls a servant and asks what is happening. The servant reports: "Your brother is come; and your father has killed the fatted calf, because he has received him safe and sound."

"And he was angry, and would not go in."

This is the crux of the chapter. The older son's refusal to enter the celebration is the narrative equivalent of the Pharisees' and scribes' murmuring in verse 2. They objected that Jesus "receives sinners and eats with them." The older son objects that his father receives the wayward son and feasts with him. The structural parallel is unmistakable: Jesus has been building toward this moment across all three parables.

The father comes out to the older son. Note: the father goes out to both sons. He ran out to the younger son when he was still far off; now he comes out to the older son who refuses to come in. The father seeks both of them.

The older son's complaint reveals his inner posture: "Lo, these many years do I serve you, neither transgressed I at any time your commandment: and yet you never gave me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends." Several features of this speech are telling:

1. **"I serve you" (*douleuō soi*)**: the verb is the one used for slave labor, not for the relationship of a son. The older son describes his life in the father's house as servitude, not as sonship. He has been physically present but relationally estranged -- he has been in the father's house without experiencing it as home.

2. **"Neither transgressed I at any time your commandment"**: this is the language of legal compliance. He frames his relationship with his father in terms of commandments kept and obedience rendered, not in terms of love, trust, or intimacy.

3. **"You never gave me a kid"**: the father had already divided the inheritance (v. 12, "he divided unto them his living"). The older son received his two-thirds. Everything the father has is already his. His complaint that he never received even a young goat is factually inaccurate -- or rather, it reveals that he never understood what he already had.

4. **"This your son" (*ho huios sou houtos*)**: he refuses to say "my brother." He distances himself from the younger son, making him the father's problem, not his concern. This mirrors the Pharisees' language -- "this man receives sinners" -- where "sinners" are treated as a category apart, not as fellow Israelites.

5. **"Which has devoured your living with harlots"**: the earlier narrative says the younger son wasted his substance with "riotous living." It does not mention prostitutes. The older brother adds this detail -- embellishing the sin, making it more lurid than the story warrants. He is constructing a narrative of maximum condemnation.

The father's response is gentle and direct: "Son (*teknon* -- child, a term of intimacy), you are always with me, and all that I have is yours." This is both reassurance and correction. The older son has not been cheated. The father's love for the younger son does not diminish what belongs to the elder. The inheritance is not a zero-sum competition.

Then: "It was fitting (*edei* -- it was necessary, it was proper, it was right) that we should make merry, and be glad: for this your brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found." The father corrects the distancing language. Not "this your son" but "this your brother." And the celebration is not optional or excessive -- it is necessary. The word *edei* carries moral weight; this is what the situation demanded.

### The Unresolved Ending

The parable ends without telling us what the older son does. Does he go in to the feast? Does he remain outside? Jesus leaves the question open. This is deliberate. The parable is being told to the Pharisees and scribes who are murmuring. They are the older son. Jesus is inviting them to enter the celebration -- to join in the joy over the recovery of the lost -- but he does not force the ending. The decision is left to them.

This open ending transforms the parable from a story into a confrontation. It is not a tale with a moral; it is a mirror held up to the audience.

## The Argument of the Chapter as a Whole

The three parables form a single, escalating argument that answers the complaint of 15:2 ("This man receives sinners and eats with them"):

1. **The lost sheep**: If even a shepherd goes after one lost animal from a hundred, how much more should the lost of Israel be sought? And there is joy in heaven when they are found.

2. **The lost coin**: If even a woman turns her house upside down for one coin from ten, how much more should the lost among God's people be sought? And God himself rejoices when they are found.

3. **The two sons**: The lost son returns, the father celebrates, and the one who should be most glad -- the older brother who remained faithful -- refuses to join the feast. This is the indictment: the Pharisees are not wrong to have stayed faithful. Their faithfulness is acknowledged ("you are always with me, and all that I have is yours"). What is wrong is their refusal to celebrate when the lost are found. Their complaint against Jesus' table fellowship with sinners reveals that they have been in the father's house as servants, not as sons -- keeping commandments but missing the heart of the father.

### The Lost are Israel's Own

The tax collectors and sinners who draw near to Jesus in 15:1 are not gentiles. They are Israelites. They are sons and daughters of Abraham who, for various reasons -- collaboration with Rome, moral failure, economic marginalization -- have been pushed to the edges of covenant life by the religious establishment. Jesus' act of eating with them is an act of restoration: he is declaring that they belong at the table.

This is precisely what Ezekiel 34 prophesied. God would come himself to seek the lost sheep of Israel because the shepherds had failed. The shepherds had driven the weak away, had not sought the scattered, had not bound up the broken (Ezek 34:4). When Jesus tells the Pharisees the parable of the lost sheep, he is not merely offering a homey illustration. He is claiming to fulfill Ezekiel 34. He is the shepherd God promised to send, and the Pharisees are the failed shepherds who scattered the flock. Hosea's cry reverberates here as well: "When Israel was a youth I loved him... the more they called them, the more they went from them" (Hos 11:1-2) -- and yet God's compassion is kindled: "How can I give you up, Ephraim?" (Hos 11:8). The father in the parable who watches the road and runs to meet the returning son is this God.

### Repentance: What It Means Here

Jesus applies the first two parables to "one sinner who repents." But the sheep did not repent -- it was found. The coin did not repent -- it was found. Even the younger son's repentance, genuine as it is, is met by a father who was already watching, already running, already prepared to restore. The emphasis throughout is on the seeker, not on the mechanics of the sinner's return. Repentance is real and necessary (Luke 13:3, 5 -- "unless you repent, you will all likewise perish"), but in these parables repentance is the human experience of being found by God, not a precondition that triggers God's willingness to seek.

This is consistent with Luke 19:10, where Jesus says of Zacchaeus: "For the Son of Man has come to seek and to save that which was lost." The seeking precedes the saving. The initiative belongs to the seeker.

### Joy as the Proper Response

The most repeated theme of the chapter is joy. The shepherd rejoices. The woman rejoices. There is joy in heaven. There is joy before the angels. The father celebrates with music, dancing, and feasting. The chapter is saturated with celebration.

The older son's refusal to celebrate is therefore not a minor personality flaw. It is a fundamental misunderstanding of what God is doing. If God rejoices when a sinner repents, then refusing to rejoice is refusing to align oneself with God's own heart. The Pharisees' murmuring in verse 2 is, in Jesus' telling, a refusal to participate in heaven's joy.

### The Structural Indictment

The three parables build toward an inescapable conclusion. The first two are non-controversial: no one would criticize a shepherd for finding a lost sheep or a woman for finding a lost coin. The audience nods along. Then the third parable introduces the older brother, and the audience suddenly recognizes itself. The logic that seemed obvious in the first two stories -- of course you seek the lost, of course you celebrate when they are found -- now becomes an indictment. If you agreed that the shepherd should seek and the woman should search, then you must also agree that the father should celebrate. And if you agree the father should celebrate, then you, the older brother standing outside the feast, are the one who is wrong.

The parable does not condemn the older son's prior faithfulness. The father affirms it. What it challenges is the older son's refusal to recognize his own brother, his transformation of sonship into servitude, and his resentment at grace extended to another. The question left hanging at the end of the chapter is whether the older son -- whether the Pharisees -- will come in.

---

## Summary

Luke 15 is a unified, three-part response to the religious leaders' complaint that Jesus eats with sinners. Drawing on Ezekiel 34's prophecy of God himself seeking Israel's lost sheep, Jesus constructs an argument that moves from the uncontroversial (a shepherd finding a sheep, a woman finding a coin) to the confrontational (an older brother refusing to celebrate his brother's return). The chapter's emphasis falls not on the lost one's worthiness to be found, but on the seeker's determination to find, the joy that erupts when the lost is recovered, and the indictment of those who refuse to share in that joy. The tax collectors and sinners are presented as lost Israelites being gathered back by the shepherd God promised to send. The Pharisees are presented as the older brother -- faithful in duty, possessing everything, yet standing outside the feast because they will not acknowledge the lost as their own. The story ends without resolution, placing the choice before the hearers: will they come in?
