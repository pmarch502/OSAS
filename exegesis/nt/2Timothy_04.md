# 2 Timothy 4 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**The document's genre and occasion:** This is the final chapter of Paul's last letter to Timothy, written from prison in Rome under the expectation of approaching death. The letter's genre -- testamentary exhortation, the farewell words of a dying figure to his successor -- reaches its climax here. Everything the letter has built toward converges in this chapter: the solemn charge to preach the word, the warning that people will abandon sound teaching, Paul's own final testimony, and the personal details of a man preparing for the end. The testamentary genre requires a final charge (vv. 1-5), a review of one's life and legacy (vv. 6-8), last instructions and personal requests (vv. 9-18), and a closing (vv. 19-22). Chapter 4 provides all of these in sequence.

The letter's setting, recoverable from its own details, has been established across the preceding chapters. Paul is imprisoned in Rome (1:17), likely in severe confinement that required Onesiphorus to search earnestly to find him (1:17). He is cold and wants his cloak (4:13). His associates have largely deserted him (1:15; 4:10, 16). Only Luke remains with him (4:11). He has already appeared in court once ("my first defense," 4:16) and anticipates that his end is near (4:6). The false teaching crisis in Ephesus that prompted 1 Timothy continues: Hymenaeus has resurfaced alongside Philetus (2:17), their teaching spreads like gangrene (2:17), and the faith of some has been overturned (2:18). Against this backdrop of desertion, danger, and doctrinal corruption, Paul writes his final words to the one person he trusts to carry the work forward.

**What the author was arguing immediately before (2 Timothy 3):** Chapter 3 opened with a warning cast in eschatological terms: "Understand this, that in the last days there will come times of difficulty" (3:1). A long vice list followed (3:2-5), describing people who possess "the appearance of godliness but deny its power" (3:5). The command was stark: "Avoid such people" (3:5). Some of these people infiltrate households, capturing "weak women, burdened with sins and led astray by various passions, always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (3:6-7). They were compared to Jannes and Jambres, the Egyptian magicians who "opposed Moses" (3:8) -- figures drawn from the Exodus narrative via Jewish tradition. Like those magicians, "their folly will be plain to all" (3:9).

Paul then turned to Timothy with a sharp contrast: "You, however, have followed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life, my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness, my persecutions and sufferings that happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra" (3:10-11). The locations are specific: these are cities in Timothy's own region, places where Timothy witnessed Paul's sufferings firsthand (Acts 13:50-14:20). Paul stated: "the Lord rescued me from them all" (3:11). The rescue is past and attested; it grounds the expectation that suffering is survivable through God's faithfulness. Paul then declared: "Indeed, all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" (3:12) -- a universal principle, not a contingent warning. Persecution is not a sign that something has gone wrong but the expected condition of faithful living.

The chapter's contrast sharpened: "evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse, deceiving and being deceived. But as for you, continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it" (3:13-14). The verb prokoptein ("to advance, to progress") appeared here as in 2:16, describing advancement in the wrong direction -- the impostors make progress into deeper deception. Against this downward trajectory, Timothy is anchored by two things: what he has learned (the content) and from whom he learned it (the source). The "whom" is ambiguous -- it could refer to Paul, to Lois and Eunice (1:5), or to the community of witnesses (2:2). All are relevant. Timothy's education was not from anonymous sources but from known, attested, trustworthy people.

The chapter concluded with a statement about Scripture: "from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (3:15). The "sacred writings" (hiera grammata) are the Scriptures of Israel -- the Torah, the Prophets, and the Writings as available in the first century. Timothy knew them from childhood, through his Jewish mother Eunice and grandmother Lois (1:5; Acts 16:1). These writings are "able to make you wise for salvation" -- but the wisdom they produce is accessed "through faith in Christ Jesus." The Scriptures point toward Christ; their salvific potential is realized through faith in him.

Then came the summary statement: "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work" (3:16-17). The adjective theopneustos ("God-breathed, breathed out by God") describes Scripture as carrying God's breath -- it comes from God in the way that breath comes from the one who breathes. Four functions are listed: teaching (didaskalia), reproof (elegmos), correction (epanorthosis), and training in righteousness (paideia en dikaiosyne). The purpose is stated: "that the man of God may be complete (artios), equipped (exertismenos) for every good work (pan ergon agathon)." The phrase "every good work" echoes 2:21, where the cleansed vessel is "prepared for every good work." The person who is shaped by Scripture and the person who cleanses himself from what is dishonorable arrive at the same destination: readiness for comprehensive service.

**How chapter 4 connects:** Chapter 3 ended with Scripture's purpose: equipping the man of God for every good work. Chapter 4 opens with a solemn charge that specifies what those good works are: "Preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching" (4:2). The four functions of Scripture listed in 3:16 (teaching, reproof, correction, training) correspond directly to the activities commanded in 4:2 (teaching, reproving, rebuking, exhorting). Scripture equips; the charge deploys. Chapter 3 supplied the weapon; chapter 4 orders the soldier into battle.

The connection is also marked by the shift from description to command. Chapter 3 described the landscape -- the false teachers, their methods, their trajectory, and the Scriptural resource available to Timothy. Chapter 4 issues the marching orders -- what Timothy must do in this landscape, and why the urgency is absolute. The conjunction oun ("therefore") that some manuscripts include at 4:1 (whether original or not, the logical connection is unmistakable) draws the consequence: given the resources described in chapter 3, Timothy must act as chapter 4 commands.

The chapter also completes the letter's testamentary structure. The farewell speech of a dying leader characteristically ends with a personal review of the speaker's life, a final expression of confidence, and last practical instructions. Paul's "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" (4:7) is the letter's climactic moment -- the dying father's summary of his life, offered as a model for the son who must now carry on alone.

## Verses 1-2: The Solemn Charge -- Preach the Word

"I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom: preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching."

**"I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus"** (diamartyromai enopion tou theou kai Christou Iesou). The verb diamartyresthai ("to solemnly charge, to testify earnestly, to adjure") is the most serious form of commissioning language available to Paul. The same verb appeared in 1 Timothy 5:21, where Paul charged Timothy "in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels" -- the same cosmic audience, the same weight of solemnity. It appeared again in 2 Timothy 2:14, where Timothy was to charge the community "before God" not to quarrel about words. The verb's prefix dia- ("through, thoroughly") intensifies the testimony: this is not a casual request but a thorough, penetrating, binding charge.

The phrase enopion tou theou kai Christou Iesou ("in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus") establishes the witnesses to the charge. The preposition enopion ("before, in the sight of, in the presence of") places the charge under divine observation. What Paul commands, God and Christ Jesus observe. Timothy receives this instruction not in a private conversation but in a courtroom, with God and Christ as witnesses. The weight is unmistakable: to neglect this charge is to fail before God himself.

**"Who is to judge the living and the dead"** (tou mellontos krinein zontas kai nekrous). The participle mellontos (from mellein, "to be about to, to be going to, to intend") with the infinitive krinein ("to judge") describes Christ Jesus as the one who is about to judge or who is destined to judge. The object of the judgment is comprehensive: zontas kai nekrous -- "living and dead." No one escapes the scope of this judgment. Those alive at the time of judgment and those who have already died are equally subject to it. The phrase echoes the early community's confession: in Acts 10:42, Peter preaches that God appointed Jesus "as judge of the living and the dead" (krites zonton kai nekron). The formula appears to be an established confessional statement that Paul invokes here to heighten the charge's gravity.

The relevance to the letter's argument is direct. Paul is about to die (4:6). Timothy will continue living and working. Both the dying apostle and the living delegate stand before the same judge. The charge Paul gives is issued under the authority of the one who will evaluate how both of them discharged their responsibilities. Paul's confidence about "that day" (1:12, 18; 4:8) and the faithful saying's warning about denial and its consequences (2:12-13) are grounded in this reality: Christ Jesus judges.

**"And by his appearing and his kingdom"** (kai ten epiphaneian autou kai ten basileian autou). The conjunction kai with the accusative creates an additional ground for the charge. Paul charges Timothy not only "in the presence of" God and Christ but also "by" or "in view of" Christ's appearing and kingdom. The nouns are governed by the verb diamartyromai, which here takes a double construction: the charge is made before divine witnesses and in view of divine events.

The noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation, visible arrival") has been used in this letter for Christ's first appearing (1:10, where Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel") and will be used later in this chapter for his future appearing (4:8, "all who have loved his appearing"). Here the reference is to the future appearing -- the return of Christ in visible manifestation, the event that will bring the judgment just described. The word carried connotations in the Hellenistic world of a god or king becoming visible, arriving in power. The emperor's visit to a city was an epiphaneia. Christ's future appearing is the ultimate such event.

The noun basileia ("kingdom, royal rule, reign, sovereignty") describes the realm or rule that Christ's appearing will establish in its fullness. The kingdom of God or of Christ is the sphere of God's sovereign authority. In the LXX, the kingdom belongs to God: Psalm 103:19 (LXX 102:19), "The LORD has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingdom rules over all." Daniel 2:44, "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed." Daniel 7:14, "to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed." The kingdom is what the faithful saying promised to those who endure: "if we endure, we will also reign with him" (2:12). Timothy's charge is issued in the shadow of that coming kingdom. What he does now will be evaluated by the king when the kingdom arrives in its fullness.

**"Preach the word"** (keryxon ton logon). The verb keryssein ("to herald, to proclaim, to announce publicly, to preach") is the activity of the keryx ("herald") described in 1:11, where Paul was appointed "a preacher (keryx) and apostle and teacher." Timothy is now commanded to do what Paul was appointed to do. The imperative keryxon is aorist, conveying urgency: do it, do it now, get on with it.

The object is ton logon -- "the word." Not "a word" or "words" but the word -- the definite article marks a specific, known body of content. In the context of this letter, "the word" is the gospel Paul has been describing: the message about the risen Davidic Messiah (2:8), the deposit entrusted to Timothy (1:14), the word of truth that must be rightly handled (2:15), the message for which Paul suffers in chains but which itself "is not bound" (2:9). "The word" is also the content of the "sacred writings" just described in 3:15-16 -- Scripture itself, which equips the man of God. The command is comprehensive: proclaim the apostolic gospel as the herald of the king, in continuity with the Scriptures of Israel.

**"Be ready in season and out of season"** (epistēthi eukairōs akairōs). The verb ephistanai ("to stand by, to be at hand, to be present, to be ready, to attend to") in the aorist imperative (epistēthi) commands readiness -- Timothy is to stand at his post, present and available. The adverbs eukairōs ("in season, at a convenient time, when the opportunity is favorable") and akairōs ("out of season, at an inconvenient time, when circumstances are unfavorable") form a pair that covers every possible moment. The words share a root: kairos ("time, season, opportune moment"). The prefix eu- ("well, good") marks favorable times; the prefix a- (alpha-privative) marks unfavorable times. Timothy is to preach in good times and bad, when it is convenient and when it is not, when the audience is receptive and when they resist, when the political climate permits and when it threatens.

The instruction is particularly pointed given what verses 3-4 will say: a time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching. That time of rejection is an akairos -- an unfavorable season. Timothy must preach even then. The readiness demanded is not dependent on the audience's response but on the herald's fidelity to his commission.

**"Reprove, rebuke, and exhort"** (elegxon, epitimēson, parakaleson). Three imperatives specify the forms the preaching takes:

(1) **Reprove** (elegxon, from elegchein, "to convict, to expose, to reprove, to bring to light"). The verb describes the act of demonstrating that someone is in error -- exposing the falsehood so that the person confronted can see it. In Titus 1:9, the overseer must "be able to give instruction in sound doctrine and also to rebuke (elegchein) those who contradict it." In Titus 1:13, "rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith." The reproof is an act of revelation: it brings what is hidden into the open.

(2) **Rebuke** (epitimēson, from epitiman, "to rebuke, to censure, to warn, to charge sternly"). The verb carries a stronger note of authority than elegchein. In the Synoptic Gospels, Jesus "rebukes" demons (Mark 1:25; 9:25), the wind and sea (Mark 4:39), and Peter (Mark 8:33). The word implies the exercise of authority over what is wrong -- not merely exposing the error but commanding it to stop. Where reproof reveals, rebuke restrains.

(3) **Exhort** (parakaleson, from parakalein, "to call alongside, to encourage, to urge, to comfort, to exhort"). The verb encompasses both encouragement and appeal. It is the verb from which the noun paraklēsis ("encouragement, exhortation, comfort") derives -- the same noun used in Romans 12:8 for the gift of exhortation, and in Acts 4:36 as the basis for Barnabas's name ("son of encouragement"). The exhortation is the positive counterpart to reproof and rebuke: where reproof corrects error and rebuke restrains wrong, exhortation urges the right course and strengthens the one who follows it.

The three verbs together describe the full range of pastoral speech: correcting what is wrong (reproof), commanding what is wrong to stop (rebuke), and encouraging what is right (exhortation). The order is not accidental: first the error is exposed, then it is confronted, then the hearer is urged toward the truth.

**"With complete patience and teaching"** (en pasē makrothymia kai didachē). The prepositional phrase en ("in, with") describes the manner in which the reproving, rebuking, and exhorting are to be carried out. Two qualities govern the manner:

(1) **Patience** (makrothymia, "patience, long-suffering, forbearance, steadfastness"). The noun is composed of makros ("long") + thymos ("passion, anger, wrath"). Literally, it describes one who is "long before anger" -- one who endures provocation without retaliating. The adjective pasē ("all, every, complete") intensifies: not partial patience but complete patience, patience that does not run out. In the LXX, makrothymia is characteristic of God himself: Exodus 34:6 (LXX), "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger (makrothymos)." God's patience with Israel's repeated failures is the foundational instance of makrothymia. Timothy is to embody this divine quality in his pastoral work.

(2) **Teaching** (didachē, "teaching, instruction, doctrine"). The noun describes the content-rich activity of systematic instruction. The reproof, rebuke, and exhortation are not to be exercised as raw authority but as acts of teaching -- grounded in content, supported by argument, rooted in the word being preached. This connects directly to 2:24-25, where the Lord's servant must be "able to teach" (didaktikon) and must correct opponents with gentleness. The teaching is the vehicle through which the patience becomes effective: Timothy does not merely wait out his opponents but teaches them through the process of correction.

The charge as a whole -- "preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching" -- is the most comprehensive statement of Timothy's commission in the entire letter. It gathers up everything Paul has been saying since the opening chapter: rekindle the gift (1:6), do not be ashamed (1:8), guard the deposit (1:14), entrust the teaching to faithful people (2:2), rightly handle the word of truth (2:15), correct opponents with gentleness (2:25), and be equipped by Scripture for every good work (3:17). The charge is the distillation of the entire letter into a single command: preach.

## Verses 3-4: The Coming Rejection of Sound Teaching

"For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions, and will turn away from listening to the truth and wander off into myths."

**"For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching"** (estai gar kairos hote tēs hygiainousēs didaskalias ouk anexontai). The conjunction gar ("for") explains why the charge of verses 1-2 is so urgent. The urgency is not merely that Timothy ought to preach but that a time is approaching -- perhaps has already begun -- when preaching will be resisted. The noun kairos ("time, season, appointed moment") describes not mere chronological time (chronos) but a qualitative season, a period defined by its character. The future verb estai ("there will be") marks this as something ahead, though the chapter 3 description of present false teachers suggests the process is already underway and will intensify.

The verb anechesthai ("to bear with, to endure, to tolerate, to put up with") in the middle voice with the negative ouk describes refusal to listen. The teaching itself is not the problem; the hearers are the problem. They will not endure -- will not tolerate, will not bear with -- the teaching. The noun didaskalia ("teaching, instruction, doctrine") is qualified by the participle hygiainousēs (from hygiainein, "to be healthy, to be sound, to be in good condition") -- "sound teaching," literally "healthy teaching." The medical metaphor characteristic of the Pastoral Epistles recurs: sound teaching is healthy teaching, teaching that produces well-being in those who receive it. The people Paul describes reject what is healthy in favor of what is diseased. The phrase hygiainousa didaskalia has appeared throughout the letters: 1 Timothy 1:10 ("whatever else is contrary to sound teaching"), 1 Timothy 6:3 ("the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that accords with godliness"), 2 Timothy 1:13 ("Follow the pattern of the sound words that you have heard from me"), Titus 1:9, 2:1. Its consistent use marks the apostolic gospel as the standard of health against which all other teaching is diagnosed as diseased.

**"But having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions"** (alla kata tas idias epithymias heautois episōreusousin didaskalous knēthomenoi tēn akoēn). The adversative alla ("but") introduces the alternative the people choose. The phrase kata tas idias epithymias ("according to their own desires, in keeping with their own passions") identifies the governing criterion: not truth but desire. The adjective idias ("their own, personal, private") emphasizes that the standard is internal and self-generated -- they select teachers based on what they want to hear, not on what is true. The noun epithymia ("desire, craving, longing, passion") in this context describes the appetite for teaching that flatters, entertains, or confirms what they already want to believe. In 2:22, Paul told Timothy to "flee youthful passions" (neoterikas epithymias); here the passions drive the selection of teachers.

The verb episōreuein ("to heap up, to accumulate, to pile on") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of epi ("upon") + sōreuein ("to heap, to pile"). The image is of piling up teachers one on top of another -- not seeking a single reliable instructor but collecting a mass of voices that collectively tell them what they want to hear. The reflexive heautois ("for themselves") underscores the self-serving nature of the accumulation: they are not seeking teachers for the community's benefit but for their own gratification.

The participle knēthomenoi (from knēthein, "to scratch, to itch, to tickle") with tēn akoēn ("the hearing, the ear") creates a vivid physical metaphor: they have "itching ears," ears that need to be scratched. The itch is the craving for novelty, for flattery, for teaching that stimulates without demanding change. The teachers they accumulate are the ones who scratch the itch -- who provide the sensation the ears demand. The metaphor is particularly apt in the context of the Pastoral Epistles' medical language: an itch is a symptom of a disorder, and scratching provides temporary relief while worsening the underlying condition. The hearers' desire for ear-scratching teachers is itself a symptom of the deeper disease that the sound teaching was meant to cure.

**"And will turn away from listening to the truth"** (kai apo men tēs alētheias tēn akoēn apostrepsousin). The verb apostrephein ("to turn away, to turn back, to divert") appeared in 1:15 for those who "turned away from" Paul (apestraphēsan me pantes hoi en tē Asia). The same verb now describes a turning away not from Paul personally but from the truth itself. The noun alētheia ("truth") has been the letter's benchmark: Hymenaeus and Philetus "swerved from the truth" (2:18); the Lord's servant corrects opponents in the hope that God will grant them "repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth" (2:25); Timothy was told to "rightly handle the word of truth" (2:15). The truth is the apostolic gospel, the content of sound teaching, the word Paul commands Timothy to preach. The people described here actively divert their hearing away from this truth. The men... de construction (apo men tēs alētheias... epi de tous mythous) creates a balanced contrast: away from the truth on one side, toward myths on the other.

**"And wander off into myths"** (epi de tous mythous ektrapēsontai). The verb ektrepesthai ("to turn aside, to wander off, to be diverted") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:6 for those who "swerved" from love, good conscience, and sincere faith, and in 1 Timothy 5:15 for widows who "turned aside after Satan." The consistent usage describes deviation from the right path -- not a sudden catastrophic departure but a gradual turning, a drifting off course. The destination of the wandering is tous mythous -- "the myths." The noun mythos ("myth, fable, invented story") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:4, where Paul warned against "myths and endless genealogies, which promote speculations rather than the stewardship from God that is by faith." In 1 Timothy 4:7, Timothy was told to "have nothing to do with irreverent, silly myths." In Titus 1:14, the command was to avoid "Jewish myths." The myths represent the alternative content that replaces the gospel when people reject sound teaching -- speculative, genealogical, and legendary material that offers intellectual stimulation without moral transformation.

The movement described in verses 3-4 is a progressive deterioration:
- Step 1: They will not endure sound teaching (rejection of the healthy).
- Step 2: They will accumulate teachers to suit their own passions (replacement with the pleasant).
- Step 3: They will turn away from the truth (active departure from what is real).
- Step 4: They will wander off into myths (arrival at what is false).

The sequence is not presented as surprising or unprecedented. It is the logical outcome of the process described throughout the letter: the false teaching that began as word-quarreling (2:14) and spread like gangrene (2:17) produces a community that has lost its appetite for truth and craves falsehood. The progression from 1 Timothy to 2 Timothy -- from managing a crisis to watching it metastasize -- finds its culmination in this prediction: the time is coming when the sound teaching will be rejected en masse.

## Verse 5: The Counter-Command to Timothy

"As for you, always be sober-minded, endure suffering, do the work of an evangelist, fulfill your ministry."

**"As for you"** (sy de). The pronoun sy ("you," emphatic, placed first) with the adversative particle de ("but, however") creates the same contrastive structure that has appeared throughout the letter: "they" will do one thing; "you, however," must do the opposite. The construction echoes 3:10 (sy de parēkolouthēsas mou tē didaskalia -- "You, however, have followed my teaching") and 3:14 (sy de mene en hois emathes -- "But as for you, continue in what you have learned"). Each time, the sy de marks the point where Paul turns from describing the behavior of the faithless to commanding the behavior of the faithful. The pattern is consistent: the world will deteriorate; Timothy must not.

**"Always be sober-minded"** (nēphe en pasin). The verb nēphein ("to be sober, to be clear-headed, to be self-controlled, to be watchful") is the same word family as the ananēphein ("to come to one's senses, to sober up") of 2:26, where Paul hoped the opponents might "come to their senses and escape from the snare of the devil." The opponents need to recover sobriety; Timothy must maintain it. The verb's primary sense is literal sobriety -- the state of being free from the distorting effects of wine or intoxicants. Applied metaphorically, it describes mental clarity, freedom from the clouded judgment that false teaching and emotional pressure produce. The phrase en pasin ("in all things, in everything, always") makes the sobriety comprehensive: not sober in some matters and distracted in others, but clear-headed in everything.

**"Endure suffering"** (kakopathēson). The verb kakopathein ("to suffer evil, to endure hardship, to bear affliction") has been one of the letter's recurring terms. In 1:8, Timothy was commanded to synkakopathēson ("share in suffering") for the gospel. In 2:3, the same command: synkakopathēson hos kalos stratiōtēs ("share in suffering as a good soldier"). In 2:9, Paul described his own experience: en hō kakopathō ("for which I suffer"). The verb runs like a thread through the entire letter, binding Paul's experience to Timothy's commission. Paul suffers; Timothy is called to suffer with him and after him. The suffering is not accidental but structural: it is the expected condition of faithful gospel ministry in a hostile environment. The imperative is aorist (kakopathēson), conveying directness: suffer. Accept it. Do not try to avoid it.

**"Do the work of an evangelist"** (ergon poiēson euangelistou). The noun euangelistēs ("evangelist, one who announces good news, a bearer of the gospel") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Acts 21:8 (Philip "the evangelist") and Ephesians 4:11 ("And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers"). The word is composed of eu ("good, well") + angellein ("to announce, to bring a message") -- one who announces good news. Paul does not say Timothy is an evangelist as a fixed office; he says Timothy is to do the work (ergon) of an evangelist -- to perform the function of gospel proclamation. The noun ergon ("work, task, deed") is the practical, concrete activity. The imperative poiēson ("do, make, perform") commands action: not merely hold the title but perform the labor.

**"Fulfill your ministry"** (tēn diakonian sou plērophoresōn). The verb plērophorein ("to fulfill completely, to carry out fully, to bring to full measure") is composed of plērēs ("full") + phorein ("to carry, to bear"). The word describes bringing something to its complete measure -- filling it to the brim, carrying it through to completion. The noun diakonia ("ministry, service, commission") is modified by sou ("your") -- this is Timothy's specific service, the particular commission entrusted to him. The command is not merely to begin or to continue but to complete: bring your ministry to its full measure, leave nothing undone, carry the work through to the end.

The four commands of verse 5 -- be sober, suffer, evangelize, fulfill -- summarize Timothy's calling in four dimensions: mental clarity (sobriety), physical endurance (suffering), public proclamation (evangelism), and vocational completion (fulfilling ministry). They answer the four-step deterioration of verses 3-4 point by point: where others lose their sobriety and become intoxicated by itching-ear teachers, Timothy must remain sober. Where others avoid the cost of faithfulness, Timothy must endure suffering. Where others turn from the truth to myths, Timothy must do the work of proclaiming the good news. Where others abandon their calling, Timothy must fulfill his.

## Verses 6-8: Paul's Final Testimony -- The Fight Finished, the Crown Awaiting

"For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing."

**"For I am already being poured out as a drink offering"** (egō gar ēdē spendomai). The conjunction gar ("for") connects Paul's testimony to the charge he has just given Timothy. The reason Timothy must fulfill his ministry is that Paul is finishing his. The pronoun egō ("I," emphatic) draws attention to Paul himself: I, for my part, am already being poured out. The shift from commanding Timothy to describing his own situation creates the letter's emotional climax.

The verb spendesthai ("to be poured out as a drink offering, to be offered as a libation") is the passive of spendein. In the LXX, the drink offering (spondē, from spendein) was a regular component of the sacrificial system. In Numbers 15:1-10, drink offerings of wine accompanied the burnt offerings and peace offerings. In Numbers 28:7, "Its drink offering shall be a quarter of a hin for each lamb. In the Holy Place you shall pour out a drink offering of strong drink to the LORD." In Exodus 29:40-41, the drink offering accompanied the daily sacrifice of lambs. The drink offering was poured out -- it was not consumed by the offerer or returned to him but was given entirely, poured onto the altar or onto the ground before the LORD. It was a total, irreversible giving.

Paul uses this specific sacrificial metaphor to describe his death. His life is the liquid being poured out; his death is the pouring. The present tense spendomai ("I am being poured out") indicates that the process is already underway -- not a future possibility but a present reality. The adverb ēdē ("already, now, at this point") confirms the immediacy. Paul is not describing a distant prospect but a current experience. He used the same verb in Philippians 2:17: "Even if I am to be poured out as a drink offering (ei kai spendomai) upon the sacrificial offering of your faith, I am glad and rejoice with you all." In Philippians, the pouring was hypothetical (ei, "if"); here in 2 Timothy, it is actual (ēdē, "already"). The hypothesis of Philippians has become the reality of 2 Timothy.

The sacrificial language reframes Paul's approaching death. He is not a victim of Roman justice; he is a priest offering the final sacrifice. His death is not a meaningless termination but a liturgical act -- the last libation poured out to God. The image dignifies the death by placing it within the framework of Israel's worship.

**"And the time of my departure has come"** (kai ho kairos tēs analseōs mou ephestēken). The noun kairos ("time, season, appointed moment") -- the same word from verse 3 for the "time" when people will not endure sound teaching -- now describes a different appointed moment: the time of Paul's departure. The noun analysis ("departure, loosing, unmooring, dissolution, release") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of ana ("up") + lysis ("loosing, releasing, untying"). The word was used for the loosing of a ship from its moorings before sailing, for the breaking of camp before a march, and for the releasing of a person from bondage. In Philippians 1:23, Paul used the related verb analysai ("to depart") when he spoke of his "desire to depart (analysai) and be with Christ, which is far better." The word does not describe death as extinction but as departure -- movement from one location to another, the untying of what holds one in place.

The verb ephistanai (ephestēken, "has come upon, has arrived, stands near") is the perfect tense: the time has arrived and stands present. This is the same verb used in verse 2 (epistēthi, "stand ready, be at hand") where Timothy was commanded to be ready. Paul's departure stands at hand; Timothy must stand at hand for his work. The parallelism is structural: Paul's readiness to depart corresponds to Timothy's readiness to preach.

**"I have fought the good fight"** (ton kalon agōna ēgōnismai). The noun agōn ("contest, struggle, fight, conflict, athletic competition") describes the entire span of Paul's apostolic career under the image of an athletic or military contest. The adjective kalos ("good, noble, beautiful, excellent") -- the same adjective used throughout the Pastoral Epistles for things worthy and honorable (the "good warfare" of 1 Timothy 1:18, the "good confession" of 1 Timothy 6:12-13, the "good deposit" of 2 Timothy 1:14, the "good soldier" of 2 Timothy 2:3) -- marks this contest as noble. The verb agōnizesthai (ēgōnismai, perfect middle/passive, "I have competed, I have fought") in the perfect tense indicates a completed action with abiding results: the fight has been fought, and the result stands. In 1 Timothy 6:12, Paul commanded Timothy: "Fight the good fight of the faith" (agōnizou ton kalon agōna tēs pisteōs). What Paul commanded, Paul has done. He offers himself as the model: I fought the fight I told you to fight.

**"I have finished the race"** (ton dromon teteleka). The noun dromos ("course, race, career, running track") describes Paul's ministry under the image of a footrace. The same word appeared in Acts 13:25, where John the Baptist said, "What do you suppose that I am? I am not he. No, but behold, after me one is coming, the sandals of whose feet I am not worthy to untie," and this is introduced with "as John was finishing his course (ton dromon)." In Acts 20:24, Paul told the Ephesian elders: "I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course (ton dromon mou) and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God." The dromos is the assigned course -- not a distance the runner chooses but a track laid out for him. Paul's commission determined his course; his faithfulness consisted in running it to the end.

The verb telein (teteleka, from telein/teleō, "to complete, to finish, to bring to an end, to accomplish") in the perfect tense indicates that the course has been completed -- Paul has crossed the finish line. The word carries the sense of fulfillment, not merely cessation. He has not quit the race; he has finished it. The same verb's cognate appears in verse 5, where Timothy was told to plērophoresōn ("fulfill") his ministry. Paul has fulfilled his; Timothy must fulfill his.

The athletic imagery connects directly to 2:5, where the athlete "is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules." Paul has competed according to the rules; therefore the crown awaits.

**"I have kept the faith"** (tēn pistin tetērēka). The verb tērein (tetērēka, "I have kept, I have guarded, I have maintained, I have observed") in the perfect tense describes the sustained guarding of something entrusted. The noun pistis ("faith, faithfulness, trust, the faith") is here best understood as "the faith" -- the body of apostolic teaching, the deposit, the content of the gospel. This is the same pistin that Timothy was told to pursue (2:22), the same faith that Hymenaeus and Philetus upset in some (2:18), the same faith that was the goal of the "sincere faith" described in 1:5. Paul has kept what he was given. He has not altered, abandoned, diluted, or corrupted the deposit. The guard duty is complete.

The three perfect tenses (ēgōnismai, teteleka, tetērēka) are cumulative: each describes a completed action with enduring results. The fight has been fought and remains won. The race has been finished and the course lies behind. The faith has been kept and stands guarded. Paul's life of service is presented as a finished work -- not a partial effort or an interrupted project but a completed performance. The three statements correspond to the three metaphors of chapter 2: the soldier (fighting), the athlete (racing), and the farmer (whose labor produces results that endure). The soldier-athlete-farmer triad finds its autobiographical fulfillment in Paul's final testimony.

**"Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness"** (loipon apokeitai moi ho tēs dikaiosynēs stephanos). The adverb loipon ("henceforth, from now on, finally, for the remaining time") marks a transition from the completed past to the anticipated future. The verb apokeisthai ("to be laid up, to be stored away, to be reserved") describes something set aside in safekeeping -- deposited and waiting for collection. In Colossians 1:5, Paul spoke of "the hope laid up (apokeimenen) for you in heaven." The crown is not uncertain; it is already stored and waiting. The certainty of the language matches the confidence of the statement "I know whom I have believed" (1:12). Paul knows his deposit is secure because he knows the one who holds it.

The noun stephanos ("crown, wreath, garland") is the victor's crown -- the wreath awarded to the winner of an athletic competition. In 2:5, the athlete "is not crowned (stephanoutai) unless he competes according to the rules." Paul has competed according to the rules; therefore the crown is his. The stephanos was not a jeweled diadem (that would be diadēma) but a wreath of leaves -- olive, laurel, celery, or pine, depending on the games. It was the visible mark of victory, worn proudly by the winner before the assembled spectators. The games of the Greek world were the most familiar competitive institution of the first-century Mediterranean, and the victor's crown was their most coveted prize.

The genitive tēs dikaiosynēs ("of righteousness") describes the crown's nature or content. The phrase could mean "the crown that consists of righteousness" (a crown whose substance is righteousness), "the crown awarded for righteousness" (righteousness as the qualifying virtue), or "the crown that belongs to the righteous" (the crown reserved for those who lived rightly). All three meanings are compatible with the context. The crown is connected to righteousness -- it is the righteous outcome that corresponds to the righteous life. The word dikaiosynē ("righteousness, justice, right standing") was one of the virtues Timothy was told to pursue (2:22: "pursue righteousness, faith, love, and peace").

**"Which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day"** (hon apodōsei moi ho kyrios en ekeinē tē hēmera, ho dikaios kritēs). The verb apodidonai ("to give back, to repay, to render, to award, to return what is owed") describes the crown as something earned and deserved -- not a gift of arbitrary generosity but a just return for faithful service. The word carries a legal or commercial sense: payment rendered for work completed, a debt discharged. In Romans 2:6, God "will render to each one according to his works" (apodōsei hekastō kata ta erga autou). The judge renders the verdict and awards the prize.

The title ho dikaios kritēs ("the righteous judge") identifies the Lord as a judge whose judgments are dikaios -- "just, righteous, fair, in accordance with what is right." The assurance is not merely that Paul will receive the crown but that the judge who awards it is righteous. The system is fair. The judge cannot be bribed, deceived, or swayed. The outcome corresponds to the reality.

The phrase en ekeinē tē hēmera ("on that day") echoes the same expression in 1:12 (Paul's confidence that God can guard his deposit "until that day") and 1:18 (the prayer that Onesiphorus would find mercy "on that day"). "That day" is the day of Christ's return and judgment -- the day when all hidden things are revealed, all faithful service is recognized, and all accounts are settled. The three occurrences of "that day" in the letter create a structural arc: Paul trusts God to guard his deposit until that day (1:12), prays for Onesiphorus to find mercy on that day (1:18), and expects to receive the crown on that day (4:8). The day is the horizon toward which the entire letter points.

**"And not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing"** (ou monon de emoi alla kai pasin tois ēgapēkosin tēn epiphaneian autou). The phrase ou monon... alla kai ("not only... but also") expands the promise beyond Paul to a universal scope. The crown is not Paul's private reward but the prize for every person who meets the stated condition. The condition is described with the perfect participle ēgapēkosin (from agapan, "to love") -- "who have loved and continue to love" -- applied to tēn epiphaneian autou ("his appearing"). The perfect tense indicates a love that began in the past and persists into the present: these are people whose love for Christ's appearing is a settled, enduring posture, not a momentary enthusiasm.

The noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation") has now appeared three times in the chapter: in verse 1 (the charge is issued "by his appearing and his kingdom"), in verse 8 (the crown awaits those who love "his appearing"), and will appear implicitly in Paul's testimony of verse 17 (where the Lord's purpose is the full proclamation of the gospel). The first-century hearer would connect the love of Christ's appearing to the life of endurance and faithfulness the letter describes: to love his appearing is to live in orientation toward it, to conduct oneself as one who expects the judge to arrive, to value his kingdom above "this present world" (4:10). Those who love the appearing are those who endure (2:12), who are not ashamed (1:8, 12, 16), who do not deny (2:12), who keep the faith (4:7). Those who do not love the appearing are those who love "this present world" (4:10) -- like Demas, whose story is about to follow.

The expansion from "me" to "all" is structurally important. Paul's experience is not unique; it is paradigmatic. What is true for the apostle is true for every believer. The fight, the race, the faith, the crown, the righteous judge, that day -- all are shared realities. Timothy, who is being charged to carry on Paul's work, stands among "all who have loved his appearing." The crown awaits him too, on the same terms.

## Verses 9-13: Personal Requests -- Desertion, Companionship, and Practical Needs

"Do your best to come to me soon. For Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me and gone to Thessalonica. Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia. Only Luke is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry. Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus. When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas, also the books, and above all the parchments."

**"Do your best to come to me soon"** (spoudason elthein pros me tacheōs). The verb spoudazein ("to be diligent, to make every effort, to hasten, to be eager") is the same word used in 2:15, where Timothy was told to "do your best (spoudason) to present yourself to God as one approved." The urgency is personal: Paul wants Timothy to come quickly (tacheōs, "quickly, soon, without delay"). The request will be repeated in verse 21 with an added condition: "before winter." The double request frames the personal section and communicates both the urgency and the reason for it -- Paul is alone, cold, and facing death.

**"For Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me"** (Dēmas gar me egkatelipen agapēsas ton nyn aiōna). The conjunction gar ("for") explains why Timothy needs to come: Paul's companions have left. Demas is named first and with the most pointed description. The verb egkataleipein ("to abandon, to forsake, to desert, to leave behind") is a strong word. In the LXX, it describes God's potential abandonment of his people -- Deuteronomy 31:6, "He will not leave you or forsake you (ou me se egkatalipē)"; Psalm 22:1 (LXX 21:2), "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me (hina ti egkatelipes me)?" -- and the corresponding human abandonment of God. The word carries the weight of covenant betrayal: Demas has not merely relocated but has forsaken Paul.

The reason is stated with devastating brevity: agapēsas ton nyn aiōna -- "having loved this present age." The participle agapēsas (from agapan, "to love") uses the same verb just employed in verse 8 for those who "have loved his appearing" (ēgapēkosin tēn epiphaneian autou). The contrast is precise and deliberate. Those who love Christ's appearing receive the crown of righteousness. Demas loved the present age instead. The two loves are mutually exclusive: one cannot simultaneously love the appearing of the judge and love the world that the judge will judge. The noun aiōn ("age, era, world-order") with nyn ("now, present") describes the current world-order as opposed to the coming age. Demas chose the age that is passing over the age that is coming. His departure to Thessalonica is the physical expression of a spiritual decision.

Demas appears in Colossians 4:14 alongside "Luke the beloved physician," and in Philemon 24 as one of Paul's "fellow workers." He was not a peripheral figure but a member of Paul's inner circle. His desertion is therefore not the departure of a casual acquaintance but the defection of a trusted co-worker. The pattern echoes 1:15, where "all who are in Asia turned away from me." The mass desertion described at the letter's beginning continues in its closing: Demas joins the long list of those who chose self-preservation over solidarity with the prisoner.

**"Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia"** (Krēskēs eporethē eis Galatian, Titos eis Dalmatian). These departures are reported without the negative language used for Demas. The verb poreuein ("to go, to travel, to journey") is neutral -- it describes travel without implying desertion. Crescens (mentioned only here in the New Testament) has gone to Galatia, and Titus (the recipient of the letter to Titus, a trusted co-worker) has gone to Dalmatia (the coastal region of the province of Illyricum, on the eastern shore of the Adriatic Sea). These may be legitimate ministry assignments rather than desertions; Paul does not accuse them as he accuses Demas. The effect, however, is the same: Paul is left with fewer and fewer companions.

**"Only Luke is with me"** (Loukas estin monos met' emou). The adverb monos ("only, alone") is placed for emphasis: Luke alone remains. Luke is identified in Colossians 4:14 as "the beloved physician" and appears as Paul's traveling companion throughout the latter portions of Acts (the "we" passages: Acts 16:10-17; 20:5-21:18; 27:1-28:16). His solitary presence at Paul's side in Rome, when all others have departed or been sent elsewhere, marks him as the one companion who did not leave.

**"Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry"** (Markon analabōn age meta seautou, estin gar moi euchrestos eis diakonian). The name Markos refers to John Mark, the figure who accompanied Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey but turned back at Pamphylia (Acts 13:13), causing a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over whether to take him on the second journey (Acts 15:37-39). The fact that Paul now wants Mark and describes him as euchrestos ("useful, serviceable, profitable") -- composed of eu ("well") + chrēstos ("usable") -- indicates a reconciliation and rehabilitation. The word euchrestos appeared in Philemon 11, where Paul described the formerly "useless" (achrestos) Onesimus as now "useful" (euchrestos). The adjective implies demonstrated competence. Mark has proven himself. The noun diakonia ("ministry, service") describes the practical gospel service for which Mark is needed.

**"Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus"** (Tychikon de apesteila eis Epheson). Tychicus was a trusted delegate. In Ephesians 6:21-22, he was described as "the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord" whom Paul sent to inform the Ephesians about his situation and "encourage your hearts." In Colossians 4:7-8, similar language describes his mission. In Titus 3:12, Paul planned to send either Artemas or Tychicus to Titus. The sending of Tychicus to Ephesus -- where Timothy is stationed -- may indicate that Tychicus is being sent as Timothy's replacement, freeing Timothy to come to Paul. Alternatively, he may be the carrier of this letter itself.

**"When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas"** (ton phailonēn hon apelipon en Trōadi para Karpō erchomenos phere). The noun phailonēs (Latin paenula, "cloak, heavy outer garment, traveling cloak") was a thick, circular garment designed for protection against cold and rain. Paul left it at Troas with a man named Carpus (mentioned only here). The request is achingly practical: the apostle who has just spoken of the crown of righteousness also needs his cloak. Winter is approaching (v. 21), and the prisoner is cold. The detail grounds the letter's lofty theology in physical reality. The man being poured out as a drink offering still has a body that feels cold.

**"Also the books, and above all the parchments"** (kai ta biblia, malista tas membranas). The noun biblion ("book, scroll, written document") in the plural (ta biblia) refers to papyrus scrolls -- likely copies of the Scriptures of Israel, the "sacred writings" of 3:15. The noun membrana (a Latin loan word, "parchment, vellum") in the plural (tas membranas) refers to sheets or codices made of animal skin, which was more durable and expensive than papyrus. The adverb malista ("especially, above all, most of all") gives the parchments priority over the books. What the parchments contained is not specified, but the possibilities include: Paul's own writings or notes, copies of his letters, legal documents relevant to his trial, or portions of Scripture in parchment codex form. The request shows a mind still active, still engaged with texts, still needing intellectual and spiritual resources even as the body approaches death. The man who commanded Timothy to "preach the word" (v. 2) practices what he commands: even in prison, he wants his books.

## Verses 14-15: Alexander the Coppersmith -- Harm and Warning

"Alexander the coppersmith did me great harm; the Lord will repay him according to his deeds. Beware of him yourself, for he strongly opposed our message."

**"Alexander the coppersmith did me great harm"** (Alexandros ho chalkeus polla moi kaka enedeixato). The noun chalkeus ("coppersmith, metalworker, worker in bronze") identifies Alexander by his trade. The name Alexander is common, but this Alexander may be the same one mentioned in 1 Timothy 1:20, who along with Hymenaeus was "handed over to Satan that they may learn not to blaspheme." If so, the pedagogical discipline of 1 Timothy 1:20 has not produced reformation in Alexander's case, and he has actively harmed Paul.

The verb endeiknysthai ("to show, to demonstrate, to display, to inflict") with kaka ("evil things, harm, bad things") and the adverb polla ("many, much, great") describes extensive, demonstrated harm -- not a single incident but a pattern of hostile action. The nature of the harm is not specified but may include testimony against Paul at his trial (connecting to the "first defense" of v. 16), active opposition to Paul's teaching, or deliberate undermining of Paul's work in the community.

**"The Lord will repay him according to his deeds"** (apodōsei autō ho kyrios kata ta erga autou). The verb apodidonai ("to repay, to render, to give back what is owed") is the same verb used in verse 8 for the Lord's awarding of the crown -- the righteous judge renders what is deserved. What the righteous judge awards to the faithful (the crown), the same righteous judge repays to the harmful (according to their deeds). The phrase kata ta erga autou ("according to his deeds") echoes the principle of Psalm 62:12 (LXX 61:13): "For you will render to each one according to his work" (sy apodōseis hekastō kata ta erga autou). In Proverbs 24:12 (LXX): "He who keeps your soul, does he not know it? And he will repay each according to his deeds." In Romans 2:6: "He will render to each one according to his works." The principle is consistent across the Scriptures: God judges according to deeds. Paul does not take vengeance himself; he commits the matter to the Lord. The language is neither vindictive nor detached -- it is an expression of trust in the righteous judge's capacity to administer justice.

**"Beware of him yourself, for he strongly opposed our message"** (hon kai sy phylassou, lian gar antestē tois hēmeterois logois). The verb phylassein ("to guard, to watch, to be on guard against, to beware") in the middle voice (phylassou) commands Timothy to protect himself from Alexander. The adverb lian ("exceedingly, greatly, very much") intensifies the opposition: Alexander's resistance was not mild disagreement but vehement hostility. The verb anthistanai (antestē, "to resist, to oppose, to stand against, to withstand") is the same verb used in 3:8 for Jannes and Jambres, who "opposed (antestēsan) Moses." The parallel is telling: Alexander's opposition to the apostolic message is compared, through verbal echo, to the Egyptian magicians' opposition to God's appointed leader. The noun logois ("words, message, teaching") with the possessive hēmeterois ("our") refers to the apostolic gospel -- "our message," the word Paul has been commanding Timothy to preach.

The warning is practical: Alexander is dangerous, he is active, and Timothy must be aware of him. The advice is not to confront Alexander but to be on guard -- the same defensive posture recommended throughout the letter for dealing with opponents who have proven resistant to correction.

## Verses 16-18: The First Defense -- Desertion by All, Rescue by the Lord

"At my first defense no one came to stand by me, but all deserted me. May it not be charged against them! But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me, so that through me the message might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear it. So I was rescued from the lion's mouth. The Lord will rescue me from every evil deed and bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom. To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen."

**"At my first defense no one came to stand by me"** (en tē prōtē mou apologia oudeis moi paregeneto). The noun apologia ("defense, speech in one's own defense, legal defense") is the technical term for a defendant's speech at a Roman trial. The adjective prōtē ("first") implies that there may be a second defense yet to come -- Roman legal procedure allowed for multiple hearings. The verb paraginesthai ("to come, to arrive, to be present, to come to the aid of") describes the failure of any supporter to appear. In Roman courts, the accused was expected to have advocates (patroni) and character witnesses (laudatores) who would speak in his favor, testify to his character, and demonstrate that he had social standing and support. The absence of any supporter (oudeis, "no one") was both legally and socially devastating. It meant Paul faced the Roman court entirely alone, without the network of relationships that constituted a person's social identity in the ancient world.

**"But all deserted me"** (alla pantes me egkatelipon). The verb egkataleipein ("to abandon, to forsake, to desert") is the same word used for Demas in verse 10. The universal scope -- pantes ("all") -- echoes the "all who are in Asia turned away from me" of 1:15. The theme of mass desertion forms an inclusio that brackets the entire letter: it opens with everyone in Asia turning away (1:15) and closes with everyone at the trial deserting (4:16). Paul's isolation is total.

**"May it not be charged against them!"** (mē autois logistheiē). The verb logizesthai ("to reckon, to charge, to account, to count") in the optative (logistheiē) expresses a prayer or wish: may the desertion not be reckoned to their account, may it not be counted against them at the judgment. The negative mē ("not") with the optative creates a gentle, prayerful negative wish. The prayer echoes Stephen's dying words in Acts 7:60: "Lord, do not hold this sin against them" (kyrie, mē stēsēs autois tautēn tēn hamartian). It also echoes Jesus' prayer from the cross in Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." Paul, approaching his own death, follows the pattern of both Jesus and Stephen: he prays for mercy on those who failed him.

The prayer is remarkable given the letter's stark treatment of desertion elsewhere. Demas is described without mercy: he "loved this present world" and deserted (v. 10). The faithful saying warned that those who deny Christ will be denied (2:12). Yet here Paul prays for those who deserted him at his trial. The distinction may be between deserting Paul (which is understandable human weakness under pressure) and denying Christ (which is a fundamental repudiation of allegiance). Paul himself drew this distinction in 1:15-16: those who turned away from Paul were described without invocation of divine wrath, while Onesiphorus was commended for not being ashamed. The desertion at the trial is a failure of courage, not necessarily a denial of Christ, and Paul's prayer asks that the failure not be held against them.

**"But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me"** (ho de kyrios moi parestē kai enedynamōsen me). The adversative de ("but") introduces the contrast: no human stood by Paul, but the Lord did. The verb paristanai (parestē, "to stand beside, to come to one's aid, to be present") is the same verb that no human performed (paregeneto, v. 16) -- but with the Lord as its subject. Where every person was absent, the Lord was present. The Lord did what the advocates and witnesses failed to do: he stood at Paul's side.

The verb endynamoun (enedynamōsen, "to strengthen, to empower, to fill with power") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:12, where Paul thanked Christ "who has given me strength" (tō endynamōsanti me Christō Iēsou), and in 2:1, where Timothy was told to "be strengthened in the grace that is in Christ Jesus" (endynamou en tē chariti). The verb runs from the opening of 1 Timothy to the closing of 2 Timothy, creating a thread of divine empowerment that spans the entire correspondence. The strength that Paul received at the beginning of his ministry (1 Timothy 1:12) is the same strength that sustained him at his trial (2 Timothy 4:17). The resource has not changed; the need has not diminished; the Lord who strengthens has not wavered.

**"So that through me the message might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear it"** (hina di' emou to kērygma plērophorēthē kai akousōsin panta ta ethnē). The hina clause ("in order that, so that") states the purpose of the Lord's strengthening: not merely Paul's personal survival but the completion of his preaching mission. The noun kērygma ("proclamation, preaching, the thing preached, the message") is the content proclaimed by the keryx ("herald") -- the same word family used in verse 2 (kēryxon, "preach") and 1:11 (kēryx, "preacher"). The verb plērophorein (plērophorēthē, "to be fulfilled completely, to be carried out fully") is the same verb used in verse 5, where Timothy was told to "fulfill" (plērophoresōn) his ministry. Paul's preaching was fulfilled -- brought to completion, carried to its full measure -- through the Lord's strengthening at the trial. The trial itself became an occasion for proclamation.

The phrase panta ta ethnē ("all the Gentiles, all the nations") identifies the audience. Paul's commission was specifically to the nations -- "I was appointed a preacher and apostle and teacher" (1:11); his gospel was for all peoples. The trial before the Roman court, attended by representatives of the empire's reach, became the venue through which "all the Gentiles" heard the gospel -- not literally every individual Gentile, but the fullness of the Gentile mission that Paul's apostleship was commissioned to accomplish.

**"So I was rescued from the lion's mouth"** (kai errysthēn ek stomatos leontos). The verb rryesthai (errysthēn, "I was rescued, I was delivered") describes divine deliverance from mortal danger. In 3:11, Paul reminded Timothy: "the Lord rescued me from them all" (ek pantōn me errysato ho kyrios), referring to the persecutions at Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra. The pattern of rescue is established across Paul's career: danger followed by divine deliverance.

The phrase ek stomatos leontos ("from the lion's mouth") draws on deep Scriptural imagery. In Psalm 22:21 (LXX 21:22): "Save me from the mouth of the lion" (sōson me ek stomatos leontos). The Psalm of the suffering righteous one -- which Jesus himself quoted from the cross (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22:1) -- contains the cry for deliverance from the lion's mouth. David's own testimony included rescue from lions (1 Samuel 17:34-37). Daniel was delivered from the lion's den (Daniel 6:22). The image of the lion as a figure for mortal danger, especially the danger of hostile power, is pervasive in Israel's literature. In the present context, the "lion's mouth" most naturally refers to the Roman court and its power to condemn Paul to death. Paul was rescued from that immediate threat -- his first defense did not result in execution.

**"The Lord will rescue me from every evil deed"** (rhysetai me ho kyrios apo pantos ergou ponērou). The verb rryesthai (rhysetai, future middle, "he will rescue") now shifts from the past (errysthēn, "I was rescued") to the future: the Lord will rescue. The shift from aorist to future is significant. The past rescue from the lion's mouth was a specific, historical deliverance. The future rescue is comprehensive: apo pantos ergou ponērou ("from every evil deed, from every wicked work"). The adjective ponēros ("evil, wicked, bad, harmful") describes works that are actively malicious. The Lord's rescue extends beyond the immediate danger to encompass every evil work that might threaten Paul.

This future rescue must be understood in light of verse 6: Paul knows he is about to die ("I am already being poured out as a drink offering"). The rescue Paul anticipates is not rescue from death but rescue through death -- deliverance not from physical danger but from evil itself. The Lord's rescue operates at a level deeper than physical survival. Paul may die, but the evil deeds of his opponents will not ultimately prevail. The Lord's faithfulness transcends the outcome of any trial.

**"And bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom"** (kai sōsei eis tēn basileian autou tēn epouranion). The verb sōzein (sōsei, "he will save, he will bring safely, he will preserve") connects to the letter's opening, where God "saved us and called us to a holy calling" (1:9). The saving that began before the ages (1:9) will be consummated in the heavenly kingdom. The noun basileia ("kingdom") appeared in verse 1 as part of the charge: the charge was issued "by his appearing and his kingdom." The adjective epouranios ("heavenly, belonging to the heavens, celestial") locates this kingdom above -- not the earthly kingdoms that imprison Paul but the heavenly kingdom that awaits him. The possessive autou ("his") identifies it as Christ's kingdom, the kingdom into which those who endure will be brought to reign (2:12).

The movement from "rescue from every evil deed" to "bring safely into his heavenly kingdom" describes Paul's trajectory: through death, past evil, into glory. The death that approaches is not a defeat but a passage -- from the present evil age (which Demas loved) to the heavenly kingdom (which Paul anticipates). The language fulfills the promise of the faithful saying: "if we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him" (2:11-12). Paul has endured; he will reign.

**"To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen."** (hō hē doxa eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn, amēn). The doxology -- an ascription of glory to God -- closes the theological section of the letter. The relative pronoun hō ("to him") refers to the Lord who stood by Paul, strengthened him, and will bring him safely into the heavenly kingdom. The noun doxa ("glory, splendor, honor, magnificence") ascribes supreme honor. The phrase eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn ("unto the ages of the ages, forever and ever") is the strongest temporal expression available: ages upon ages, without end. The Hebrew-derived amēn ("truly, so be it, let it be confirmed") seals the doxology as a communal affirmation.

The doxology is not decorative. It is the theological conclusion to Paul's final testimony. Having described his approaching death, his completed race, his awaiting crown, and the Lord's rescue, Paul directs all the resulting glory away from himself and toward the Lord. The glory belongs to God, not to Paul. The faith has been kept, but the keeper does not claim the honor; he ascribes it to the one who kept him.

## Verses 19-22: Final Greetings, Last Instructions, and Closing

"Greet Prisca and Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus. Erastus remained at Corinth, and I left Trophimus, who was ill, at Miletus. Do your best to come before winter. Eubulus sends greetings to you, as do Pudens and Linus and Claudia and all the brothers. The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you."

**"Greet Prisca and Aquila"** (aspasai Priskan kai Akylan). Prisca (also called Priscilla) and Aquila are well known from the narrative of Acts and from Paul's other letters. In Acts 18:2-3, Paul met them in Corinth; they were Jewish tentmakers who had been expelled from Rome by Claudius's edict. In Acts 18:18-19, they accompanied Paul to Ephesus. In Acts 18:26, they instructed Apollos "more accurately" in the Way of God. In Romans 16:3-5, Paul greeted them as "my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their necks for my life." In 1 Corinthians 16:19, they sent greetings from Asia along with "the church in their house." Their presence in Ephesus (if the greeting locates them there, which is likely since Timothy is in Ephesus) demonstrates their continued ministry in the region. Paul names Prisca first, as in Romans 16:3 -- a detail that suggests her prominence in the partnership.

**"And the household of Onesiphorus"** (kai ton Onēsiphorou oikon). Onesiphorus, whose faithfulness was celebrated in 1:16-18, is greeted here through his household (oikos). As noted in the analysis of 1:16-18, the greeting of the household rather than Onesiphorus personally may indicate that Onesiphorus is absent -- still traveling, still in Rome with Paul, or possibly deceased. The text does not resolve the question, but the pattern of greeting "the household of Onesiphorus" rather than "Onesiphorus" is consistent with his absence from the household.

**"Erastus remained at Corinth"** (Erastos emeinen en Korinthō). Erastus is likely the same person mentioned in Acts 19:22, where Paul "sent into Macedonia two of those who ministered to him, Timothy and Erastus." In Romans 16:23, "Erastus, the city treasurer (oikonomos tēs poleōs), greets you." An inscription found at Corinth reads "Erastus, in return for his aedileship, laid this pavement at his own expense" -- possibly the same person. The verb menein (emeinen, "remained, stayed") is neutral: Erastus stayed at Corinth, where he apparently had responsibilities.

**"And I left Trophimus, who was ill, at Miletus"** (Trophimon de apelipon en Milētō asthenonta). Trophimus was an Ephesian (Acts 21:29) and a companion of Paul (Acts 20:4). The participle asthenonta (from asthenein, "to be weak, to be ill, to be sick") explains why Trophimus was left behind: he was too ill to travel. The verb apoleipein (apelipon, "I left, I left behind") indicates Paul's decision to continue without him. The detail is striking in its ordinariness: a man who traveled with an apostle endowed with the gift of healing (Acts 19:11-12) was left behind sick. The text does not comment on why Trophimus was not healed; it simply reports the fact. Miletus was a port city about 30 miles south of Ephesus -- the same city where Paul delivered his farewell address to the Ephesian elders (Acts 20:17-38).

**"Do your best to come before winter"** (spoudason pro cheimōnos elthein). The verb spoudazein ("to hasten, to be diligent") repeats the urgency of verse 9. The new element is the deadline: pro cheimōnos ("before winter"). The reason is practical. Sea travel in the Mediterranean essentially ceased during winter. The sailing season typically ended by mid-November, and the sea was considered closed until at least February or March. If Timothy does not come before winter, he will not be able to come until spring -- and Paul may not survive until spring. The request for the cloak (v. 13) also reflects the approaching cold. The deadline sharpens every instruction in the letter: there is limited time, and the window is closing.

**"Eubulus sends greetings to you, as do Pudens and Linus and Claudia and all the brothers"** (aspazetai se Euboulos kai Poudēs kai Linos kai Klaudia kai hoi adelphoi pantes). Four individuals and "all the brothers" send greetings to Timothy from Rome. Eubulus, Pudens, and Claudia appear nowhere else in the New Testament. Linus is identified by early church tradition (Irenaeus, Adversus Haereses 3.3.3) as a subsequent leader of the Roman community, though the text itself makes no such claim. The presence of "all the brothers" (hoi adelphoi pantes) indicates that Paul is not entirely without a community in Rome -- despite the statement that "only Luke is with me" (v. 11), there are believers in the city who know Timothy and send greetings. The apparent tension between "only Luke is with me" and the greetings from multiple people may be resolved by understanding "only Luke is with me" to refer to Paul's regular companions and co-workers, while the greeters are members of the Roman community who are not Paul's personal attendants.

**"The Lord be with your spirit"** (ho kyrios meta tou pneumatos sou). The closing benediction is addressed to Timothy personally. The noun pneuma ("spirit") with the possessive sou ("your") refers to Timothy's inner person, his spirit. The prayer is that the Lord (ho kyrios) would be present with Timothy's spirit -- accompanying, sustaining, and guiding him from within. The word pneuma here corresponds to the "spirit not of cowardice but of power and love and self-discipline" that God gave (1:7). The Lord's presence with Timothy's spirit is the resource that makes everything else possible: the preaching, the suffering, the faithfulness, the completion of ministry.

**"Grace be with you"** (hē charis meth' hymōn). The final word is charis -- "grace," the free, unmerited favor of God. The pronoun hymōn ("you," plural) extends the benediction beyond Timothy to the community that will hear the letter read. The plural ending suggests that while the letter is addressed to Timothy, it was intended for a wider audience. The word charis frames the entire letter: it appeared in the greeting (1:2, "grace, mercy, and peace") and now closes the letter. Grace is the first word of God's engagement with his people and the last. The letter that began with the "promise of life that is in Christ Jesus" (1:1) ends with grace. The man who is about to be poured out as a drink offering commits his beloved child and the community to the grace of God.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

2 Timothy 4 is the culmination of Paul's final letter -- the chapter where the testamentary exhortation reaches its climax and the dying father delivers his last words to the son who must carry on alone.

(1) **Verses 1-2** issue the letter's most solemn charge. Under the witness of God and Christ Jesus -- the one who will judge the living and the dead -- and in view of Christ's appearing and kingdom, Timothy is commanded to preach the word. The charge encompasses the full range of pastoral speech: reproof, rebuke, exhortation, all conducted with complete patience and teaching. The charge gathers up every instruction in the letter and distills it into a single imperative: preach.

(2) **Verses 3-4** provide the reason for the charge's urgency. A time is coming -- and has already begun -- when people will not tolerate sound teaching. They will accumulate teachers who scratch their itching ears, they will turn from the truth, and they will wander into myths. The progressive deterioration from rejection of health to embrace of falsehood describes the trajectory the letter has been tracking since the first mention of false teaching in 1 Timothy.

(3) **Verse 5** counters with four commands directed specifically at Timothy: be sober-minded, endure suffering, do the work of an evangelist, fulfill your ministry. These commands correspond inversely to the four stages of deterioration in verses 3-4 and summarize the letter's entire vision of faithful service.

(4) **Verses 6-8** contain Paul's final testimony -- the emotional and theological climax of the letter. Paul describes his approaching death as a sacrificial offering (the drink offering being poured out) and a departure (the ship being untied from its moorings). Three perfect-tense verbs summarize his completed life: he fought the good fight, finished the race, kept the faith. The crown of righteousness awaits -- awarded by the righteous judge on that day, not to Paul alone but to all who have loved Christ's appearing. The three completed actions correspond to the soldier, athlete, and farmer metaphors of chapter 2, now autobiographically fulfilled.

(5) **Verses 9-13** turn to personal requests that ground the letter's theology in physical reality. Paul wants Timothy to come quickly because Demas has deserted, having loved this present world (the direct antithesis of loving Christ's appearing). Others have departed on legitimate missions. Only Luke remains. Paul wants Mark brought, Tychicus has been sent to Ephesus, and Paul needs his cloak, books, and parchments. The apostle who speaks of crowns and kingdoms also needs a coat for winter.

(6) **Verses 14-15** warn about Alexander the coppersmith, whose great harm to Paul will be repaid by the Lord according to his deeds. Timothy is warned to be on guard, because Alexander fiercely opposed the apostolic message.

(7) **Verses 16-18** describe Paul's first defense in court, where no one stood with him and all deserted -- but the Lord stood by him and strengthened him, so that the gospel might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear it. Paul was rescued from the lion's mouth. He expresses confidence that the Lord will rescue him from every evil deed and bring him safely into the heavenly kingdom. The prayer that the deserters not be charged echoes Jesus' prayer from the cross and Stephen's dying prayer. The doxology -- "To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen" -- seals the testimony.

(8) **Verses 19-22** close the letter with greetings, final travel notes, and the benediction. The greeting of Onesiphorus's household recalls the faithful man of 1:16-18. The request to come before winter adds the last note of urgency. The closing benedictions -- "The Lord be with your spirit" and "Grace be with you" -- commit Timothy and the community to the Lord's presence and God's grace.

The chapter's controlling structure is the contrast between two orientations: love of the present age versus love of Christ's appearing. Demas loved the present age and deserted (v. 10). Paul and all the faithful love Christ's appearing and receive the crown (v. 8). The people who reject sound teaching choose teachers who suit their present passions (vv. 3-4). The Lord's servant preaches the word that prepares people for the coming judgment (vv. 1-2). Everything in the chapter -- the charge, the warning, the testimony, the personal details, the benediction -- is oriented toward "that day" (v. 8), the day when the righteous judge renders his verdict. The letter that began with "the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus" (1:1) ends with the confidence that the Lord "will bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom" (v. 18). The life promised at the beginning is the kingdom entered at the end.

The chapter also completes the letter's pattern of exemplary pairs -- faithfulness contrasted with desertion. Chapter 1 presented the desertion of all in Asia versus the faithfulness of Onesiphorus. Chapter 4 presents the desertion of Demas and all at the trial versus the faithfulness of the Lord who stood by Paul. The human examples fail progressively: in chapter 1, one man was faithful; in chapter 4, only the Lord himself stands by Paul. The trajectory of human desertion drives the reader toward the only reliable constant: the Lord who "remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself" (2:13). At the end of the letter, when every human support has been stripped away, the Lord alone stands -- and that is sufficient.

Paul's final testimony -- "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" -- is offered not as self-congratulation but as a model. Timothy is to fight the same fight (1 Timothy 6:12), run the same race (cf. the athlete of 2:5), and keep the same faith. The crown awaits Timothy on the same terms it awaits Paul: faithful endurance, legitimate competition, love of the appearing. The letter's last word to Timothy is not an instruction but a benediction: "The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you." The Lord who stood by Paul in the courtroom will stand by Timothy in the years ahead. The grace that sustained Paul through chains, desertion, and death will sustain Timothy through the ministry that lies before him. The deposit has been passed. The chain of transmission -- Paul to Timothy to faithful people to others (2:2) -- now depends on the next link.
