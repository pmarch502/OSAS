# Acts 1 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 24):** The final chapter of the author's first volume narrated the discovery of the empty tomb, the angelic command to remember Jesus's predictions, the Emmaus road encounter where Jesus opened the scriptures and was recognized in the breaking of bread, his bodily appearance to the gathered community in Jerusalem, and his commissioning of the disciples as witnesses. Three "openings" structured the chapter: Jesus opened the scriptures (24:32), their eyes were opened at the breaking of bread (24:31), and he opened their minds to understand (24:45). The commission was specific: repentance for forgiveness of sins proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem (24:47). But the commission came with a condition: "stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high" (24:49). The Gospel closed with Jesus blessing the disciples as he was carried up into heaven, and the community returning to the temple in Jerusalem with great joy, continually praising God (24:50-53). The first volume ended with a community that had seen the risen Lord, received a commission, been given a promise -- but had not yet received the promised power. They are waiting.

**What the author argues immediately after (Acts 2):** The day of Pentecost arrives and the promised Holy Spirit comes upon the gathered community with the sound of a rushing wind and visible tongues of fire (2:1-4). They speak in other languages, which the multinational crowd in Jerusalem hears and understands. Peter stands and delivers the first public proclamation of the gospel, interpreting the event through Joel 2:28-32 and the psalms of David (Psalm 16, Psalm 110), declaring that the crucified Jesus has been made "both Lord and Christ" (2:36). The crowd is cut to the heart and asks "What shall we do?" (2:37). Peter's answer is the content of the commission from Luke 24:47 now enacted: "Repent, and each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (2:38). Three thousand are added that day, and the community is described as devoted to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer (2:42). Everything Acts 1 sets up -- the waiting, the restored apostolic number, the geographical starting point of Jerusalem -- bears fruit in Acts 2.

**How chapter 1 connects these:** Acts 1 is the hinge between promise and fulfillment. Luke 24 ended with a commission given and power promised; Acts 2 opens with power arriving and the commission being carried out. Acts 1 occupies the space between. It recapitulates the end of the Gospel (vv. 1-5), narrates the ascension with greater specificity (vv. 9-11), defines the scope of the coming mission (v. 8), establishes the community's posture of unified prayer during the waiting period (vv. 12-14), and resolves the brokenness in the apostolic body caused by Judas's defection (vv. 15-26). Every element in this chapter is preparatory: the author is showing how the community was made ready to receive what was promised. The twelve must be complete before the Spirit falls. The community must be gathered and unified before power comes. The geographical program -- Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the end of the earth -- must be articulated before the narrative begins to trace its fulfillment. Acts 1 is organized around the question: what had to happen between the resurrection and Pentecost so that the community would be ready?

## Verses 1-2: The Prologue and the Link to the First Volume

"The first account I composed, Theophilus, about all that Jesus began to do and teach, until the day when He was taken up to heaven, after He had by the Holy Spirit given orders to the apostles whom He had chosen" (1:1-2).

The opening identifies this as the second of two volumes addressed to the same recipient. "Theophilus" (Theophile) was also the addressee of the Gospel (Luke 1:3), where the author described his purpose as writing "an orderly account" (kathexes) so that Theophilus would "know the exact truth about the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:4). The name Theophilus means "friend of God" or "one who loves God." Whether this is a specific individual or a literary designation for the God-fearing reader is uncertain, but the author writes as if addressing someone particular -- "most excellent Theophilus" (kratiste Theophile) in Luke 1:3, though the honorific is dropped here in Acts 1:1.

"The first account" (ton proton logon) refers to the Gospel of Luke. The word logos here means a written composition, a treatise. The phrase establishes literary continuity: what follows is part two of a single work.

The summary of the first volume's content is revealing: "all that Jesus began to do and teach" (panton hon erxato ho Iesous poiein te kai didaskein). The word erxato ("began") is theologically loaded. The author does not say "all that Jesus did and taught" but "all that Jesus began to do and teach." The implication is that the ministry narrated in the Gospel was a beginning, not a completion. What Jesus began in person during his earthly ministry, he will continue through his apostles and through the Holy Spirit in this second volume. The doing and teaching do not stop at the ascension; they continue through the community. Acts, in this framing, is the account of what Jesus continued to do and teach through his witnesses.

"Until the day when He was taken up to heaven" (achri hes hemeras ... anelempthe) -- the ascension is the terminal point of the first volume and the starting point of the second. The verb analambano ("to take up") is used for the ascension throughout Acts (1:2, 11, 22) and echoes the LXX language for Elijah's ascent: "Elijah went up by a whirlwind to heaven" (2 Kings 2:11, LXX: anelempthe Elias). The parallel is significant: as Elijah was taken up and his spirit rested on Elisha in double portion (2 Kings 2:9-15), so Jesus is taken up and his Spirit will come upon his followers. The Elijah-Elisha succession pattern underlies the transition from Gospel to Acts.

"After He had by the Holy Spirit given orders to the apostles whom He had chosen" (enteilamenos tois apostolois dia pneumatos hagiou hous exelexato). The orders (enteilamenos -- "having commanded") were those given in Luke 24:47-49: proclaim repentance and forgiveness to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem, and wait for the promise of the Father. The phrase "by the Holy Spirit" (dia pneumatos hagiou) may modify either the commanding (Jesus gave orders through the Holy Spirit) or the choosing (the apostles chosen through the Holy Spirit). In either case, the Holy Spirit is already operative in Jesus's actions before Pentecost. The Spirit's role in Acts does not begin at Pentecost; it begins with Jesus himself, who was conceived by the Spirit (Luke 1:35), baptized with the Spirit (Luke 3:22), led by the Spirit (Luke 4:1), empowered by the Spirit (Luke 4:14, 18), and now commands through the Spirit.

The term "apostles" (apostolois) designates those Jesus chose and commissioned. Luke 6:13 narrates the specific moment: "He chose twelve of them, whom He also named as apostles." The word apostolos means "one sent," deriving from apostello ("to send"). They are defined by their commissioning -- they are the ones Jesus selected and sent. This definition will matter when the chapter addresses the need to replace Judas.

## Verses 3-5: The Forty Days and the Promise of the Spirit

"To these He also presented Himself alive after His suffering, by many convincing proofs, appearing to them over a period of forty days and speaking of the things concerning the kingdom of God" (1:3).

"To these" (hois kai) -- the apostles just mentioned. Jesus "presented Himself alive" (parestesen heauton zonta) -- the verb paristemi means to present, to place alongside, to make available for inspection. The same word appears in the LXX for presenting offerings before God (Leviticus 16:7, Numbers 3:6) and in Acts 9:41 when Peter "presented her alive" after raising Tabitha. It connotes a deliberate, purposeful act of making oneself visible and examinable. Jesus did not merely appear; he presented himself -- he made his living reality available for verification.

"After His suffering" (meta to pathein auton) -- the word pathein ("suffering") encompasses everything narrated in Luke 22-23: the agony in the garden, the arrest, the trials before the Sanhedrin and Pilate and Herod, the scourging, the crucifixion, the death. The author uses the same word Jesus used in Luke 24:26: "Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer (pathein) these things and to enter into His glory?" The suffering is treated as a single, unified event that the resurrection follows.

"By many convincing proofs" (en pollois tekmeriois) -- the word tekmerion is the strongest Greek word for evidence. It appears only here in the New Testament. In Aristotle's Rhetoric (1.2.16), a tekmerion is a sign that constitutes irrefutable proof, as distinguished from a semeion, which is merely indicative. The author is making a claim about the quality of the evidence: what the apostles received was not ambiguous signs but decisive, conclusive demonstrations. The plural "many" (pollois) indicates multiple instances across the forty-day period. Luke 24 narrated some of these: the display of hands and feet, the invitation to touch, the eating of fish. The "many convincing proofs" over forty days encompasses additional encounters that the author does not narrate but attests to.

"Appearing to them over a period of forty days" (di hemeron tesserakonta optanomenos autois) -- the number forty is dense with scriptural resonance. Moses was on Sinai for forty days receiving the Torah (Exodus 24:18, 34:28). Israel wandered in the wilderness for forty years (Numbers 14:33-34, Deuteronomy 8:2). Elijah traveled forty days and nights to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). Jesus fasted in the wilderness for forty days before beginning his ministry (Luke 4:2). In each case, the forty-day (or forty-year) period is a time of preparation, instruction, and formation before a decisive new phase of God's action. The forty days between resurrection and ascension follow this pattern: it is the period of preparation during which the risen Jesus teaches his apostles before the new phase of the Spirit's work begins.

The verb optanomenos ("appearing") is the present middle participle, indicating repeated, ongoing appearances -- not a single event but a series of encounters spread across the period. This is consistent with Paul's summary in 1 Corinthians 15:5-8, which lists multiple appearances to different individuals and groups.

"Speaking of the things concerning the kingdom of God" (legon ta peri tes basileias tou theou) -- the kingdom of God (basileia tou theou) has been the central subject of Jesus's teaching throughout the Gospel. His ministry opened with the proclamation of the kingdom (Luke 4:43: "I must preach the kingdom of God to the other cities also, for I was sent for this purpose"). He sent the twelve to "proclaim the kingdom of God" (Luke 9:2). He sent the seventy-two with the message "the kingdom of God has come near to you" (Luke 10:9, 11). The parables are about the kingdom (Luke 8:10, 13:18-21). Now, after the resurrection, the subject has not changed. The risen Jesus continues to teach about the kingdom. The resurrection has not replaced the kingdom message; it has validated and deepened it. The kingdom that Jesus proclaimed during his ministry is the same kingdom he teaches about after his resurrection.

"Gathering them together, He commanded them not to leave Jerusalem, but to wait for what the Father had promised, 'Which,' He said, 'you heard of from Me; for John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days from now'" (1:4-5).

"Gathering them together" (synalizomenos) -- the word can mean "eating together with them" (from syn + hals, "salt") or "assembling together" (from syn + halizo, "to gather"). The ambiguity may be intentional, as both senses fit the context: the risen Jesus both eats with and gathers his community. The shared meal continues to be central to the risen Jesus's interaction with his followers, as at Emmaus (Luke 24:30) and in the upper room (Luke 24:41-43).

The command not to leave Jerusalem (me chorizesthai apo Ierosolymon) restates Luke 24:49: "stay in the city." Jerusalem is the required location for what is about to happen. The prophetic expectations converge on Jerusalem: Joel 2:32 places the outpouring of the Spirit in the context of calling on the name of the Lord "in Jerusalem" (LXX). Isaiah 2:3 envisions the word of the Lord going forth "from Jerusalem." Zechariah 14:8 describes living waters flowing "from Jerusalem." The city from which the gospel must go out (Luke 24:47: "beginning from Jerusalem") is the city in which the power must first arrive.

"What the Father had promised" (ten epangelian tou patros) is identified: the baptism with the Holy Spirit. The phrase "you heard of from Me" (en ekousate mou) points back to Luke 24:49: "I am sending forth the promise of My Father upon you." The promise is not new; it is being reiterated and specified.

"John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit" (1:5) -- this contrast was established at the very beginning of the Gospel. John himself declared it: "I baptize you with water; but One is coming who is mightier than I... He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Luke 3:16). What John prophesied about the coming one is now about to happen. The entire narrative arc from Luke 3 to Acts 2 can be read as the fulfillment of John's prediction. The water baptism was preparatory; the Spirit baptism is the reality to which it pointed. The phrase "not many days from now" (ou meta pollas tautas hemeras) creates urgency and anticipation without specifying the exact day. The fulfillment comes at Pentecost, ten days after the ascension (the forty days of appearances, plus ten days of waiting, equals the fifty days between Passover and Pentecost).

## Verses 6-8: The Question About Israel's Kingdom and Jesus's Redirection

"So when they had come together, they were asking Him, saying, 'Lord, is it at this time You are restoring the kingdom to Israel?'" (1:6).

The question reveals what the disciples are thinking. They have heard about the kingdom of God (v. 3). They have been promised the Holy Spirit (vv. 4-5). They know from the prophets that the Spirit's outpouring is associated with Israel's restoration. They draw what seems to them a logical conclusion: the Spirit is coming; therefore the national restoration must be imminent.

The verb apokathistaneis ("are You restoring") is significant. The root apokathistemi means "to restore to a former condition," "to reconstitute." In the LXX, the word cluster appears in contexts of national restoration. Jeremiah 16:15: God will bring Israel back to the land. Jeremiah 24:6: "I will build them up and not tear them down; I will plant them and not uproot them." Ezekiel 16:55: "Your sisters, Sodom with her daughters and Samaria with her daughters, will return to their former state, and you with your daughters will also return to your former state." The prophetic hope of restoration -- return from exile, reconstitution of the nation, renewal of God's dwelling among his people -- is what the disciples have in view.

The phrase "the kingdom to Israel" (ten basileian to Israel) specifies the content of their expectation: a restored Israelite kingdom. The prophetic texts supported this hope. Amos 9:11: "In that day I will raise up the fallen booth of David, and wall up its breaches; I will also raise up its ruins and rebuild it as in the days of old" (which James will cite in Acts 15:16-17). Isaiah 9:6-7: the child on David's throne with an everlasting government. Ezekiel 37:21-28: the reunification of Israel and Judah under one king, one shepherd, with God's sanctuary in their midst forever. The question is not foolish or unfounded; it is rooted in the prophetic testimony.

Jesus does not rebuke the question or deny that restoration will occur. He redirects from timing to mission.

"He said to them, 'It is not for you to know times or epochs which the Father has fixed by His own authority'" (1:7).

"Times or epochs" (chronous e kairous) -- chronos refers to duration, the passage of time; kairos refers to a specific, decisive moment, an appointed time. The distinction, while not absolute, suggests that the Father controls both the general timeline and the specific moments of fulfillment. The phrase "which the Father has fixed by His own authority" (hous ho pater etheto en te idia exousia) places the timing entirely within the Father's sovereign decision. The verb etheto ("placed, fixed, determined") is aorist, indicating a completed act -- the Father has already determined the times and seasons. The phrase "by His own authority" (en te idia exousia) underscores exclusive divine prerogative. This is not knowledge withheld arbitrarily; it is knowledge that belongs to the Father's domain.

This aligns with Jesus's teaching in Luke 21:24 about "the times of the Gentiles" (kairoi ethnon) that must be fulfilled before Jerusalem's restoration, and with Luke 17:20-21, where he told the Pharisees "the kingdom of God is not coming with signs to be observed." The consistent teaching across both volumes is that the Father's timing is not subject to human prediction or demand.

"But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you shall be My witnesses both in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8).

This verse is the programmatic statement for the entire book of Acts. It has three elements.

First, the source of power: "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you" (lempsesthe dynamin epelthontos tou hagiou pneumatos eph hymas). The word dynamis ("power") echoes Luke 24:49: "clothed with power from on high." The power is not abstract; it comes through the arrival (epelthontos -- "having come upon") of the Holy Spirit. The verb eperchomai is used in the LXX and in Luke for the Spirit's coming upon individuals: the angel told Mary, "The Holy Spirit will come upon you" (Luke 1:35, epeleusetai epi se). The same language now describes what will happen to the apostles. What happened to Mary at the conception -- the Spirit coming upon her -- will happen to the community at Pentecost.

Second, the identity they will carry: "you shall be My witnesses" (esesthe mou martyres). The possessive "My" (mou) is emphatic: they are witnesses belonging to Jesus, testifying about Jesus. The word martyres ("witnesses") was defined in Luke 24:48: "You are witnesses of these things" -- the suffering, resurrection, and scriptural fulfillment. The witness role is not self-generated; it is assigned. They do not choose to be witnesses; they are made witnesses by what they have seen and by the power they will receive.

Third, the geographical scope: "both in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (en te Ierousalem kai en pase te Ioudaia kai Samareia kai heos eschatou tes ges). This sentence provides the structural outline of Acts:
- Jerusalem: Acts 1-7
- Judea and Samaria: Acts 8-12
- The end of the earth: Acts 13-28

The phrase "to the end of the earth" (heos eschatou tes ges) is drawn from Isaiah 49:6: "I will also make You a light of the nations so that My salvation may reach to the end of the earth." This is the Servant Song that describes God's servant as having a mission that extends beyond Israel to encompass all peoples. Simeon quoted this text over the infant Jesus in the temple: "a light of revelation to the Gentiles, and the glory of Your people Israel" (Luke 2:32). Paul and Barnabas will explicitly cite Isaiah 49:6 in Acts 13:47 as the scriptural basis for turning to the Gentiles. The phrase "end of the earth" in a first-century context would evoke the farthest reaches of the known world -- and for a Roman audience, Rome itself sits at the practical boundary of the Mediterranean world. Acts will end with Paul in Rome (Acts 28:14-31), preaching the kingdom of God. The geographical program of 1:8 is fulfilled by the narrative trajectory of the entire book.

The relationship between the disciples' question and Jesus's answer is not contradiction but reframing. They ask about the restoration of the kingdom to Israel. Jesus does not say no; he says the timing is the Father's business, and in the meantime, their task is to be his witnesses to the end of the earth. The mission to the nations and the restoration of Israel are not opposed in the prophetic vision -- Isaiah 49:6 holds them together. The servant's mission to be a light to the nations is precisely how Israel's glory is realized. What the disciples imagine as a political event in a single moment, the author of Acts will narrate as a Spirit-driven mission unfolding across decades and continents.

## Verses 9-11: The Ascension

"And after He had said these things, He was lifted up while they were looking on, and a cloud received Him out of their sight" (1:9).

The ascension is narrated here with details that the Gospel's account (Luke 24:51) compressed. The verb eparthe ("He was lifted up") is passive -- as in Luke 24:51, Jesus does not ascend under his own power; he is lifted. The action is God's. The phrase "while they were looking on" (bleponten auton) emphasizes that this is an observed event, not an inferred one. The apostles are eyewitnesses of the ascension just as they are eyewitnesses of the resurrection.

"A cloud received Him out of their sight" (nephele hypelaben auton apo ton ophthalmon auton). The cloud is not merely atmospheric; it is the cloud of divine presence that pervades Israel's history. The cloud led Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21-22). The cloud covered Sinai when God descended (Exodus 19:16, 24:15-16). The cloud filled the tabernacle so that Moses could not enter (Exodus 40:34-35). The cloud filled Solomon's temple at its dedication (1 Kings 8:10-11). The cloud appeared at the transfiguration, and a voice spoke from it: "This is My Son, My Chosen One; listen to Him!" (Luke 9:34-35). In Daniel 7:13, "one like a Son of Man was coming with the clouds of heaven," presented before the Ancient of Days to receive dominion and glory and an everlasting kingdom. The cloud that receives Jesus is the same cloud that signifies God's presence throughout Israel's story. Jesus enters the cloud -- he enters the divine presence, the throne room of God, the place where the Son of Man receives the kingdom that Daniel described.

The phrase "out of their sight" (apo ton ophthalmon auton) marks the end of the visible, physical encounters with the risen Jesus. From this point forward, the community will not see Jesus bodily (with the exception of Stephen's vision in Acts 7:56 and Paul's encounter on the Damascus road in Acts 9:3-6). The period of visible appearances -- the forty days -- is complete. What replaces physical sight is the Holy Spirit, who will make the absent Jesus present and powerful through the community's witness.

"And as they were gazing intently into the sky while He was going, behold, two men in white clothing stood beside them" (1:10).

"Gazing intently" (atenizontes esan) is a strong word meaning to fix one's eyes, to stare without looking away. The imperfect tense (esan atenizontes) indicates they kept staring -- they remained fixed on the sky even after the cloud had hidden Jesus. Their gaze is upward, toward the place where Jesus has gone.

"Two men in white clothing" (andres dyo ... en esthesesi leukais) recall the "two men in dazzling clothing" at the empty tomb (Luke 24:4). The pattern of two angelic witnesses is maintained from the Gospel into Acts. In the Gospel, two men at the tomb announced the resurrection; now two men at the ascension announce the return. The Deuteronomic requirement of two witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15) applies to heavenly testimony as much as earthly. Their white clothing marks them as belonging to the heavenly realm -- the same garments associated with angelic figures throughout Jewish apocalyptic literature (Daniel 7:9, 10:5-6; 2 Maccabees 11:8).

"They also said, 'Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking into the sky? This Jesus, who has been taken up from you into heaven, will come in just the same way as you have watched Him go into heaven'" (1:11).

"Men of Galilee" (andres Galilaioi) identifies the apostles by their geographical origin. All of the Twelve (except Judas, who is from Kerioth in Judea) are Galileans. The designation connects them to the beginning of the story: Jesus's ministry began in Galilee (Luke 4:14-15), and it was in Galilee that he called these men (Luke 5:1-11, 6:12-16).

The question "Why do you stand looking into the sky?" gently redirects their attention. Their gaze is upward, toward the departed Jesus. But their task is not to stare at heaven; it is to be witnesses on earth. The question is functional: stop watching the sky and begin the work you have been given.

The promise is specific and concrete: "This Jesus" (houtos ho Iesous) -- not a different figure, not a spiritual principle, but this same person -- "will come in just the same way as you have watched Him go" (houtos eleusetai hon tropon etheasasthe auton poreuomenon). The manner of his going is the manner of his return: visibly, bodily, with the cloud. The verb etheasasthe ("you watched, you beheld") is the root of theaomai, which denotes careful, attentive observation -- they saw the departure, and the return will match what they saw. The cloud that received him will accompany his return. This aligns with Luke 21:27: "Then they will see the Son of Man coming in a cloud with power and great glory," and with Daniel 7:13: "one like a Son of Man was coming with the clouds of heaven."

The statement simultaneously provides comfort and creates expectation. He is gone, but he is coming back. The ascension is not a permanent departure but an interim absence. The community lives in the period between the ascension and the return -- the same period in which they are to be witnesses "to the end of the earth." The two men do not say when the return will happen; like Jesus's answer in verse 7, the timing is left to the Father. What is certain is the fact of the return, its manner, and its visibility.

## Verses 12-14: The Return to Jerusalem and the Gathered Community

"Then they returned to Jerusalem from the mount called Olivet, which is near Jerusalem, a Sabbath day's journey away" (1:12).

The Mount of Olives (Olivet) is the location of the ascension. The author specifies the distance: "a Sabbath day's journey" (sabbatou echon hodon), which is approximately 2,000 cubits or about half a mile. The detail serves geographical precision and also places the ascension within the boundaries that Jewish law permitted for Sabbath travel, though there is no indication this occurred on a Sabbath. The Mount of Olives carries its own prophetic weight. Zechariah 14:4: "In that day His feet will stand on the Mount of Olives, which is in front of Jerusalem on the east." The place from which God's agent departs in Acts 1 is the place where God's presence will stand in Zechariah's vision. The correspondence would not be lost on a Jewish reader familiar with the prophetic texts.

"When they had entered the city, they went up to the upper room where they were staying; that is, Peter and John and James and Andrew, Philip and Thomas, Bartholomew and Matthew, James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon the Zealot, and Judas the son of James" (1:13).

The "upper room" (to hyperoon) is likely the same location as the "large, furnished upper room" (anagaion mega estromenon) where Jesus shared the Passover meal with his disciples (Luke 22:12). If so, the community is gathering in the same space where Jesus instituted the new covenant meal. The location carries memory and meaning.

The list of names corresponds to Luke 6:14-16, with one critical absence: Judas Iscariot. The author lists eleven names. The order differs slightly from the Gospel list, but the names are the same minus the betrayer. Peter heads the list, as in every New Testament apostolic list. The arrangement pairs individuals in a way that recalls the Gospel's sending of the Twelve in pairs (Luke 10:1, Mark 6:7). "Judas the son of James" (Ioudas Iakobou) is the figure called "Thaddaeus" in some other lists (Matthew 10:3, Mark 3:18). His mention here is notable because his name is the same as the betrayer's (Judas), and the author carefully distinguishes them -- this is Judas of James, not Judas of Kerioth.

"These all with one mind were continually devoting themselves to prayer, along with the women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with His brothers" (1:14).

The phrase "with one mind" (homothymadon) is a key word in Acts, appearing eleven times in the book and only once elsewhere in the New Testament (Romans 15:6). It denotes unanimity, shared purpose, harmonious agreement. The community is unified in their waiting. The word will reappear at Pentecost (2:1, "they were all together in one place"; some manuscripts read homothymadon) and throughout the early community's life (2:46, 4:24, 5:12).

"Continually devoting themselves to prayer" (proskarterountes te proseuche) -- the verb proskartereo means to persist in, to be steadfastly devoted to, to continue earnestly in. The community's posture during the waiting period is sustained, collective prayer. They are obeying the command to wait (1:4) by filling the waiting with prayer. This is not passive waiting; it is active, prayerful preparation. The pattern will characterize the community throughout Acts: before every major action of the Spirit, the community is at prayer (2:1, 4:24-31, 10:9, 13:2-3).

"Along with the women" (syn gynaixin) -- this phrase identifies the women who followed Jesus from Galilee and who were the first witnesses of the empty tomb (Luke 23:49, 55; 24:1-10). They are named in Luke 24:10: Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and others. These women are part of the praying, waiting community. Their inclusion is not incidental; they were the first to hear the angelic announcement and the first to report the resurrection. They belong to the community as full participants.

"And Mary the mother of Jesus" (kai Mariam te metri tou Iesou) -- Mary is named specifically, distinguished from the other women. She has not appeared by name since Luke 2:51, though she was alluded to in Luke 8:19-21 (when Jesus's mother and brothers sought him) and 11:27-28 (when a woman blessed the womb that bore him and Jesus redirected attention to those who hear and obey God's word). Her presence here, at the birth of the community, mirrors her presence at the birth of Jesus. The woman who conceived by the Holy Spirit (Luke 1:35) is present when the community is about to be conceived by the same Spirit.

"And with His brothers" (kai syn tois adelphois autou) -- Jesus's brothers were mentioned in Luke 8:19-20 alongside his mother. They appear to have been outside the circle of faith during the ministry; in Mark 3:21, "His own people" said "He has lost His senses," and in John 7:5, "not even His brothers were believing in Him." Their presence now, among the praying community in the upper room, indicates a change. Something between the ministry and this moment has brought them into the community of faith. Paul reports that the risen Jesus "appeared to James" (1 Corinthians 15:7), which may account for the transformation. James, the brother of Jesus, will become a leader of the Jerusalem community (Acts 12:17, 15:13, 21:18; Galatians 1:19, 2:9).

## Verses 15-20: Peter's Speech About Judas

"At this time Peter stood up in the midst of the brethren (a gathering of about one hundred and twenty persons was there together), and said..." (1:15).

Peter takes the initiative to speak, consistent with his role as the one who leads the apostolic group throughout Luke-Acts. Jesus told Peter, "when once you have turned again, strengthen your brothers" (Luke 22:32). Peter's leadership here -- standing, addressing the assembly, guiding the community through a necessary decision -- is the fulfillment of that charge. The number "about one hundred and twenty" (ochlos onomaton ... hekaton eikosi) defines the size of the pre-Pentecost community. The number is approximately ten times the number of apostles (twelve), which may echo the Jewish legal principle that a community (edah) requires a minimum number for official action. But more concretely, it describes a community that is larger than the apostles alone but still small enough to gather in a single room.

"Brethren, the Scripture had to be fulfilled, which the Holy Spirit foretold by the mouth of David concerning Judas, who became a guide to those who arrested Jesus" (1:16).

Peter's hermeneutical principle is stated at the outset: "the Scripture had to be fulfilled" (edei plerothenai ten graphen). The word edei ("had to," "it was necessary") is the same dei that has governed the entire narrative of divine necessity since Luke 2:49. What happened with Judas was not an accident or a failure in God's plan; it was something scripture foretold and divine necessity required. This does not absolve Judas of responsibility -- the author will describe his end in graphic terms -- but it places his betrayal within the framework of God's determined plan, just as Luke 22:22 stated: "the Son of Man is going as it has been determined; but woe to that man by whom He is betrayed."

The scripture is attributed to the Holy Spirit speaking "by the mouth of David" (dia stomatos Dauid). The psalms are understood as David's words, but more than that, they are the Holy Spirit's words spoken through David. This is the author's consistent hermeneutic: scripture is the Spirit's speech through human agents (cf. Acts 4:25, 28:25).

Judas's role is described with precise language: he "became a guide" (genomenos hodegos) to those who arrested Jesus. The word hodegos means "one who leads the way, a guide" -- it is used in Luke 6:39 for a blind person leading the blind. Judas did not merely betray Jesus; he led the arrest party to the location. Luke 22:47 narrates this: "Judas, one of the twelve, was preceding them; and he approached Jesus to kiss Him." The word genomenos ("having become") implies a transition: Judas was not always a guide for the arrest party; he became one. He was an apostle who became a traitor.

"For he was counted among us and received his share in this ministry" (1:17).

The verb katarithmemenos ("counted among, numbered with") indicates that Judas was fully part of the apostolic group -- not an outsider, not an infiltrator, but one of the chosen. The phrase "received his share" (elachen ton kleron) uses the word kleros ("lot, portion, allotment") -- the same word that will appear in verse 26 for the casting of lots. The term echoes the LXX language of Israel's land allotment (Joshua 14-19), where each tribe received its kleros, its assigned portion. Judas received his portion in "this ministry" (tes diakonias tautes) -- the service of being an apostle. The word diakonia ("ministry, service") will be used repeatedly in Acts for the apostolic task (1:25, 6:4, 20:24, 21:19).

"(Now this man acquired a field with the price of his wickedness, and falling headlong, he burst open in the middle and all his intestines gushed out. And it became known to all who were living in Jerusalem; so that in their own language that field was called Hakeldama, that is, Field of Blood.)" (1:18-19).

The author inserts a parenthetical explanation of Judas's fate and the resulting place name, directed at readers who may not know the Jerusalem tradition. The phrase "the price of his wickedness" (ek misthou tes adikias) -- misthos means "reward, wage, payment"; adikia means "unrighteousness, wickedness." The payment Judas received for the betrayal is characterized as the wages of his injustice.

"Acquired a field" (ektesato chorion) -- the verb ktaomai means "to acquire, to purchase." The field is connected to the payment for the betrayal. Matthew's account (Matthew 27:3-10) describes the chief priests purchasing the field after Judas returned the money and hanged himself. The differences between the two accounts are well-known to first-century readers of multiple traditions, but the author here presents the acquisition as attributed to Judas because the field was bought with his money, whether directly or through intermediaries. In Semitic usage, what one's money accomplishes can be attributed to the person.

The description of Judas's death -- "falling headlong, he burst open in the middle and all his intestines gushed out" (prenes genomenos elakesen mesos kai exechythe panta ta splanchna autou) -- is graphic and visceral. The word prenes ("headlong, prone, face-first") and the verb lakeo ("to burst open, to crack") create an image of violent, sudden destruction. The phrase "all his intestines gushed out" (exechythe panta ta splanchna autou) uses the word splanchna, which in other contexts means "compassion" or "inner feelings" (Luke 1:78, the "tender mercies" of God) but here refers literally to internal organs. The effect is stark: the betrayer's body itself comes apart.

The literary echoes are significant. The death of wicked figures who opposed God's purposes is described in similarly graphic terms in Israel's tradition. The death of Antiochus IV Epiphanes in 2 Maccabees 9:5-9 involves his intestines spilling out. The traitor Nadan in the Aramaic story of Ahiqar swells and bursts. The theological implication within the author's framework is that Judas's end corresponds to his deed: the man who guided others to arrest the Lord of life meets a death of gruesome disintegration.

"Hakeldama" (Hakeldamach) -- the Aramaic name is transliterated and then translated: "Field of Blood" (chorion haimatos). The author explains the Aramaic for his readers, indicating an audience that does not speak Aramaic natively. The field's name in the common language of Jerusalem preserves the memory of what happened there.

"For it is written in the book of Psalms, 'Let his homestead be made desolate, and let no one dwell in it'; and, 'Let another man take his office'" (1:20).

Peter cites two psalms. The first is Psalm 69:25 (LXX 68:26): "Let their habitation be made desolate; let no one dwell in their tents" (genetheito he epaulis autou eremos kai me esto ho katoikon en aute). The original psalm context is the righteous sufferer calling for judgment on his persecutors. The shift from plural ("their habitation") to singular ("his homestead") adapts the psalm to the specific case of Judas. The word epaulis means "homestead, dwelling place, habitation" -- Judas's place, now associated with the field of his death, is desolate.

The second citation is Psalm 109:8 (LXX 108:8): "Let another man take his office" (ten episkopen autou labeto heteros). The word episkope is the key term. In the LXX, it means "visitation," "oversight," "office," "charge." It is used for God's visitation (Luke 19:44: "the time of your visitation," ten kairon tes episkopes sou) and for official positions of responsibility. Peter reads this psalm as speaking prophetically about the need to replace Judas: his office -- his position of apostolic oversight -- must be filled by another.

The hermeneutical move is notable: Peter reads these psalms as the Holy Spirit speaking through David (v. 16) about a specific future event -- the betrayal and its aftermath. The psalms of the righteous sufferer are read as testimonies about the Christ (the righteous sufferer par excellence) and, by extension, about the betrayer of the Christ. This is the same christological reading of the psalms that Jesus demonstrated in Luke 24:44 ("all things which are written about Me in... the Psalms must be fulfilled").

## Verses 21-22: The Qualifications for Replacing Judas

"Therefore it is necessary that of the men who have accompanied us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us -- beginning with the baptism of John until the day that He was taken up from us -- one of these must become a witness with us of His resurrection" (1:21-22).

Peter defines the qualifications for the replacement apostle with two criteria.

First, the candidate must have been present throughout the entire ministry of Jesus, from beginning to end. "Accompanied us all the time" (synelthonton hemin ... en panti chrono) means continuous companionship with the apostolic group during the whole period of Jesus's ministry. The phrase "went in and out among us" (eiselthen kai exelthen eph hemas ho kyrios Iesous) is a Semitic idiom for daily life and activity (Deuteronomy 31:2, 1 Samuel 29:6, 2 Samuel 3:25, Psalm 121:8). It describes Jesus's entire public life and ministry, his comings and goings among them.

The temporal boundaries are precisely defined. The starting point: "beginning with the baptism of John" (arxamenos apo tou baptismatos Ioannou). The ministry of Jesus, in the author's framework, begins with John's baptism -- the event at which the Spirit descended and the voice from heaven declared "You are My beloved Son" (Luke 3:21-22). This is where the public story starts. The ending point: "until the day that He was taken up from us" (heos tes hemeras hes anelempthe aph hemon). The ascension is the terminal boundary. The replacement apostle must have been present for the full arc from John's baptism to the ascension.

Second, the candidate must "become a witness with us of His resurrection" (martyra tes anastaseos autou syn hemin genesthai). The specific content of apostolic witness is "His resurrection" (tes anastaseos autou). The apostles are, above all, witnesses to the fact that Jesus rose from the dead. This is the center of the apostolic testimony, as Peter's Pentecost sermon will make clear: "This Jesus God raised up again, to which we are all witnesses" (Acts 2:32). The resurrection is not one item among many in the apostolic message; it is the defining claim that everything else hangs on.

The word syn hemin ("with us") is important: the replacement will serve alongside the existing eleven, not independently. The apostolic witness is collective, not individual. The community of witnesses validates and supports each other's testimony.

These qualifications define what an apostle is in the author's understanding: one who was present from the beginning of Jesus's ministry through the ascension, and who can therefore personally testify to the resurrection. This definition has implications: it means the apostolic office, in this strict sense, is limited to the first generation. There is a fixed pool of candidates -- those who accompanied Jesus throughout his ministry -- and no new candidates can arise once that generation is gone.

## Verses 23-26: The Selection of Matthias

"So they put forward two men, Joseph called Barsabbas (who was also called Justus), and Matthias" (1:23).

The community identifies two candidates who meet Peter's criteria. Both men have been with the group from John's baptism through the ascension. Neither has appeared by name earlier in Luke-Acts, but they have been among the broader group of disciples -- part of the "all the rest" (Luke 24:9), the larger community beyond the Twelve.

Joseph called Barsabbas, also called Justus, bears three names: his given name (Joseph), his patronymic or family name (Barsabbas, meaning "son of the Sabbath" or "son of the elder"), and a Latin cognomen (Justus, meaning "righteous" or "just"). The multiple names suggest a man known in different communities -- Jewish, Aramaic-speaking, and Roman/Greek-speaking contexts.

Matthias (Matthias or Maththias) -- the name is a shortened form of Mattithiah, meaning "gift of YHWH." Nothing further is said about his background here.

"And they prayed and said, 'You, Lord, who know the hearts of all men, show which one of these two You have chosen to occupy this ministry and apostleship from which Judas turned aside to go to his own place'" (1:24-25).

The prayer is addressed to "Lord" (kyrie). Whether this is addressed to God the Father or to Jesus is debated by the language itself. The description "who know the hearts of all men" (kardiognostes panton) -- the compound word kardiognostes ("heart-knower") appears only here and in Acts 15:8, where it clearly refers to God the Father. On the other hand, the request is to show "which one of these two You have chosen" (hon exelexo ek touton ton dyo hena) -- and the verb eklegomai ("to choose, to select") was used in verse 2 and in Luke 6:13 for Jesus's selection of the apostles: "He chose twelve of them." The prayer may address the Lord Jesus as the one who originally chose the Twelve and therefore should designate the replacement. The ambiguity may be intentional, reflecting the early community's understanding that the risen Jesus exercises divine prerogative.

The phrase "this ministry and apostleship" (ton topon tes diakonias tautes kai apostoles) defines what is being filled: a specific position (topos, "place") of service (diakonia, "ministry") and commissioned authority (apostole, "apostleship"). These are not synonyms but complementary descriptions: the apostle both serves (ministers) and speaks with the authority of the one who sent him.

"From which Judas turned aside to go to his own place" (aph hes parebs Ioudas poreuthevai eis ton topon ton idion). The verb parebv (from parabaino, "to turn aside, to transgress, to deviate") indicates that Judas departed from the apostolic calling. He did not lose his position involuntarily; he "turned aside" -- he chose to leave the path. The phrase "to go to his own place" (eis ton topon ton idion) is a solemn expression. The contrast with the "place" (topon) of ministry in the preceding phrase is deliberate: Judas left the place of apostleship to go to his own place. The phrase carries the sense of a destination that corresponds to one's choices and character. In Jewish literature, the idea that the righteous and the wicked each go to their appointed place after death is well established (cf. 1 Enoch 22, where the dead are divided into separate places according to their deeds).

"And they drew lots for them, and the lot fell to Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles" (1:26).

The method of selection is the casting of lots (edokan klerous autois). The practice of casting lots to discern God's will has deep roots in Israel's history. The lot was used to divide the promised land among the tribes (Numbers 26:55-56, Joshua 14:2). The lot determined the scapegoat on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:8). The lot identified Achan as the transgressor (Joshua 7:14-18). Saul was identified as king by lot (1 Samuel 10:20-21). The priestly courses were assigned by lot (1 Chronicles 24:5), and it was "by lot" that Zechariah was chosen to enter the temple and burn incense at the beginning of this author's Gospel (Luke 1:9). Proverbs 16:33 states the principle: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD."

The lot is not a random device in this worldview; it is a means by which God's choice is revealed. The community has prayed to the heart-knowing Lord (v. 24) to show his choice; the lot is the mechanism through which that choice is made known. The prayer and the lot together -- human petition and divinely guided selection -- constitute the process.

"The lot fell to Matthias" (epesen ho kleros epi Maththian) -- the verb epesen ("fell") is the standard language for the result of lot-casting. "And he was added to the eleven apostles" (synkatepsephisthe meta ton hendeka apostolon) -- the verb synkatapsephizo means "to vote with, to count with, to reckon among." Matthias is counted as one of the apostles. The Eleven become the Twelve again. The apostolic body is restored to its full number.

The significance of the number twelve is rooted in Israel's structure. Israel had twelve tribes, descended from Jacob's twelve sons. Jesus chose twelve apostles as the foundation of a renewed Israel -- Luke 22:30: "you will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel." The twelve cannot remain eleven. The number must be complete before the Spirit comes and the new phase begins. Matthias's addition restores the apostolic body to the number that corresponds to the twelve tribes, symbolizing the reconstitution of Israel around the risen Jesus. The restored Twelve will receive the Spirit at Pentecost (Acts 2), and Peter will stand with "the eleven" (Acts 2:14) -- the Twelve, minus Peter who is standing, the number now complete -- to deliver the first proclamation of the gospel.

## Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **The chapter establishes literary and theological continuity between the two volumes.** The opening verses (1-2) explicitly link Acts to "the first account" and define the Gospel as the record of what Jesus "began" to do and teach. This framing makes Acts the continuation of Jesus's work, carried forward through the Spirit and the apostolic community. The same Jesus, the same kingdom, the same scriptural framework, the same divine necessity (dei) that governed the Gospel now governs Acts.

2. **The forty-day period of post-resurrection instruction is presented as a deliberate preparatory phase.** Like Moses on Sinai, like Israel in the wilderness, like Elijah's journey to Horeb, the forty days are a time of formation. The risen Jesus teaches about the kingdom of God, providing the apostles with the interpretive framework they will need for their mission. The many convincing proofs (tekmeria) establish the factual basis of the resurrection beyond question.

3. **The disciples' question about Israel's restoration is not rebuked but redirected.** The author does not present the hope for Israel's restoration as wrong; he presents the fixation on timing as misplaced and the scope of the mission as broader than the disciples initially imagine. The restoration of Israel and the mission to the nations are held together in the prophetic vision (Isaiah 49:6), and Jesus redirects the disciples from the when to the what: "you will be My witnesses... to the end of the earth."

4. **Acts 1:8 provides the structural outline for the entire book.** The geographical progression -- Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, the end of the earth -- maps directly onto the narrative structure of Acts. The verse functions as a table of contents for the second volume, announcing in advance the trajectory that the Spirit-empowered community will follow.

5. **The ascension is narrated as enthronement, not abandonment.** Jesus is received into the cloud of divine presence -- the same cloud of Sinai, the tabernacle, the temple, and Daniel's throne room. He enters the position of authority that Luke 22:69 predicted: "the right hand of the power of God." The ascension completes the pattern of suffering-resurrection-glory that Jesus explained in Luke 24:26. The two angelic witnesses confirm that the departure is temporary: he will return in the same manner.

6. **The community's posture during the waiting period is defined by unity and prayer.** The repeated emphasis on homothymadon ("with one mind") and proskarterountes te proseuche ("continually devoting themselves to prayer") establishes the spiritual conditions under which the Spirit will come. The community is not passive; they are actively, collectively, persistently praying. This pattern -- corporate prayer preceding divine action -- will recur throughout Acts.

7. **The composition of the waiting community is inclusive and significant.** The Eleven are named, but the community also includes the women who followed Jesus from Galilee, Mary his mother, and his brothers. The women who were the first resurrection witnesses are present. Mary, who conceived by the Spirit, is present as the Spirit is about to fall. Jesus's brothers, previously outside the circle of faith, are now inside it. The community is not limited to the apostolic leadership; it encompasses all who have followed Jesus.

8. **Peter's speech about Judas serves both theological and practical purposes.** Theologically, it places the betrayal within the framework of scriptural fulfillment and divine necessity -- what happened was foretold by the Holy Spirit through David. Practically, it addresses the problem that the apostolic body is incomplete. The twelve-tribe structure of the reconstituted Israel requires twelve apostles. Peter's speech identifies the problem, establishes the qualifications for a replacement, and sets the process in motion.

9. **The qualifications for an apostle define the nature of apostolic witness.** An apostle must have been present from John's baptism through the ascension and must be able to testify personally to the resurrection. This definition is bounded by historical experience: only those who walked with Jesus throughout his ministry and encountered the risen Lord can serve in this role. The apostolic office is thus unrepeatable -- it belongs to the founding generation and cannot be extended beyond those who meet these criteria.

10. **The selection of Matthias by lot connects the new community to Israel's ancient practices.** The lot was God's instrument for revealing his will throughout Israel's history -- from the land distribution to the temple service. By using the lot, the community signals that they understand themselves as continuous with Israel, acting within Israel's established means of discerning divine guidance. The casting of lots is also the last recorded instance of this practice in Acts; after Pentecost, the Spirit provides direct guidance (Acts 13:2, 15:28, 16:6-7), and the lot is no longer needed. The transition from lot to Spirit marks the transition from the old mode of divine communication to the new.

11. **The restoration of the Twelve is the final preparatory act before Pentecost.** Everything in this chapter points forward to Acts 2. The commission has been given (v. 8). The ascension has occurred (vv. 9-11). The community is gathered and praying (vv. 12-14). The apostolic body is restored to its full number (v. 26). The stage is set. The promise of the Father -- the baptism with the Holy Spirit that John foretold and Jesus confirmed -- is about to arrive. Acts 1 is the chapter of readiness: the community is made ready, in every necessary way, to receive what has been promised.
