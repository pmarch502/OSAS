# Galatians 3 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**Where this stands in the letter:** Chapters 1 and 2 were autobiography deployed as argument -- Paul's commission, his distance from Jerusalem, the meeting there, and the confrontation at Antioch. At 2:15 the letter turned from narrative to argument, and chapter 3 carries that argument in two registers, in this order: first from what the Galatians themselves experienced (3:1-5), then from scripture (3:6-29).

The order matters. Paul had already ruled out arguing from his own authority when he pronounced a curse on himself, hypothetically, at 1:8. What remains available to him is evidence, and he begins with the evidence his readers can check without consulting anyone.

**The chapter's question.** Underneath the presenting dispute lies a single question: **who counts as Abraham's offspring?** This is not a side issue. The promises that matter -- land, descendants, blessing to the nations -- were made to Abraham and to his offspring, and inheriting them depends on being counted among them. Circumcision is the sign given to Abraham in Genesis 17 and given specifically as the covenant sign for him and his descendants. So a Gentile who wants to belong to Abraham's family has an obvious route in front of him, and the teachers in Galatia appear to have been pointing to it.

Paul's counter-move is to go two chapters earlier in the same narrative. In Genesis 15, before any sign is instituted, Abraham believes God and it is reckoned to him as righteousness. Paul builds his case from there.

**The chapter is built on six scriptural citations, and he adapts several of them.** Genesis 15:6 at 3:6; Genesis 12:3 and 18:18 conflated at 3:8; Deuteronomy 27:26 at 3:10; Habakkuk 2:4 at 3:11; Leviticus 18:5 at 3:12; Deuteronomy 21:23 at 3:13. The argument's force comes from setting these against one another, and three of the six are quoted in a form that differs from the sources. Those differences are set out in the comments below, because they are not incidental -- in each case the change is what makes the citation bear on the argument.

**What the author argues immediately after (Galatians 4):** Chapter 4 continues without a break, extending the household metaphor of 3:23-25: the minor heir under guardians comes of age at the date his father set (4:1-7). Paul then applies the argument to the Galatians' present situation with a sharp warning (4:8-11), abandons argument for personal appeal (4:12-20), and closes with an allegory of Hagar and Sarah that restates the whole case in narrative form (4:21-31).

**How chapter 3 connects to what follows:** The connections are tight and verbal. The paidagogos of 3:24-25 becomes the guardians and stewards of 4:2. The release from the paidagogos becomes the heir's coming of age. The "sons of God" of 3:26 becomes the adoption of 4:5 and the Abba cry of 4:6. The "heirs according to promise" of 3:29 becomes "no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir" at 4:7. Chapters 3 and 4 are a single movement and should be read as one.

## Verses 1-5: The Argument from Their Own Experience

> O foolish Galatians! Who has bewitched you, before whose eyes Jesus Christ was publicly placarded as crucified? This only I want to learn from you: did you receive the Spirit by works of law or by a hearing of faith? Are you so foolish? Having begun by the Spirit, are you now being perfected by flesh? Did you experience so many things in vain -- if indeed it was in vain? Does he then who supplies the Spirit to you and works miracles among you do so by works of law, or by a hearing of faith?

**Verse 1: "O foolish Galatians! Who has bewitched you, before whose eyes Jesus Christ was publicly placarded as crucified?"**

Anoetoi is built on a-, "not," and noeō, "to perceive with the mind, to understand." It is a charge of failing to think, not of lacking information. Paul repeats it two verses later.

"Who has bewitched you?" is tis hymas ebaskanen, from baskainō. This verb belongs to the world of the evil eye, and the belief behind it needs stating for a reader to whom it is unfamiliar, because Paul has chosen the image deliberately.

Across the ancient Mediterranean, and in Jewish sources as well as Greek and Roman ones, it was widely believed that a person could be harmed by another's malicious or envious *look*. The gaze itself was thought to carry the injury -- particularly the gaze of the envious upon someone flourishing. Protective amulets against the evil eye are among the commonest surviving small objects from the period. The Greek scriptures use the related expression at Deuteronomy 15:9 and 28:54, and Proverbs 23:6 and 28:22 warn against the man with a "grudging eye." The verb baskainō is the technical term for casting it.

Paul is not making a literal charge of sorcery; the question is rhetorical and it stings. But the image is chosen for what it sets up. The Galatians have been affected by a *look* -- and Paul immediately reminds them of a very different thing that was set before their eyes.

Hois kat' ophthalmous Iēsous Christos proegraphē estaurōmenos. The verb prographō combines pro ("before, in front of, publicly") with graphō ("to write"). It can mean "to write beforehand," but its other and more vivid sense is "to post up publicly, to placard" -- the language of a public notice fixed where everyone can read it, an edict or a proclamation displayed in the open.

That is the contrast Paul is drawing. Against a covert glance that harms, a public placard that anyone may read; against the evil eye, an event set out in the open before their eyes.

The participle estaurōmenos is perfect passive: "having been crucified," a completed action whose result stands. Not the event of the crucifixion but the crucified one as an abiding reality. And the placard, on this image, was Paul's own preaching -- he had put the crucified Christ up in front of them where they could see him.

**Verse 2: "This only I want to learn from you: did you receive the Spirit by works of law or by a hearing of faith?"**

The whole paragraph turns on one question, asked twice (here and at verse 5), and it is a question about something that happened to the readers.

Notice the method. Paul does not begin from scripture -- that comes at verse 6. He does not begin from his apostolic standing -- he has forfeited that as a criterion at 1:8. He begins from an event the Galatians can attest without anyone's help: they received the Spirit. Whatever that consisted of in their experience, verse 5 indicates it included visible phenomena, and it is treated throughout as something nobody in the conversation disputes.

Touto monon thelō mathein aph' hymōn ("this only I want to learn from you") is a debating posture: I concede everything else and ask one thing.

**Ex akoēs pisteōs** ("by a hearing of faith") is ambiguous in both of its nouns, and the ambiguity should be reported rather than resolved by translation.

*Akoē* can mean the act of hearing, or the thing heard -- a report, a message. The second sense is the one in Isaiah 53:1 in the Greek: tis episteusen tē akoē hēmōn, "who has believed our report?" Paul quotes that verse at Romans 10:16 and then glosses it in the next verse: "so faith comes from hearing (akoēs), and hearing through the word of Christ."

*Pisteōs* is a genitive and may indicate source ("hearing that springs from faith"), content ("the message about faith"), or description ("believing hearing").

The phrase therefore might be rendered "by hearing with faith," "by the message of faith," or "by believing what you heard." What carries the argument is the contrast, and the contrast survives every resolution of the ambiguity: on one side something *done*, on the other something *heard*.

**Verse 3: "Are you so foolish? Having begun by the Spirit, are you now being perfected by flesh?"**

Enarxamenoi pneumati nyn sarki epiteleisthe. The two verbs are a matched pair, and both belong to the vocabulary of religious rites: enarchomai, "to begin, to make the initial offering, to inaugurate," and epiteleō, "to complete, to bring to its proper end, to perform fully." Both are used in Greek of the beginning and completion of a sacred action. The question is therefore about a single process: something inaugurated in one medium and now being finished in another.

**Sarx, "flesh,"** appears here for the first time in the letter's argument, and it is the letter's most flexible and most loaded word. Its range in Galatians:

- plain bodily existence, with no negative charge: "the life I now live in the flesh" (2:20); "a weakness of the flesh" (4:13)
- human descent or human contrivance: Ishmael "born according to flesh" (4:23, 29)
- the disordered self in its opposition to the Spirit: the "works of the flesh" (5:19-21), "crucified the flesh" (5:24)
- and, specifically in this letter's polemic, **circumcision** -- the operation performed on the flesh: "that they may make a good showing in the flesh... that they may boast in your flesh" (6:12-13)

In a chapter about whether Gentile believers should undergo circumcision, that last sense is difficult to exclude here, and the sentence would then read with a bite that translations tend to lose: you began with the Spirit; are you now being brought to completion by a surgical procedure?

**Verse 4: "Did you experience so many things in vain -- if indeed it was in vain?"**

Tosauta epathete. The verb paschō most often means "to suffer," and it may mean that here -- pointing to persecution the Galatians had undergone. But the verb can also be neutral, "to undergo, to experience," and the surrounding verses are about the Spirit and about miracles rather than about hardship, which may indicate that what they "experienced" is what verses 2 and 5 describe.

Eikē means "without cause, to no purpose, in vain."

Then Paul corrects himself: ei ge kai eikē -- "if indeed it really was in vain." He raises the possibility that everything they experienced has come to nothing and immediately declines to assert it. This mid-sentence retraction is characteristic of the letter's whole posture: the situation is grave and it is not over.

**Verse 5: "Does he then who supplies the Spirit to you and works miracles among you do so by works of law, or by a hearing of faith?"**

The question of verse 2 returns with two changes.

*The tense.* Both participles are present: ho epichorēgōn ("the one supplying") and energōn ("working"). Verse 2 asked about the beginning; verse 5 asks about now. The Spirit is not only how they started; it is what is currently happening among them.

*The addition.* Dynameis, "acts of power, mighty works, miracles," is named alongside the Spirit. Whatever the Galatian congregations were experiencing, Paul can appeal to it as public and undisputed.

Epichorēgeō is worth a note because the word has a history. A chorēgos in classical Athens was a wealthy citizen assigned the public duty of financing a chorus for a dramatic festival -- paying for the training, the costumes, the whole production, out of his own pocket, as a civic honour. The verb came to mean supplying generously and at one's own cost. The compound adds epi-, intensifying it further. God is pictured as bearing the whole expense.

**The form of the argument, and why it comes first.** Verses 2 and 5 pose the same disjunction twice, and it is an argument from evidence rather than from authority. The Spirit came among these Gentile congregations before any question of circumcision arose, and it is still at work. Whatever standing the teachers are offering to confer, its possession is already demonstrated. A man being offered a certificate is holding the thing the certificate would certify.

## Verses 6-9: Abraham and the Nations

> Just as Abraham "believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness" -- know then that those of faith, these are sons of Abraham. And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham: "In you shall all the nations be blessed." So then those of faith are blessed along with Abraham, the man of faith.

**Verse 6: "Just as Abraham 'believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness.'"**

The citation is Genesis 15:6, quoted in the Greek form: episteusen Abraam tō theō kai elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn.

Logizomai is a bookkeeping word: to reckon, to enter in an account, to credit. It belongs to commerce and administration before it belongs to theology, and its use here means that something was entered on Abraham's side of a ledger.

**The position of this verse in Abraham's story is the argument.** This has to be stated clearly because the whole strategy depends on it.

Genesis 15 is the chapter in which God brings Abram outside, shows him the stars, promises him offspring beyond counting, and Abram believes. Genesis 17 is the chapter, two chapters and (by the narrative's own reckoning) some thirteen years later, in which circumcision is instituted as the covenant sign for Abraham and his descendants.

If the teachers in Galatia were arguing from Genesis 17 -- this is Abraham's covenant sign, therefore those who would join Abraham's family must receive it -- then Paul's answer is to go to Genesis 15 and point out that the man was reckoned righteous before the sign existed. Whatever made Abraham right with God, it was not the thing the Galatians are being urged to undergo, because Abraham did not yet have it.

Paul makes this argument at greater length at Romans 4:9-12, where he draws the timing out explicitly. Here he simply cites the verse and lets its place in the narrative do the work.

**Verse 7: "Know then that those of faith, these are sons of Abraham."**

Hoi ek pisteōs ("those out of faith") is a new construction, formed on a pattern the letter uses to name groups by what characterizes them: hoi ek peritomēs ("those of the circumcision," 2:12), hoi ex ergōn nomou ("those of works of law," 3:10). The preposition ek indicates source or origin -- people whose defining derivation is faith.

Houtoi huioi eisin Abraam ("these are sons of Abraham"). The demonstrative is emphatic: *these* are the ones.

Descent from Abraham is the contested category in this entire dispute, and the claim being made is not that faith provides a substitute family for those who cannot have the real one. It is that faith identifies who the real family is.

**Verse 8: "And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham: 'In you shall all the nations be blessed.'"**

Two features of this verse are remarkable, and both are easy to read past.

*First, scripture is the subject of two active verbs.* Hē graphē ("the scripture") *foresaw* (proidousa) and *preached the gospel beforehand* (proeuēngelisato). Scripture is personified as an agent that anticipated the present situation. The same personification recurs at 3:22 ("the scripture shut up everything under sin").

*Second, the promise to Abraham is called the gospel.* Proeuangelizomai ("to announce good news in advance") occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, and Paul appears to have formed it for this sentence. The claim it makes is substantial: what Paul preaches to Gentiles is not a new message that has arrived to supplement an older arrangement. It is the message God announced to Abraham, arriving on time.

The citation conflates Genesis 12:3 ("in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed") with Genesis 18:18 ("all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him"), taking "all the nations" from the latter. The word is panta ta ethnē -- the same word rendered "Gentiles" throughout this letter. In its own terms, without any interpretive pressure, the promise to Abraham was about the nations.

This is the positive engine of the whole argument, and it is often overshadowed by the negative material about law and curse that follows. Paul is not arguing that Gentiles may be admitted to something that was not designed for them. He is arguing that the arrangement was aimed at them from its first announcement.

**Verse 9: "So then those of faith are blessed along with Abraham, the man of faith."**

Syn tō pistō Abraam ("with faithful Abraham"). The adjective pistos means both "believing" and "faithful," and here it names Abraham by the quality the citation attributed to him.

The preposition syn ("with, together with") is precise: the blessing is shared with Abraham, not transferred from him to someone else. Nobody is displaced.

**What has been established by verse 9, before the difficult material begins.** Abraham was reckoned righteous by believing, before circumcision existed. The promise made to him named the nations as its beneficiaries. Those characterized by faith are his sons and share his blessing. Every category that will govern the rest of the chapter -- promise, blessing, offspring, inheritance -- is Abrahamic, and all of them are in place before the law has been mentioned once.

## Verses 10-14: Curse and Blessing

> For as many as are of works of law are under a curse; for it is written, "Cursed is everyone who does not abide by all things written in the book of the law, to do them." Now that no one is justified before God by law is evident, because "the righteous shall live by faith." But the law is not of faith; rather, "the one who does them shall live by them." Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us -- for it is written, "Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree" -- so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.

**Verse 10: "For as many as are of works of law are under a curse; for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who does not abide by all things written in the book of the law, to do them.'"**

**The citation and its adjustments.** The source is Deuteronomy 27:26, and Paul's wording matches neither the Hebrew nor the standard Greek exactly.

The Hebrew reads: "Cursed be anyone who does not confirm the words of this law by doing them."

The Greek version adds "all": "Cursed is every person who does not continue in *all* the words of this law to do them."

Paul writes: "Cursed is everyone who does not abide by all things written **in the book of the law**, to do them." The phrase "the book of the law" is not in Deuteronomy 27:26; it is drawn from elsewhere in the same book (28:58, 61; 29:20-21; 30:10), where it designates the written covenant document as a whole.

**The context of the verse he is quoting matters as much as its wording.** Deuteronomy 27-30 is the covenant-sanctions section of the book. Chapter 27 stages a ceremony at Gerizim and Ebal in which the tribes pronounce a series of curses, and 27:26 is the last of them, summarizing the rest. Chapter 28 sets out the blessings for covenant faithfulness and then, at much greater length, the curses for covenant infidelity, culminating in defeat, deportation and exile. Chapters 29-30 look past the exile to a restoration.

So the sentence Paul quotes is not a general observation about human capability. It is the summary curse of a covenant document, in a section about what happens to the nation if it is unfaithful.

**The verse's logic has a gap, and how one fills it determines much of the letter.**

Paul states a conclusion -- those of works of law are under a curse -- and supports it with a citation -- cursed is everyone who does not do all. Getting from the citation to the conclusion requires a middle term, and Paul does not supply one. Three candidates have been proposed, and the evidence for each should be stated.

*First: nobody does all of it.* On this reading the unstated premise is that comprehensive obedience is not achievable, so everyone in the category is under the curse. This has been the dominant reading in Western Christianity since the sixteenth century.

The evidence bearing against it should be given its weight. **Deuteronomy 30:11-14**, three chapters after the verse Paul quotes and in the same covenant-sanctions block, says the opposite in as many words: "this commandment that I command you today is not too hard for you, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that you should say, 'Who will ascend to heaven for us and bring it to us, that we may hear it and do it?'... But the word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart, so that you can do it." **Philippians 3:6** has Paul say of his own past that "as to righteousness under the law" he was *blameless* -- he does not describe a man who tried and failed. **Luke 1:6** describes Zechariah and Elizabeth as "righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments and statutes of the Lord." And the Torah itself contains an elaborate provision for atonement -- the sin offering, the guilt offering, the Day of Atonement -- which presupposes failure and supplies its remedy. A covenant that builds in a remedy for failure is not a pass-or-fail examination, and treating unmet perfection as the covenant's implicit standard requires setting the remedy aside.

*Second: the curse is the covenant's outstanding sanction.* On this reading Paul's point is corporate and historical rather than individual and psychological. The curses of Deuteronomy 27-30 fell; the exile happened; and in the view of many Jews of this period the restoration promised in Deuteronomy 30 and the prophets had not yet fully arrived (Nehemiah 9:36, after the return: "behold, we are slaves this day"). To take on the covenant's badge now is therefore to enter under a judgment that is still in force rather than into a blessing.

Supporting evidence: the passage cited is a covenant-sanctions text, not a statement about human capacity; and verses 13-14 will describe a curse being *lifted from* one group ("us") so that a blessing may reach another ("the Gentiles"), which fits a specific covenantal curse better than a universal human condition.

*Third: the emphasis falls on the doing.* The citation ends tou poiēsai auta, "to do them," and on this reading the curse rests on those who take on the law's obligations without the corresponding life. Paul's target would then be the specific proposal in Galatia: adopting the marker without the substance.

Supporting evidence: 5:3, "he is a debtor to do the whole law," and 6:13, "not even those who are circumcised keep the law themselves" -- both of which press exactly this point.

**The verse does not name its own premise, but the three candidates are not equally placed.** The second and third are supported from within the letter and from the passage Paul is quoting. The first is supported chiefly by its long dominance, and it has to be held against Deuteronomy 30:11-14 in the same covenant block, against Paul's own description of himself at Philippians 3:6, against Luke 1:6, and against the existence of the atonement provisions. That is a burden the other two do not carry.

A reader should notice which premise a given interpretation has assumed -- and should notice that the most familiar one is the one with the most to answer for.

**Verse 11: "Now that no one is justified before God by law is evident, because 'the righteous shall live by faith.'"**

The citation is Habakkuk 2:4, and here too Paul has modified what he found.

The Greek version reads ho de dikaios ek pisteōs mou zēsetai -- "the righteous shall live by **my** faithfulness," where the pronoun refers to God: the righteous person lives by God's reliability.

The Hebrew reads "the righteous shall live by **his** faithfulness" (be'emunato) -- referring to the righteous person's own steadfastness.

Paul writes ho dikaios ek pisteōs zēsetai -- **dropping the pronoun entirely.**

That is a deliberate act. He had two versions in front of him, each of which resolved the question one way, and he quoted it in a form that resolves it neither way. This bears directly on the pistis Christou question raised at 2:16: at the one place where Paul cites a text about faith and *could* have specified whose, he removed the specification.

It is worth adding what the underlying Hebrew word carries. Emunah comes from a root meaning firmness or reliability (the same root as "amen"). It denotes both trusting and being trustworthy, and Hebrew does not divide the two. The question "whose faith, and is it trust or fidelity?" may be a question the source text does not recognize.

**Verse 12: "But the law is not of faith; rather, 'the one who does them shall live by them.'"**

The citation is Leviticus 18:5, quoted almost exactly.

Paul is setting two scriptural statements about *living* against each other: Habakkuk, where the righteous live by faith; Leviticus, where the one who does them lives by them. The contrast is between two operating principles, and it is worth being precise about what the contrast does and does not assert.

It does not assert that the law is bad, or that Leviticus 18:5 is untrue. Paul quotes it as scripture, without qualification.

It asserts that the two work differently, and that they are not the same principle.

**What "life" means in Leviticus 18:5 is worth setting out**, because much depends on it and the verse is regularly read at a higher pitch than its own context supports. The chapter is a body of legislation given to a people about to enter a land, and the verse stands at its head: "You shall therefore keep my statutes and my rules; if a person does them, he shall live by them." Deuteronomy states the same alternative in the same register: "I have set before you today life and good, death and evil... choose life, that you and your offspring may live, loving the LORD your God, obeying his voice and holding fast to him, for he is your life and length of days, that you may dwell in the land" (Deuteronomy 30:15-20). The life on offer in these texts is life in the land, under blessing, in the presence of God -- long life, flourishing, continuance as a people.

Whether that horizon extends to resurrection and the age to come is a question the verse does not raise. Paul cites it here for one purpose: to mark the law's operating principle as a different principle from the one Habakkuk names.

**Verse 13: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us -- for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree.'"**

Exēgorasen ("redeemed") is a commercial word. Agorazō is "to buy in the marketplace" (the agora), and the prefix ex- adds "out of" -- to buy something out of a condition, to purchase and remove. The word was used of buying a slave out of slavery. The same verb appears at 4:5, where Christ redeems "those under law."

The related word-group in the Greek scriptures is the exodus vocabulary: God "redeemed you from the house of slavery" (Deuteronomy 7:8; 9:26; 13:5; 15:15).

What they are bought out of is stated precisely: ek tēs kataras tou nomou, "from the curse **of the law**" -- the specific curse named in verse 10, not a curse in general.

**The citation, and Paul's two changes to it.** The source is Deuteronomy 21:23.

The Greek version reads: kekatēramenos hypo theou pas kremamenos epi xylou -- "cursed **by God** is everyone hanged on a tree."

Paul writes: epikataratos pas ho kremamenos epi xylou.

*Change one: the word for "cursed."* He replaces kekatēramenos with **epikataratos** -- which is the exact word he used three verses earlier in the Deuteronomy 27:26 citation. After the substitution the two curses are named by the same term. The effect is to weld them: the thing Christ becomes in verse 13 is verbally identical to the thing the covenant pronounces in verse 10.

*Change two: he drops "by God."* The Greek version says God curses the hanged man. Paul omits it. The law's verdict is left standing as the agent, and the sentence does not say that God cursed him.

**On applying Deuteronomy 21:23 to crucifixion.** A reader may object that the passage does not describe crucifixion at all, and the objection is worth taking seriously. In Deuteronomy the sequence is: a man commits a capital crime, is put to death, and *then* his body is hung on a tree as a public display; the law's concern is that the body be taken down and buried the same day, "for a hanged man is cursed by God." The hanging is exposure after death, not the means of death. Crucifixion reverses that: the hanging is what kills.

But the application of the statute to crucifixion had already been made in Judea before Paul wrote, and it was a legal extension rather than a poetic association.

- The **Temple Scroll** from Qumran (11Q19, column 64) legislates for the traitor and the informer: "you shall hang him on the tree **and he shall die**" -- making the hanging the mode of execution -- and then quotes Deuteronomy 21:22-23 over the ruling.
- The **Nahum commentary** from Qumran (4Q169) describes "the Lion of Wrath... who hangs men alive on the tree," referring to a ruler's crucifixion of his opponents.
- **John 19:31** records that the bodies were taken down before sunset because of the approaching sabbath -- which is Deuteronomy 21:23's same-day burial requirement being observed over crucified men.
- The early preaching in Acts consistently calls the cross **xylon**, "tree" or "wood" (Acts 5:30; 10:39; 13:29), as does 1 Peter 2:24 -- a word choice that only earns its keep if Deuteronomy 21:23 is in view.

So in Paul's setting the statute was already understood to reach crucifixion. The identification is a recognized halakhic extension, not a figure of speech, even though the procedure Deuteronomy describes is not the procedure Rome used.

**Verse 14: "So that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith."**

Two purpose clauses, and the pronouns move between them. This movement is easy to miss and it matters.

"Christ redeemed **us** from the curse of the law" (v. 13)
→ "so that the blessing of Abraham might come **to the Gentiles**"
→ "so that **we** might receive the promise of the Spirit."

Note what the middle clause does *not* say. It does not say "so that the Gentiles might be redeemed from the curse of the law." What arrives among the nations is **the blessing of Abraham** -- which is the older promise of verse 8, made before Sinai and directed at the nations from its first announcement.

So two things are named in one sentence: a redemption *from the law's curse*, and the arrival of the *Abrahamic blessing* among the Gentiles, the second following from the first as its purpose. A reading of the letter has to say how these two are related, and the pronoun seam is where the question is posed.

"The promise of the Spirit" (tēn epangelian tou pneumatos) identifies what the Galatians already possess (3:2-5) as the promised thing itself. The argument has come full circle: the Spirit they can attest is the inheritance in question.

## Verses 15-18: A Ratified Will

> Brothers, I speak in human terms: even with a man's covenant, once it has been ratified, no one annuls it or adds a codicil to it. Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. It does not say "and to offsprings," as of many, but as of one: "and to your offspring," who is Christ. This is what I mean: the law, which came four hundred and thirty years afterward, does not annul a covenant previously ratified by God, so as to void the promise. For if the inheritance is by law, it is no longer by promise; but God gave it to Abraham by promise.

**Verse 15: "Brothers, I speak in human terms: even with a man's covenant, once it has been ratified, no one annuls it or adds a codicil to it."**

Kata anthrōpon legō ("I speak according to man") signals an argument from ordinary human practice rather than from revelation.

**Diathēkē is the pivot word of this paragraph, and it has two lives.** In ordinary Greek usage of this period, a diathēkē is a *will* -- a testamentary disposition of an estate, drawn up by one party, taking effect on defined terms. In the Greek scriptures, however, diathēkē is the standard rendering of the Hebrew berit, "covenant" -- the term for God's arrangements with Noah, Abraham, Israel at Sinai, and David.

Paul exploits both senses at once. The analogy works because a *will*, once executed, cannot be modified by outside parties. The application works because diathēkē is the word for what God made with Abraham.

Kekyrōmenēn ("ratified") is the perfect passive of kyroō, "to confirm, to validate, to make authoritative" -- a legal term for an instrument that has taken effect.

Two things cannot be done to such an instrument: oudeis athetei ("no one annuls it") or epidiatassetai ("adds to it"). The second verb is precise: epi-diatassomai is to make an additional disposition -- what a will's codicil does. Nobody can bolt a new clause onto a document already in force.

**Verse 16: "Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. It does not say 'and to offsprings,' as of many, but as of one: 'and to your offspring,' who is Christ."**

The promise formula tō spermati sou ("to your offspring") appears at Genesis 13:15, 17:8 and 24:7.

**This argument requires honest handling, because it has a well-known difficulty.** Paul's point rests on the singular form of sperma. The difficulty is that sperma in Greek, like zera' in Hebrew, is a **collective singular**: the singular form regularly denotes a whole body of descendants, and Genesis uses it that way constantly -- "I will make your offspring as the dust of the earth, so that if one can count the dust of the earth, your offspring also can be counted" (Genesis 13:16), where the singular noun plainly means a countless multitude. English "offspring" behaves the same way.

Paul knows this, and demonstrates that he knows it thirteen verses later: at 3:29 he writes "you are Abraham's **sperma**" of a whole community. He is not confused about Greek grammar.

What is happening, then, is not a claim about what the grammar compels but a reading of a kind attested in Jewish interpretation of the period, in which the *form* of a word in scripture is taken to carry significance. And what the reading establishes is a structure rather than a grammatical fact: **the promise runs through one, and others enter it by belonging to him.** That is exactly the structure 3:29 states -- "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring." The singular is the hinge on which the many are attached.

**Verse 17: "This is what I mean: the law, which came four hundred and thirty years afterward, does not annul a covenant previously ratified by God, so as to void the promise."**

Meta tetrakosia kai triakonta etē ("after 430 years") follows the Greek version of Exodus 12:40, which counts 430 years for the sojourn in Egypt *and* Canaan together; the Hebrew of that verse counts 430 years in Egypt alone. Paul is using the figure that measures from the patriarchs to the exodus, which is what his argument needs.

Ou akyroi ("does not invalidate") is the negated form of kyroō from verse 15 -- the instrument was made valid, and the later arrangement does not un-validate it.

**Verse 18: "For if the inheritance is by law, it is no longer by promise; but God gave it to Abraham by promise."**

Klēronomia ("inheritance") enters here and becomes the chapter's closing term at 3:29. In the Greek scriptures it is the word for Israel's allotted portion of the land (Deuteronomy 4:21, 38; 15:4; Joshua 11:23) and also, in the other direction, for Israel as God's own portion (Deuteronomy 9:26, 29; 32:9). The word carries the idea of something received because of who one is, not something earned.

The disjunction ei... ouketi ("if... then no longer") sets law and promise as alternative bases for one and the same inheritance.

**It is worth being exact about what this argument claims and what it does not.** It does not claim that the promise is a superior kind of thing to the covenant at Sinai. It claims that the two are different instruments with different logics, that the promise was ratified first, and that a later instrument cannot rewrite an earlier one that is already in force. The operative words in verse 17 are ou akyroi -- it does not *invalidate*. Priority and irrevocability are the terms of the argument, not rank.

The distinction matters. An argument from rank invites the conclusion that the lesser thing may be retired. An argument from priority does not: it leaves the later instrument standing and simply denies that it can overwrite the earlier one.

## Verses 19-22: Why Then the Law?

> Why then the law? It was added because of transgressions, until the offspring should come to whom the promise had been made, having been ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator. Now a mediator is not of one, but God is one. Is the law then against the promises of God? Certainly not! For if a law had been given that was able to make alive, then righteousness would indeed have been by law. But the scripture shut up everything under sin, so that the promise by faith of Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe.

**Verse 19a: "Why then the law?"**

Ti oun ho nomos is the question the preceding argument forces. If the promise came first and cannot be added to, and if the inheritance runs on promise rather than law, then Sinai needs accounting for. The question is an objection Paul raises against himself, and the answer occupies the rest of the chapter.

**Verse 19b: "It was added because of transgressions."**

**Prosetethē ("it was added")** is the first part of the answer and it is a word about relation. The law is an addition to something already standing -- the promise of verses 15-18. It is not a replacement for it, not an independent arrangement, and not the thing the promise was leading up to. Whatever else follows, the letter's own term for how Sinai relates to the Abrahamic promise is "added."

**Tōn parabaseōn charin ("because of transgressions")** is ambiguous. The preposition charin with the genitive means "for the sake of, on account of, with a view to," and three readings are available:

*To restrain them.* The law was added to hold transgression in check -- a curb.

*To produce or expose them.* The law was added so that wrongdoing would be identified as transgression. This is the line Paul takes elsewhere: "where there is no law, neither is there transgression" (Romans 4:15); "the law came in that the trespass might increase" (Romans 5:20); and the extended argument of Romans 7:7-13.

*To deal with them.* The law was added because transgression existed and a system for handling it was needed -- which is what the sacrificial and purity legislation supplies.

The noun itself is worth noting. Parabasis is formed from parabainō, "to step across," and denotes crossing a line that has been drawn. It differs from hamartia, "missing the mark," in presupposing a boundary already marked out. A transgression requires a stated law to be transgressed, which is what makes the second reading grammatically natural even though the third fits the sacrificial system better.

**Verse 19c: "Until the offspring should come to whom the promise had been made."**

Achris hou elthē to sperma sets a terminus, and the question is what it terminates.

Grammatically, a temporal clause limits the clause it modifies. What is being limited here is the *adding-because-of-transgressions* -- the function just described -- rather than the law's existence. The letter maintains this pattern consistently: at 3:25 it does not say "the law is abolished" but "we are no longer **under** a paidagōgos." What ends, in every formulation Paul uses, is a relation to the law rather than the law itself.

Whether that distinction bears the weight some readers place on it is a fair question, but the distinction is in the text and is not an interpreter's invention.

**Verse 19d: "Having been ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator."**

Diatageis di' angelōn en cheiri mesitou.

**The tradition that angels attended the giving of the law was widespread, and in most of its occurrences it is honorific.** This should be known before the phrase is read as disparagement.

- **Deuteronomy 33:2** in the Greek version reads: "The LORD came from Sinai... at his right hand, **angels** with him." (The Hebrew is different and more obscure; the Greek supplies the angels.)
- **Acts 7:53**, in Stephen's speech, has Israel "receive the law by the ordinances of angels" -- eis diatagas angelōn, from the same root as Paul's diatageis.
- **Hebrews 2:2** speaks of "the word spoken through angels."
- **Josephus** puts into Herod's mouth the claim that the Jews "learned the noblest of our doctrines and the holiest of our laws **through angels sent from God**" (Antiquities 15.136) -- and it is said as a boast.
- **Jubilees 1:27-2:1** has the angel of the presence dictate the law to Moses.

The presence of angels at Sinai was therefore, in most Jewish usage, part of the glory of the occasion, not a diminishment of it.

"By the hand of a mediator" (en cheiri mesitou) is a Semitic idiom for agency, and the mediator is Moses, who stood between God and the people at Sinai at their own request (Exodus 20:19; Deuteronomy 5:5, "I stood between the LORD and you at that time, to declare to you the word of the LORD").

Whether Paul is invoking the honorific tradition or turning it to a different purpose depends on how the next sentence is read.

**Verse 20: "Now a mediator is not of one, but God is one."**

This is the most obscure sentence in the letter, and honesty requires saying so. Commentators have catalogued hundreds of proposed interpretations. The elements are: a mediator (mesitēs) implies more than one party; God is one.

*The least strained reading* takes it as an argument about directness. A mediated arrangement requires two parties and an intermediary between them. The promise to Abraham had no such structure: God spoke, and there was no second contracting party and no go-between. On this reading verse 20 continues the argument of 15-18 -- a unilateral instrument cannot be overturned by a later bilateral one.

*Other proposals* have taken the "one" to be the single offspring of verse 16; or read it as a denial of the law's divine origin; or as an affirmation that the one God stands behind both arrangements, so that no conflict can arise between them.

The sentence does not decide among these, and it should not be made to carry weight its obscurity cannot bear. Whatever else is true, "God is one" is the opening line of Israel's daily confession (Deuteronomy 6:4, the Shema), and Paul is asserting something about God's unity in the middle of an argument about two arrangements. Beyond that, caution is warranted.

**Verse 21: "Is the law then against the promises of God? Certainly not! For if a law had been given that was able to make alive, then righteousness would indeed have been by law."**

The question ho nomos kata tōn epangeliōn tou theou ("is the law against the promises of God?") is the one everything since verse 19 invites. Paul rejects it with mē genoito, his strongest negation.

**The reason he gives is the clearest statement in the letter about what the law was and was not for, and it should be read exactly.**

Ei gar edothē nomos ho dynamenos zōopoiēsai, ontōs ek nomou an ēn hē dikaiosynē -- "if a law had been given that was able to make alive, righteousness would indeed have been by law."

*The verb.* Zōopoieō is "to make alive, to give life," and its use elsewhere is resurrection language: God "gives life to the dead" (Romans 4:17); "he will also give life to your mortal bodies" (Romans 8:11); "in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, and again at 15:36, 45).

*The grammar.* This is a contrary-to-fact conditional -- the construction used for a condition known to be untrue. No such law was given.

*What it therefore asserts.* The law is not opposed to the promise, because it was never issued to do the promise's work. It was not a failed attempt at giving life; it was not an attempt at all. This is a statement about the law's **remit**, not about human performance under it.

The distinction is worth pressing, because the two are easily conflated. "A law was given that could make alive, but nobody managed to keep it" is a different proposition from "no law able to make alive was given." Paul writes the second. And he writes it as the *defence* of the law against the charge of standing against God's promises -- which means that on his own account, the law's inability to give life is not a deficiency in it but a fact about what it was for.

**Verse 22: "But the scripture shut up everything under sin, so that the promise by faith of Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe."**

Synekleisen ("shut up together, enclosed, confined") is used in the Greek scriptures of shutting someone into a place from which they cannot get out -- "you have not shut me up into the hand of the enemy" (Psalm 31:8, LXX 30:9); of the men of Keilah shutting David in (1 Samuel 23:11-12).

The subject is hē graphē, "the scripture," personified as at verse 8.

Ta panta is neuter plural, "all things" -- broader than "all people." The confinement is comprehensive rather than a statement about individuals.

The purpose is stated: hina hē epangelia ek pisteōs Iēsou Christou dothē tois pisteuousin. The same ambiguous genitive from 2:16 recurs; the same options apply.

## Verses 23-25: The Escort

> But before faith came, we were kept in custody under law, confined until the coming faith should be revealed. So that the law has become our paidagogos unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a paidagogos.

**Verse 23: "But before faith came, we were kept in custody under law, confined until the coming faith should be revealed."**

Ephrouroumetha ("we were being guarded") is from phroureō, a military term: to garrison, to keep under guard, to post a watch. It is worth knowing that this verb does not settle its own connotation. Paul uses it at Philippians 4:7 in an unambiguously *protective* sense: "the peace of God... will guard (phrourēsei) your hearts and minds in Christ Jesus." A garrison may confine a prisoner or protect a city; the word alone does not say which.

It is paired here with synkleiomenoi, the participle of the verb from verse 22, which does suggest confinement.

Whether the custody in view is protective (Israel kept safe until the appointed time) or restrictive (Israel held under constraint) is therefore not settled by the vocabulary, and the choice colours everything that follows in the paragraph.

The temporal frame is notable: pro tou elthein tēn pistin ("before the faith came") and eis tēn mellousan pistin apokalyphthēnai ("until the faith about to be revealed"). "Faith" here appears with the article and functions almost as a period marker -- an era that arrived at a point in time -- rather than as an individual's act of believing. Something came; before it came, things were arranged one way.

**Verses 24-25: "So that the law has become our paidagogos unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a paidagogos."**

**What a paidagogos was.** This needs setting out plainly, because the English "tutor," "schoolmaster" and "guardian" all mislead, and the image is doing the argument's work.

In a Greco-Roman household of any means, the paidagōgos was a **slave** -- typically an older, trusted one -- assigned to a boy from roughly the age of six until he came of age in his mid-teens. His job was custodial and disciplinary, not educational. He walked the boy to the schoolmaster and home again, carried his things, kept him out of trouble, supervised his manners, and administered correction. He was frequently depicted in ancient art with a staff or cane. He did *not* give the lessons; the didaskalos did that.

Two things follow, and both bear on the argument.

*The paidagōgos was not the teacher.* He was the escort who got the boy to the teacher.

*His term ended at the boy's majority, and it ended by the calendar rather than by any failure on his part.* Nothing about the paidagōgos being discharged implies he was bad at his job, or that the household repudiated him. The boy grew up. That is all.

**Eis Christon ("unto Christ")** can be read two ways and the choice matters.

*Temporally:* "until Christ." The paidagōgos's term ran until the Messiah came.

*Purposively:* "to lead us to Christ." The law's function was to bring people to Christ -- and, in the traditional Protestant development of this reading, to do so by exposing sin and thereby driving a person to seek grace.

The evidence favours the temporal reading. Verse 25 says the paidagōgos's term has **ended** now that faith has come, which is a statement about time and not about pedagogy. The parallel with achris hou at 3:19 ("until the offspring should come") points the same way. And the household image itself resists the purposive reading: the escort did not teach the lesson or convince the boy of anything; he delivered him to the door.

**Note also what verse 25 says and does not say.** It does not say the law is abolished, or ended, or set aside. It says "we are no longer **under** a paidagōgos" -- ouketi hypo paidagōgon esmen. What has ended is a position under a supervision.

**The pronouns, and the problem they pose.** Verses 23-25 are first person plural throughout: *we* were kept in custody; *we* are no longer under a paidagōgos. Verse 26 shifts abruptly to the second person: "for *you* are all sons of God."

The most natural reading of the shift is that "we" designates Jews and "you" the Galatian Gentile recipients -- which would mean that the custody and its ending are stated of Jews, with Paul including himself. The alternative is that "we" is a rhetorical inclusive which Paul then narrows for emphasis.

The shift is real on any account, and any reading of the passage has to say what the ending of the paidagōgos's term consists of for the group Paul places himself in.

## Verses 26-29: Sons, Offspring, and Heirs

> For you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus. For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ. There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus. And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise.

**Verse 26: "For you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus."**

Pantes gar huioi theou este. The pronoun becomes second person and pantes ("all") is emphatic.

**Sonship of God is Israel's designation in the scriptures**, and the reader should hear it as such rather than as a general religious idea. "Israel is my firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22). "You are the sons of the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 14:1). "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1). "I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is my firstborn" (Jeremiah 31:9). To tell Gentile congregations that they are all sons of God is to apply Israel's own family language to them.

**Verse 27: "For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ."**

Christon enedysasthe ("you put on Christ"), from endyō, "to clothe, to put on a garment."

The clothing metaphor is well established in the Greek scriptures for being invested with a quality or condition: "he has clothed me with the garments of salvation" (Isaiah 61:10); "I put on righteousness, and it clothed me" (Job 29:14); "let your priests be clothed with righteousness" (Psalm 132:9); and, of God's own equipping, "he put on righteousness as a breastplate" (Isaiah 59:17). To put on Christ is to be invested with him -- to appear, and to be dealt with, as what one now wears.

Baptism is named here as the point at which this incorporation occurred, without further elaboration. The letter does not develop it, and nothing in the argument turns on the mechanics.

**Verse 28: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus."**

**The three pairs, and the different construction of the third.** The first two are joined by oude ("nor"): Ioudaios oude Hellēn; doulos oude eleutheros. The third uses kai ("and"): arsen kai thēly.

That change is not random. Arsen kai thēly is the wording of **Genesis 1:27** in the Greek version: arsen kai thēly epoiēsen autous, "male and female he made them." The third pair is quoted from the creation account, which points the formula back past Abraham, past Sinai, to the making of humanity.

**What ouk eni denies.** The verb eni is a shortened form of enestin, "there is in" -- so the statement is that within the sphere named, en Christō Iēsou, these distinctions are not present.

The question is what kind of absence is meant, and the text requires care here.

The distinctions plainly continue to exist as facts. Paul goes on addressing slaves and free people, men and women, Jews and Greeks throughout his correspondence as the categories they are. This very letter's entire argument requires that Jews and Gentiles remain distinguishable -- there would be nothing to dispute otherwise, and Paul describes himself as a Jew by birth at 2:15 without apology.

What the formula most naturally denies, then, is their bearing on **standing within the sphere named**: none of the three determines who is a son of God, who has put on Christ, who inherits. That is what the surrounding verses are about -- 26 and 27 before it, 29 after it.

Whether more than that is intended -- whether the formula also envisages the eventual dissolution of the distinctions themselves, or their preservation within a unity that does not rank them -- the verse does not say. It says what does not obtain, and then it says something positive: pantes gar hymeis heis este en Christō Iēsou. And the "one" is heis, masculine singular: not "one thing," but one person. The community is described as a single individual.

**Verse 29: "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise."**

The chapter closes on the terms with which its scriptural argument began.

Sperma ("offspring") is the word from verse 16, now used collectively of a whole community -- which is precisely the structure the singular argument was built to produce. The promise ran to one; the many belong to it by belonging to him. Verse 16 and verse 29 are the two halves of a single move, and neither works without the other.

Kat' epangelian klēronomoi ("heirs according to promise") joins the two categories that have governed everything since verse 18: the route to the inheritance is the promise, not the law.

And the qualification is stated in two words: **hymeis Christou**, "you are Christ's." That is the whole condition. Circumcision does not appear in the answer to the question the chapter set out to settle.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. **The argument begins from evidence rather than from authority, and this is deliberate.** Verses 2 and 5 pose the same disjunction twice: did the Spirit come by works of law or by a hearing of faith? The Galatians can answer that from their own history, without consulting anyone, and the answer preceded any question of circumcision. Paul had ruled out his own apostolic standing as a criterion at 1:8; what remains is what his readers can check. The whole scriptural argument that follows is offered in support of a conclusion they already possess the evidence for.

2. **Abraham is claimed from Genesis 15 rather than Genesis 17, and the sequence is the argument.** If the teachers in Galatia were pointing to the covenant sign given to Abraham's household, Paul answers by going two chapters earlier, to the point at which Abraham is reckoned righteous by believing and no sign yet exists. And he adds the promise's own stated scope: "in you shall all the nations be blessed" (3:8). The inclusion of Gentiles is not an extension of the Abrahamic covenant; it is what the covenant announced.

3. **Paul reshapes three of his six citations, and each change is what makes the citation work.** Deuteronomy 27:26 is given with "the book of the law" imported from later in Deuteronomy. Habakkuk 2:4 is quoted with the pronoun removed, so that "by *my* faithfulness" becomes simply "by faith" -- at the one point where he could have specified whose faith, he declined. Deuteronomy 21:23 is given with its verb changed to match the curse-word of 3:10, welding the two curses into one, and with "by God" deleted. A reader who checks the sources against the letter will find this pattern throughout, and it began at 2:16.

4. **The logic of 3:10 rests on a premise the letter never states, and the candidates are not equally placed.** Three were set out above: that nobody keeps the whole law; that the covenant's curse stands unresolved so that taking the badge now means taking the outstanding judgment; that the curse falls on assuming the obligation without the doing. The second and third are supported from within the letter and from the passage being quoted. The first is supported chiefly by its dominance in Western reading since the sixteenth century, and it must be held against Deuteronomy 30:11-14 in the same covenant block, against Paul's own "blameless" at Philippians 3:6, against Luke 1:6, and against the Torah's atonement provisions -- a burden the other two do not carry. This is the single most consequential interpretive decision in the letter, and the most familiar answer is the one with the most to answer for.

5. **3:21 is the letter's clearest statement of the law's remit, and it is made in the law's defence.** "If a law had been given that was able to make alive, righteousness would indeed have been by law" -- a contrary-to-fact conditional, so no such law was given. And the sentence answers the charge that the law stands *against* God's promises: it does not, because it was not issued for the promise's work. This is a claim about purpose, not about performance, and the two should not be run together.

6. **The law is described as an addition with a term, and every formulation ends a relation rather than the thing.** Prosetethē ("added") makes Sinai an addendum to an instrument already in force. Achris hou ("until") limits the function just named. And 3:25 says "we are no longer **under** a paidagōgos" -- not that the law has ceased. The consistency of this across the chapter is a datum, whatever weight one finally places on it.

7. **The paidagogos was a household slave who escorted a boy to school, not the teacher, and his term ended at the boy's majority.** This tells against reading eis Christon purposively -- as the law exposing sin and driving one to grace -- because verse 25 makes the ending temporal ("now that faith has come") and because the escort in the image did not teach anything. Chapter 4:1-2 will confirm the reading by continuing the same household metaphor with the heir reaching the date his father set.

8. **The pronoun shift at 3:23-26 is the passage's hardest datum and should not be smoothed over.** "We" are held in custody and released; "you" are sons of God. If "we" means Jews, then the ending of the custodial arrangement is stated of Jews, Paul included -- a stronger claim than anything in 2:15-16, and any account of the chapter has to say what that ending consists of.

9. **3:28 states what does not obtain in Christ without stating what becomes of the distinctions themselves.** The change of conjunction in the third pair quotes Genesis 1:27 and points the formula back to creation. The categories continue to exist -- the letter's own argument requires Jews and Gentiles to remain distinguishable -- and what the formula most naturally denies is their bearing on standing. Whether more is intended, the verse does not say, and readers should notice when an interpretation has supplied an answer the text withholds.

10. **The chapter ends where it needed to arrive, and the answer contains no rite.** The question was who is Abraham's offspring and therefore who inherits. The answer: those who are Christ's -- because the promise ran to one offspring, and belonging to him makes the many heirs. Verse 16 and verse 29 are the two halves of that single move.
