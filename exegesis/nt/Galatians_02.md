# Galatians 2 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**Where this stands in the letter:** Chapter 1 argued one half of Paul's case about his own standing -- that his message did not come from Jerusalem, because for years he was not there and when he finally went he stayed a fortnight and met two men. Chapter 2 argues the other half: when he did eventually lay his gospel before the Jerusalem leadership, they did not correct it.

The chapter has two narrative scenes and then a theological statement that grows directly out of the second.

The first scene (2:1-10) is a meeting in Jerusalem, fourteen years after the events of chapter 1. Paul goes up "because of a revelation," presents the gospel he preaches among the Gentiles, and reports two outcomes. A test case is decided: Titus, an uncircumcised Greek who came with him, was not compelled to be circumcised. And a division of spheres is agreed and sealed with a handshake: Paul to the uncircumcised, Peter to the circumcised.

The second scene (2:11-14) goes the other way. At Antioch, Paul does not receive confirmation from Peter but confronts him. Peter had been eating with Gentile believers; when men arrived from James, he withdrew. Paul's charge is that the withdrawal, whatever Peter intended by it, functions as compulsion: it leaves Gentile believers with only one route back to full fellowship, and that route is conversion.

From 2:15 the letter's theological argument begins, and it does not stop until the end of chapter 4.

**Where does the speech to Peter end?** Greek manuscripts of this period were written without quotation marks, without spaces between words, and without the punctuation that would mark where a quotation closes. The point at which Paul's reported words to Cephas end and his address to the Galatians begins is therefore not marked in the text, and it must be inferred. Verse 14 is a natural stopping point, since it completes the rebuke as a question. Verse 21 is also natural, since 2:15-21 reads as a continuous argument. Interpreters differ, and the letter's substance is not affected either way -- except that on the longer reading, the whole of 2:15-21 is addressed to a Jewish apostle rather than to Gentile readers, which affects how one hears its repeated "we."

**What the author argues immediately after (Galatians 3):** Chapter 3 turns from Peter to the Galatians directly -- "O foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you?" (3:1) -- and immediately grounds the argument in something they can verify from their own history: did they receive the Spirit by works of law or by a hearing of faith (3:2)? From there the argument moves to Abraham, who was reckoned righteous by believing before circumcision existed (3:6-9); to the curse and the blessing (3:10-14); to the priority of the promise over the law (3:15-18); to the law's purpose and its term (3:19-25); and to the identity of Abraham's offspring (3:26-29). The three contested terms introduced in 2:16 -- "works of law," "justified," "faith of Jesus Christ" -- carry into all of it.

**How chapter 2 connects to what follows:** The Antioch scene supplies the concrete case from which the theological argument is generalized. What happened at a dinner table in a Syrian city becomes an argument about how anyone at all is reckoned to be in the right with God. The transition is made at 2:15-16, which states its central proposition not as a discovery but as something already held in common -- "knowing that..." -- and chapter 3 then demonstrates from scripture what chapter 2 has assumed.

## Verses 1-5: The Journey and the Test Case

> Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me. I went up because of a revelation, and I set before them the gospel that I preach among the Gentiles, but privately before those who seemed influential, lest somehow I should be running, or had run, in vain. But not even Titus, who was with me, though he was a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised -- yet because of false brothers secretly brought in, who slipped in to spy out our freedom that we have in Christ Jesus, so that they might bring us into slavery -- to them we did not yield in submission even for an hour, so that the truth of the gospel might remain with you.

**Verse 1: "Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me."**

The chronology continues the argument of chapter 1. Dia dekatessaron eton ("after fourteen years") is a second long interval, and it is the second time Paul has established that a great deal of time passed without Jerusalem's involvement. Whether the fourteen years are counted from the call or from the first visit of 1:18 is not specified; on either reckoning the period between Paul's commission and any substantive Jerusalem consultation is very long.

Two companions are named, and both are chosen for what they demonstrate.

**Barnabas** appears in Acts as Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, given the name Barnabas by the apostles and glossed there as "son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36). He is the man who vouched for Paul in Jerusalem when the disciples were afraid of him (Acts 9:27), and who fetched Paul from Tarsus to Antioch (Acts 11:25-26). He is a figure trusted on both sides -- Jerusalem's man and Paul's partner -- and his presence at this meeting means Paul did not go alone or unvouched.

**Titus** appears nowhere in Acts. He is named here and at 2:3, and elsewhere in Paul's correspondence as a co-worker entrusted with delicate missions (2 Corinthians 2:13; 7:6-15; 8:6, 16-23; 2 Timothy 4:10; Titus 1:4). What matters here is a single fact stated in verse 3: he is a Greek, and he was uncircumcised. Paul brought him to Jerusalem, which means Paul brought the question in the flesh, as a man rather than as a proposition.

**Verse 2: "I went up because of a revelation, and I set before them the gospel that I preach among the Gentiles, but privately before those who seemed influential, lest somehow I should be running, or had run, in vain."**

"Because of a revelation" (kata apokalypsin) states the reason for the journey, and it is doing work in the argument. Paul was not summoned; he did not go because Jerusalem sent for him, and he did not go to submit to review. The initiative is presented as God's. The word apokalypsis is the same one used at 1:12 for the origin of his gospel.

"I set before them" is anethemēn autois, from anatithēmi -- "to lay before, to set out, to place before someone for consideration." This is the simple form of the compound prosanatithēmi that appeared at 1:16 ("I did not confer with flesh and blood") and will appear again at 2:6 ("they added nothing to me"). The distinction Paul is maintaining across the two chapters is precise: he laid his message out before them; he did not submit it to them for correction.

"To those who seemed influential" is tois dokousin, literally "to the ones seeming" or "to the ones of repute." The participle occurs three times in this passage (2:2, 6, 9) and its tone is disputed. It may be entirely neutral -- "the recognized leaders," a normal way of designating those in authority. It may carry a faint edge, echoing the way Paul's opponents in Galatia had been invoking these men's reputations. The text does not settle it, and readers should be wary of hearing sarcasm that may not be there.

"Lest somehow I should be running, or had run, in vain" (mē pōs eis kenon trechō ē edramon). The metaphor is athletic -- the footrace, a familiar image in a Greek city. Paul uses running for his apostolic labour at 5:7 ("you were running well"), Philippians 2:16 and 1 Corinthians 9:24-26.

The concern needs careful statement, because the natural reading is the wrong one. Paul is not saying that Jerusalem's approval would determine whether his gospel was true; the whole argument of chapter 1 denies that anyone could adjudicate that. What is at stake is whether the mission would hold together. If Jerusalem repudiated his Gentile congregations, there would be two movements rather than one, and his converts would be permanently second-class in the eyes of the movement's mother community. The race is run in vain not if the message was wrong but if the result is fractured.

**Verse 3: "But not even Titus, who was with me, though he was a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised."**

This is the test case, and Paul states it in the narrowest possible terms.

Titus is identified as Hellēn, "a Greek" -- which in this context means a Gentile, someone outside the covenant people, and not merely someone from Greece. The point is his uncircumcision.

The verb is ēnankasthē, aorist passive of anankazō, "to compel, to force, to constrain." The word will recur twice more in the letter and both occurrences matter. At 2:14 Paul asks Peter how he can "compel the Gentiles to Judaize." At 6:12 he says the other teachers "compel you to be circumcised." All three uses concern pressure applied to Gentiles over circumcision. Whatever else Paul is objecting to, he objects to that verb.

Note what the sentence does not say. It does not say the question was never raised -- the "but not even" implies that it might have been, and verses 4-5 indicate there was a fight. It says only that no compulsion resulted. Titus went to Jerusalem uncircumcised and left uncircumcised.

**Verses 4-5: "Yet because of false brothers secretly brought in, who slipped in to spy out our freedom that we have in Christ Jesus, so that they might bring us into slavery -- to them we did not yield in submission even for an hour, so that the truth of the gospel might remain with you."**

The Greek here is broken. The sentence begins with a subordinate clause and never completes its main construction -- what grammarians call an anacoluthon, a sentence that changes course and leaves its opening stranded. It happens in Paul at moments of high feeling, and it happens again at 2:6. Translations tidy it; the original does not.

The opponents are described with two rare compounds, both built on para-, "alongside," and both suggesting entry that was not authorized.

Pareisaktous means "brought in secretly, smuggled in, introduced under false pretences." Pareisēlthon means "they came in alongside, they slipped in." Whether they were brought in by someone or came on their own, the picture is of infiltration rather than of a party arriving openly to make a case.

"To spy out" is kataskopēsai, from kataskopeō -- a term from military reconnaissance and espionage. The related noun is what Israel's scriptures use for the men sent to reconnoitre the land: the spies of Numbers 21:32, and the two Joshua sent to Jericho who lodged with Rahab (Joshua 2:1; 6:22-25). The word implies covert assessment of something in order to act against it.

What they came to assess is tēn eleutherian hēmōn ("our freedom"). This is the letter's first use of the word, and it is worth registering how early it appears. Freedom and slavery are established as the terms of this dispute in chapter 2, before a single argument about law has been made. They will govern 4:1-11, dominate 4:21-31, and produce the letter's central imperative at 5:1.

Their intent is hina hēmas katadoulōsousin ("so that they might enslave us"). Katadouloō is an intensified form of douloō, "to enslave" -- to bring thoroughly into bondage.

"We did not yield in submission even for an hour" (hois oude pros hōran eixamen tē hypotagē). The verb eikō means "to give way, to yield ground," as one yields in a physical contest. Hypotagē is "subjection, submission." Pros hōran, "for an hour," means for the briefest moment.

The clause that closes the sentence -- hina hē alētheia tou euangeliou diameinē pros hymas, "so that the truth of the gospel might remain with you" -- reaches forward across fourteen years and several hundred miles. The Galatians are named as the beneficiaries of a stand taken before their congregations existed. The phrase "the truth of the gospel" will recur at 2:14, where Paul says Peter and the others were not walking straight toward it.

## Verses 6-10: What Jerusalem Added

> And from those who seemed to be something -- what they were makes no difference to me; God shows no partiality -- those, I say, who seemed influential added nothing to me. On the contrary, when they saw that I had been entrusted with the gospel to the uncircumcised, just as Peter had been entrusted with the gospel to the circumcised -- for he who worked through Peter for his apostleship to the circumcised worked also through me for the Gentiles -- and when James and Cephas and John, who seemed to be pillars, perceived the grace that was given to me, they gave to me and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship, that we should go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised. Only they asked us to remember the poor, the very thing I was eager to do.

**Verse 6: "And from those who seemed to be something -- what they were makes no difference to me; God shows no partiality -- those, I say, who seemed influential added nothing to me."**

The sentence breaks again, interrupted by a parenthesis, and then resumes with its subject restated.

The parenthesis contains an idiom worth unpacking. Prosōpon ho theos anthrōpou ou lambanei is, word for word, "God does not receive the face of a man." This is a Hebrew expression (nasa' panim, "to lift the face") rendered into Greek with the words but not the syntax of Greek, and it belongs to the vocabulary of judicial impartiality. Its background is in the law itself: "You shall do no injustice in court. You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great" (Leviticus 19:15); "the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, the mighty and the awesome God, who is not partial and takes no bribe" (Deuteronomy 10:17); "with the LORD our God there is no injustice or partiality or taking of bribes" (2 Chronicles 19:7); "who shows no partiality to princes, nor regards the rich more than the poor" (Job 34:19).

The idiom therefore does two things at once. It states a principle drawn from Torah, and it applies that principle to the very men whose status Paul's opponents have been invoking. If God does not weigh a man's standing, neither will Paul.

The main clause is the point: emoi gar hoi dokountes ouden prosanethento -- "to me the influential ones added nothing." The verb is prosanatithēmi again, and here it carries the other sense available to it: "to add, to contribute, to impose in addition." The two chapters therefore make a matched pair with one word. Paul did not go to them to have a matter laid before them for judgment (1:16); and when he came, they laid nothing additional on him (2:6).

What is being denied is specific. Not that they said nothing, not that no discussion occurred, but that no requirement was added to what Paul was already preaching.

**Verses 7-8: "On the contrary, when they saw that I had been entrusted with the gospel to the uncircumcised, just as Peter had been entrusted with the gospel to the circumcised -- for he who worked through Peter for his apostleship to the circumcised worked also through me for the Gentiles."**

The construction here is precise and is often misread, so it repays slow attention.

The Greek is pepisteumai to euangelion tēs akrobystias kathōs Petros tēs peritomēs -- literally, "I have been entrusted with the gospel of the uncircumcision, just as Peter with that of the circumcision."

Read carelessly, that sounds like two gospels: a Gentile one and a Jewish one. It cannot mean that, and the reason is fifteen verses earlier. At 1:6-9 Paul has just said there is no second gospel and pronounced a curse twice on anyone who preaches one. He is not now conceding two.

The genitives are therefore best read as designating the *audience* rather than the content: the gospel *for* the uncircumcised and the gospel *for* the circumcised. What is divided is not the message but the mission field. The nouns "uncircumcision" (akrobystia) and "circumcision" (peritomē) are being used, as they often are in this period, as collective terms for the two groups of humanity as Jews divided them -- Gentiles and Jews.

The ground for the arrangement follows in verse 8, and it is not administrative. Ho gar energēsas Petrō... enērgēsen kai emoi -- "for the one who worked in Peter... worked also in me." The verb energeō means "to be operative, to be at work, to produce an effect." The same God is described as the effective agent in both missions. What Jerusalem did, on this account, was recognize something that was already the case rather than authorize something new.

**Verse 9: "And when James and Cephas and John, who seemed to be pillars, perceived the grace that was given to me, they gave to me and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship, that we should go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised."**

Three men are named, with James first -- which is notable, since Peter is the figure Paul has just been discussing and the one who will dominate the following scene. Whatever the order signifies about relative standing in Jerusalem, Paul does not comment on it.

"Pillars" is styloi, and the metaphor is architectural. The most resonant background is the temple: Solomon set up two bronze pillars at the vestibule of the house, named Jachin and Boaz (1 Kings 7:15-22; 2 Chronicles 3:15-17). To call the leaders of the Jerusalem community "pillars" is to picture them as the supports of a structure, and the temple association is close at hand for anyone thinking of a community as God's dwelling.

"They perceived the grace that was given to me" -- gnontes tēn charin tēn dotheisan moi. What they recognized is not Paul's competence, credentials or arguments, but a gift.

"The right hand of fellowship" (dexias... koinōnias) is a gesture of compact. Extending the right hand to seal an agreement is attested across the ancient Mediterranean and in Jewish sources (1 Maccabees 6:58; 11:50, 62; 13:50). Koinōnia is not merely goodwill; it denotes partnership, having something in common, a joint enterprise with shared stakes.

The content of the agreement is a division of destinations: hēmeis eis ta ethnē, autoi de eis tēn peritomēn ("we to the Gentiles, they to the circumcised"). Two spheres of mission are recognized within one movement.

**Verse 10: "Only they asked us to remember the poor, the very thing I was eager to do."**

Monon ("only") introduces the sole stipulation, and it is worth dwelling on what it is and what it is not.

What it is: material support. "Remember the poor" in this context is not a request for sympathy but for money. The collection for the poor among the saints in Jerusalem occupies a large place in Paul's later correspondence -- two full chapters of 2 Corinthians (8-9), instructions in 1 Corinthians 16:1-4, and a report of its delivery in Romans 15:25-28.

What it is not: any requirement touching circumcision, food, calendar or covenant status. This is the argumentative payoff of the whole scene. At a meeting where the question was whether Gentile believers must become Jews, with an uncircumcised Greek standing in the room, the leadership of the Jerusalem community imposed exactly one obligation on the Gentile mission, and it was charitable.

"The very thing I was eager to do" (ho kai espoudasa auto touto poiēsai) uses spoudazō, "to be earnest, to make every effort." Paul accepts the one stipulation with enthusiasm, which is itself part of the point: he is not describing a man who rejects Jerusalem, but a man who owes them nothing doctrinally and gives them everything he can materially.

## Verses 11-14: Antioch

> But when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned. For before certain men came from James, he was eating with the Gentiles; but when they came, he drew back and separated himself, fearing those of the circumcision. And the rest of the Jews joined him in this hypocrisy, so that even Barnabas was carried away by their hypocrisy. But when I saw that they were not walking straight toward the truth of the gospel, I said to Cephas before them all: "If you, being a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you compel the Gentiles to Judaize?"

**Verse 11: "But when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned."**

The scene reverses the one before it. In Jerusalem, Paul's gospel was recognized by Cephas among others; in Antioch, Paul opposes the same man.

Antioch on the Orontes was the third city of the Roman empire, with a large Jewish population and, by this period, a mixed congregation of Jewish and Gentile believers. Acts 11:19-26 describes its founding and says that it was there that the disciples were first called Christianoi.

"I opposed him to his face" is kata prosōpon autō antestēn. The idiom kata prosōpon ("face to face, in person, publicly") is the same expression that appeared in the parenthesis at 2:6 in the idiom for partiality -- there of God not receiving a man's face, here of Paul confronting one directly. Antistēmi means "to set oneself against, to withstand." The confrontation was open, not private.

"Because he stood condemned" is hoti kategnōsmenos ēn -- a perfect passive participle with the verb "to be," describing a settled state rather than an action. Kataginōskō means "to find fault with, to condemn, to give a verdict against." Paul notably does not say "because I condemned him"; the passive leaves the verdict standing on its own, as though the facts convicted Peter without Paul's needing to.

**Verse 12: "For before certain men came from James, he was eating with the Gentiles; but when they came, he drew back and separated himself, fearing those of the circumcision."**

"He was eating with the Gentiles" -- meta tōn ethnōn synēsthien. The tense is imperfect, which in Greek describes ongoing or repeated action in the past. This was not a single meal. Peter's regular practice in Antioch was to eat at mixed tables with Gentile believers.

The change is described with two more imperfects, which makes it a process rather than a single decision: hypestellen kai aphōrizen heauton ("he was drawing back and separating himself"). Hypostellō is used of furling a sail, of drawing back troops, of withdrawing from an exposed position. Aphorizō is "to mark off by a boundary, to separate, to set apart" -- and it is precisely the verb Paul used of himself at 1:15, where God "set me apart from my mother's womb" for the Gentile mission. The same verb that named Paul's commissioning toward Gentiles now names Peter's withdrawal from them.

The stated cause is phoboumenos tous ek peritomēs ("fearing those of the circumcision"). Not conviction, not obedience, not a scruple: fear. Who "those of the circumcision" are -- the men from James, a wider party in Jerusalem, or Jewish opinion in Judea that would hear of it -- the text does not specify.

**What is actually at issue in this scene.** This needs stating plainly, because a reader who assumes the dispute is about food will misread the whole chapter.

The written Torah legislates extensively about *what* an Israelite may eat -- the clean and unclean creatures of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14, the prohibition of blood (Leviticus 17:10-14), the treatment of what dies of itself. It contains no commandment against eating in the company of Gentiles.

The restriction Peter is honouring appears in his own mouth in Acts 10:28: "You yourselves know how unlawful it is for a Jew to associate with or to visit anyone of another nation." The word rendered "unlawful" there is athemiton, which does not mean "contrary to Torah" -- it means something closer to "not done, taboo, contrary to established custom" -- and Peter cites no commandment, because there is none to cite.

Separation at table was nonetheless a real and widespread practice, and it had real reasons behind it: the difficulty of knowing whether meat had been slaughtered acceptably or offered to an idol, whether wine had been poured out in libation, whether vessels were clean. Jewish sources of the period reflect the practice (Jubilees 22:16 warns against eating with Gentiles; Josephus, Antiquities 14.245-246, records arrangements securing the right of Jews in Gentile cities to follow their own food customs). But it is custom and its rationale, not a written commandment, and that distinction is what makes Paul's next sentence possible.

The text does not say which aspect was at issue in Antioch -- the food served, the company kept, or the vessels used. What it says is that a practice Peter had been following changed when observers arrived, and that Paul calls the change by a name that is not "obedience."

**Verse 13: "And the rest of the Jews joined him in this hypocrisy, so that even Barnabas was carried away by their hypocrisy."**

Synypekrithēsan is a compound of syn- ("together with") and hypokrinomai, whose root sense is theatrical: to answer from behind a mask, to play a part on stage. A hypokritēs was an actor. The charge Paul is making is therefore precise, and it is not that Peter changed his mind. It is that Peter's behaviour no longer matched his conviction -- that he was playing a role.

"Even Barnabas" (kai Barnabas) registers a loss. Barnabas had gone to Jerusalem with Paul (2:1), had been included in the handshake (2:9), and had been his partner in the Gentile mission. Synapēchthē ("was carried away with") suggests being swept along by a current rather than choosing a course.

**Verse 14: "But when I saw that they were not walking straight toward the truth of the gospel, I said to Cephas before them all: 'If you, being a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you compel the Gentiles to Judaize?'"**

"They were not walking straight" is orthopodousin, from orthos ("straight, upright") and pous ("foot"). The word appears nowhere else in the New Testament, nowhere in the Greek scriptures, and in no earlier Greek literature that survives. Paul appears to have coined it. The image is of a party that is not holding a straight line toward a destination.

"Before them all" (emprosthen pantōn) matches the offence: a public withdrawal is answered by a public rebuke.

The rebuke itself has two halves and each is difficult in its own way.

*First half:* ei sy Ioudaios hyparchōn ethnikōs kai ouchi Ioudaikōs zēs -- "if you, being a Jew, live in a Gentile manner and not in a Jewish manner." The two adverbs, ethnikōs and Ioudaikōs, occur nowhere else in the New Testament.

The condition is stated as a fact, not a hypothesis: Paul is describing what Peter was doing before the men from James arrived. And this is the striking thing about the sentence. It is stated without any hint of censure. It is not the accusation; it is the *premise from which the accusation proceeds*. Paul takes Peter's mixed-table practice as the position consistent with the truth of the gospel, and treats the withdrawal as the deviation from it. Whatever "living like a Gentile" meant in practice -- and the text does not tell us how far it went -- Paul regarded it as the correct behaviour of a Jewish apostle among Gentile believers.

*Second half:* pōs ta ethnē anankazeis ioudaizein -- "how do you compel the Gentiles to Judaize?"

The verb ioudaizō means to adopt Jewish practice, to live as a Jew. Its most instructive occurrence anywhere is Esther 8:17 in the Greek version, which describes what happened after Mordecai's elevation: "and many of the Gentiles were circumcised and Judaized (perietemnonto kai ioudaizon) for fear of the Jews." There the verb is paired directly with circumcision and describes Gentiles taking on Jewish identity under pressure. That is exactly the situation Paul is describing, including the pressure.

**How the charge works.** Peter has issued no command. He has not told anyone to be circumcised. Paul's accusation is nevertheless "you are compelling," and the logic is worth making explicit because it is the letter's most concrete illustration of what it is arguing against.

When the senior apostle withdraws from the common table, the Gentile believers are not merely inconvenienced. They are placed outside the fellowship of the community's recognized leadership. There remains exactly one route back to full inclusion, and it runs through becoming Jews. No one has to say so. The structure of the situation says it.

That is why the verb is anankazō, "compel" -- the same verb used of Titus at 2:3 and of the Galatian teachers at 6:12. Pressure exerted by circumstance is what the letter opposes, and Paul is prepared to name a friend's social withdrawal as an instance of it.

## Verses 15-16: What Jews Know

> We ourselves are Jews by birth and not Gentile sinners; yet knowing that a person is not justified by works of law but through faith of Jesus Christ, even we believed in Christ Jesus, in order that we might be justified by faith of Christ and not by works of law, because by works of law no flesh will be justified.

This sentence is the hinge of the letter. Everything before it is narrative; everything after it is argument. It also contains three expressions whose meaning is contested and on which the reading of the entire letter turns. Each is set out below with the evidence available for deciding it.

**Verse 15: "We ourselves are Jews by birth and not Gentile sinners."**

Hēmeis physei Ioudaioi kai ouk ex ethnōn hamartōloi. Physei means "by nature, by birth" -- Jews by descent rather than by conversion, which is a distinction that matters in a letter about proselytes.

"Gentile sinners" is the insider's idiom, and Paul uses it without softening. In this usage a Gentile is a "sinner" by definition and by category rather than by conduct: outside the covenant, without the Torah, and therefore not living the ordered life the Torah sets out. The usage is well attested: "they fell into the hands of the Gentiles" is paralleled with sinners at 1 Maccabees 2:44 ("they struck down sinners in their anger and lawless men in their wrath"); the Psalms of Solomon set "sinners" against the righteous of Israel (1:1; 2:1-2); and the Gospels preserve the pairing "tax collectors and sinners."

Paul quotes the category and then, two verses later, destabilizes it: at 2:17 "we ourselves were found to be sinners." The sentence is built to be undermined.

**Verse 16, first term: erga nomou, "works of law."**

The phrase appears three times in this verse -- it is the most repeated expression in the sentence -- and it is the letter's central contested term. Two readings are current, and the evidence for each should be stated fairly.

*Reading one: the practices that mark the boundary between Jew and Gentile.* On this account, "works of law" denotes not obedience in general but the observances by which Jewish identity is publicly maintained -- circumcision, food laws, sabbath and festival calendar.

The evidence: a document from Qumran, conventionally called 4QMMT, uses the Hebrew phrase miqsat ma'ase ha-torah, "some of the works of the Torah," for a specific set of halakhic rulings on purity, sacrifice and marriage that distinguish the writers' practice from that of their opponents. The phrase in a first-century Jewish setting is thus attested for a defined subset of Torah practice functioning as a boundary, rather than for obedience as a whole. Supporting this from the letter itself: the presenting issues in Galatia are circumcision (2:3; 5:2-3; 6:12-13), table fellowship (2:11-14) and calendar (4:10) -- which is to say, precisely the boundary markers, and precisely the matters at stake in the scene that has just been narrated.

*Reading two: Torah-doing in general.* On this account, "works of law" means anything done in obedience to the law considered as a basis for standing before God.

The evidence: at 3:10 Paul quotes Deuteronomy 27:26 with its reference to "all things written in the book of the law," which is not a subset; and at 5:3 he says the circumcised man is a debtor "to do the whole law," which likewise embraces everything.

The two readings are not always as far apart as the debate suggests, since on either account what Paul opposes is a route to standing rather than obedience as such. But they diverge in what they imply about the letter's scope, and the reader should decide deliberately rather than by default.

**Verse 16, second term: dikaioō, "to justify."**

In the Greek scriptures this is a courtroom word. It means to declare in the right, to give a favourable verdict, to acquit -- and it is emphatically not a word for making someone good.

The usage is clearest where a judicial setting is explicit. "You shall not justify (dikaiōseis) the wicked for a bribe" (Exodus 23:7). Of judges: "they shall justify the righteous and condemn the guilty" (Deuteronomy 25:1) -- where the pairing with condemnation makes the forensic sense unmistakable. "Woe to those who justify the wicked for a bribe and take away the justice of the righteous" (Isaiah 5:23). "He who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous are both alike an abomination to the LORD" (Proverbs 17:15). In none of these does the verb mean to make a wicked man good; it means to pronounce him in the right, which is exactly why doing it wrongly is an abomination.

So to be "justified" is to have a verdict pronounced in one's favour: to be reckoned, declared, counted as being in the right.

What the verse does not specify is *when*. Paul uses this vocabulary in both directions elsewhere -- as accomplished ("having been justified by faith, we have peace with God," Romans 5:1) and as awaited ("the doers of the law will be justified," Romans 2:13; and in this letter, "we await the hope of righteousness," Galatians 5:5). Whether the verdict in view at 2:16 is a present reckoning of who belongs to God's people or a final acquittal at the end is not stated here, and the letter does not resolve it in one direction.

**Verse 16, third term: pistis Iēsou Christou, "faith of Jesus Christ."**

The Greek is a genitive construction, and Greek genitives are ambiguous in a way English possessives are not. The phrase can mean:

*Faith directed toward Jesus Christ* -- an objective genitive, where Christ is the object of the believer's trust. This is the traditional English rendering, "faith in Jesus Christ."

*The faithfulness of Jesus Christ* -- a subjective genitive, where Christ is the one who is faithful, and his faithfulness is what secures the verdict.

There is a structural argument for the second, and it lies inside this verse. Paul writes: "even we **believed in Christ Jesus** (kai hēmeis eis Christon Iēsoun episteusamen), in order that we might be justified **ek pisteōs Christou**." On the objective reading, that sentence says: we believed in Christ in order to be justified by believing in Christ -- which is circular, and an odd thing to write. On the subjective reading it says: we believed, in order to be justified by his faithfulness -- which is not circular and gives the clause something to do.

The argument on the other side is that Paul uses the verb "believe" with Christ as its object without difficulty, and that at 3:6-9 he develops Abraham's own act of believing as the model, which suits a reading in which the believer's faith is in view.

A third position holds that the alternatives are less separable than the debate implies. The Greek pistis renders the Hebrew emunah in the scriptures, and emunah covers trust and steadfast reliability without a seam between them -- it is used of a person's faithfulness and of their trust in God, and Hebrew does not partition the two. On this account the question "whose faith?" imposes a division the underlying category does not make. It is worth noting in this connection that at 3:11 Paul quotes Habakkuk 2:4, where the Greek version reads "by *my* faithfulness" (God's) and the Hebrew reads "by *his* faithfulness" (the righteous person's) -- and Paul drops the pronoun, quoting it simply as "by faith." He had two disambiguating options in front of him and took neither.

**The citation at the end of the verse, and Paul's modification of it.**

The last clause is scriptural: hoti ex ergōn nomou ou dikaiōthēsetai pasa sarx.

Psalm 143:2 (LXX 142:2) reads: "for no living thing (pas zōn) will be justified before you."

Paul writes: "because **by works of law** no **flesh** will be justified."

Two changes. He has inserted ex ergōn nomou, which is not in the Psalm at all -- the Psalm's statement was a general one about creatures before God and had nothing to do with law. And he has changed "living thing" to sarx, "flesh," which is a word this letter uses repeatedly for circumcision itself: "having begun by the Spirit, are you now being perfected by flesh?" (3:3); "they want to make a good showing in the flesh... that they may boast in your flesh" (6:12-13).

Whether the second change is deliberate wordplay is not certain and should not be pressed hard. The first is unquestionably deliberate, and it is the first of four adapted citations in this letter (see also 3:10, 3:11, 3:13 and 4:30). Paul is a writer who reshapes the texts he quotes so that they bear on the argument at hand, and a reader who checks his citations against their sources will find this consistently.

**One feature of the verse that any reading must account for.** The first person plural is sustained and emphatic throughout: *we* are Jews by birth, *we* know this, *even we* believed, in order that *we* might be justified. Whatever the sentence claims about works of law and justification, it claims it about Paul and Peter -- about Jews -- and not only about the Gentiles under pressure at Antioch. Readings that treat "not by works of law" as concerning Gentile admission alone have to say what this run of pronouns is doing.

## Verses 17-21: Died to Law, Alive to God

> But if, in seeking to be justified in Christ, we ourselves were found to be sinners, is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not! For if I rebuild what I tore down, I prove myself a transgressor. For through law I died to law, so that I might live to God. I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me. And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me. I do not nullify the grace of God; for if righteousness comes through law, then Christ died for nothing.

**Verse 17: "But if, in seeking to be justified in Christ, we ourselves were found to be sinners, is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not!"**

Hamartōloi ("sinners") is the word from verse 15, where it designated Gentiles as a category. Paul now applies it to "we ourselves" -- the Jews of verse 15.

The observation being made is sharp. In seeking to be reckoned right *in Christ*, Jews have placed themselves in the position they had assigned to Gentiles: needing a verdict they do not possess by birth. The line that separated the two groups has collapsed at exactly the point where justification is in view. And in the immediate context of Antioch there is a further edge: by eating with Gentiles, Peter had been living in a way his own tradition would have called the way of a "sinner."

The objection Paul raises against himself -- ara Christos hamartias diakonos ("is Christ then a servant of sin?") -- is the natural retort of someone defending the separation: if being in Christ puts a Jew in the sinners' category, then Christ is in the business of promoting sin.

Mē genoito is Paul's strongest negation, an optative construction that has no smooth English equivalent; "may it never be," "God forbid," "certainly not" all approximate it. He uses it fourteen times, ten of them in Romans, always to reject an inference drawn from his own position. It recurs in this letter at 3:21 and 6:14.

**Verse 18: "For if I rebuild what I tore down, I prove myself a transgressor."**

The metaphor is construction: what was demolished (katelysa) might be built again (oikodomō).

What was torn down is not named, and the immediate context supplies the most economical answer: the separation between Jewish and Gentile believers at the common table. It had been dismantled in Antioch -- Peter had been eating with Gentiles as a settled practice -- and Peter's withdrawal reconstructs it.

Parabatēn emauton synistanō ("I establish myself a transgressor"). Parabatēs is a legal term formed from parabainō, "to step across" -- one who crosses a line, a lawbreaker. It is not a general word for a sinner but a specific word for someone who violates a stated boundary.

The claim is arresting on any reading of the passage, and it should be allowed to be arresting. The act that constitutes the transgression is the *reimposition* of the separation, not its abandonment. The man who is defending Jewish practice in this letter says that rebuilding this particular wall would make him the lawbreaker.

**Verse 19: "For through law I died to law, so that I might live to God. I have been crucified with Christ."**

Egō gar dia nomou nomō apethanon hina theō zēsō.

The sentence turns on its first preposition. It was **dia nomou**, "through law," that the death to law occurred. Whatever this death is, the law was the instrument of it -- not the obstacle to it, not the thing escaped from by some other route, but the means.

Two readings of that instrumentality are available in the letter.

*The law's own sentence was the instrument.* At 3:13 Paul will say that Christ "became a curse for us," citing Deuteronomy 21:23 -- so the law's own curse provision is what fell, and the claim it had is thereby discharged. On this reading, the law ended its hold by its own terms rather than by being set aside.

*The law's own witness pointed beyond itself.* On this reading, attending to what the law said led Paul out from under it -- an argument the letter makes in a different form at 4:21 ("you who want to be under law, do you not hear the law?").

Then: Christō synestaurōmai -- "I have been crucified with Christ."

Two features of this clause carry the weight.

The tense is perfect, which in Greek describes a completed action whose result stands. The crucifixion is not something Paul underwent repeatedly or is undergoing; it happened, and its effect is his present condition.

The verb is a compound: syn- ("with, together with") + stauroō ("to crucify"). The death is therefore participatory, not parallel. Paul is not saying that he died in a manner resembling Christ's death, or that he died as a result of it. He is saying that he was in it.

A word about what crucifixion was, since the metaphor loses its force if the reality behind it is unfamiliar. It was a Roman method of public execution reserved for slaves, rebels and non-citizens. The condemned was fixed to a beam and left in the open to die slowly of exhaustion and asphyxiation, in view of passers-by, often for days, and frequently left unburied afterward. Its purpose was as much deterrence as punishment, and it carried a stigma that made it nearly unmentionable in polite Roman writing. Paul uses it as the image for his own relation to the law and, at 5:24 and 6:14, for the believer's relation to the flesh and to the world.

**What "dying to law" can and cannot mean.** The letter itself supplies a control on this, and it comes at 4:4-5, where Christ is described as "born under law (hypo nomon), so that he might redeem those under law." Since Christ observed Torah, "under law" in that verse cannot be a description of Torah practice -- the sentence would otherwise say that he needed rescuing from his own faithfulness. It must denote a standing or jurisdiction: being within the law's authority and subject to its sanctions.

That bears directly on 2:19. If "under law" names a jurisdiction rather than a practice, then "dying to law" most naturally names the end of that jurisdiction rather than the abandonment of the practice. The verse does not say which, and readers should note that the control comes from two chapters later rather than from here.

**Verse 20: "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me. And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me."**

The paradox is stated in three moves and it is not resolved: I no longer live; Christ lives in me; and yet I do live, in the flesh.

Ho de nyn zō en sarki ("what I now live in flesh") uses sarx in its plain sense of bodily existence -- ordinary embodied life, without any of the polemical charge the word carries at 3:3 or 6:12-13. The same word does very different work in different places in this letter, and the reader should not import one sense into another.

En pistei zō tē tou huiou tou theou ("I live by faith of the Son of God") repeats the ambiguous genitive of 2:16, and the same options apply. Some early manuscripts, including the third-century P46 and Codex Vaticanus, read "faith of God and Christ" instead of "faith of the Son of God."

The verse closes with two participles: tou agapēsantos me kai paradontos heauton hyper emou -- "who loved me and gave himself for me." Both are singular, and this is the only place in the letter where Paul puts the cross in the first person singular. The greeting had it in the plural: "who gave himself for our sins" (1:4). Here, in the middle of an argument about Jews and Gentiles and covenants and jurisdiction, it contracts to one person.

**Verse 21: "I do not nullify the grace of God; for if righteousness comes through law, then Christ died for nothing."**

Ouk athetō tēn charin tou theou ("I do not set aside the grace of God"). Atheteō means "to render void, to set aside, to reject as invalid" -- it is used of annulling an agreement. The denial implies an accusation: someone has said that Paul's position nullifies something. He answers by returning the charge, and the conditional that follows identifies which position actually does the nullifying.

Ei gar dia nomou dikaiosynē, ara Christos dōrean apethanen -- "for if righteousness is through law, then Christ died gratuitously."

Dōrean is the adverb of dōron, "gift." It means "as a gift, freely" and, by extension, "for nothing, without cause, to no purpose." The same word appears at Romans 3:24 in the positive sense (justified "freely by his grace") and at John 15:25 in the negative ("they hated me without cause").

**The form of the argument matters as much as its content.** This is a reductio: assume the proposition, and an unacceptable consequence follows. And it is worth noting precisely what Paul does *not* argue here.

He does not say the law is bad.
He does not say that no one keeps the law.
He does not say the law was a failed attempt at something.

He says that *if* righteousness comes by that route, then one thing follows: the death was purposeless. The argument runs on the incompatibility of two routes to the same thing, not on the deficiency of one of them.

The positive counterpart to this negative statement arrives at 3:21, and the two verses should be read together: "if a law had been given that was able to make alive, then righteousness would indeed have been by law." No such law was given. The law is not opposed to the promise; it was not issued to do the promise's work. That is a claim about the law's remit, and it is the letter's own answer to the question this verse raises.

## The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. **The two scenes are structured as opposites, and both serve one point.** In Jerusalem, Paul's gospel is examined and confirmed, and the leadership adds nothing. In Antioch, Paul confronts one of the men who confirmed it. Together they establish a position that neither scene could establish alone: Paul's message is not derived from Jerusalem, and it is not at odds with Jerusalem -- and when Antioch practice diverged from what Jerusalem had agreed, it was Paul who held the line.

2. **What was decided about Titus is narrow and load-bearing.** An uncircumcised Greek was in the room, and he "was not compelled to be circumcised." The agreement that followed divided mission fields, not gospels -- one message, two spheres, sealed by a handshake. And the only obligation laid on the Gentile mission was to remember the poor. At the meeting where the question was whether Gentiles must become Jews, the answer imposed one requirement and it was charitable.

3. **The Antioch dispute concerns who may eat together, and the distinction from what may be eaten is decisive.** The written Torah regulates food; it contains no commandment against Gentile company. The restriction Peter honours is called athemiton in his own mouth at Acts 10:28 -- a word for custom, with no commandment cited, because there is none. This is what allows Paul to describe Peter's earlier practice approvingly and to call the withdrawal hypocrisy rather than obedience.

4. **The charge against Peter is that circumstance can function as compulsion.** Peter commands nothing. But a senior apostle's withdrawal leaves Gentile believers outside the fellowship of the leadership with exactly one route back, and that route is conversion. The verb anankazō ("compel") is used of this, and it is the same verb used of Titus at 2:3 and of the Galatian teachers at 6:12 -- the letter's three instances of pressure applied to Gentiles.

5. **Paul states Peter's mixed-table practice as the premise of the rebuke, not as part of the offence.** "If you, being a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew" is said without censure; it is the ground from which the accusation proceeds. Whatever it meant in practice, Paul treats it as consistent with the truth of the gospel and treats the withdrawal as the deviation.

6. **2:15-16 presents its central claim as shared knowledge rather than as news.** "Knowing that a person is not justified by works of law" states a premise Paul expects to be granted, from which Peter's conduct is then shown to deviate. He is not instructing a Jewish apostle in something novel; he is holding him to something both already hold.

7. **The first person plural in 2:15-16 is sustained, and it is the chapter's hardest datum.** "We are Jews by birth... even we believed... that we might be justified." Whatever the sentence claims, it claims about Jews, including Paul. Any reading that confines the issue to how Gentiles are admitted must account for these pronouns.

8. **Three decisions in 2:16 govern the rest of the letter, and none of them is settled by the verse.** What erga nomou denotes -- the boundary-marking observances, on the analogy of 4QMMT's "some of the works of the Torah," or Torah-doing as such. Whether dikaioō names a present reckoning or a final verdict, since Paul uses it in both directions. And whether pistis Iēsou Christou means faith directed toward Christ, Christ's own faithfulness, or a term that does not divide that way, given that the underlying Hebrew emunah does not. The evidence for each has been set out above; the verse does not adjudicate, and readings of the letter diverge accordingly.

9. **Paul reshapes the scripture he cites, and this is the first of several instances.** Psalm 143:2 says no living thing will be justified before God. Paul writes that no *flesh* will be justified *by works of law*, inserting the phrase that makes the citation bear on his argument. The Psalm did not concern law; in his hand it does. The same practice appears at 3:10, 3:11, 3:13 and 4:30, and a reader who checks the citations will find it consistently.

10. **2:19-20 describes the death as participatory, and 2:21 argues by reductio.** Paul does not say he abandoned the law; he says he died to it, through it, and that the death was a crucifixion *with* Christ -- a compound verb in the perfect tense, a completed event whose effect is his present condition. And he does not argue that law-righteousness fails because nobody achieves it. He argues that if it were available, the death would have been pointless. The positive form of the same claim is at 3:21: no law able to make alive was given.
