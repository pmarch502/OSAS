# Ephesians 2 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Ephesians 1):** The letter opened with a standard epistolary greeting identifying the sender as Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, and the recipients as "the saints who are in Ephesus and are faithful in Christ Jesus" (1:1-2). Immediately following, the author launched into an extended berakah -- a blessing formula modeled on Jewish liturgical patterns: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ" (1:3). This berakah runs unbroken through 1:3-14 in the Greek, forming a single complex sentence.

The pronoun pattern in this berakah is critical and must be tracked precisely. In 1:3-12, the first-person plural dominates: "blessed us" (hemas, 1:3), "he chose us" (exelexato hemas, 1:4), "he predestined us" (proorisas hemas, 1:5), "he has blessed us" (echaritosen hemas, 1:6), "we have redemption" (echomen ten apolytroosin, 1:7), "he lavished upon us" (eperisseusen eis hemas, 1:8), "he made known to us" (gnoorisas hemin, 1:9), "we who first hoped in Christ" (tous proelpikatos en to Christo, 1:12). The "we/us" throughout this section describes a group that "first hoped" (proelpikatos) in Christ -- that is, a group whose hope in the Messiah preceded that of the recipients being addressed. In the context of a letter from a Jewish apostle to a mixed community with a significant Gentile component, the most natural reading is that "we/us" in 1:3-12 refers to Jewish believers -- those who had the prior messianic hope rooted in Israel's scriptures and covenants.

Then in 1:13-14, the pronoun shifts sharply to second person: "In him you also (kai hymeis), when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed in him, were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the guarantee (arrabon) of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it, to the praise of his glory" (1:13-14). The "you also" (kai hymeis) marks a distinct group -- those who came to faith through hearing the gospel proclamation, as distinct from those who "first hoped." The shift signals the inclusion of Gentile believers into the blessings that had been described in corporate Jewish-messianic terms. The arrabon ("down payment, guarantee") of the Spirit seals this new group's participation in an inheritance that was first promised to Israel.

Following the berakah, the author shifted to a prayer report (1:15-23). He prayed that the recipients would receive "the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him" (1:17), that they would know "the hope to which he has called you, the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints, and the immeasurable greatness of his power toward us who believe" (1:18-19). The prayer culminated in a statement about Christ's cosmic authority: God "raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (1:20-21), and "put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church (ekklesia), which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all" (1:22-23). This final claim -- that the ekklesia is Christ's body and the fullness (pleroma) of the one who fills all things -- sets up the argument of chapter 2, which will explain how this body came to be constituted from two formerly divided groups.

**What the author argues immediately after (Ephesians 3):** Chapter 3 opens with Paul identifying himself as "the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles" (3:1). The phrase hyper hymon ton ethnon ("on behalf of you Gentiles") explicitly names the audience addressed by the second-person "you" throughout the letter: they are Gentiles. Paul then describes "the stewardship of God's grace that was given to me for you" (3:2) and the "mystery" (mysterion) that was made known to him by revelation (3:3). The content of this mystery, which "was not made known to the sons of men in other generations as it has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit" (3:5), is stated with precision: "the Gentiles are fellow heirs (synkleronomoi), members of the same body (syssoma), and partakers of the promise (symmetocha tes epangelias) in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (3:6). The three syn- compounds -- synkleronomoi, syssoma, symmetocha -- all carry the prefix meaning "together with" or "co-." The mystery is not the gospel itself but the Gentile inclusion in what had been Israel's inheritance, body, and promise. This is the specific content of "the mystery of Christ" (3:4).

Chapter 3 continues with Paul describing his apostolic commission to preach this mystery to the Gentiles (3:7-13), and then offers a second prayer (3:14-21) that the recipients would be strengthened by the Spirit, rooted in love, and able to comprehend "the breadth and length and height and depth" of Christ's love (3:18-19). The chapter closes with a doxology: "to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think, according to the power at work within us, to him be glory in the church (ekklesia) and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, forever and ever. Amen" (3:20-21).

**How chapter 2 connects these:** Chapter 1 praised God for blessings bestowed on "us" (Jewish believers who first hoped) and then included "you also" (Gentile believers who heard and believed). The chapter concluded with a prayer that the recipients would understand the power of God that raised and enthroned Christ as head of the ekklesia. Chapter 3 will explicitly name the mystery: Gentile co-participation in Israel's inheritance. Chapter 2 is the narrative bridge between these two -- it tells the story of how both groups arrived at their present shared condition. It does this in two movements. The first movement (2:1-10) narrates the transition from death to life that both groups experienced: "you" (Gentiles, 2:1-2) were dead in trespasses; "we all" (including Jewish believers, 2:3) likewise lived under the same condition; but God, in his mercy, "made us alive together with Christ" (2:5) and "raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places" (2:6). This section explains how both groups were transferred from the domain of death into the domain of life and enthronement with Christ. The second movement (2:11-22) addresses the social and covenantal dimension: the Gentile recipients were formerly excluded from Israel's commonwealth and covenants, but Christ has broken the dividing wall and created "one new humanity" (2:15) from the two, reconciling both to God in one body. The chapter thus provides the experiential and historical narrative that chapter 1's praise assumed and that chapter 3's mystery will name explicitly.

## Verses 1-3: The Former Condition -- Death in Trespasses

"And you were dead in the trespasses and sins" (2:1).

The kai ("and") connects this verse directly to the preceding. The author has just described God's power in raising Christ from the dead and enthroning him (1:20-21). The same power that worked in Christ now works toward "us who believe" (1:19). Verse 2:1 begins to explain what that power accomplished: it raised the dead. The "you" (hymas) is second person plural -- the same "you" that appeared in 1:13 (kai hymeis, "you also") to mark the Gentile believers' entry into the narrative. The author is addressing the Gentile recipients directly: you were the ones who were dead.

The word nekrous ("dead") is not metaphorical in the sense of "somewhat spiritually impaired." The author uses the same word that described Christ's condition before God raised him (1:20, ek nekron, "from the dead"). The parallel is deliberate: Christ was dead (nekros) and God raised him; you were dead (nekrous) and God made you alive. The death is located in the dative: tois paraptoomasin kai tais hamartiais -- "in the trespasses and sins." The dative may be instrumental ("dead by means of trespasses and sins" -- trespasses caused the death), locative ("dead in the sphere of trespasses and sins" -- trespasses were the domain of the death), or causal ("dead because of trespasses and sins"). The meaning in any case is that trespasses (paraptoomata, "false steps, fallings aside, transgressions") and sins (hamartiai, "missings of the mark, failures, sins") constituted the condition of death. The two words together form a comprehensive description: paraptoomata emphasizes the deviation from a path, the stumbling or falling away; hamartia emphasizes the failure to hit a target. Together they describe a condition of total moral misdirection.

The concept of corporate death through transgression has precedent in Israel's scriptures. In Genesis 2:17, God warned Adam: "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (LXX: thanato apothaneisthe). Adam transgressed and the sentence was death -- not merely biological cessation but estrangement from the source of life. Ezekiel 37:1-14, the vision of the valley of dry bones, presents Israel as a dead people: "Son of man, can these bones live?" (37:3). The bones are identified: "these bones are the whole house of Israel" (37:11). God promises: "I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people" (37:12), and "I will put my Spirit within you, and you shall live" (37:14). The corporate death of a people and their revival by God's Spirit is an established prophetic pattern. The author of Ephesians applies this pattern to the Gentile recipients: they too were dead, and God revived them.

"In which you once walked, following the course of this world, following the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that is now at work in the sons of disobedience" (2:2).

The phrase en hais pote periepatesate ("in which you once walked") uses the verb peripateo ("to walk about, to conduct one's life"), the standard metaphor for the manner of life -- Hebrew halak, which pervades the Torah's descriptions of obedient and disobedient conduct (Genesis 5:22, "Enoch walked with God"; Deuteronomy 5:33, "You shall walk in all the way that the LORD your God has commanded you"). The adverb pote ("once, formerly, at one time") marks this walking as past -- a former way of life that has been abandoned. The recipients once conducted their lives in the sphere defined by trespasses and sins.

Three characterizations of this former walk are given. First: kata ton aiona tou kosmou toutou -- "according to the age of this world" or "according to the course of this world." The word aion ("age, era, epoch") combined with kosmos ("world, world-order, the ordered arrangement of things") creates a phrase that names the present world-order as a governing standard. Walking kata ("according to, in conformity with") this age means living in conformity with the patterns, values, and direction of the current world-system. The phrase echoes Paul's usage in Romans 12:2: "Do not be conformed to this age (to aioni touto)" and in Galatians 1:4, where Christ "gave himself for our sins to deliver us from the present evil age." The "age of this world" is not merely a temporal designation but a characterization of a system opposed to God's purposes.

Second: kata ton archonta tes exousias tou aeros -- "according to the ruler of the authority of the air." The word archon ("ruler, prince, chief") designates a governing figure. In the LXX and in second-temple Jewish literature, spiritual beings exercised authority (exousia, "authority, power, domain") over various domains. The "air" (aer) as the domain of this ruler reflects first-century Jewish cosmology in which the region between earth and heaven was understood as populated by spiritual forces (cf. Testament of Benjamin 3:4; 1 Enoch 15:8-10; Philo, De Gigantibus 6-9, where the air is filled with souls/spirits). The "ruler of the authority of the air" is a personal spiritual power who exercises governance over a domain -- the same kind of spiritual authority that Christ has been placed "far above" (1:21, hyperano pases arches kai exousias, "above every rule and authority"). The Gentile recipients formerly walked in alignment with this ruler's governance.

Third: tou pneumatos tou nyn energountos en tois huiois tes apeitheias -- "the spirit now working in the sons of disobedience." The genitive tou pneumatos ("the spirit") stands in apposition to ton archonta: the ruler is identified as a spirit that is presently active (energountos, present active participle of energeo, "to work, to be active, to operate") in the "sons of disobedience" (tois huiois tes apeitheias). The Semitic idiom "sons of" indicates characterization: "sons of disobedience" are people characterized by disobedience (apeitheia, "unwillingness to be persuaded, refusal to comply, disobedience"). This is the same idiom as "sons of light" (huioi photos, 1 Thessalonians 5:5) or "sons of wrath" (tekna orges, which appears in verse 3 below). The present tense of energountos ("now working") contrasts with the past tense of the recipients' former walk (periepatesate, "you walked"): the spirit still operates in those characterized by disobedience, but the recipients have been transferred out of that sphere.

"Among whom we all once lived in the passions of our flesh, carrying out the desires of the body and the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, like the rest of mankind" (2:3).

The pronoun shift here is decisive. After two verses of "you" (hymas, hymeis) addressing Gentile believers, the author now writes en hois kai hemeis pantes -- "among whom we also all" or "in which we all also." The word hemeis ("we") includes the author and, given the context, Jewish believers. The kai ("also") and pantes ("all") are emphatic: not just you Gentiles, but we also -- all of us, without exception. The author is insisting that the Jewish believers were in the same condition as the Gentile believers before God acted. The distinction between "you" (Gentile) and "we" (Jewish) that was carefully maintained in chapter 1 is now collapsed: both groups shared the same predicament.

The phrase anestraphemen pote ("we once conducted ourselves," aorist passive of anastrepho, "to turn about, to conduct oneself, to live") describes the former manner of life. The word anastrepho in its middle/passive sense means "to live, to behave, to go about one's daily conduct" -- a near synonym of peripateo ("to walk") from verse 2, but with a slightly different nuance: anastrepho implies the total pattern of life, the way one turns and moves within one's world.

The sphere of this conduct is specified: en tais epithymiais tes sarkos hemon -- "in the desires of our flesh." The word epithymia ("desire, craving, longing") is not inherently negative in Greek (Luke 22:15, Jesus says "I have earnestly desired" -- epithymia epethymesa), but when attached to sarx ("flesh"), it takes on the sense of the cravings that arise from human existence apart from God's Spirit. The flesh (sarx) here, as in Romans 7-8, is not the physical body per se but the whole mode of human existence under the power of sin. The genitive tes sarkos hemon ("of our flesh") identifies the flesh as the source of these desires.

The participial phrase poiountes ta thelemata tes sarkos kai ton dianoion ("doing the wishes of the flesh and of the thoughts") further specifies the conduct. The word thelemata ("wishes, wills, desires") is the plural of thelema ("will, wish, desire, purpose"). The believers formerly carried out the wishes generated by the flesh and by the dianoia ("thought, mind, understanding, intention"). The dianoia is the faculty of thinking and planning -- not just bodily impulses but deliberate mental orientations. The inclusion of dianoia alongside sarx ensures that the description covers the whole person: not merely bodily appetites but also the patterns of thought, intention, and reasoning that operated apart from God.

The concluding phrase is stark: kai emetha tekna physei orges hos kai hoi loipoi -- "and we were by nature children of wrath, like the rest." The word physei ("by nature," dative of physis, "nature, natural constitution, innate quality") indicates that this condition was not accidental but constitutive. The Jewish believers were not merely caught up in sin but were, in their very nature -- their Adamic humanity -- "children of wrath" (tekna orges). The Semitic idiom "children of wrath" (tekna orges) means "people characterized by wrath" or "people who are the objects of wrath" -- people upon whom wrath rests. The word orge ("wrath, anger, indignation") in Paul's usage consistently refers to God's settled response to human sin: Romans 1:18, "the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness"; Romans 2:5, "you are storing up wrath for yourself"; Romans 5:9, "saved from the wrath of God through him." The "wrath" is not emotional outburst but judicial response -- God's righteous reaction to human rebellion.

The phrase hos kai hoi loipoi ("like also the rest," or "just as the rest of humanity") levels the distinction completely. The Jewish believers were in the same condition physei -- by nature -- as everyone else. The Torah, the covenants, the election of Israel -- none of these exempted Jewish believers from the Adamic condition of being tekna orges. This is the same argument Paul made in Romans 2-3: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23), and the Jew is not exempt from this universal verdict (Romans 2:17-24, 3:9). The author of Ephesians compresses that argument into a single phrase: we were children of wrath by nature, just like everyone else.

The pronoun pattern of verses 1-3 thus establishes a crucial structure: "you" (Gentiles) were dead in sins (vv. 1-2), and "we" (Jews) were equally dead, children of wrath by nature like the rest (v. 3). Both groups shared the same condition. This shared death is the baseline from which God's action in verses 4-10 will be described.

## Verses 4-7: God's Action -- Making Alive, Raising, Enthroning

"But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us" (2:4).

The phrase ho de theos ("but God") is the turning point of the entire section. The de ("but") is adversative -- it marks a sharp contrast between the condition described in verses 1-3 (dead, walking in sin, children of wrath) and what follows. Everything before the de described the human condition; everything after it describes God's initiative. The subject is emphatically theos -- God. Not human effort, not moral improvement, not religious attainment, but God. The contrast between human deadness and divine action is absolute: dead people cannot revive themselves. Only an external agent can raise the dead.

Two attributes motivate God's action. First: plousios on en eleei -- "being rich in mercy." The word plousios ("rich, wealthy, abundant") describes not a bare minimum of mercy but an overflowing, excessive supply. The word eleos ("mercy, compassion, pity") in the LXX regularly translates the Hebrew chesed ("steadfast love, covenant loyalty, merciful kindness") and rachamim ("compassions, bowels of mercy"). Eleos is the attribute by which God acts toward those in distress despite their unworthiness -- it is the quality that moved God to hear Israel's cries in Egypt (Exodus 2:24-25), to forgive after the golden calf (Exodus 34:6-7, "merciful and gracious"), and to restore after exile (Isaiah 54:7-8, "with great compassion I will gather you"). Mercy is God's response to wretchedness, not to merit.

Second: dia ten pollen agapen autou hen egapesen hemas -- "because of the great love with which he loved us." The word agape ("love") is modified by pollen ("great, much, abundant"). The verb egapesen ("he loved," aorist active of agapao) points to a specific act or settled disposition of love. The object is hemas ("us") -- a first-person plural that, coming after the collapse of "you" and "we" in verse 3, now encompasses both groups: Jewish and Gentile believers together. The motivation for God's act is internal to God: his own mercy, his own love. Nothing in the description of the dead humans in verses 1-3 supplies a reason for God to act. The reason comes entirely from within God's character.

"Even when we were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ -- by grace you have been saved" (2:5).

The participial phrase kai ontas hemas nekrous tois paraptoomasin ("even though we were dead in trespasses") recapitulates the condition from verse 1 but now using the first-person plural hemas ("us") rather than the second-person hymas ("you") of verse 1. The death that was initially described as the Gentile condition (v. 1) was then extended to include the Jewish condition (v. 3), and now in verse 5 the combined group appears: "us" -- all of us, dead in trespasses. The temporal concession is emphatic: God acted while we were still dead, not after we had begun to recover. The dead were raised as dead, not as recovering.

The main verb is synezoopoiesen ("he made alive together with," aorist active of syzoopoieo). This is a compound word: syn ("together with") + zoopoieo ("to make alive, to give life to"). The syn- prefix indicates that the making alive happened "together with" someone: to Christo ("with Christ," dative of association). The recipients were made alive together with Christ. The verb zoopoieo in the LXX is used for God's life-giving activity: Nehemiah 9:6, "you give life to all things" (sy zoopoieis ta panta); Ezekiel 37:6, "I will put breath in you, and you shall live" (cf. LXX, zoopoieso hymas). The making alive echoes the Ezekiel 37 vision directly: a dead people, incapable of self-revival, is made alive by God's action and God's Spirit. The syn- prefix ties the recipients' resurrection to Christ's resurrection: the same act of God that raised Christ from the dead (1:20) is the act that raised them from their spiritual death. Their life is not independent of Christ's life; it is derived from it, co-extensive with it, parasitic upon it (in the botanical sense -- grafted into the living vine).

The parenthetical statement chariti este sesoosmenoi ("by grace you have been saved") interrupts the flow to make a critical assertion. The word chariti (dative of charis, "grace, unmerited favor, free gift") is placed in the emphatic position -- "by grace" comes first in the clause. The verb este sesoosmenoi is a periphrastic perfect: the present tense of eimi ("you are") combined with the perfect passive participle of sozo ("to save"). The periphrastic perfect emphasizes the present state resulting from a past action: you are, right now, in a state of having been saved. The salvation is completed in its initiation but ongoing in its effects -- the recipients presently exist in the condition of "saved." The parenthetical is remarkable because it breaks the syntactic flow to insist on the point: this making alive was an act of grace, not of merit. The author considers this important enough to interrupt his own sentence.

"And raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus" (2:6).

The verse continues the chain of syn- compounds that began with synezoopoiesen in verse 5. Two further actions are named, both with the prefix syn-:

First: synegieren ("he raised up together with," aorist active of synegeiro, "to raise together with"). The simple verb egeiro ("to raise") was used in 1:20 for God raising Christ from the dead. Now the believers are raised with Christ -- their raising is not a separate event from Christ's raising but a participation in it. The syn- prefix makes this inseparable: Christ's resurrection and the believers' resurrection are one event viewed from two angles.

Second: synekathisen ("he seated together with," aorist active of synkathizo, "to cause to sit down together, to seat together with"). The simple verb kathizo ("to seat, to cause to sit") was used in 1:20 for God seating Christ "at his right hand in the heavenly places." Now the believers are seated with Christ. The action that exalted Christ to cosmic authority over all rule and power (1:20-21) is now said to include the believers: they have been seated with him.

The phrase en tois epouraniois ("in the heavenly places") appeared in 1:3 (God has blessed us "in the heavenly places"), 1:20 (God seated Christ "in the heavenly places"), and will appear again in 3:10 (the wisdom of God is now made known "in the heavenly places" to the rulers and authorities). The "heavenly places" in this letter is not a remote afterlife location but the sphere of spiritual reality where God's purposes are enacted and displayed. The believers' seating "in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus" (en Christo Iesou, the locative phrase repeated from 1:3) describes their present positional reality: they are, in Christ, already seated in the sphere of divine authority.

The three verbs -- made alive with, raised with, seated with -- parallel the three actions described for Christ in 1:20: God raised him from the dead, seated him at his right hand, and placed him above all rule and authority. What God did for Christ, God did with the believers. The believers' experience is patterned on Christ's experience, joined to Christ's experience, derived from Christ's experience. The syn- prefix on each verb makes the connection explicit: the believers do not have a separate resurrection, a separate raising, a separate enthronement. They share in Christ's.

All three verbs are in the aorist tense, indicating completed action. The author treats the making alive, the raising, and the seating as accomplished facts, not future hopes. This is consistent with the author's perspective in 1:3 ("has blessed us") and with the letter's emphasis on what God has already done. The tension with the "not yet" dimension of Christian hope (which appears later in the letter, e.g., 1:14, the Spirit as arrabon of the inheritance "until we acquire possession of it"; 4:30, "sealed for the day of redemption") is not resolved but held: the positional reality is complete, the experiential reality is in process.

"So that in the coming ages he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus" (2:7).

The purpose clause hina ("in order that, so that") reveals God's motive for the making alive, raising, and seating. The phrase en tois aiosin tois eperchomenois ("in the ages that are coming upon us," or "in the coming ages") is a temporal reference to future periods of time. The word aion ("age, epoch") is plural -- not one future age but multiple ages. The concept of successive ages is rooted in Jewish apocalyptic thought, which divided history into periods (cf. Daniel's four kingdoms, the Enochic periodization of history, the rabbinical distinction between "this age" and "the age to come"). The author envisions multiple future ages in which God's purpose will be on display.

What God intends to display is specified: to hyperballon ploutos tes charitos autou -- "the surpassing riches of his grace." The word hyperballon (present active participle of hyperballo, "to throw beyond, to surpass, to exceed") describes something that exceeds all measurement. The word ploutos ("riches, wealth") appeared in 1:7 ("the riches of his grace") and 1:18 ("the riches of his glorious inheritance"). The grace is not merely adequate but immeasurably rich -- it overflows beyond any capacity to contain it.

The instrument of display is specified: en chrestoteti eph' hemas en Christo Iesou -- "in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus." The word chrestotes ("kindness, goodness, generosity, benevolence") in the LXX translates Hebrew tov ("goodness") and chesed ("steadfast love"): Psalm 31:19 (LXX 30:20), "How abundant is your goodness (chrestotes), which you have stored up for those who fear you"; Psalm 145:9 (LXX 144:9), "The LORD is good (chrestos) to all." God's kindness toward the believers in Christ Jesus is the vehicle through which his grace will be displayed throughout the coming ages. The believers themselves become the exhibit -- the evidence presented to the watching ages of how generous God's grace is.

This purpose statement has implications for how the recipients understand their own identity. They are not merely saved for their own benefit; they are saved for display -- to serve as the ongoing demonstration of God's character across future ages. Their existence as a redeemed people is vocational: they are called to embody and exhibit the riches of grace.

## Verses 8-10: Grace, Faith, and Created Purpose

"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast" (2:8-9).

The statement te gar chariti este sesoosmenoi dia pisteos ("for by grace you have been saved through faith") repeats and expands the parenthetical from verse 5. The repetition signals its importance: the author considered the point so essential that he stated it once as an interruption (v. 5b) and now returns to it as a formal argument (v. 8).

The dative chariti ("by grace") identifies the ground or means of salvation. The word charis ("grace, unmerited favor, gift") in the LXX and in Paul's usage consistently refers to God's free, unearned disposition of favor. In Romans 3:24, justification comes dorean te autou chariti ("freely by his grace"); in Romans 11:6, "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace." Grace and works are mutually exclusive grounds: if grace, then not works; if works, then not grace. The author of Ephesians stands in the same tradition.

The prepositional phrase dia pisteos ("through faith") identifies the channel or instrument through which the gracious salvation is received. The word pistis ("faith, trust, faithfulness, fidelity") in this context refers to the human response of trust in God's action -- the believing that was mentioned in 1:13 ("you heard the word of truth... and believed [pisteusantes]"). Grace is the ground; faith is the instrument. Grace provides; faith receives.

The next phrase is syntactically significant: kai touto ouk ex hymon -- "and this is not from you." The demonstrative touto ("this") is neuter, while both charis and pistis are feminine nouns. The neuter touto does not agree grammatically with either noun individually. It most naturally refers to the entire preceding reality -- the whole complex of being saved by grace through faith. The entire arrangement -- the grace, the faith, the salvation -- is "not from you" (ouk ex hymon). The preposition ek ("from, out of") with hymon ("you") indicates origin or source: the source is not you. It does not originate in the recipients.

The positive alternative: theou to doron -- "God's gift" (or "the gift of God"). The word doron ("gift, present, offering") names what was just described negatively. The salvation by grace through faith is God's doron -- something given, not earned, not generated by the recipient.

The further specification: ouk ex ergon -- "not from works." The word erga ("works, deeds, accomplishments") in Paul's letters regularly refers to human moral or religious performance, especially (though not exclusively) "works of the law" (erga nomou, Galatians 2:16; Romans 3:20, 28). The denial that salvation comes "from works" stands in the tradition of Paul's argument in Romans 3-4 and Galatians 2-3: the righteousness of God is received through faith, not produced by human performance of Torah observance or moral achievement.

The purpose clause: hina me tis kauchesesetai -- "so that no one might boast." The verb kauchaomai ("to boast, to glory, to take pride in") is a major theme in Paul's letters. In Romans 3:27, Paul asks: "Then what becomes of our boasting (kauchesis)? It is excluded. By what kind of law? By works? No, by the law of faith." In 1 Corinthians 1:29, God chose what is foolish and weak "so that no human being might boast in the presence of God." The purpose of the grace-not-works arrangement is the elimination of human boasting before God. If salvation were the product of human effort, the saved person could claim credit. Because it is God's gift, the only appropriate response is gratitude, not self-congratulation.

A first-century Jewish hearer would recognize in this the logic of Israel's own election. Deuteronomy 7:7-8: "It was not because you were more in number than any other people that the LORD set his love on you and chose you, for you were the fewest of all peoples, but it is because the LORD loves you and is keeping the oath that he swore to your fathers." Israel's election was never grounded in Israel's merit; it was grounded in God's love and God's oath. The same logic applies to the new community: their inclusion is grounded in God's grace and gift, not in their works.

"For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them" (2:10).

The word poiema ("workmanship, thing made, product of making") comes from the verb poieo ("to make, to create, to do"). A poiema is something that has been made -- a product, a creation. In the LXX, the word appears in contexts of God's creative activity: Psalm 143:5 (LXX 142:5), "I meditate on all that you have done (poiemata); I muse on the work of your hands"; Psalm 92:4 (LXX 91:5), "you make me glad by your work (poieemati)." The believers are God's poiema -- God's crafted work, God's artistic production. This reinforces the denial of human boasting: one does not boast about being made. The potter does not congratulate the clay.

The participle ktisthentes ("having been created," aorist passive participle of ktizo, "to create, to bring into being, to found") introduces explicit creation language. The verb ktizo in the LXX is used for God's original creation: Genesis 14:19 (LXX), "God Most High, who created (ektisen) heaven and earth"; Deuteronomy 4:32, "the day that God created (ektisen) man on the earth." The word also appears in prophetic visions of new creation: Isaiah 43:1, "Thus says the LORD, he who created (ktisas) you, O Jacob, he who formed you, O Israel"; Isaiah 65:17-18, "For behold, I create (ktizo) new heavens and a new earth... I create (ktizo) Jerusalem." The creation language signals that what God has done in the believers is not renovation but new creation. The dead were not repaired; they were created anew. This echoes Paul's language in 2 Corinthians 5:17: "If anyone is in Christ, new creation (kaine ktisis)" and Galatians 6:15: "neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation."

The creation is located en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") -- the same locative formula that has governed the letter. The new creation is not autonomous; it exists "in" Christ, dependent on him, derived from him, located within the sphere of his lordship.

The purpose of the creation is specified: epi ergois agathois -- "for good works." The preposition epi with the dative indicates purpose or destination: the good works are what the new creation is aimed toward. This is significant in light of verses 8-9: the denial that salvation comes "from works" (ouk ex ergon, v. 9) is immediately followed by the affirmation that salvation is "for good works" (epi ergois agathois, v. 10). Works are not the root of salvation; they are the fruit. Works are not the cause of new creation; they are the purpose. The relationship is sequential, not causal: created first, then for good works.

The final clause: hois proetoimasen ho theos hina en autois peripatesomen -- "which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." The verb proetoimasen ("prepared beforehand," aorist active of proetoimazo, "to prepare in advance, to make ready ahead of time") has the prefix pro- ("before") -- God prepared the good works in advance. The works are not the believers' invention; they are God's prior arrangement. The verb peripateo ("to walk") returns from verse 2, but now with a transformed content: the recipients once "walked" in trespasses and sins (v. 2, en hais periepatesate); they are now to "walk" in good works (v. 10, en autois peripatesomen). The same metaphor -- walking, conducting one's life -- now has a new sphere: not trespasses but good works. The transformation is complete: the dead have been made alive, the trespass-walkers have become good-works-walkers, the children of wrath have become God's workmanship.

The pronoun shift from "you" (este sesoosmenoi, "you have been saved," v. 8) to "we" (peripatesomen, "we should walk," v. 10) is consistent with the pattern: the author moves between addressing the Gentile recipients specifically and including himself and all believers in the shared experience.

## Verses 11-12: The Former Gentile Condition -- Exclusion from Israel's Covenants

"Therefore remember that at one time you Gentiles in the flesh, called 'the uncircumcision' by what is called 'the circumcision,' which is made in the flesh by hands" (2:11).

The word dio ("therefore, for this reason") connects what follows to what precedes. Because of everything stated in verses 1-10 -- the former death, the divine rescue, the new creation -- the recipients should now "remember" (mnemoneuete, present active imperative of mnemoneuo, "to remember, to keep in mind, to call to mind"). The imperative mood makes this a command: do not forget where you came from.

What they are to remember is their former identity: hymeis ta ethne en sarki -- "you, the Gentiles in the flesh." The word ethne ("nations, Gentiles, non-Jews") here directly names the recipients. This confirms what the pronoun analysis of 1:13 and 2:1-2 indicated: the "you" throughout the letter refers to Gentile believers. The phrase en sarki ("in the flesh") specifies the domain of their Gentile identity -- it is a fleshly, physical, ethnic designation.

They were called he akrobystia ("the uncircumcision") by those called he peritome ("the circumcision"). The terms are identity markers based on the physical rite of circumcision (Genesis 17:9-14), which was the covenant sign distinguishing Israel from the nations. The author adds a qualifier: tes legomenes peritomes en sarki cheiropoietou -- "the so-called circumcision made in the flesh by hands." The word cheiropoietos ("made by hands, handmade") in the LXX and in the prophetic tradition is regularly applied to idols -- things made by human hands as opposed to the work of God (Isaiah 2:8, "the work of their hands"; Isaiah 46:6; Acts 7:48, "the Most High does not dwell in houses made with hands [cheiropoietois]"). The author's use of this word to describe physical circumcision implies a critical distance: the circumcision that separated Jew from Gentile was "made by hands" -- a physical, external marker. The implication is that a deeper, non-handmade reality exists (cf. Colossians 2:11, "circumcision not made with hands [acheiropoieto]"). The author is not dismissing circumcision but relativizing it: the fleshly, handmade distinction between circumcision and uncircumcision was real but penultimate.

"Remember that you were at that time separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (2:12).

The author now catalogs five dimensions of the Gentiles' former exclusion. Each item names something the Gentiles lacked that Israel possessed:

First: choris Christou -- "apart from Christ" or "without Christ." The word choris ("separate from, apart from, without") indicates absence. The Gentiles were without the Messiah -- they had no messianic hope, no expectation of an anointed deliverer. The concept of Messiah (mashiach, christos) was rooted in Israel's scriptures: the anointed king of the Davidic line (2 Samuel 7:12-16; Psalm 2; Psalm 110; Isaiah 9:6-7; Isaiah 11:1-10). The nations had no share in this expectation. Being "without Christ" means being outside the narrative of promise and fulfillment that constituted Israel's hope.

Second: apelotriomenoi tes politeias tou Israel -- "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel." The verb apallotrioo ("to alienate, to estrange, to exclude") is a compound: apo ("away from") + allotrioo ("to make foreign, to make other"). The perfect passive participle indicates a settled state of estrangement. The word politeia ("citizenship, commonwealth, polity, way of civic life") is a political term. In the LXX, it appears in 2 Maccabees 4:11 for the Jewish politeia -- the organized civic and religious life of the Jewish people. The politeia tou Israel is Israel's ordered community life as God's covenant people -- not merely a political entity but the entire arrangement of corporate existence as the people of God: laws, festivals, temple worship, land, king, prophets, priesthood. The Gentiles were excluded from this entire system. They were aliens, outsiders, foreigners to Israel's corporate life.

Third: xenoi ton diathekon tes epangelias -- "strangers to the covenants of the promise." The word xenoi ("strangers, foreigners, aliens") describes someone who has no legal standing, no rights, no claim. The word diatheke ("covenant, testament, arrangement") in the LXX translates the Hebrew berit -- the binding agreement between God and his people. The plural diathekon ("covenants") is significant: not one covenant but multiple covenants. The plural likely refers to the successive covenants God made with Abraham (Genesis 15, 17), with Israel at Sinai (Exodus 19-24), with David (2 Samuel 7), and the promised new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31-34). Together these are called "the covenants of the promise" (tes epangelias, singular -- the one overarching promise of which the covenants are expressions and instruments). The Gentiles were strangers to all of these -- they stood outside the entire covenantal history of God's dealing with Israel.

Fourth: elpida me echontes -- "having no hope." The negation is absolute: not merely diminished hope but no hope at all. The word elpis ("hope, expectation") in this context is the messianic and covenantal hope -- the expectation of God's future redemptive act. Israel had this hope in abundance: the prophets promised restoration, a new king, a new covenant, a new creation. The Gentiles, standing outside this narrative, had no such hope. The cultural context confirms this: the Greco-Roman world, for all its philosophical sophistication, had no equivalent of Israel's eschatological hope. The epitaphs of the ancient world are frequently marked by despair: "I was not, I was, I am not, I care not" (a common Roman epitaph formula). The Stoics counseled acceptance of fate; the Epicureans denied afterlife. Israel's prophetic hope was unique.

Fifth: atheoi en to kosmo -- "without God in the world." The word atheoi ("godless, without God") does not mean the Gentiles were philosophical atheists who denied the existence of deity; the Greco-Roman world was saturated with gods. The word means they were without the true God -- without the God of Israel, without the God who created heaven and earth, without the God who made covenants and promised redemption. They worshipped, but they worshipped what Paul calls "those that by nature are not gods" (Galatians 4:8). In the kosmos ("world, world-system"), they were atheoi -- they lived in the world without the God who made it.

The five-fold catalog builds a picture of total exclusion. The Gentile recipients were without the Messiah, without the community, without the covenants, without the hope, and without God. They were as alienated from Israel's blessings as was possible. The catalog serves a rhetorical purpose: it magnifies the scope of what God has done. The more dire the former condition, the more glorious the transformation.

## Verses 13-18: The Reconciliation -- One New Humanity

"But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (2:13).

The phrase nyni de ("but now") is the temporal-eschatological marker that divides the old condition from the new. The word nyni ("now") is the same temporal marker Paul used in Romans 3:21 ("but now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law") and Romans 6:22 ("but now, having been freed from sin"). It marks the turning point in redemptive history -- not merely "at this point in time" but "in this new age inaugurated by Christ."

The phrase en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") locates the transformation: it happened within the sphere of Christ's lordship. The recipients are identified as hoi pote ontes makran -- "you who were once far off." The word makran ("far, far off, at a distance") in the LXX has a specific resonance. Isaiah 57:19 (LXX): eirenen ep' eirenen tois makran kai tois engys -- "peace upon peace to those far off and to those near." The "far off" and "near" in Isaiah designate two groups -- the dispersed and the present, or in the prophetic vision, those distant from God and those close. In rabbinic interpretation, "those far off" became a standard designation for Gentile proselytes, while "those near" designated born Israelites. The author of Ephesians adopts this language: the Gentile recipients were "far off" -- distant from God, from Israel, from the covenants.

But they egenethete engys ("have been brought near," or literally "have become near"). The verb ginomai ("to become, to come to be") in the aorist passive indicates a transformation: they were made to become near. The agent is specified: en to haimati tou Christou -- "by the blood of Christ." The word haima ("blood") in the LXX is fundamentally sacrificial. The blood of the Passover lamb marked the Israelite houses for protection (Exodus 12:7, 13). The blood of the covenant ratified the Sinai agreement (Exodus 24:8, "Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you"). The Day of Atonement required blood sprinkled on the mercy seat (Leviticus 16:14-15). Blood was the means by which estrangement was overcome, covenant was ratified, and access to God's presence was secured. "The blood of Christ" names Christ's death as the sacrificial event by which those who were far off were brought near -- the sacrifice that overcame their alienation from Israel's covenants and from God.

"For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (2:14).

The declaration autos gar estin he eirene hemon ("for he himself is our peace") is emphatic. The pronoun autos ("he himself") is intensive -- not merely that he brings peace or produces peace, but that he is peace. The word eirene ("peace") in the LXX translates the Hebrew shalom, which connotes not merely absence of conflict but wholeness, well-being, right-relationship, and covenantal harmony. The Messiah was expected to be the embodiment of peace: Isaiah 9:6, "his name shall be called... Prince of Peace (sar shalom)"; Micah 5:5 (LXX 5:4), "this one shall be peace (houtos estai eirene)." The author identifies Christ with the peace that was prophesied: he does not merely broker peace between two parties; he constitutes the peace in his own person.

The first action: ho poiesas ta amphotera hen -- "the one who made the two things one." The neuter plural ta amphotera ("the both, the two") refers to the two groups -- Jews and Gentiles, the near and the far off, the circumcision and the uncircumcision. The numeral hen ("one") is also neuter -- the two things have become one thing. The making is a creative act (poiesas, aorist active participle of poieo, the same verb root as poiema in v. 10). The one who made the believers his workmanship (v. 10) also made the two groups into one entity (v. 14).

The second action: to mesotoichon tou phragmou lysas -- "having broken down the middle wall of the partition" or "having destroyed the dividing wall of the barrier." The word mesotoichon ("middle wall, partition wall, dividing wall") is a compound: mesos ("middle") + toichos ("wall"). The word phragmos ("fence, barrier, partition, hedge") adds the sense of enclosure or separation. Together they describe a wall that stands between two spaces and prevents passage from one to the other.

A first-century hearer in Ephesus or anywhere in the Roman world familiar with the Jerusalem temple would immediately think of the soreg -- the low stone barrier in the temple complex that separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts. Josephus describes this wall (Jewish War 5.193-194; Antiquities 15.417) and notes that it bore inscriptions in Greek and Latin warning that any foreigner (allogenous) who crossed it would be responsible for his own death. One such inscription was discovered archaeologically in 1871 and reads: "No foreigner is to enter within the balustrade and embankment around the sacred precinct. Whoever is caught will be responsible for his own death that will ensue." This physical wall was the most concrete embodiment of the separation between Jew and Gentile in the first-century world -- literally a wall of exclusion built into the architecture of God's house.

The author declares that Christ lysas ("having broken down, having destroyed") this wall. The verb lyo ("to loose, to break, to dissolve, to destroy") is used for the dissolution of something that previously held firm. Whether or not the physical soreg still stood when this letter was written, the theological reality it represented -- the exclusion of Gentiles from access to God's presence -- has been demolished by Christ.

The phrase ten echthran ("the hostility, the enmity") is in apposition to the mesotoichon: the dividing wall is the hostility. The wall was not merely a physical or legal barrier; it embodied enmity -- the antagonism between Jew and Gentile that the separation created and sustained. The word echthra ("hostility, enmity") is the same word Paul used in Romans 8:7 for the flesh-mindset's hostility toward God. Here the hostility is between the two human groups, and between both groups and God (as verse 16 will make clear).

The phrase en te sarki autou ("in his flesh") locates where the wall was broken down: in Christ's physical body, in his crucifixion. The "flesh" of Christ is the instrument through which the barrier was destroyed -- the same flesh in which sin was condemned (Romans 8:3) and through which reconciliation was accomplished (Colossians 1:22, "he has now reconciled in his body of flesh through his death"). The destruction of the wall was not achieved by legislative decree or philosophical argument but by the physical death of Christ.

"By abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances, that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace" (2:15).

The phrase ton nomon ton entolon en dogmasin katargesas ("having abolished the law of the commandments in ordinances/decrees") identifies what the "dividing wall" consisted of. The word nomos ("law") here refers to the Torah -- specifically, those aspects of the Torah that functioned as boundary markers between Jew and Gentile. The word entole ("commandment, ordinance, injunction") specifies the commands, and dogma ("decree, ordinance, regulation, public edict") specifies the form: regulations, rulings, prescriptions. The combination "the law of the commandments in decrees" describes the Torah insofar as it functioned as a system of separating regulations -- the dietary laws (kashrut), the circumcision requirement, the sabbath observances, the purity codes -- that drew the line between Israel and the nations. These regulations were not bad (Paul affirmed the law's holiness in Romans 7:12); they served the purpose of distinguishing Israel as God's separated people. But that very function created the "wall" between Jew and Gentile.

The verb katargeo ("to make ineffective, to nullify, to abolish, to render inoperative") is the same word Paul used in Romans 3:31 ("Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means!") and Romans 7:2, 6 (the law's binding authority is annulled through death). The katargeo here is not a blanket cancellation of the Torah as scripture or as revelation of God's character; it is the abolition of the Torah's function as the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile. The commandments "in decrees" -- the specific regulatory system that marked the boundary -- have been rendered inoperative as a barrier.

The purpose of this abolition is stated: hina tous duo ktise en hauto eis hena kainon anthropon -- "in order that he might create the two, in himself, into one new human being." The verb ktizo ("to create") returns from verse 10 -- the same creative verb used for God's original creation and for the new creation of believers. Here it is used for the creation of a new corporate entity: one new humanity out of the two former groups. The word kainos ("new, fresh, unprecedented") describes something qualitatively new -- not a renovation of the old but something that has not existed before. The word anthropos ("human being, person") is singular: one new anthropos. Christ did not make the Gentiles into Jews or the Jews into Gentiles; he created a third entity -- one new humanity -- that transcends and replaces the former division.

The phrase en hauto ("in himself") is critical. The new humanity is created in Christ, within his person. Christ is both the location and the agent of the new creation. The two groups do not merge on neutral ground; they are re-created within Christ's own person. As the wall was broken in his flesh (v. 14), the new humanity is created in himself (v. 15).

The result: poion eirenen -- "making peace." The participle poion ("making") describes the ongoing result of the creative act: peace is produced. The peace of verse 14 ("he himself is our peace") is now shown to be the product of a specific historical and theological action: the abolition of the dividing regulations and the creation of one new humanity. Peace is not sentiment; it is a new social and covenantal reality brought into existence by Christ's work.

"And might reconcile us both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the hostility" (2:16).

The verb apokatallaxe ("might reconcile," aorist active subjunctive of apokatallasso, "to reconcile fully, to restore to a former state of relationship") is a compound: apo (intensifier) + katallasso ("to reconcile, to exchange enmity for friendship"). The simple form katallasso appeared in Romans 5:10 ("while we were enemies we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son") and 2 Corinthians 5:18-20 (the ministry of reconciliation). The compound form with apo- intensifies the action: a thorough, complete reconciliation.

Two dimensions of reconciliation are named. First, the reconciliation is of tous amphoterous ("both" -- Jews and Gentiles) to God (to theo). The enmity that needed resolution was not only horizontal (between Jew and Gentile) but vertical (between both groups and God). Verse 3 stated that both Jews and Gentiles were "children of wrath by nature." Both groups needed reconciliation with God, not merely with each other.

Second, the reconciliation occurs en heni somati ("in one body"). The word soma ("body") here carries a double reference. It refers to the body of Christ on the cross -- the physical body in which the reconciliation was accomplished (as "in his flesh" in v. 14). It also refers to the one body that is the ekklesia -- the new corporate entity, the "one new humanity" of verse 15, which is Christ's body (1:23). The cross produced both the physical reconciliation (in Christ's crucified body) and the corporate result (one body composed of the reconciled).

The instrument: dia tou staurou -- "through the cross." The stauros ("cross, stake, instrument of execution") is the specific historical means by which the reconciliation was accomplished. The cross is not a symbol here but a reference to the actual execution of Christ -- the event in which the wall was broken, the hostility was killed, the two were made one, and both were reconciled to God.

The final participial phrase: apokteinnas ten echthran en auto -- "having killed the hostility in it" (or "in him"). The verb apokteinno ("to kill, to put to death, to slay") is violent and definitive -- the enmity was not merely suppressed or managed; it was killed, executed, put to death. The en auto ("in it/in him") can refer to the cross ("in it," the instrument by which the killing occurred) or to Christ ("in himself," the person in whom the killing occurred). Either reading produces the same meaning: in the event of the cross, in the person of Christ, the enmity between Jew and Gentile, and between both and God, was executed.

The irony is pointed: the cross, which was Rome's instrument for killing enemies of the state, became God's instrument for killing enmity itself. The one killed on the cross was not the final victim; enmity was.

"And he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near" (2:17).

The verb elthon ("having come") refers to Christ's coming -- either his incarnation and earthly ministry, or his post-resurrection coming through the apostolic proclamation, or both. The verb euengelisato ("preached good news, proclaimed the gospel," aorist middle of euangelizo) is the verb from which "gospel" (euangelion) derives. The content of the proclamation is eirenen ("peace") -- the same peace that Christ "is" (v. 14) and that he "made" (v. 15).

The recipients of the proclamation are identified with the Isaiah 57:19 language introduced in verse 13: hymin tois makran -- "to you who were far off" (the Gentiles), and eirenen tois engys -- "and peace to those who were near" (the Jews). The citation of Isaiah 57:19 is now made explicit. The prophet's vision of peace for those far and near is fulfilled in Christ's proclamation. Both groups -- the far-off Gentiles and the near Jews -- receive the same message: peace. The gospel does not offer two different things to two different groups; it offers one reality -- peace with God and peace with each other -- to both.

"For through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father" (2:18).

The word prosagoge ("access, approach, introduction, audience before a ruler") appears also in Romans 5:2 ("through him we have gained access [prosagogen] by faith into this grace in which we stand"). The word carries the sense of being brought into the presence of a ruler or dignitary -- being introduced into the audience chamber. In the LXX, the concept of access to God was mediated through the temple, the priesthood, and the sacrificial system. The high priest alone entered the Holy of Holies, and only once a year, on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). The people had degrees of access: Israelite men could enter the Court of Israel; Israelite women could enter the Court of Women; Gentiles could enter only the Court of the Gentiles, beyond which stood the dividing wall (the soreg of v. 14). Now, through Christ (di' autou, "through him"), both groups (hoi amphoteroi, "we both") have prosagoge -- direct access, audience, approach -- to the Father (pros ton patera).

The access is en heni pneumati -- "in one Spirit." The Spirit is the medium of access: believers approach the Father through the Son in the Spirit. The "one Spirit" (heni pneumati) is critical: the same Spirit indwells Jew and Gentile, the same Spirit grants access to both, the same Spirit constitutes the unity of the new body. There is not one Spirit for Jews and another for Gentiles; the one Spirit is shared, and the shared Spirit is the basis of the shared access.

The verse is implicitly Trinitarian in structure: through him (the Son), in one Spirit, to the Father. The three persons are distinguished by function -- the Son mediates, the Spirit provides the environment, the Father is the destination -- but united in the single reality of access. A first-century Jewish hearer would recognize the radical nature of this claim: the access that was formerly restricted, graduated, and mediated through physical institutions is now open, equal, and mediated through Christ and the Spirit.

## Verses 19-22: The New Identity -- Citizens, Family, Temple

"So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (2:19).

The phrase ara oun ("so then, therefore, consequently") draws the conclusion from everything argued since verse 11. The word xenoi ("strangers, foreigners") was used in verse 12 for the Gentiles' exclusion from Israel's covenants. The word paroikoi ("resident aliens, sojourners, foreigners dwelling nearby") describes a person living in a place where they have no citizenship rights -- a tolerated foreigner with limited legal standing. In the LXX, the word describes Israel's status in Egypt (Genesis 15:13, LXX: paroikon estai to sperma sou, "your offspring shall be sojourners") and the status of resident aliens in Israel's land (Leviticus 25:23, "you are strangers and sojourners [paroikoi] with me"). The paroikos lives in the community but does not belong to it.

The Gentile recipients are now told: ouketi este -- "you are no longer" strangers and aliens. The negation is emphatic: the former condition has been terminated.

The positive replacement has two dimensions. First: sympolita ton hagion -- "fellow citizens with the saints." The word sympolites ("fellow citizen, co-citizen") is a compound: syn ("with") + polites ("citizen"). It is the political counterpart to the politeia ("commonwealth") of verse 12: those who were alienated from the commonwealth are now fellow citizens within it. The word hagioi ("saints, holy ones, set-apart ones") designates the people of God. In the LXX, the hagioi are Israel -- God's set-apart people (Daniel 7:18, 22, 27, "the saints of the Most High shall receive the kingdom"). The Gentile believers are now fellow citizens with the people of God -- full members of the covenant community, with all the rights, standing, and identity that entails.

Second: oikeioi tou theou -- "members of God's household" or "members of the family of God." The word oikeios ("belonging to a household, domestic, familial") is stronger than sympolites: it moves from the civic metaphor (citizenship) to the familial metaphor (household membership). The Gentile believers are not merely citizens of a polity; they are family members of God's own household. The word oikos ("house, household") in the LXX encompasses the entire extended family, including servants, dependents, and those under the master's care. The "household of God" is the family headed by God as paterfamilias.

"Built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (2:20).

The metaphor shifts from household to building. The verb epoikodomeethentes ("having been built upon," aorist passive participle of epoikodomeo, "to build upon, to erect upon a foundation") introduces architectural imagery. The passive voice indicates that the building was done by God, not by the believers themselves -- consistent with the entire chapter's emphasis on divine agency.

The foundation is named: epi to themelio ton apostolon kai propheton -- "upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets." The word themelios ("foundation, base, groundwork") is the lowest course of stones upon which the entire structure rests. The genitive ton apostolon kai propheton ("of the apostles and prophets") can be read as subjective genitive ("the foundation that the apostles and prophets laid" -- their preaching and teaching formed the base) or appositional genitive ("the foundation that consists of the apostles and prophets" -- the apostles and prophets themselves are the foundation stones). Either reading produces a similar meaning: the community rests upon the apostolic and prophetic witness.

The identity of "prophets" (propheton) here is debated. In the LXX and Jewish tradition, "prophets" naturally refers to the Hebrew prophets -- Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the rest. But the letter itself speaks of prophets as a present gift in the ekklesia (3:5, "his holy apostles and prophets"; 4:11, "he gave some to be apostles, some prophets"). The pairing "apostles and prophets" with apostles listed first (unlike the typical Jewish ordering "Moses and the prophets") may suggest a reference to apostles and Christian prophets -- the foundational witnesses of the new covenant community. However, given the letter's sustained argument that the new community inherits the covenantal heritage of Israel, the Hebrew prophets cannot be excluded. The foundation includes both: the prophetic scriptures and the apostolic testimony converge.

The cornerstone is identified: ontos akrogoniaiou autou Christou Iesou -- "Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone." The word akrogoniaios ("cornerstone, capstone, chief corner-stone") is a compound: akros ("extreme, uttermost, chief") + gonia ("corner, angle"). In the LXX, the word appears in Isaiah 28:16: idou ego emballoo eis ta themelia Sion lithon polytelee eklekton akrogoniaion entimon -- "Behold, I am laying in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone." The Isaiah text describes God laying a foundation stone in Zion -- a tested, valuable, chosen stone that anchors the structure. The author of Ephesians identifies this stone as Christ Jesus. The cornerstone in ancient construction served a crucial function: it was the first stone set in place, the stone from which all measurements were taken, the stone that determined the alignment of the entire building. Every other stone in the structure was oriented in relation to the cornerstone. Christ Jesus, as the akrogoniaios, determines the shape, alignment, and orientation of the entire building that is the community of God.

The stone imagery has additional resonance with Psalm 118:22 (LXX 117:22): lithon hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes, houtos egenethe eis kephalen gonias -- "the stone that the builders rejected has become the head of the corner." This text, cited repeatedly in the New Testament (Matthew 21:42; Mark 12:10; Luke 20:17; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7), identifies the rejected-then-exalted stone as the Messiah. The author of Ephesians assumes this identification: Christ, the rejected stone, is the cornerstone of the new structure.

"In whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord" (2:21).

The phrase en ho ("in whom") refers to Christ Jesus the cornerstone. The entire structure exists "in him" -- the locative en continues to dominate. The phrase pasa oikodome ("the whole building, every building, the entire structure") describes the corporate entity being constructed. The participle synarmologoumene ("being joined together, being fitted together," present passive participle of synarmologeo, "to join together, to fit together, to frame together") is a compound: syn ("together") + harmologeo ("to join, to fit") from harmos ("a joint, a fitting"). The image is of stones being carefully fitted together at their joints, each stone shaped and positioned to interlock with its neighbors. The present tense indicates an ongoing process: the fitting together is not complete but continues.

The result: auxei eis naon hagion en kyrio -- "grows into a holy temple in the Lord." The verb auxei ("grows," present active of auxano, "to grow, to increase") introduces an organic metaphor alongside the architectural one -- the building grows. The combination is unusual: buildings do not normally grow. But the community of believers is both a constructed and a living entity; it is being built and it is growing. The destination of the growth is naos hagios -- "a holy temple." The word naos (as distinct from hieron, the broader temple complex) designates the inner sanctuary -- the dwelling place of God, the place where God's presence resided. In the LXX, naos refers to the innermost part of the temple (1 Kings 6:3, 5, 17; 2 Chronicles 3:17; Psalm 18:6 [LXX 17:7], "from his temple [naou] he heard my voice"). The community is growing into a naos -- not a building for human convenience but a dwelling place for God. The word hagios ("holy, set apart, consecrated") qualifies the temple: it is set apart for God's exclusive use.

The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") locates the growth: it occurs within the sphere of the Lord's authority and presence. The Lord (kyrios) is likely Christ, consistent with the letter's usage (1:15, "faith in the Lord Jesus"; cf. 4:1, 5, 17).

"In whom you also are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (2:22).

The final verse of the chapter brings the "you" pronoun back to the foreground: en ho kai hymeis -- "in whom you also." The "you" is the Gentile recipients. They are not merely observing the construction; they are part of it. They are being built into it.

The verb synoikodomeisthe ("you are being built together," present passive of synoikodomeo, "to build together, to construct jointly") has the syn- prefix that has pervaded the chapter (synezoopoiesen, synegieren, synekathisen, sympolita, synarmologoumene): together with. The Gentile believers are being built together with the Jewish believers into one structure. The present tense indicates that the process is ongoing -- the building is not finished. The passive voice again indicates divine agency: the recipients are being built, not building themselves.

The purpose: eis katoiketerion tou theou en pneumati -- "into a dwelling place of God in the Spirit." The word katoiketerion ("dwelling place, habitation, permanent residence") is a compound: kata (intensifying prefix) + oiketerion ("habitation"). In the LXX, the word appears in contexts describing God's dwelling: Exodus 15:17 (LXX), "the sanctuary, O Lord, which your hands have established... you will bring them in and plant them on the mountain of your inheritance, the place you have made for your dwelling (katoiketerion), O Lord." The katoiketerion is not a temporary lodging but a permanent habitation. The community is being constructed as God's permanent dwelling place.

The phrase en pneumati ("in the Spirit" or "by the Spirit") can be instrumental ("by means of the Spirit" -- the Spirit is the agent who effects the dwelling) or locative ("in the sphere of the Spirit" -- the Spirit is the environment in which God dwells). Both senses are operative: the Spirit is the means by which God takes up residence in the community, and the Spirit is the medium in which God's presence is experienced. This connects to 1:13-14, where the Spirit was given as the arrabon ("down payment") of the inheritance. The Spirit's presence in the community is not merely a gift to individuals but the constitutive element of the community as God's temple. Without the Spirit, the stones are an unorganized pile; in the Spirit, they are God's dwelling.

The temple imagery is the climax of the chapter's argument. The Gentile recipients began as dead (v. 1), as far off (v. 13), as strangers to the covenants (v. 12), as atheoi in the world (v. 12). They have been made alive (v. 5), brought near (v. 13), made fellow citizens (v. 19), and now constituted as living stones in the temple of God (v. 22). The trajectory is from maximum alienation to maximum intimacy: from being without God in the world to being the place where God lives.

## The Argument of the Chapter as a Whole

Ephesians 2 makes a single sustained argument in two movements, both driven by the same underlying logic: God has acted to reverse an impossible human condition.

The first movement (2:1-10) addresses the universal human predicament -- death in trespasses -- and God's response. The author begins with the Gentile recipients' particular form of that death (vv. 1-2: walking according to the world's course, under the ruler of the air's governance) and then extends the diagnosis to include Jewish believers (v. 3: we all lived the same way, children of wrath by nature like the rest). The shared death establishes the shared need. God's response is described in three verbs with the syn- prefix: made alive together with Christ, raised together with Christ, seated together with Christ (vv. 5-6). The purpose is cosmic and eschatological: to display the riches of grace throughout the coming ages (v. 7). The means is grace, received through faith, not produced by works (vv. 8-9). The result is a new creation designed for good works (v. 10).

The second movement (2:11-22) addresses the particular dimension of the human predicament that afflicted the Gentiles specifically -- their exclusion from Israel's covenantal blessings -- and God's response. The author commands the Gentile recipients to remember their former alienation from every dimension of Israel's covenant life: without Messiah, without community, without covenants, without hope, without God (vv. 11-12). God's response is described through the work of Christ on the cross: breaking the dividing wall, abolishing the regulatory system that separated, creating one new humanity from the two, reconciling both to God in one body, killing the enmity (vv. 14-16). Christ proclaimed peace to both groups (v. 17), and both now have access to the Father through one Spirit (v. 18). The result is a new identity (fellow citizens, household members, v. 19) and a new corporate reality (a temple being built on the apostolic foundation with Christ as the cornerstone, growing into God's dwelling place by the Spirit, vv. 20-22).

The two movements are parallel in structure:
- Former condition: dead in sins (vv. 1-3) / alienated from Israel's covenants (vv. 11-12)
- Divine action: God made alive, raised, enthroned (vv. 4-6) / Christ broke the wall, created one new humanity, reconciled (vv. 14-16)
- Means: grace through faith, God's gift (vv. 8-9) / the blood and flesh and cross of Christ (vv. 13-14, 16)
- Result: new creation for good works (v. 10) / new temple as God's dwelling (vv. 20-22)

The pronoun pattern is the chapter's structural backbone. "You" (Gentile recipients) appears in verses 1-2, 8, 11-13, 17, 19, and 22. "We/us" (Jewish believers included) appears in verses 3-7, 10, 14-18. The alternation is not random; it reflects the chapter's argument about two groups being brought into one. The "you" addresses the Gentile recipients' specific experience; the "we" includes the author and Jewish believers in the shared condition and shared salvation. By the end of the chapter, "you" and "we" have converged into one reality: "you also are being built together" (v. 22) -- the "you" (Gentiles) are joined with the implied "we" (Jews) into one dwelling place. The pronoun pattern enacts the theological argument: the chapter moves from separation (you/we) toward unity (both/one).

The chapter's argument connects seamlessly to what precedes and follows. Chapter 1's berakah praised God for blessings bestowed on "us" (1:3-12) and extended to "you also" (1:13). Chapter 2 explains the mechanism: the "us" were dead and made alive (2:3-6); the "you" were dead and made alive (2:1-2, 5-6); both were separated and have been united (2:14-18). Chapter 3 will name this unity explicitly as "the mystery" (3:3-6) -- that the Gentiles are fellow heirs, fellow members of one body, fellow partakers of the promise. Chapter 2 is the narrative content of that mystery: it tells the story of how the mystery was accomplished.

A word about what this chapter is and is not arguing. The chapter is arguing about two things: the rescue of dead humans by divine grace (first movement) and the unification of two formerly divided groups into one new humanity (second movement). The chapter's primary concern in both movements is corporate and cosmic: it describes what God has done for and with groups of people -- Gentile and Jewish believers together -- and how their union constitutes a new temple, a new humanity, a display piece for the ages. The language of "saved by grace through faith, not of works" (vv. 8-9) functions within this corporate argument as the explanation of how the Gentile recipients came to share in Israel's blessings: not by performing the works of the Torah that separated Jew from Gentile, but by grace through faith. The denial of works-based salvation in verse 9 and the affirmation of created purpose in good works in verse 10 are held together without tension: the entry into the community is by grace; the life within the community is characterized by the good works God prepared.

The temple imagery in the closing verses (vv. 20-22) deserves particular attention for its implications. If the community of Jewish and Gentile believers is God's naos -- God's inner sanctuary, God's dwelling place -- then the physical temple in Jerusalem, with its courts of separation and its wall of exclusion, is not merely augmented but superseded. The very structure that embodied the division (the soreg, the graduated courts) is replaced by a structure that embodies the unity (one temple, built of living stones from both groups, indwelt by one Spirit). The first-century hearer who knew the Jerusalem temple would understand the radical claim being made: the locus of God's presence on earth is no longer a building made with hands but a community of people -- Jews and Gentiles together -- being built by God into a habitation for his Spirit.
