# Revelation 17 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 16):** Chapter 16 narrated the pouring out of the seven bowls of wrath that chapter 15 had introduced and prepared. After the seven angels emerged from the heavenly temple clothed in priestly garments and received the golden bowls from one of the four living creatures (15:5-8), a loud voice from the temple commanded: "Go and pour out on the earth the seven bowls of the wrath of God" (16:1). The bowls replicated and intensified the Exodus plagues in systematic fashion: the first produced loathsome and malignant sores on those who bore the mark of the beast and worshiped his image (16:2; cf. Exodus 9:8-12, the plague of boils); the second turned the sea to blood "like that of a dead man," and every living thing in the sea died (16:3; cf. Exodus 7:20-21); the third turned the rivers and springs to blood (16:4), at which the angel of the waters declared God righteous: "because they poured out the blood of saints and prophets, you have given them blood to drink. They deserve it" (16:5-6), and the altar confirmed: "Yes, Lord God, the Almighty, true and righteous are your judgments" (16:7) -- directly echoing the conquerors' song in 15:3-4; the fourth caused the sun to scorch people with fire, and they "blasphemed the name of God who has the power over these plagues, and they did not repent so as to give him glory" (16:8-9); the fifth was poured on the throne of the beast, and his kingdom became darkened, and people "gnawed their tongues because of pain, and they blasphemed the God of heaven because of their pains and their sores; and they did not repent of their deeds" (16:10-11; cf. Exodus 10:21-23, the plague of darkness); the sixth was poured on the great river Euphrates and its water dried up to prepare the way for the kings from the east (16:12), and three unclean spirits like frogs came from the mouths of the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet, performing signs and gathering the kings of the whole world to "the war of the great day of God, the Almighty" at "the place which in Hebrew is called Har-Magedon" (16:13-16); the seventh was poured into the air, and a loud voice from the throne declared "It is done" (Gegonen, 16:17), followed by flashes of lightning, sounds, thunder, and the greatest earthquake ever (16:18), at which "the great city was split into three parts, and the cities of the nations fell. Babylon the great was remembered before God, to give her the cup of the wine of his fierce wrath" (16:19). Every island fled and every mountain vanished, and enormous hailstones fell from heaven, and people "blasphemed God because of the plague of hail" (16:20-21).

Two elements of chapter 16 are essential for understanding chapter 17. First, the consistent response of those under judgment was blasphemy and refusal to repent (16:9, 11, 21). The bowls did not produce repentance; they hardened those already committed to the beast's system, exactly as the Exodus plagues hardened Pharaoh. Second, the seventh bowl specifically named "Babylon the great" as the object of God's wrath (16:19): "Babylon the great was remembered before God, to give her the cup of the wine of his fierce wrath." This mention of Babylon at the climax of the bowls is the trigger for what follows in chapters 17-18. The bowls have completed the execution of wrath (15:1: "in them the wrath of God is finished"), but the author has not yet explained who Babylon is or narrated her fall in detail. The second angel of 14:8 announced "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great," and 16:19 recorded that God "remembered" her. Chapters 17-18 now unpack this: who is Babylon, what is her relationship to the beast, and what does her fall mean?

**What the author argues immediately after (Revelation 18):** Chapter 18 narrates the detailed lamentation over Babylon's fall. An angel descends from heaven with great authority, the earth illuminated by his glory, and cries out: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!" (18:2) -- repeating the announcement of 14:8 but now with elaboration: she has "become a dwelling place of demons and a prison of every unclean spirit" (18:2). Her sins are specified: "all the nations have drunk of the wine of the passion of her immorality, and the kings of the earth have committed acts of immorality with her, and the merchants of the earth have become rich by the wealth of her sensuality" (18:3). A voice from heaven commands: "Come out of her, my people, so that you will not participate in her sins and receive of her plagues" (18:4). Three groups then mourn Babylon's destruction in succession: the kings of the earth who "committed acts of immorality and lived sensuously with her" weep and stand at a distance saying "Woe, woe, the great city, Babylon, the strong city!" (18:9-10); the merchants of the earth weep because "no one buys their cargoes any more" (18:11), and the lengthy inventory of luxury goods is listed (18:12-13) -- gold, silver, precious stones, fine linen, purple, silk, scarlet, all kinds of citron wood, ivory, bronze, iron, marble, cinnamon, spice, incense, ointment, frankincense, wine, olive oil, fine flour, wheat, cattle, sheep, horses, chariots, and "bodies and souls of men" (18:13); and the shipmasters and sailors weep because "the great city, in which all who had ships at sea became rich by her wealth" has been laid waste "in one hour" (18:17-19). Then heaven, the saints, apostles, and prophets are commanded to "rejoice over her" because "God has pronounced judgment for you against her" (18:20). A strong angel picks up a stone like a great millstone and throws it into the sea, saying "So will Babylon, the great city, be thrown down with violence, and will not be found any longer" (18:21). The chapter ends with the final verdict: "in her was found the blood of prophets and of saints and of all who have been slain on the earth" (18:24).

**How chapter 17 connects the surrounding argument:** The connection is seamless. The seventh bowl (16:17-21) announced that "Babylon the great was remembered before God." The audience has heard the name "Babylon" since 14:8, has seen the bowls poured out, and has heard Babylon named at the climax of the seventh bowl. But the author has not yet shown who Babylon is, what she looks like, how she operates, or how her fall comes about. Chapter 17 is the interpretive unveiling of the entity that has been named but not yet fully seen. One of the seven bowl-angels -- the very angels who poured out the wrath that "remembered" Babylon -- now serves as John's guide to a vision that explains Babylon's identity, her relationship to the beast, and the mechanism of her destruction. Chapter 18 then narrates the consequences of that destruction in the form of a funeral dirge. The sequence is: the bowls execute wrath and name Babylon (chapter 16), the angel interprets who Babylon is and how she falls (chapter 17), and the earthly response to her fall is lamented and celebrated (chapter 18). Chapters 17 and 18 together are the expansion of the single phrase in 16:19: "Babylon the great was remembered before God, to give her the cup of the wine of his fierce wrath."

The subject has not changed. From the moment the second angel of 14:8 announced Babylon's fall, the author has been building toward this full narration. The bowls were the instruments; chapters 17-18 are the interpretation and dramatization.

## Verses 1-2: The Bowl-Angel's Invitation

"Then one of the seven angels who had the seven bowls came and spoke with me, saying, 'Come here, I will show you the judgment of the great harlot who sits on many waters, with whom the kings of the earth committed acts of immorality, and those who dwell on the earth were made drunk with the wine of her immorality.'"

**"Then one of the seven angels who had the seven bowls came and spoke with me" (kai ēlthen heis ek tōn hepta angelōn tōn echontōn tas hepta phialas kai elalēsen met' emou).** The guide-angel is not a new figure but one of the seven who poured out the bowls in chapter 16. The identification is deliberate: the same angel who participated in the execution of God's wrath now serves as the interpreter who explains what that wrath was directed against and why. The author creates a structural parallel that will become fully visible in 21:9, where again "one of the seven angels who had the seven bowls" comes to John and says "Come here, I will show you the bride, the wife of the Lamb." The two invitations form a mirror pair: one angel shows the harlot; a corresponding angel shows the bride. Babylon and the new Jerusalem are presented as deliberate counterparts, seen through the same angelic lens.

The verb elalēsen met' emou ("spoke with me") describes personal, conversational interaction. This is not a distant proclamation but an intimate exchange between the angel and the seer. The angel will explain the vision as it unfolds, providing interpretive commentary that the author presents as authoritative.

**"Come here, I will show you the judgment of the great harlot who sits on many waters" (deuro, deixō soi to krima tēs pornēs tēs megalēs tēs kathēmenēs epi hydatōn pollōn).** The invitation uses the imperative deuro ("come, come here") followed by the future deixō ("I will show"). The angel promises to show John the krima -- the "judgment, verdict, sentence" -- of the harlot. The word krima can denote both the judicial sentence itself and its execution. What follows in chapters 17-18 is both the verdict (why Babylon is condemned) and the sentence (how she is destroyed).

The figure is called hē pornē hē megalē -- "the great harlot" or "the great prostitute." The word pornē ("prostitute, harlot") in prophetic usage designates a city or nation that has been unfaithful to God through idolatry, or a city that seduces others into spiritual-political corruption. The prophetic background is decisive for understanding this figure.

Isaiah 23:15-17 applies the harlot image to Tyre, the great commercial city: "At the end of seventy years the LORD will visit Tyre. Then she will go back to her harlot's wages (to pornikon) and will play the harlot (porneusei) with all the kingdoms on the face of the earth" (LXX). Tyre's "harlotry" is her commercial alliances and seductions -- the way a trading city draws all nations into profitable but corrupting relationships.

Nahum 3:4 calls Nineveh, the capital of Assyria, "the charming one, the mistress of sorceries, who sells nations by her harlotries (tais porneiais autēs) and families by her sorceries." Nineveh's "harlotry" is her imperial seduction of the nations, drawing them into her political and religious orbit through attractive but ultimately destructive alliances.

Most significantly, Ezekiel 16 and 23 apply the harlot image to Jerusalem itself. In Ezekiel 16:15, God says to Jerusalem: "But you trusted in your beauty and played the harlot (eporneusen) because of your fame, and you poured out your harlotries (tēn porneian sou) on every passer-by." In Ezekiel 23, the two sisters Oholah (Samaria) and Oholibah (Jerusalem) both "played the harlot" with foreign nations. The prophetic tradition of the harlot-city is thus not limited to foreign oppressors; it can also designate God's own city when she has become unfaithful.

The phrase "who sits on many waters" (tēs kathēmenēs epi hydatōn pollōn) derives directly from Jeremiah 51:13 (LXX 28:13), where God addresses ancient Babylon: "O you who dwell by many waters (epi hydasi pollois), rich in treasures, your end has come, the measure of your covetousness." Historical Babylon was built on the Euphrates and surrounded by an elaborate canal system. The "many waters" in Jeremiah refer to the literal waterways that sustained Babylon's wealth and commerce. In verse 15, the angel will interpret the waters symbolically: "The waters which you saw where the harlot sits, are peoples and multitudes and nations and tongues" (17:15). The "many waters" are not geographical but demographic -- the vast populations over which the harlot exercises her influence. She "sits on" them, meaning she is supported by, sustained by, and exercises dominion over the multitudes.

**"With whom the kings of the earth committed acts of immorality" (meth' hēs eporneusan hoi basileis tēs gēs).** The verb eporneusan ("committed sexual immorality") describes the political-religious alliances that the kings of the earth have formed with the harlot. In the prophetic framework, the "immorality" is not literal sexual behavior but the seductive entanglement of rulers with a corrupt system. The kings of the earth are drawn into the harlot's orbit, entering into relationships of mutual benefit and mutual corruption. They receive her luxuries; she receives their political support. The arrangement is presented in the language of prostitution: it is transactional, corrupting, and a betrayal of the allegiance that properly belongs to God alone.

Ezekiel 16:26-29 describes Jerusalem's "harlotry" with Egypt, Assyria, and Chaldea in similar terms: political alliances framed as sexual liaisons. The difference is that in Ezekiel, Jerusalem is the unfaithful wife of God who prostitutes herself with foreign powers. In Revelation 17, the harlot-city draws the kings of the earth to herself. She is not the unfaithful wife but the seductress who corrupts others.

**"And those who dwell on the earth were made drunk with the wine of her immorality" (kai emethysthēsan hoi katoikountes tēn gēn ek tou oinou tēs porneias autēs).** The earth-dwellers (hoi katoikountes tēn gēn) -- the recurring designation in this document for those whose identity and allegiance are bound to the earthly order (3:10; 6:10; 8:13; 11:10; 13:8, 12, 14) -- have been made drunk (emethysthēsan, aorist passive: "were made drunk," indicating the harlot's agency in intoxicating them). The wine of her immorality (ek tou oinou tēs porneias autēs) is the same phrase used in the second angel's announcement in 14:8: "she who has made all the nations drink of the wine of the passion of her immorality." The intoxication is the condition of those who have been seduced by the harlot's system -- they are not thinking clearly, not perceiving reality accurately, not capable of the sober judgment that would lead them to "fear God and give him glory" (14:7). The harlot's wine is the opposite of the sober wakefulness that the document demands of the faithful (16:15: "Blessed is the one who stays awake and keeps his clothes").

Jeremiah 51:7 (LXX 28:7) again provides the source: "Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand, making all the earth drunk. The nations drank of her wine; therefore the nations went mad." The "madness" (emainonto) that follows drinking Babylon's wine is the inability to perceive reality -- the nations cannot see that their alliance with the harlot is self-destructive, that her wealth is built on blood, and that her fall is inevitable.

## Verse 3: The Woman on the Scarlet Beast in the Wilderness

"And he carried me away in the Spirit into a wilderness; and I saw a woman sitting on a scarlet beast, full of blasphemous names, having seven heads and ten horns."

**"And he carried me away in the Spirit into a wilderness" (kai apēnenken me eis erēmon en pneumati).** The phrase en pneumati ("in the Spirit") marks a visionary transport. The same phrase appeared in 1:10 ("I was in the Spirit on the Lord's day"), 4:2 ("immediately I was in the Spirit, and behold, a throne was standing in heaven"), and will appear in 21:10 ("And he carried me away in the Spirit to a great and high mountain, and showed me the holy city, Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God"). Each use of en pneumati introduces a major visionary sequence. The transport here takes John to a wilderness (erēmon) -- a detail whose significance should not be overlooked.

The wilderness (erēmos) has appeared already in this document. In 12:6, the woman (Israel/the covenant community) "fled into the wilderness (eis tēn erēmon) where she had a place prepared by God, so that there she would be nourished for one thousand two hundred and sixty days." In 12:14, the woman was given eagle's wings to fly "into the wilderness to her place, where she was nourished for a time and times and half a time, from the presence of the serpent." The wilderness in chapter 12 was a place of divine protection and nourishment -- the same wilderness where Israel was sustained after the Exodus (Deuteronomy 8:2-3, 15-16). In the prophetic tradition, the wilderness can also be the place where God confronts a corrupt city. Isaiah 21:1 introduces the oracle against Babylon as "the oracle concerning the wilderness of the sea" (to horama tēs erēmou, LXX). Jeremiah 51:43 (LXX 28:43) describes Babylon's future: "Her cities have become an object of horror, a parched land and a wilderness (erēmos), a land in which no man lives."

There is an important contrast being drawn. In chapter 12, the faithful woman was nourished in the wilderness -- the wilderness as refuge. In chapter 17, the harlot is shown to John in the wilderness -- the wilderness as the place of desolation and judgment. The woman of chapter 12 fled to the wilderness for safety; the harlot of chapter 17 is found in the wilderness, the domain of her coming desolation. The author has constructed a deliberate counterpart: two women, both associated with the wilderness, but in opposite roles. The faithful woman is protected there; the harlot is exposed there. This woman-to-woman contrast will continue throughout the chapter and find its completion in the bride of chapters 21-22.

**"And I saw a woman sitting on a scarlet beast, full of blasphemous names, having seven heads and ten horns" (kai eidon gynaika kathēmenēn epi thērion kokkinon, gemon onomatōn blasphēmias, echon kephalas hepta kai kerata deka).** The woman and the beast are presented as distinct but intimately connected figures. The woman is riding the beast -- she is seated upon it, supported by it, carried by it. Their relationship is one of mutual dependence: the beast gives the woman her position and mobility, while the woman presumably gives the beast a certain appearance of legitimacy and attractiveness.

The beast is immediately recognizable. It has "seven heads and ten horns" (kephalas hepta kai kerata deka) -- the same configuration as the beast from the sea in 13:1 and the dragon in 12:3. The continuity is deliberate: this is the same beast system that the dragon empowered in chapter 13. The beast is described as kokkinon ("scarlet, crimson") -- a color that connects it to the dragon of 12:3, who was pyrros ("fiery red"). The colors are in the same range: the dragon was red, the beast is scarlet. The beast's color also anticipates the woman's clothing in verse 4: she is dressed in purple and scarlet. The beast and the woman share the same color palette, visually linking them as parts of a single system.

The beast is gemon onomatōn blasphēmias -- "full of blasphemous names." In 13:1, the beast had "blasphemous names" (onomata blasphēmias) on its heads. Here the blasphemous names cover the entire beast, not just the heads. The proliferation of blasphemous names indicates the intensification or comprehensive nature of the beast's self-deifying claims. Every surface of this creature declares what belongs to God alone. As discussed in the analysis of 13:1, the "blasphemous names" refer to the divine titles arrogated by imperial power -- titles such as Sebastos/Augustus, Soter, Kyrios, and Theos that a first-century hearer in Roman Asia would associate with the emperor's official titulature.

## Verses 4-5: The Woman's Appearance and Name

"The woman was clothed in purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls, having in her hand a gold cup full of abominations and of the unclean things of her immorality, and on her forehead a name was written, a mystery, 'BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS AND OF THE ABOMINATIONS OF THE EARTH.'"

**"The woman was clothed in purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls" (kai hē gynē ēn peribeblēmenē porphyroun kai kokkinon, kai kechrysōmenē chrysiō kai lithō timiō kai margaritais).** The description of the harlot's appearance is deliberate and symbolically dense.

Purple (porphyroun) and scarlet (kokkinon) are the colors of imperial luxury and royal power. In the Roman world, purple-dyed cloth was extraordinarily expensive, produced from the mucus of the murex sea snail in a laborious process, and was associated with senatorial and imperial rank. The toga praetexta bordered in purple was a mark of Roman magistrates; the toga purpurea entirely of purple was reserved for triumphing generals and emperors. Scarlet (from the kermes insect) was similarly expensive and associated with military rank and royal display. Together, purple and scarlet communicate immense wealth, political power, and self-display.

These colors also have a significant scriptural resonance. In the construction of the tabernacle, purple (porphyra, LXX) and scarlet (kokkinon, LXX) were among the materials used for the curtains and priestly vestments (Exodus 25:4; 26:1, 31, 36; 28:5-6, 8, 15, 33). The tabernacle employed blue, purple, and scarlet thread together (Exodus 26:1). The harlot wears purple and scarlet -- two of the three tabernacle colors -- but without the blue (hyakinthos) that represents the heavenly dimension in the LXX tabernacle tradition. The omission of blue may be significant: the harlot possesses the outward appearance of sacred richness but lacks the heavenly component. Her adornment is a counterfeit of priestly splendor.

The gold, precious stones, and pearls further elaborate the display of extreme wealth. In 18:12, these items will appear in the catalogue of Babylon's trade goods: "cargoes of gold and silver and precious stones and pearls." In 18:16, the mourners will lament: "Woe, woe, the great city, she who was clothed in fine linen and purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls." The harlot's personal adornment matches her commercial empire -- she wears what she sells.

The contrast with the bride of the Lamb is pointed. In 19:8, the bride is given "fine linen, bright and clean" (byssinon lampron katharon), and the fine linen is identified as "the righteous acts of the saints" (ta dikaiōmata tōn hagiōn). The harlot's clothing is purchased luxury; the bride's clothing is earned righteousness. The harlot adorns herself with gold and stones; the bride is clothed in the pure deeds of the faithful. The two women are dressed in ways that reveal their respective characters.

**"Having in her hand a gold cup full of abominations and of the unclean things of her immorality" (echousa potērion chrysoun en tē cheiri autēs gemon bdelygmatōn kai ta akatharta tēs porneias autēs).** The golden cup (potērion chrysoun) recalls Jeremiah 51:7 (LXX 28:7): "Babylon was a golden cup (potērion chrysoun) in the LORD's hand, making all the earth drunk." In Jeremiah, the cup is in God's hand -- Babylon is the instrument through which God administers judgment on the nations. In Revelation 17, the cup is in the harlot's own hand -- she drinks from it and gives it to others. The shift from God's hand to the harlot's hand indicates her autonomy in dispensing her intoxicating influence. She is not consciously serving as God's instrument; she is acting on her own behalf to seduce the nations.

The cup is "full of abominations" (gemon bdelygmatōn). The word bdelygma ("abomination, detestable thing") is the LXX's standard term for the idolatrous practices that God finds repulsive. Deuteronomy 7:25-26: "The graven images of their gods you are to burn with fire; you shall not covet the silver or the gold that is on them, nor take it for yourselves... you shall not bring an abomination (bdelygma) into your house." Deuteronomy 18:12: "whoever does these things is an abomination (bdelygma) to the LORD." Deuteronomy 29:17: "you have seen their abominations (bdelygmata) and their idols." The word appears in Daniel 9:27 and 11:31 and 12:11 in the phrase "the abomination of desolation" (bdelygma tēs erēmōseōs) -- the desecrating object placed in the temple. The harlot's cup is filled with these abominations -- the concentrated essence of everything that God's covenant people were commanded to destroy and avoid.

The phrase ta akatharta tēs porneias autēs ("the unclean things of her immorality") adds the language of ritual impurity. The word akathartos ("unclean, impure") is the opposite of katharos ("clean, pure") -- the fundamental purity distinction of the Levitical system. The harlot's cup contains not merely what is offensive but what renders unclean, what defiles, what contaminates everything it touches. Those who drink from her cup are polluted by what they consume.

**"And on her forehead a name was written, a mystery" (kai epi to metōpon autēs onoma gegrammenon, mystērion).** The placement of a name on the forehead recalls the competing forehead inscriptions that have structured the document's imagery of allegiance. The 144,000 bear the Lamb's name and the Father's name on their foreheads (14:1). The servants of God are sealed on their foreheads (7:3). The beast's followers bear the mark on their foreheads (13:16). The high priest bore "Holy to the LORD" on his forehead (Exodus 28:36-38). The harlot bears her own name on her forehead -- a self-identification that declares what she is.

The word mystērion ("mystery, secret, hidden meaning") functions here as a descriptor of the name that follows. In the author's usage, mystērion denotes something whose true meaning is hidden behind its surface appearance and requires revelation to understand. In 1:20, the "mystery of the seven stars" and the "seven golden lampstands" was explained: the stars are angels and the lampstands are churches. The term signals that what follows is symbolic -- the name is not a literal label but a code that requires interpretation. The word may also be read as part of the inscription itself (i.e., the name written is "Mystery: Babylon the Great..."), but the author's use of mystērion elsewhere as a hermeneutical marker suggests it functions as a signal to the reader that the name that follows carries hidden significance.

**"BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS AND OF THE ABOMINATIONS OF THE EARTH" (BABYLŌN HĒ MEGALĒ, HĒ MĒTĒR TŌN PORNŌN KAI TŌN BDELYGMATŌN TĒS GĒS).** The name has three elements.

First, "Babylon the Great" (Babylōn hē megalē) -- the title already introduced in 14:8 and 16:19. As discussed in the analysis of 14:8, the epithet "the great" echoes Nebuchadnezzar's boast in Daniel 4:30. The name "Babylon" is typological: it identifies the present oppressive power with the archetypal oppressor of God's people from Israel's history.

Second, "the mother of harlots" (hē mētēr tōn pornōn). The harlot is not merely one prostitute among many but the origin, source, and exemplar of all harlotry. She is the mother -- the one who generates and sustains the entire system of spiritual-political corruption. Every lesser instance of the nations' seduction proceeds from her. The cities, vassal kingdoms, and provincial authorities that imitate her ways are her offspring. The word mētēr ("mother") indicates generative power: the harlot does not merely corrupt; she reproduces her corruption.

Third, "the mother of the abominations of the earth" (hē mētēr tōn bdelygmatōn tēs gēs). She is the source not only of the harlotry but of the abominations -- the idolatrous, defiling practices that constitute the content of her golden cup. The "abominations of the earth" encompass the entire range of false worship, blasphemous claims, and dehumanizing practices that the beast system has imposed.

Roman practice provides a first-century referent for this self-identification on the forehead. Prostitutes in the Roman world were sometimes identified by a label (titulus) worn on the forehead or displayed above their doors, advertising their trade name. Seneca (Controversiae 1.2) and Juvenal (Satires 6.122-123) attest to this practice. The harlot Babylon wears her name on her forehead as a prostitute would -- openly displaying what she is. The irony is that despite this open display, the world does not recognize her for what she is (they are "drunk" with her wine, 17:2). Only the seer, guided by the angel, perceives the truth.

## Verse 6: The Harlot Drunk with Blood

"And I saw the woman drunk with the blood of the saints, and with the blood of the witnesses of Jesus. When I saw her, I wondered greatly."

**"And I saw the woman drunk with the blood of the saints, and with the blood of the witnesses of Jesus" (kai eidon tēn gynaika methyousan ek tou haimatos tōn hagiōn kai ek tou haimatos tōn martyrōn Iēsou).** The image shifts from the harlot as one who intoxicates others (verse 2: she made the earth-dwellers drunk with her wine) to the harlot as one who is herself drunk -- but not with wine. Her intoxicant is blood. The verb methyousan (present participle of methyō, "to be drunk, to be intoxicated") portrays the harlot in a state of ongoing, active inebriation. She is presently drunk, not merely stained. The present tense indicates that the bloodshed is not a past act she regrets but a continuing condition she enjoys. She is insatiable.

The blood is identified by two sources: "the blood of the saints" (tou haimatos tōn hagiōn) and "the blood of the witnesses of Jesus" (tou haimatos tōn martyrōn Iēsou). The saints (hagioi) are the consecrated people of God -- the same community persecuted by the beast in 13:7 and commended for their perseverance in 13:10 and 14:12. The "witnesses of Jesus" (martyres Iēsou) are those whose testimony (martyria) about Jesus cost them their lives. The word martyrs (martyrōn) in this document carries the double weight of its first-century usage: a martys is one who testifies, a witness who declares what they have seen and known. But because the testimony these witnesses bear draws lethal opposition from the beast system, the word has already begun its semantic journey toward the later meaning of "martyr" -- one who dies for testimony. In 6:9, the souls under the altar had been "slain because of the word of God, and because of the testimony (martyrian) which they had maintained." In 11:7, the beast "overcame and killed" the two witnesses (martyras). In 12:11, the brothers overcame the accuser "by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony (martyrias), and they did not love their life even when faced with death." The harlot is drunk with the blood of these people.

The two phrases -- "blood of the saints" and "blood of the witnesses" -- may describe the same group from two angles (the saints are the witnesses, and the witnesses are the saints), or they may distinguish two categories within the persecuted community (the holy ones in general and those specifically killed for bearing testimony to Jesus). The kai ("and") is most naturally read as epexegetical or additive: the harlot's victims are God's consecrated people who bore witness to Jesus.

The image of a city drunk on the blood of the righteous has prophetic precedent. In Jeremiah 51:49 (LXX 28:49): "As Babylon has caused the slain of Israel to fall, so at Babylon the slain of all the earth will fall." Isaiah 34:7 (on Edom): "the land will be soaked with blood" (LXX). The harlot's blood-drunkenness reveals the true nature of the luxury described in verse 4. Behind the gold, the pearls, the purple robes, and the golden cup lies violent persecution. The wealth and splendor are built on the murder of the faithful. The system sustains itself by eliminating those who refuse to participate. This will be stated definitively in 18:24: "And in her was found the blood of prophets and of saints and of all who have been slain on the earth."

**"When I saw her, I wondered greatly" (kai ethaumasa idōn autēn thauma mega).** The verb ethaumasa ("I marveled, I wondered, I was astonished") and the cognate noun thauma ("wonder, marvel, astonishment") describe John's visceral response to the vision. The word thaumazō in this document has a specific valence. In 13:3, "the whole earth was amazed (ethaumasthē) and followed after the beast" -- the "wonder" of the earth-dwellers led them to worship. In 17:8, the earth-dwellers "will wonder (thaumasthēsontai) when they see the beast." Wondering/marveling in this document is typically the precursor to being deceived. John's reaction is significant: he is drawn in by what he sees, experiencing the same astonishment that leads the earth-dwellers astray. The angel's interpretation in the following verses serves to correct this response -- to move John from astonishment to understanding, from wonder to discernment.

The content of John's wonder may be the jarring juxtaposition of splendor and horror: a woman dressed in royal finery, dripping with gold and jewels, holding a golden cup -- but drunk with blood. The cognitive dissonance between her beauty and her violence is precisely what makes her effective as a seductress. The world sees the gold and pearls; John sees the blood. Or perhaps John marvels because the figure is simultaneously attractive and repulsive, and the tension between these qualities produces the vertigo of wonder. The angel will immediately address this: "Why do you wonder?" (17:7).

## Verses 7-8: The Angel's Interpretation Begins -- The Mystery of the Beast

"And the angel said to me, 'Why do you wonder? I will tell you the mystery of the woman and of the beast that carries her, which has the seven heads and the ten horns. The beast that you saw was, and is not, and is about to come up out of the abyss and go to destruction. And those who dwell on the earth, whose name has not been written in the book of life from the foundation of the world, will wonder when they see the beast, that he was and is not and will come.'"

**"Why do you wonder? I will tell you the mystery of the woman and of the beast that carries her" (dia ti ethaumasas; egō erō soi to mystērion tēs gynaikos kai tou thēriou tou bastazontos autēn).** The angel's question implies a gentle rebuke: John should not wonder (thaumazō) at the harlot the way the earth-dwellers wonder at the beast. The remedy for misdirected wonder is interpretation -- the angel will tell (erō, future of legō) the mystērion ("mystery, hidden meaning") of both the woman and the beast. The word mystērion appears here for the second time in the chapter (cf. verse 5, the inscription on the forehead). The figure's true identity is a "mystery" -- it is not self-evident but requires angelic revelation to decode. The surface appearance (gold, jewels, royal garments) conceals the reality (blood, abomination, blasphemy). The angel's interpretation will strip away the surface and reveal what lies beneath.

The beast is described as tou bastazontos autēn -- "the one carrying her." The verb bastazō ("to carry, to bear, to support") indicates that the beast is the vehicle and support for the woman. She rides; the beast carries. The relationship between the two figures -- the harlot and the beast -- is now made structural: the beast provides the political-military power on which the harlot's system depends. Without the beast, the woman has no platform. Without the woman, the beast has no attractive veneer. Together they form a single system: raw power (the beast) dressed in seductive luxury (the woman).

**"The beast that you saw was, and is not, and is about to come up out of the abyss and go to destruction" (to thērion ho eides ēn kai ouk estin kai mellei anabainein ek tēs abyssou kai eis apōleian hypagei).** This is one of the most theologically dense statements in the chapter. The beast is described with a threefold temporal formula: ēn ("was"), ouk estin ("is not"), mellei anabainein ("is about to come up"). This formula is a deliberate parody of the divine self-identification that runs throughout the document.

In 1:4, God is described as "him who is and who was and who is to come" (ho ōn kai ho ēn kai ho erchomenos). In 1:8: "I am the Alpha and the Omega, says the Lord God, who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty." In 4:8, the four living creatures declare "Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God, the Almighty, who was and who is and who is to come." In 11:17: "We give you thanks, O Lord God, the Almighty, who are and who were." In 16:5: "Righteous are you, who are and who were, O Holy One."

God's threefold temporal designation expresses his eternal, sovereign existence across all time. He is (present), he was (past), he is coming (future). The beast's formula inverts this. The beast "was" (past existence), "is not" (present non-existence), and "is about to come up" (future reappearance). Where God eternally is, the beast currently is not. Where God's "coming" is sovereign and salvific, the beast's "coming up" is from the abyss (ek tēs abyssou) -- the domain of demonic powers and imprisonment (9:1-2, 11; 11:7; 20:1-3) -- and terminates in "destruction" (apōleia). The beast's trajectory is from non-existence through a brief reappearance to permanent ruin. God's existence has no beginning and no end; the beast's existence is interrupted, temporary, and doomed.

The phrase ek tēs abyssou ("from the abyss") connects this beast directly to 11:7, where "the beast that comes up out of the abyss" (to thērion to anabainon ek tēs abyssou) killed the two witnesses. The abyss is the prison of destructive forces: in 9:1-2, the shaft of the abyss was opened and smoke and locusts poured out; in 9:11, the king of the locusts was "the angel of the abyss." In 20:1-3, an angel will seize the dragon and throw him into the abyss, sealing it over him. The beast's origin from the abyss identifies it as a creature of the underworld, not of heaven -- its power comes from below, not from above. And its destination is apōleia -- "destruction, perdition, ruin" -- the same word used in 2 Thessalonians 2:3 for "the man of lawlessness, the son of destruction (tēs apōleias)." The beast's arc is from ruin to ruin, with a brief interlude of apparent power.

For the first-century hearer, the "was, is not, and is about to come" may evoke the Nero redivivus expectation discussed in the analysis of 13:3 and 13:18. Nero "was" (he lived and reigned), "is not" (he died in 68 CE), and popular expectation held that he would return -- "is about to come up." The author may be using this widely known expectation as the vehicle for his theological point: the beast's power is a parody of God's eternal existence. Even if the beast reappears, its trajectory ends in destruction, not in eternal reign.

**"And those who dwell on the earth, whose name has not been written in the book of life from the foundation of the world, will wonder when they see the beast, that he was and is not and will come" (kai thaumasthēsontai hoi katoikountes epi tēs gēs, hōn ou gegraptai to onoma epi to biblion tēs zōēs apo katabolēs kosmou, blepontes to thērion hoti ēn kai ouk estin kai parestai).** The earth-dwellers' response to the beast is thaumasthēsontai -- "they will wonder, they will be amazed." This is the same response noted in 13:3: "the whole earth was amazed (ethaumasthē) and followed after the beast." The wonder of the earth-dwellers is the precursor to worship, as established in 13:3-4: amazement leads to following, and following leads to worship. The earth-dwellers marvel at the beast's apparent conquest of death -- the "was, is not, and will come" formula impresses them as evidence of supernatural power. They see a counterfeit of divine eternity and are deceived.

The qualification is the same as in 13:8: the earth-dwellers whose names have not been written in the book of life are the ones who wonder. Those whose names are written do not wonder -- they see through the beast's pretension because they know the truly eternal One, "who is and who was and who is to come." The "book of life from the foundation of the world" (to biblion tēs zōēs apo katabolēs kosmou) reasserts that God's registration of his people precedes history. The phrase functions pastorally: the faithful are not newly or tenuously inscribed but have been known to God since the beginning.

The final word in the beast's description shifts from mellei anabainein ("is about to come up") in verse 8a to parestai ("will come" or "will be present") in verse 8b. The verb parestai (future of pareimi, "to be present, to arrive") may allude to the concept of parousia -- the "coming" or "presence" of a divine or royal figure. If so, the beast's parestai is a parody of Christ's parousia: the beast has its own "coming," its own moment of appearing, but it terminates in destruction, not in eternal reign.

## Verses 9-11: The Seven Heads Interpreted

"Here is the mind which has wisdom. The seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman sits, and they are seven kings; five have fallen, one is, the other has not yet come; and when he comes, he must remain a little while. The beast which was and is not, is himself also an eighth and is one of the seven, and he goes to destruction."

**"Here is the mind which has wisdom" (hōde ho nous ho echōn sophian).** This formula parallels 13:18: "Here is wisdom. Let him who has understanding calculate the number of the beast." Both formulae introduce interpretive riddles that require discernment. The word nous ("mind, understanding, intellect") indicates that what follows is not transparent but demands reflection. The sophia ("wisdom") required is not worldly cleverness but the spiritual perception that comes from knowing God's ways -- the wisdom that enables the hearer to decode the imagery.

**"The seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman sits" (hai hepta kephalai hepta orē eisin, hopou hē gynē kathētai ep' autōn).** The first identification: the seven heads are seven mountains (hepta orē). The word oros ("mountain, hill") in Greek does not distinguish between a mountain and a hill. The identification of a city with seven hills/mountains would be immediately recognizable to any first-century hearer in the Greco-Roman world. Rome was universally known as the city built on seven hills (septem montes/colles): the Palatine, Aventine, Caelian, Esquiline, Viminal, Quirinal, and Capitoline. Roman poets and writers routinely referred to Rome by this designation. Virgil (Georgics 2.535) described "Rome who has compassed for herself seven hills with a single wall." Martial (Epigrams 4.64) called Rome "the city of the seven dominating hills" (septem dominos montes). Cicero (Ad Atticum 6.5) referenced the same. Ovid, Propertius, and others use the phrase. Coins from the late Republic and early Empire depicted Roma seated on seven hills. The festival of the Septimontium celebrated the seven hills explicitly. A first-century audience hearing "seven mountains on which the woman sits" would not need the angel's subsequent explanation: they would recognize Rome.

The woman "sits" (kathētai) on the mountains -- the same verb used in verse 1 for the harlot "sitting" on many waters. She sits on the mountains and on the waters: on the geographical foundation of the city and on the peoples who sustain her. Her sitting is a posture of enthroned authority -- she is seated as on a throne, established and secure. The irony is that her throne is the beast itself, and the beast's trajectory is toward destruction.

**"And they are seven kings" (kai basileis hepta eisin).** The second identification: the seven heads are also seven kings (basileis). The heads carry a double meaning -- they are both geographical (mountains) and political (kings). The word basileus ("king") in the Roman context most naturally refers to the emperor, the supreme ruler. The fact that the heads are simultaneously mountains (the city's geography) and kings (the city's rulers) merges the identity of Rome-the-place with Rome-the-power. The city and its rulers are inseparable.

**"Five have fallen, one is, the other has not yet come; and when he comes, he must remain a little while" (hoi pente epesan, ho heis estin, ho allos oupō ēlthen, kai hotan elthē oligon auton dei meinai).** The seven kings are distributed across three temporal categories: five have fallen (past), one currently exists (present), and one has not yet come (future). The statement "five have fallen" (hoi pente epesan) uses the verb epiptō ("to fall"), which in this document describes the fall of cities and powers -- Babylon "fallen, fallen" (14:8; 18:2) uses the same verb (epesen). The five fallen kings are five past rulers whose reigns have ended.

The identification of these kings with specific Roman emperors has been debated extensively even among first-century listeners, and the text itself does not name them. The difficulty lies in determining where to begin the count (with Julius Caesar, with Augustus, or with another figure) and whether to include the three short-lived emperors of 68-69 CE (Galba, Otho, Vitellius). Several arrangements are possible:

If the count begins with Augustus (the first princeps, the formal founder of the imperial system), the sequence would be: (1) Augustus, (2) Tiberius, (3) Caligula, (4) Claudius, (5) Nero -- "five have fallen"; (6) the one who "is" would be the sixth in sequence. If the three short-lived emperors of 68-69 are skipped (as many ancient lists did, treating them as interruptions rather than legitimate reigns), the sixth would be Vespasian, and the seventh who "has not yet come" and "must remain a little while" would be Titus, whose reign lasted only about two years (79-81 CE) -- which fits "a little while" (oligon) precisely.

If the count begins with Julius Caesar (whom some ancient historians treated as the first ruler of the imperial system, as in Suetonius' Lives of the Twelve Caesars), the sequence shifts forward by one, and the "one who is" would be Nero or a later emperor, depending on inclusions.

The text does not resolve this question definitively, and the author may not intend a strict one-to-one correlation. The number seven, throughout this document, is the number of completeness. Seven kings may represent the complete sequence of imperial rulers -- the fullness of the beast's political expression -- rather than a precise historical inventory. The statement that "five have fallen, one is, the other has not yet come" may function to locate the audience in time (the document is written during the reign of the sixth) while also indicating that the entire imperial system is nearing its end (only one more to come, and his stay is brief).

**"The beast which was and is not, is himself also an eighth and is one of the seven, and he goes to destruction" (kai to thērion ho ēn kai ouk estin, kai autos ogdoos estin kai ek tōn hepta estin, kai eis apōleian hypagei).** The beast itself -- not merely one of its heads but the beast as a whole -- is an eighth (ogdoos) king, yet "one of the seven" (ek tōn hepta). This paradoxical statement indicates that the beast transcends the sequence of individual rulers while being embodied in one of them. The beast is more than any single king; it is the imperial system as such, the principle of blasphemous, coercive, blood-drunk power. And yet it is also "one of the seven" -- it manifests in a particular ruler, one who "was and is not" (a ruler who lived, died, and is expected to return).

The Nero redivivus expectation again provides the most natural first-century referent. Nero was one of the seven emperors (one of the "heads"). He died ("was and is not"). Popular belief held he would return ("is about to come up from the abyss"). The author seems to use this expectation to describe the beast's own nature: the beast embodies the worst of imperial power (Nero being the paradigmatic persecutor of the faithful, having executed believers in Rome after the fire of 64 CE, as attested by Tacitus, Annals 15.44), and its future manifestation will recapitulate Nero's character.

The phrase kai eis apōleian hypagei ("and he goes to destruction") repeats the verdict of verse 8. The beast's destination is not eternal reign but apōleia -- ruin, perdition. This is the third time destruction has been named as the beast's end-point (17:8a, 17:8b, 17:11). The repetition is emphatic: regardless of the beast's impressive reappearance, its trajectory is fixed and terminal.

## Verses 12-14: The Ten Horns Interpreted -- Kings Who Wage War on the Lamb

"The ten horns which you saw are ten kings who have not yet received a kingdom, but they receive authority as kings with the beast for one hour. These have one purpose, and they give their power and authority to the beast. These will wage war against the Lamb, and the Lamb will overcome them, because he is Lord of lords and King of kings, and those who are with him are the called and chosen and faithful."

**"The ten horns which you saw are ten kings who have not yet received a kingdom" (kai ta deka kerata ha eides deka basileis eisin, hoitines basileian oupō elabon).** The ten horns, like the seven heads, are identified as kings (basileis). But unlike the seven head-kings, these ten have "not yet received a kingdom" (basileian oupō elabon). They are kings-in-waiting, rulers who have not yet assumed their power. In Daniel 7:24, the ten horns of the fourth beast are likewise ten kings: "The ten horns are ten kings who will arise from this kingdom." In Daniel's vision, the horns grow from the fourth beast's head and represent rulers who emerge from within the imperial system. The author of Revelation follows Daniel closely: the ten horns are kings who derive their existence and authority from the beast system.

The fact that they have "not yet received a kingdom" places them in the future from the author's stated temporal perspective (the "one who is" of verse 10). These are not current rulers but anticipated ones -- figures who will arise within the beast system at a later stage.

**"But they receive authority as kings with the beast for one hour" (alla exousian hōs basileis mian hōran lambanousin meta tou thēriou).** Their authority (exousia) is exercised "as kings" (hōs basileis) -- they function with royal power but perhaps lack the formal, established status of the seven head-kings. And their reign is extraordinarily brief: "one hour" (mian hōran). The "hour" (hōra) in this document is the decisive but brief moment of crisis. In 3:10, the Philadelphian assembly was promised protection from "the hour of testing" (tēs hōras tou peirasmou). In 9:15, the four angels at the Euphrates were prepared for "the hour and day and month and year." In 14:7, "the hour of his judgment has come." In 18:10, 17, 19, the mourners over Babylon will lament that her judgment came "in one hour" (mia hōra). The ten kings' authority lasts for the same "one hour" as Babylon's destruction -- their brief reign coincides with the final crisis. Their power is real but fleeting, given and taken in the space of the climactic moment.

The phrase meta tou thēriou ("with the beast") indicates that their authority is exercised in partnership with, or as subordinates of, the beast. They do not reign independently but as the beast's allies.

**"These have one purpose, and they give their power and authority to the beast" (houtoi mian gnōmēn echousin, kai tēn dynamin kai exousian autōn tō thēriō didoasin).** The ten kings share "one purpose" (mian gnōmēn -- literally "one mind, one counsel, one resolve"). The word gnōmē ("opinion, purpose, resolve, judgment") indicates unified will. The ten kings are not merely aligned politically; they are of a single mind. They voluntarily transfer their dynamis ("power, might") and exousia ("authority") to the beast. The transfer inverts the proper order of authority described in this document. In 4:11, "worthy are you, our Lord and our God, to receive glory and honor and power (dynamin)" -- power properly flows upward to God. The ten kings direct their power downward to the beast. The beast system operates through willing subordination: the kings choose to serve the beast, surrendering their sovereignty to the beast's purposes.

**"These will wage war against the Lamb, and the Lamb will overcome them" (houtoi meta tou arniou polemēsousin, kai to arnion nikēsei autous).** The ten kings and the beast make war against the Lamb. The verb polemēsousin ("will make war") echoes the dragon's war against the woman's offspring (12:17), the beast's war against the saints (13:7), and the dragon's war in heaven (12:7). The entire document has been building toward this confrontation: the dragon empowered the beast, the beast made war on the saints, and now the beast and its royal allies directly engage the Lamb.

The outcome is stated with absolute brevity: kai to arnion nikēsei autous -- "and the Lamb will overcome them." The verb nikēsei (future of nikaō, "to conquer, to overcome") declares the Lamb's victory in a single word. No description of the battle is given here; the full narration will come in 19:11-21, where the rider on the white horse -- "The Word of God" -- defeats the beast and the kings of the earth with "the sword which came from his mouth" (19:15, 21). Here the victory is simply announced as a certainty.

**"Because he is Lord of lords and King of kings" (hoti kyrios kyriōn estin kai basileus basileōn).** The basis of the Lamb's victory is stated: he bears the supreme title. "Lord of lords and King of kings" (kyrios kyriōn kai basileus basileōn) is the title of ultimate sovereignty, asserting authority over all other lords and kings. The title appears in the LXX in Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the LORD your God is the God of gods and the Lord of lords (kyrios tōn kyriōn), the great God (ho theos ho megas), mighty and awesome." Daniel 2:47: "Your God is indeed God of gods and Lord of kings (kyrios tōn basileōn) and a revealer of mysteries." In both passages, the title expresses God's supremacy over all competing authorities.

In this document, the same title appears in 19:16 on the rider's robe and thigh: "KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS." The Lamb who appears slain (5:6), who was rejected and killed, who is the seeming opposite of a conquering king, bears the title that belongs to the supreme sovereign of all creation. The ten kings gave their power to the beast; the Lamb does not need donated power. He is the source of all authority.

**"And those who are with him are the called and chosen and faithful" (kai hoi met' autou klētoi kai eklektoi kai pistoi).** The Lamb's army is described with three adjectives, not with military terminology. His companions are not warriors in the conventional sense but klētoi ("called"), eklektoi ("chosen, elect"), and pistoi ("faithful, trustworthy"). Each term carries scriptural weight.

Klētoi ("called") designates those whom God has summoned. In Isaiah 41:9 (LXX), God says to Israel: "I called you (ekalesa se) from the ends of the earth, and from its remote parts I called you (ekalesa se), and said to you, 'You are my servant, I have chosen you and not rejected you.'" The called are those whom God has actively summoned into relationship and service.

Eklektoi ("chosen, elect") in the LXX refers to those whom God has selected for his purposes. In Isaiah 42:1 (LXX): "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; my chosen one (ho eklektos mou) in whom my soul delights." In Isaiah 43:20: "my chosen people" (ton laon mou ton eklekton). In this document's broader scriptural context, election is primarily corporate and vocational -- God chooses a people for a purpose (service, witness, being a light to the nations), not merely individuals for personal salvation.

Pistoi ("faithful") is the crowning term. The word describes those who have maintained loyalty -- who have endured the beast's pressure, refused the mark, kept the commandments of God and the testimony of Jesus (12:17; 14:12). Faithfulness is the quality that has been demanded throughout the letters to the seven churches: "Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life" (2:10). The Lamb's army consists of those who were called by God, chosen for his purposes, and proved faithful through the ordeal.

The order is significant: called first (God's initiative), chosen second (God's purpose), faithful third (the human response). The sequence moves from divine action to human fidelity. The faithful did not choose themselves; they were called and chosen. But their inclusion among the Lamb's companions is confirmed by their faithfulness -- they responded to the call and fulfilled the purpose of their election by enduring. The three terms together describe a community whose identity originates in God's action and is validated by their perseverance.

## Verses 15-18: The Waters, the Horns' Betrayal, and the Woman Identified

"And he said to me, 'The waters which you saw where the harlot sits, are peoples and multitudes and nations and tongues. And the ten horns which you saw, and the beast, these will hate the harlot and will make her desolate and naked, and will eat her flesh and will burn her up with fire. For God has put it in their hearts to execute his purpose by having a common purpose, and by giving their kingdom to the beast, until the words of God will be fulfilled. The woman whom you saw is the great city, which reigns over the kings of the earth.'"

**"The waters which you saw where the harlot sits, are peoples and multitudes and nations and tongues" (ta hydata ha eides, hou hē pornē kathētai, laoi kai ochloi eisin kai ethnē kai glōssai).** The angel now interprets the "many waters" of verse 1. The waters are not literal seas or rivers but peoples (laoi), multitudes (ochloi), nations (ethnē), and tongues (glōssai). This fourfold formula is the author's standard expression for universal humanity, used throughout the document (5:9; 7:9; 10:11; 11:9; 13:7; 14:6). The harlot "sits" on the peoples as she sits on the beast and on the mountains -- she is sustained by, enthroned upon, and exercises dominion over the populations of the earth. Her power rests on the masses who support, serve, and enrich her.

The image of waters as nations is drawn from Isaiah 17:12-13 (LXX): "Woe, the multitude of many nations (ethnōn pollōn), like the roaring of the sea (hōs thalassa), they will roar. And many nations (ethnē polla) will roar like the roaring of many waters (hydatōn pollōn)." Psalm 93:3-4 (LXX 92:3-4): "The floods have lifted up, O LORD, the floods have lifted up their voice... The LORD on high is mightier than the sound of many waters (hydatōn pollōn), mightier than the breakers of the sea." In both texts, the turbulent waters represent the surging nations that threaten but are ultimately subject to God's authority.

**"And the ten horns which you saw, and the beast, these will hate the harlot and will make her desolate and naked, and will eat her flesh and will burn her up with fire" (kai ta deka kerata ha eides kai to thērion, houtoi misēsousin tēn pornēn kai ērēmōmenēn poiēsousin autēn kai gymnēn, kai tas sarkas autēs phagontai kai autēn katakausousin en pyri).** This is the critical turning point in the chapter. The beast and its ten horns -- the very power structure that carried the harlot and sustained her position -- now turn against her. The alliance that supported Babylon is revealed to be unstable, and the destruction of the harlot comes not from an external attack but from within the beast system itself.

The verbs describe total destruction through four actions:

First, they "will hate" (misēsousin) her -- the emotional foundation of the betrayal is visceral hostility. The alliance that seemed solid was held together by self-interest, not by genuine loyalty. When the interests shift, hatred replaces partnership.

Second, they "will make her desolate and naked" (ērēmōmenēn poiēsousin autēn kai gymnēn). The word ērēmōmenēn ("made desolate, laid waste") is the same root as erēmos ("wilderness, desert") -- they will reduce her to the desolation of the wilderness. The word gymnēn ("naked") indicates the stripping away of her luxurious garments. The woman who was "clothed in purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls" (verse 4) is now stripped bare. The exposure of nakedness in the prophetic tradition is a standard image of the judgment on a faithless city.

Ezekiel 16:39 (against Jerusalem as harlot): "They will strip you of your clothing and take your beautiful jewels, and they will leave you naked and bare" (LXX: gymnēn kai aischynēn). Ezekiel 23:26-29 (against Oholibah/Jerusalem): "They will strip you of your garments and take away your fine jewelry... and leave you naked and bare (gymnēn kai aischynēn), and the nakedness (aschēmosynē) of your harlotries will be uncovered."

Hosea 2:3 (LXX 2:5): God threatens the unfaithful wife Israel: "I will strip her naked (ekdysō autēn gymnēn) and make her as in the day she was born, and make her like a wilderness (hōs erēmon)."

The stripping and desolation of the harlot recapitulates the prophetic judgment on unfaithful cities. The luxury that seemed permanent is torn away; the beauty that seduced the nations is exposed as a veneer.

Third, they "will eat her flesh" (tas sarkas autēs phagontai). The language of consuming flesh appears in Psalm 27:2 (LXX 26:2): "When evildoers came upon me to eat my flesh (tou phagein tas sarkas mou), my adversaries and my enemies, they stumbled and fell." Micah 3:3 accuses the leaders of Israel: "who eat the flesh (tas sarkas) of my people." But the most direct precedent is the fate of Jezebel in 2 Kings 9:36 (LXX 4 Kingdoms 9:36): "Dogs shall eat the flesh (tas sarkas) of Jezebel in the plot of Jezreel." Jezebel -- the foreign queen who promoted Baal worship in Israel, who killed the prophets of the Lord (1 Kings 18:4, 13), who had Naboth murdered for his vineyard (1 Kings 21) -- was consumed by dogs. The reference to Jezebel is not accidental in a document that has already named "Jezebel" as a figure in the church at Thyatira who teaches and misleads God's servants to practice immorality and eat things sacrificed to idols (2:20). The harlot Babylon shares Jezebel's fate: her flesh is consumed. The alliance that sustained her becomes the instrument that devours her.

Fourth, they "will burn her up with fire" (autēn katakausousin en pyri). Burning with fire is the prescribed punishment for certain severe transgressions in the Torah. Leviticus 21:9: "If the daughter of a priest profanes herself by playing the harlot (ekporneusē), she profanes her father; she shall be burned with fire (en pyri katakauthēsetai)." The specific punishment for a priest's daughter who commits harlotry is burning -- the same fate visited on the harlot Babylon. If the harlot has dressed in the colors of the tabernacle (verse 4, purple and scarlet) and has assumed a priestly identity, then her punishment as a prostituting priest's daughter is contextually appropriate. In 18:8, the harlot's destruction by fire is confirmed: "she will be burned up with fire (en pyri katakauthēsetai); for the Lord God who judges her is strong."

The burning of the harlot by her own allies is also prefigured in Ezekiel 23:25, 47, where God decrees that the unfaithful women will be subjected to fire: "They will burn their houses with fire" (23:47). The instrument of God's judgment is the very power that once sustained the corrupt city.

**"For God has put it in their hearts to execute his purpose by having a common purpose, and by giving their kingdom to the beast, until the words of God will be fulfilled" (ho gar theos edōken eis tas kardias autōn poiēsai tēn gnōmēn autou, kai poiēsai mian gnōmēn kai dounai tēn basileian autōn tō thēriō, achri telesthēsontai hoi logoi tou theou).** This verse pulls back the curtain on the theological mechanism behind the harlot's destruction. The ten horns' betrayal of the harlot is not random political chaos; it is God's own action. God "put it in their hearts" (edōken eis tas kardias autōn) -- the same divine passive (edōken, "gave") used throughout chapter 13 for every capacity the beast received. God is the hidden agent behind the beast's actions, using the beast system's own internal contradictions to accomplish his sovereign purpose.

The word gnōmēn ("purpose, resolve, plan") appears twice. The ten kings execute God's gnōmē ("his purpose") by acting with a common gnōmē ("one purpose") among themselves. The irony is extraordinary: the kings think they are pursuing their own agenda by turning on the harlot, but they are actually carrying out God's plan. Their self-interested betrayal serves the divine will. The very unity that the kings display in giving their kingdom to the beast (verse 13) is the unity that God uses to destroy the harlot. The beast system destroys itself, and God orchestrates the self-destruction.

This pattern has precedent in Israel's scriptures. In Judges 9:23, "God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem; and the men of Shechem dealt treacherously with Abimelech." The alliance between Abimelech and Shechem collapsed through divine agency, and both were destroyed. In 2 Chronicles 20:22-23, God set the Ammonites and Moabites against each other, and they destroyed one another without Israel having to fight. The principle is consistent: God uses the internal fractures of hostile powers to accomplish their defeat.

The phrase achri telesthēsontai hoi logoi tou theou ("until the words of God will be fulfilled") introduces a temporal limit. The word telesthēsontai (from teleō, "to bring to completion, to fulfill") is the same verb used in 15:1 for the completion of God's wrath (etelesthē) and in 10:7 for the "mystery of God" being "finished" (etelesthē) at the seventh trumpet. The kings will continue in their role "until" God's words are accomplished. After that, their function ceases. The beast system is a temporary instrument -- it serves God's purpose for a bounded period and then is discarded. The words of God (hoi logoi tou theou) that must be fulfilled include the entire sequence of prophetic announcements in the document: the fall of Babylon (14:8), the completion of the bowls (15:1), and the ultimate vindication of the saints.

**"The woman whom you saw is the great city, which reigns over the kings of the earth" (kai hē gynē hēn eides estin hē polis hē megalē hē echousa basileian epi tōn basileōn tēs gēs).** The angel's final identification is the most direct statement in the chapter. The woman is not an individual person but a polis -- a city. She is "the great city" (hē polis hē megalē), the same designation used in 11:8 ("the great city which mystically is called Sodom and Egypt, where also their Lord was crucified") and in 16:19 ("the great city was split into three parts"). She "reigns over" (echousa basileian epi, literally "having kingship/kingdom over") the kings of the earth.

For a first-century hearer in the Roman province of Asia, the identification is unmistakable. What city, in the late first century, sat on seven hills (verse 9), reigned over the kings of the earth (verse 18), grew wealthy through international commerce (the cargo list of 18:12-13), was drunk with the blood of the saints (verse 6, recalling Nero's persecution and the ongoing pressures of the imperial cult), and was sustained by a vast network of subject peoples and nations (verse 15)? The answer is Rome. The identification with Rome does not require any interpretive gymnastics; it is the straightforward reading that any first-century audience would make.

However, the author has not merely identified a particular first-century city. He has used the name "Babylon" -- the code-name that draws on the entire prophetic tradition of imperial oppression. The typological identification works on multiple levels: as Babylon was to sixth-century Judah (the destroyer of the temple, the enslaver of God's people, the arrogant blasphemer of God's name), so Rome is to the first-century assemblies. And as Babylon fell (Isaiah 13-14, 21; Jeremiah 50-51), so Rome will fall. The prophetic pattern is not exhausted by any single historical referent; the harlot-city is every city that sets itself up as the center of the world, that sustains itself on the blood of the faithful, and that seduces the nations into idolatrous allegiance. Rome is the present instance; the principle is comprehensive.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Revelation 17 answers the question that has been hanging since 14:8: who is Babylon the great, and how does she fall? The chapter is an interpretive vision, guided by one of the bowl-angels, in which the harlot-city is unveiled, described, and explained.

The chapter makes five major arguments:

First, the harlot and the beast are distinct but interdependent. The harlot rides the beast -- she depends on the beast's political-military power for her position. The beast carries the harlot -- it provides the infrastructure of coercion (military force, legal authority, economic control) on which the harlot's seductive system operates. The relationship is symbiotic: the beast gives power; the harlot gives legitimacy, attractiveness, and the intoxicating wine that keeps the earth-dwellers compliant. Yet the relationship is not permanent. The beast system will eventually turn on the harlot and destroy her (verse 16). The alliance is held together by self-interest, not by genuine loyalty, and when God redirects the kings' purposes (verse 17), the alliance collapses. The harlot is destroyed not by the Lamb's direct intervention but by the self-consuming violence of the beast system itself.

Second, the beast is a parody of God. The threefold formula "was, and is not, and is about to come" (verse 8) counterfeits the divine self-identification "who is and who was and who is to come" (1:4, 8; 4:8). Where God eternally exists, the beast has gaps in its existence. Where God's "coming" brings salvation, the beast's coming up is from the abyss and terminates in destruction. The beast impresses the earth-dwellers with its apparent transcendence of death (the healed mortal wound of 13:3; the "was and is not and will come" of 17:8), but this is a counterfeit resurrection. The beast's trajectory is fixed: from the abyss to destruction. Its entire career is bounded by these two poles.

Third, the harlot is identified as a specific historical entity with universal significance. The seven mountains (verse 9) point to Rome. The reign over the kings of the earth (verse 18) confirms the identification. The blood of the saints and witnesses (verse 6) corresponds to the historical reality of Roman persecution. The luxury goods and commercial dominance will be elaborated in chapter 18. Yet the name "Babylon" signals that the identification is typological, not merely historical. The harlot is Rome as Rome occupies the role that Babylon occupied before it. The prophetic principle transcends any single city: every imperial system that sustains itself on violence, seduces the nations with luxury, and demands allegiance that belongs to God alone stands under this judgment.

Fourth, God is sovereign over the beast system's internal politics. The ten kings' betrayal of the harlot is not random chaos but God's own action (verse 17). God put it in their hearts to execute his purpose. The beast system destroys itself, and the self-destruction is divinely orchestrated. The same kings who voluntarily gave their power to the beast (verse 13) are the ones who turn on the harlot and devour her (verse 16). The irony is that the beast's allies become the instruments of God's judgment on the beast's consort. The alliance that seemed monolithic and invincible in chapter 13 is revealed to be fragile and self-contradictory.

Fifth, the Lamb's victory is certain and his companions are identified. The war of the ten kings and the beast against the Lamb is stated in verse 14, and its outcome is declared in a single clause: "the Lamb will overcome them." The basis of this victory is the Lamb's supreme title: "Lord of lords and King of kings." Those who accompany the Lamb are not described in military terms but in covenantal terms: "called, chosen, and faithful." Their identity originates in God's initiative (called, chosen) and is validated by their perseverance (faithful). The faithful community that has endured the beast's persecution is revealed as the Lamb's army -- not despite their suffering but through it.

The chapter's pastoral function for its first-century audience is to strip away the harlot's allure. The empire that seems invincible, wealthy, and permanent is revealed as a blood-drunk prostitute riding a doomed beast. The luxury is built on murder. The splendor conceals abomination. The alliance that sustains the system is unstable and will collapse. The angel's interpretation teaches the assemblies to see through the surface -- to recognize that the gold cup holds blood, that the purple robes conceal violence, and that the beast's impressive "was, is not, and will come" is a pathetic imitation of the God who truly is, who truly was, and who is truly coming. The assemblies are called not to wonder at the harlot (as John initially did, verse 6) but to discern her identity, understand her doom, and remain among the Lamb's companions -- the called, the chosen, and the faithful.
