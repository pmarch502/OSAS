# Revelation 10 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 9):** The fifth and sixth trumpets delivered the first two of the three woes announced by the eagle in 8:13. The fifth trumpet (9:1-12) released the locusts of the abyss -- supernatural agents of torment, forbidden from killing but authorized to afflict "the men who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads" (9:4) for five months. The sixth trumpet (9:13-19) released four angels bound at the Euphrates along with a cavalry of two hundred million whose horses breathed fire, smoke, and brimstone, killing a third of humanity. The chapter's theological climax came in its final two verses (9:20-21): "The rest of mankind, who were not killed by these plagues, did not repent of the works of their hands, so as not to worship demons, and the idols of gold and of silver and of brass and of stone and of wood, which can neither see nor hear nor walk; and they did not repent of their murders nor of their sorceries nor of their immorality nor of their thefts." Despite the escalating severity of six trumpet judgments -- from partial destruction of the natural world (trumpets 1-4, chapter 8) to agonizing torment (trumpet 5) to the killing of a third of humanity (trumpet 6) -- the survivors refused to repent. The refusal replicated the pattern of Pharaoh during the Egyptian plagues: escalating judgment met by unyielding obstinacy. The trumpet sequence, which began as the divine response to the prayers of the saints offered on the golden altar (8:3-5), had reached its penultimate stage and produced no change in the earth-dwellers.

**What the author argues immediately after (Revelation 11):** The interlude that begins at 10:1 continues through 11:13. In 11:1-2, John is given a measuring rod and told to measure the temple of God, the altar, and those who worship in it -- but to exclude the outer courtyard, which "has been given to the nations" who "will trample the holy city for forty-two months." In 11:3-13, two witnesses prophesy for 1,260 days, are killed by "the beast that comes up out of the abyss" (11:7 -- the same abyss opened in 9:1-2), lie dead in the street for three and a half days, are raised to life by "the breath of life from God" (11:11), and ascend to heaven. An earthquake destroys a tenth of the city and kills seven thousand people, and then -- in a crucial turn -- "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). This response contrasts sharply with 9:20-21: where the survivors of the sixth trumpet refused to repent, the survivors of the earthquake give glory to God. The interlude thus shows a different outcome: faithful prophetic witness, even unto death, producing in some the acknowledgment of God that the trumpet judgments alone did not produce.

After the interlude, the announcement comes: "The second woe is past; behold, the third woe is coming quickly" (11:14). Then the seventh angel sounds (11:15), and loud voices in heaven declare: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ; and He will reign forever and ever." The twenty-four elders worship, declaring that God has "taken Your great power and have begun to reign" (11:17). This is the culmination that 10:7 announced: "the mystery of God is finished."

**How chapter 10 connects the surrounding argument:** Between the sixth and seventh seals, the author inserted an interlude (chapter 7) that addressed the question raised by the sixth seal's cosmic upheaval -- "Who is able to stand?" (6:17) -- by showing the sealed servants of God and the innumerable multitude who had come through the great tribulation. Between the sixth and seventh trumpets, the author inserts a parallel interlude (10:1-11:13) that addresses the crisis raised by the sixth trumpet's aftermath -- the earth-dwellers' refusal to repent (9:20-21). The crisis is not merely military or cosmic; it is spiritual. If escalating judgment does not produce repentance, what will? The interlude answers: the prophetic word, internalized and proclaimed even at the cost of the prophets' lives.

Chapter 10 thus occupies a pivotal structural position. It stands between the failed response of 9:20-21 and the successful response of 11:13, and it addresses the mechanism by which the transition occurs. The prophet must eat the scroll -- internalize the divine word -- and prophesy again to all peoples, nations, languages, and kings. The prophetic witness of the two witnesses in 11:3-13 enacts what 10:11 commissions. The interlude is not a digression from the trumpet sequence but its interpretive key: the judgments are not self-sufficient; they require the prophetic word to accomplish God's purpose.

The chapter also answers the question that has been building since 6:10, when the souls under the altar cried, "How long, O Lord, holy and true, will You refrain from judging and avenging our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" They were told to wait "a little while longer" (6:11). Now, six trumpets later, the angel swears: "there will be no more delay" (10:6). The waiting is over. The mystery of God will be completed when the seventh angel sounds.

## Verses 1-2: The Strong Angel Descends with the Open Scroll

"I saw another strong angel coming down from heaven, clothed with a cloud; and the rainbow was on his head, and his face was like the sun, and his feet like pillars of fire; and he had in his hand a little scroll, which was open. He placed his right foot on the sea and his left on the land."

**"Another strong angel" (allon angelon ischyron).** The phrase echoes 5:2, where John saw "a strong angel proclaiming with a loud voice, 'Who is worthy to open the scroll and to break its seals?'" The word allon ("another") connects this figure to the strong angel of chapter 5 while distinguishing him from that figure. The strong angel of 5:2 posed the question that drove the heavenly drama: who can open the sealed scroll? Now, another strong angel appears holding a scroll that is already open. The question has been answered. The seals have been broken. The content is accessible. The cycle that began with the sealed scroll on the throne (5:1) has advanced to the point where a scroll stands open in an angel's hand.

**"Coming down from heaven" (katabainonta ek tou ouranou).** The angel descends -- his movement is from the heavenly realm to the earthly. The same verb (katabaino, "to come down, to descend") describes the descent of the new Jerusalem (3:12; 21:2, 10) and of fire from heaven (13:13; 20:9). The angel's descent signals that the heavenly initiative is now reaching into the earthly sphere. Until this point in the trumpet sequence, the judgments have been dispatched from heaven; now a heavenly figure physically enters the earthly scene where John stands.

**"Clothed with a cloud" (peribeblemenon nephelen).** The cloud is not incidental decoration. In Israel's scriptures and in this document, the cloud is the signature of divine presence. The pillar of cloud led Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21-22). The cloud covered Mount Sinai when God descended to give the Torah (Exodus 19:9, 16; 24:15-18). The cloud filled the tabernacle so that Moses could not enter (Exodus 40:34-35). The cloud filled Solomon's temple at its dedication so that the priests could not stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10-11). In Ezekiel 1:4, the prophet's throne-chariot vision begins with "a great cloud" (nephele megale, LXX). In Daniel 7:13, "one like a son of man was coming with the clouds of heaven" (meta ton nephelon tou ouranou, LXX). In this document, the risen Christ is introduced in 1:7 with the declaration: "Behold, He is coming with the clouds" (meta ton nephelon). The cloud is not merely weather but theophany -- the visible sign that God is present and acting.

The verb periballo ("to clothe, to wrap around") is used throughout this document for what figures are wearing: the twenty-four elders are "clothed in white garments" (4:4), the great multitude is "clothed in white robes" (7:9, 13), the woman of chapter 12 is "clothed with the sun" (12:1). This angel is clothed with a cloud in the same way others are clothed with garments. The cloud is not above him or around him but on him -- he wears the divine presence the way others wear robes.

**"The rainbow was on his head" (he iris epi ten kephalen autou).** The rainbow (iris) appeared previously in this document at 4:3, where "there was a rainbow around the throne, like an emerald in appearance." The rainbow in 4:3 encircled the throne of God himself. Its scriptural root is Genesis 9:12-17, where God established the rainbow as the sign of his covenant with Noah and all flesh: "I have set My rainbow in the cloud, and it shall serve as a sign of a covenant between Me and the earth... and never again shall the water become a flood to destroy all flesh" (Genesis 9:13, 15). The rainbow is the visible marker of God's commitment not to destroy the earth by water -- a promise made in the context of judgment (the flood had just occurred) and mercy (the promise restrains future total destruction).

In Ezekiel 1:28, the prophet saw the glory of the LORD on the throne-chariot and described it: "Like the appearance of the rainbow in the clouds on a rainy day, so was the appearance of the surrounding radiance. Such was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD." For Ezekiel, the rainbow-radiance was the visible form of God's glory. The angel in Revelation 10 bears on his head the same sign that encircled God's throne in chapter 4 and that constituted the appearance of God's glory in Ezekiel 1. He carries the covenant sign -- the reminder that God's judgments, however severe, operate within the bounds of his promises.

**"His face was like the sun" (to prosopon autou hos ho helios).** This is the same language used to describe the risen Christ in 1:16: "His face was like the sun shining in its strength." The parallel is precise: the same simile, the same noun, the same comparison. In the Hebrew scriptures, the association between a face and the sun appears in Judges 5:31: "Let all Your enemies perish, O LORD; but let those who love Him be like the rising of the sun in its might." Moses's face shone when he came down from Sinai after speaking with God (Exodus 34:29-30, 35) -- the reflected glory of the divine presence made his face radiant. In Daniel 10:6, the man in linen had a "face with the appearance of lightning" -- a different but related image of overwhelming luminosity.

**"His feet like pillars of fire" (hoi podes autou hos styloi pyros).** The image combines two elements from Israel's foundational narrative. First, the pillar of fire (stylos pyros) that led Israel through the wilderness by night: "The LORD was going before them in a pillar of cloud by day to lead them on the way, and in a pillar of fire by night to give them light" (Exodus 13:21). The pillar of fire was the visible manifestation of God's guiding, protecting presence during the exodus journey. Second, the feet of the risen Christ in 1:15: "His feet were like burnished bronze when it has been heated to a glow in a furnace." In Daniel 10:6, the man in linen had "arms and feet like the gleam of polished bronze." The angel's feet are not merely fiery but pillar-like -- they suggest the solidity and permanence of the architectural pillars in the temple (cf. the promise to the overcomer in 3:12: "I will make him a pillar in the temple of My God") combined with the fire of the exodus theophany.

**The accumulation of divine attributes.** The angel bears the cloud of God's presence, the rainbow of God's covenant, the sun-face of the risen Christ, and the fire-pillar feet of the exodus theophany. Each attribute, taken individually, belongs to either God or Christ elsewhere in this document and in the Hebrew scriptures. The question naturally arises: is this angel the risen Christ himself?

The text does not support that identification. The word angelos ("angel, messenger") is used without qualification, and the qualifier allon ("another") places this figure in a series with the strong angel of 5:2, who was plainly an angelic being distinct from both God and the Lamb. In the Hebrew scriptures, angelic figures sometimes bear the attributes of divine presence without being identified as God himself. The angel of the LORD who appeared to Moses in the burning bush was surrounded by fire and spoke God's words, yet was distinguished from the LORD who spoke from within the bush (Exodus 3:2-4). In Daniel 10:5-6, the man in linen bore attributes -- face like lightning, eyes like flaming torches, arms and feet like polished bronze -- that closely parallel the description of the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9 and the divine figure in Ezekiel 1:26-28. The pattern is consistent: angelic messengers who carry out the divine commission bear the marks of the one who sends them. This angel comes clothed in the insignia of divine authority because he speaks and acts on behalf of the God who sits on the throne and the Lamb who opened the seals. His attributes indicate the weight of his commission, not his personal identity.

**"He had in his hand a little scroll, which was open" (echon en te cheiri autou biblaridion eneogmenon).** The word biblaridion is a diminutive form, meaning "little scroll" or "small book." The full-sized scroll (biblion) introduced in 5:1 was "written inside and on the back, sealed up with seven seals." That scroll could not be opened until the Lamb was found worthy (5:5-7). Its opening through the seven seals constituted the judgments of chapters 6-8. Now a different scroll appears -- smaller, and critically, already open (eneogmenon, perfect passive participle of anoigo: "having been opened" -- the opening was completed in the past, and the scroll remains in its opened state).

The relationship between the two scrolls has been debated, but the text provides internal guidance. The great scroll of chapter 5 was sealed; this little scroll is open. The great scroll's contents were progressively revealed through the seals and trumpets; the little scroll is ready to be consumed and proclaimed. The diminutive form (biblaridion rather than biblion) need not mean the content is less important -- diminutives in Greek frequently express endearment or specificity rather than insignificance. What the text makes clear is that this scroll is accessible. Its content is not hidden behind seals that must be broken but is available to be taken, eaten, and proclaimed. In the logic of the document's narrative, the progressive unsealing of the great scroll through seals and trumpets has reached the point where the content is now ready for direct prophetic engagement. The little scroll is the content that the prophet must internalize before he can prophesy again.

**"He placed his right foot on the sea and his left on the land" (etheken ton poda autou ton dexion epi tes thalasses ton de euonymon epi tes ges).** The posture is one of comprehensive dominion. Sea and land together constitute the entire created surface of the earth (Genesis 1:9-10). To plant a foot on something is to claim authority over it -- as God told Joshua: "Every place on which the sole of your foot treads, I have given it to you" (Joshua 1:3). The angel's stance claims the full extent of the earthly domain for the message he is about to deliver.

The positioning also anticipates a development later in the document. In 13:1, a beast rises "out of the sea" (ek tes thalasses), and in 13:11, another beast comes up "out of the earth" (ek tes ges). The two domains from which the beasts will emerge are the same two domains over which the angel now plants his feet. Before the beasts arise from sea and land, the angel has already claimed both domains. The divine authority over sea and land is asserted before any counterfeit authority attempts to occupy those same territories.

## Verses 3-4: The Lion-Roar, the Seven Thunders, and the Sealed Words

"And he cried out with a loud voice, as when a lion roars; and when he had cried out, the seven peals of thunder uttered their voices. When the seven peals of thunder had spoken, I was about to write; and I heard a voice from heaven, saying, 'Seal up the things which the seven peals of thunder have spoken, and do not write them.'"

**"He cried out with a loud voice, as when a lion roars" (ekraxen phone megale hosper leon mykatai).** The verb krazo ("to cry out, to shout") describes an authoritative, powerful utterance -- not ordinary speech but proclamation. The comparison to a lion's roar has specific prophetic resonance. In Amos 3:7-8, the connection between the lion's roar and prophetic compulsion is explicit: "Surely the Lord GOD does nothing unless He reveals His secret plan to His servants the prophets. A lion has roared! Who will not fear? The Lord GOD has spoken! Who can do anything but prophesy?" In Amos, the lion's roar is God's voice, and the roar compels prophetic response. The linkage is: God roars (acts, speaks), the prophet must prophesy. The angel's lion-roar in Revelation 10:3 initiates the same sequence that will culminate in the command: "You must prophesy again" (10:11).

The lion association also connects backward within this document. In 5:5, one of the elders identified the Lamb as "the Lion that is from the tribe of Judah, the Root of David," who has overcome and is worthy to open the scroll. The lion roar of the angel in 10:3 carries the resonance of that identification: the authority behind the angel's cry is the authority of the Lion-Lamb who opened the seals.

In Hosea 11:10, God himself is the roaring lion: "They will walk after the LORD; He will roar like a lion; indeed He will roar, and His sons will come trembling from the west." In Joel 3:16 (LXX 4:16): "The LORD roars from Zion and utters His voice from Jerusalem, and the heavens and the earth tremble." The lion-roar in prophetic tradition is the voice of God speaking in power, demanding attention and obedience.

**"The seven peals of thunder uttered their voices" (elalesan hai hepta brontai tas heauton phonas).** The "seven thunders" (hai hepta brontai) appear with the definite article -- "the seven thunders," as if they are a known entity, not introduced for the first time. The definite article suggests that the original audience would have recognized the reference. The most likely source is Psalm 29, which contains exactly seven occurrences of "the voice of the LORD" (qol YHWH, LXX phone kyriou), each associated with thunder and its effects:

- "The voice of the LORD is on the waters; the God of glory thunders" (29:3)
- "The voice of the LORD is powerful" (29:4a)
- "The voice of the LORD is majestic" (29:4b)
- "The voice of the LORD breaks the cedars" (29:5)
- "The voice of the LORD divides flames of fire" (29:7)
- "The voice of the LORD shakes the wilderness" (29:8)
- "The voice of the LORD makes the deer give birth" (29:9)

In Psalm 29, the sevenfold "voice of the LORD" is the complete expression of divine power over the created order -- over waters, cedars, fire, wilderness, and all living things. The psalm concludes: "The LORD sat as King at the flood; yes, the LORD sits as King forever" (29:10). The seven thunders in Revelation 10 appear to be the same phenomenon: the full, sevenfold divine voice -- God's complete word spoken with the authority of the King who reigns over all creation.

Thunder in Israel's scriptures is consistently the voice of God in action. At Sinai, "God answered him with thunder" (Exodus 19:19). In 1 Samuel 7:10, "the LORD thundered with a great thunder on that day against the Philistines." In Job 37:2-5: "Listen closely to the thunder of His voice... God thunders with His voice wondrously." In this document, thunder emanates from the throne (4:5; 8:5; 11:19; 16:18) -- it is part of the Sinai-theophany formula that marks climactic moments of divine disclosure.

The seven thunders speak with their own voices (tas heauton phonas -- "their own voices," the reflexive pronoun emphasizing that the thunders are not echoing someone else's speech but articulating their own content). The thunders deliver intelligible speech -- they "spoke" (elalesan, from laleo, "to speak, to utter words"), and John understood enough to begin writing.

**"I was about to write" (emellon graphein).** John's instinct to write what the thunders said follows from his commission in 1:11: "Write what you see in a scroll and send it to the seven churches." He has been writing down what he sees and hears throughout the vision. The fact that he was about to write confirms that the thunders' speech was comprehensible -- it contained specific content that could be recorded.

**"Seal up the things which the seven peals of thunder have spoken, and do not write them" (sphragison ha elalesan hai hepta brontai, kai me auta grapses).** The command to seal (sphragizo) what the thunders said is a deliberate withholding of revelation. John heard the content, understood it well enough to write it, and was prohibited from recording it. The voice from heaven -- not the angel, but a separate heavenly voice -- intervenes to restrict the scope of John's written prophecy.

The primary scriptural parallel is Daniel 12:4, 9, where Daniel was commanded: "Keep these words secret and seal up the book until the end of time" (12:4) and "Go your way, Daniel, for these words will be kept secret and sealed up until the end time" (12:9). In Daniel, the sealing was temporal -- the content was sealed because the end had not yet arrived. The revelation would become accessible when the time came.

The contrast between Daniel's sealing and Revelation's treatment of the rest of its content is significant. In 22:10, John is told: "Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near." The book as a whole is explicitly not sealed -- the time has arrived, and the revelation is to be proclaimed openly. But the thunders' words are sealed within the unsealed book. The author creates a category of divine communication that exists -- it was spoken, it was heard -- but is not disclosed. Not all of God's counsel is made available to the audience. The revelation is genuine and extensive, but it has boundaries. The prophet is authorized to deliver what he is given, not everything that exists to be known.

The sealing of the thunders' words also functions within the document's literary architecture. Throughout the seal and trumpet sequences, the audience has been given detailed descriptions of each judgment. The seven thunders interrupt this pattern: here is a divine communication that the audience is told occurred but whose content is withheld. The effect is to remind the audience that the vision they are receiving, however comprehensive it may seem, does not exhaust the totality of God's purposes. There is more than what is written.

## Verses 5-7: The Angel's Oath -- No More Delay, the Mystery Finished

"Then the angel whom I saw standing on the sea and on the land raised his right hand to heaven, and swore by Him who lives forever and ever, who created heaven and the things in it, and the earth and the things in it, and the sea and the things in it, that there will no longer be a delay, but in the days of the voice of the seventh angel, when he is about to sound, then the mystery of God is finished, as He announced to His servants the prophets."

**"Raised his right hand to heaven" (eren ten cheira autou ten dexian eis ton ouranon).** The gesture is the standard oath-taking posture in the ancient world. It has a precise scriptural parallel in Daniel 12:7: the man dressed in linen, "who was above the waters of the stream," raised "his right hand and his left toward heaven, and swore by Him who lives forever." The verbal and gestural parallels between Revelation 10:5-6 and Daniel 12:7 are too exact to be coincidental. The author is deliberately reenacting the Danielic scene.

In Daniel 12:7, the man in linen raised both hands; here the angel raises only his right hand. The variation may be significant: the right hand is the hand of authority and power throughout the scriptures (Exodus 15:6: "Your right hand, O LORD, is majestic in power"; Psalm 110:1: "Sit at My right hand"). The angel raises the hand of authority to heaven -- toward the one by whom he swears.

In Deuteronomy 32:40, God himself makes the oath-gesture: "Indeed, I raise My hand to heaven, and say, as I live forever." The same gesture, the same oath formula. In Genesis 14:22, Abraham told the king of Sodom: "I have raised my hand to the LORD God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth." The raised hand toward heaven is the solemn, binding gesture by which the most consequential commitments are made.

**"Swore by Him who lives forever and ever, who created heaven and the things in it, and the earth and the things in it, and the sea and the things in it" (omosen en to zonti eis tous aionas ton aionon, hos ektisen ton ouranon kai ta en auto kai ten gen kai ta en aute kai ten thalassan kai ta en aute).** The oath formula has two components: the identity of the one by whom the angel swears, and the content of the oath.

The one by whom the oath is sworn is identified by two attributes. First, he is the one "who lives forever and ever" (ho zon eis tous aionas ton aionon). This title appeared in Daniel 12:7, where the man in linen "swore by Him who lives forever" (LXX: omosen en to zonti eis ton aiona). It identifies God as the eternal one whose life has no end and whose word therefore cannot be revoked. It also appears in this document at 4:9-10, where the living creatures give "glory and honor and thanks to Him who sits on the throne, to Him who lives forever and ever," and the elders fall down and worship. The title places the oath on the most unshakeable foundation: the life of the God who cannot die and whose purposes cannot be frustrated.

Second, he is the Creator "who created heaven and the things in it, and the earth and the things in it, and the sea and the things in it." This threefold Creator formula echoes Exodus 20:11, the rationale for the Sabbath commandment: "For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth, the sea, and all that is in them." It also echoes Psalm 146:6: "Who made heaven and earth, the sea and everything in them." And Nehemiah 9:6: "You alone are the LORD. You made the heavens, the heaven of heavens with all their hosts, the earth and all that is on it, the seas and all that is in them." The threefold formula (heaven, earth, sea) comprehensively covers all of creation. The angel swears by the God who made everything on which the angel is standing. The three domains -- heaven, earth, sea -- correspond to the three zones the angel occupies: he has descended from heaven, stands on the earth and the sea, and swears toward heaven. The Creator's authority over every domain is the guarantee of the oath.

**"That there will no longer be a delay" (hoti chronos ouketi estai).** The word chronos means "time, delay, period of time." The phrase can be translated either "there will be no more time" or "there will be no more delay." The context favors "no more delay," because the next clause specifies when the culmination will occur: "in the days of the voice of the seventh angel." Time itself is not ending; rather, the waiting period is ending. The interval between the promise and its fulfillment is being declared closed.

This announcement directly answers the martyrs' cry in 6:10: "How long, O Lord, holy and true, will You refrain from judging and avenging our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" In 6:11, they were told to wait "a little while longer" (eti chronon mikron -- note the same word chronon). The delay (chronos) that was imposed in 6:11 is now removed in 10:6 (chronos ouketi estai). The waiting that the martyrs endured is over. The vindication they cried for is imminent. The arc from 6:10-11 to 10:6 is one of the document's most carefully constructed trajectories: the question "How long?" receives the answer "No longer."

**"But in the days of the voice of the seventh angel, when he is about to sound" (all' en tais hemerais tes phones tou hebdomou angelou, hotan melle salpizein).** The culmination is assigned a specific temporal marker: the sounding of the seventh trumpet. The seventh trumpet will sound in 11:15, and when it does, the voices in heaven will declare: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ; and He will reign forever and ever." The angel's oath thus points forward to 11:15 as the moment of fulfillment. The phrase "the days of the voice" (en tais hemerais tes phones) -- not merely "the moment" but "the days" -- suggests that the seventh trumpet's sounding inaugurates a period during which its effects unfold, not an instantaneous event.

**"Then the mystery of God is finished" (etelesthen to mysterion tou theou).** The word mysterion ("mystery") in Jewish apocalyptic literature refers to God's hidden plan -- the divine purpose that is concealed from human understanding but revealed to chosen recipients. In Daniel 2:28-29, 47, the Aramaic equivalent raz describes Nebuchadnezzar's dream: "There is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries" (Daniel 2:28). The "mystery of God" in Revelation 10:7 is God's total redemptive and judicial purpose -- the full plan by which he brings creation to its intended end. This mystery has been partially disclosed throughout Israel's prophetic tradition, progressively unveiled through the seals and trumpets, and will be "finished" (etelesthen, from teleo, "to complete, to bring to an end, to accomplish") when the seventh trumpet sounds.

The verb teleo carries the sense of completion and accomplishment. In John 19:30, Jesus on the cross said tetelestai ("it is finished") -- the same root, signifying the completion of the work assigned. The mystery of God will be fully accomplished, not merely stopped or interrupted but brought to its designed completion.

**"As He announced to His servants the prophets" (hos euengelisen tous autou doulous tous prophetas).** The verb euengelizo means "to announce good news, to proclaim the gospel." Its use here is striking: the completion of the mystery -- which includes judgment and wrath -- is called "good news." The judgments that have unfolded through seals and trumpets are not mere destruction but the working out of a purpose that was announced as good news to the prophets. The establishment of God's kingdom, the vindication of the righteous, the overthrow of evil -- these constitute good news even though they involve devastating judgment on those who refuse to repent.

The phrase "His servants the prophets" (tous autou doulous tous prophetas) echoes Amos 3:7 directly: "Surely the Lord GOD does nothing unless He reveals His secret plan to His servants the prophets." The connection is precise: Amos says God reveals his plan (sod, "secret counsel") to the prophets; Revelation says God announced (euengelisen) the mystery to the prophets. The same relationship, the same vocabulary. God does not act without prior announcement through the prophetic tradition. The judgments of Revelation are not innovations but the fulfillment of what the prophets declared.

The identity of "the prophets" here is broad. It includes the Hebrew prophets who foretold the Day of the LORD (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Joel, Amos, Zechariah, Malachi), Daniel who received visions of the end, and the prophetic tradition as a whole that anticipated God's ultimate assertion of sovereignty over the nations. The mystery now being completed is the same reality they glimpsed and proclaimed.

## Verses 8-10: John Eats the Scroll -- Sweet and Bitter

"Then the voice which I heard from heaven, I heard again speaking with me, and saying, 'Go, take the scroll which is open in the hand of the angel who stands on the sea and on the land.' And I went to the angel, telling him to give me the little scroll. And he said to me, 'Take it and eat it; it will make your stomach bitter, but in your mouth it will be sweet as honey.' I took the little scroll from the angel's hand and ate it, and in my mouth it was sweet as honey; and when I had eaten it, my stomach was made bitter."

**"The voice which I heard from heaven, I heard again speaking with me" (he phone hen ekousa ek tou ouranou, palin lalousan met' emou).** The voice that speaks is the same heavenly voice that commanded John to seal the thunders' words (v. 4). This voice operates independently of the angel -- it is a distinct authority, speaking from heaven, directing John's actions. The voice acts as a mediator between the heavenly realm and the prophet, issuing the commands that govern what John may and must do. It told him what not to write (v. 4); now it tells him what to do (take the scroll).

**"Go, take the scroll which is open in the hand of the angel" (hypage labe to biblion to eneogmenon en te cheiri tou angelou).** The command uses hypage ("go") -- John must physically approach the angel. In the vision, John moves. He is not a passive recipient but an active participant who must go, take, eat, and then prophesy. The description of the scroll as "open" (eneogmenon) is repeated from verse 2, emphasizing its accessibility. The content is available. The scroll is no longer sealed, no longer beyond reach.

**"Take it and eat it" (labe kai kataphage auto).** The command to eat the scroll draws directly on Ezekiel 2:8-3:3, the closest Old Testament parallel. In Ezekiel's commissioning, God said: "Son of man, eat what you find; eat this scroll, and go, speak to the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 3:1). Ezekiel obeyed: "So I opened my mouth, and He fed me this scroll. And He said to me, 'Son of man, feed your stomach and fill your body with this scroll which I am giving you.' Then I ate it, and it was sweet as honey in my mouth" (Ezekiel 3:2-3). The scroll Ezekiel ate was "written on the front and back, and written on it were songs of mourning, sighing, and woe" (Ezekiel 2:10).

The eating of a scroll is a prophetic act of internalization. The prophet does not merely hear the message or read it externally; he consumes it, takes it into his body, makes it part of himself. The prophetic word is not information to be relayed but substance to be embodied. What the prophet speaks will come from within him, not from a text held at arm's length. Jeremiah described the same reality in different terms: "Your words were found and I ate them, and Your words became a joy to me and the delight of my heart" (Jeremiah 15:16). Psalm 119:103: "How sweet are Your words to my taste! Yes, sweeter than honey to my mouth!" The metaphor of eating/tasting God's word is deeply embedded in the prophetic and wisdom traditions.

The verb kataphage (from katesthio, "to eat up, to devour, to consume completely") is more intensive than the simple phage ("eat"). John is to devour the scroll entirely -- to take in every word, leaving nothing unconsumed.

**"It will make your stomach bitter, but in your mouth it will be sweet as honey" (pikranei sou ten koilian, all' en to stomati sou estai glyky hos meli).** The angel reverses the expected order: he mentions the bitterness first (the ultimate effect) and the sweetness second (the immediate experience). The sweetness is real -- God's word is inherently good, desirable, nourishing. The psalms and prophets consistently describe God's word as sweet. But the content of this particular word produces bitterness when fully digested.

The sweetness and bitterness represent two dimensions of the same prophetic message. God's word is sweet because it comes from God -- it reveals his purposes, discloses his plan, establishes his sovereignty. The word of God is always a gift, always an act of grace toward the one who receives it. Psalm 19:10: the LORD's ordinances are "sweeter than honey and the drippings of the honeycomb." But the specific content of this word -- what John must proclaim "concerning many peoples, nations, languages, and kings" (v. 11) -- is devastating. It includes judgment, destruction, suffering, and the consequences of the earth-dwellers' refusal to repent. The prophet who internalizes this message experiences both realities: the sweetness of being entrusted with God's word and the bitterness of knowing what that word contains.

**"In my mouth it was sweet as honey; and when I had eaten it, my stomach was made bitter" (en to stomati mou hos meli glyky, kai hote ephagon auto epikranthe he koilia mou).** John's experience confirms the angel's prediction exactly. The sweetness is immediate and real; the bitterness follows upon full consumption. The sequence mirrors Ezekiel's experience, though with a difference. Ezekiel reported the sweetness explicitly: "it was sweet as honey in my mouth" (Ezekiel 3:3). He did not report stomach bitterness directly. But in Ezekiel 3:14, after the Spirit carried him away, he described his state: "I went embittered in the rage of my spirit, and the hand of the LORD was strong on me." Ezekiel's bitterness came after the eating, as the weight of his prophetic commission pressed upon him. The author of Revelation makes the bitterness an immediate, physical part of the eating itself -- the prophetic burden is inseparable from the prophetic gift.

The verb pikraino ("to make bitter, to embitter") appears elsewhere in the document only at 8:11, where the waters were made bitter (epikranthesan) by the star called Wormwood. The word links John's internal experience to the external judgment: the same bitterness that fell on the waters of the world now fills the prophet's stomach. The prophet carries within himself the weight of the judgment he must proclaim.

## Verse 11: The Re-Commissioning -- Prophesy Again

"And they said to me, 'You must prophesy again concerning many peoples, nations, languages, and kings.'"

**"They said to me" (legousin moi).** The verb is plural -- "they say" -- but no specific speaker is identified. The plural is striking after the singular "voice from heaven" (v. 8) and the singular angel (v. 9). Who are "they"? The text does not specify. The effect of the indefinite plural is to give the commission the weight of the entire heavenly council rather than a single speaker. The command comes from above, from the collective authority of the heavenly realm. The unidentified "they" may include the heavenly voice, the angel, and other unnamed heavenly figures. The lack of a specific attribution makes the commission feel comprehensive and inescapable.

**"You must prophesy again" (dei se palin propheteusai).** The word dei ("it is necessary, one must") expresses divine compulsion -- not suggestion, not invitation, but necessity. This is the same dei that governs the entire document. In 1:1: "the things which must (dei) soon take place." In 4:1: "what must (dei) take place after these things." In 11:5: the fire that "must" (dei) come from the witnesses' mouths. In 17:10: the king who "must" (dei) remain a little while. In 20:3: Satan who "must" (dei) be released. The necessity is rooted in God's purpose: what must happen will happen because God has determined it. John's prophesying is not optional but is part of the same divine necessity that governs the seals, trumpets, and bowls. The prophet's proclamation is woven into the fabric of divine action.

The word palin ("again") indicates that John's prophetic activity is being renewed, not initiated for the first time. He has already been prophesying -- the content of chapters 1-9 is his prophetic testimony. Now he is being re-equipped and re-commissioned for what lies ahead. The eating of the scroll was the preparation; the command to prophesy is the deployment. The content of chapters 11 through the end of the document -- the two witnesses, the woman and the dragon, the beasts, the bowls, Babylon's fall, and the new creation -- constitutes the prophecy that this re-commissioning authorizes.

**"Concerning many peoples, nations, languages, and kings" (epi laois kai ethnesin kai glossais kai basileusin pollois).** The fourfold formula "peoples, nations, languages" recurs throughout this document as the standard designation for the totality of humanity:

- 5:9: the Lamb purchased people "from every tribe, language, people, and nation"
- 7:9: the great multitude "from every nation and all tribes and peoples and tongues"
- 11:9: "those from the peoples, tribes, languages, and nations" who view the witnesses' bodies
- 13:7: the beast is given authority "over every tribe, people, language, and nation"
- 14:6: the eternal gospel is preached "to every nation, tribe, language, and people"
- 17:15: the waters are "peoples and multitudes, and nations and languages"

In each occurrence, the fourfold list signifies the comprehensive scope of what is being described -- no ethnic, linguistic, or political group is excluded. John's re-commission is universal in scope. He must prophesy not to a single nation (as Ezekiel was sent to Israel, Ezekiel 3:1, 4-5) but concerning all the peoples of the earth.

The substitution of "kings" (basileusin) for the more common "tribes" (phylai) in the fourth position is deliberate and significant. The remaining chapters of the document will give extensive attention to political power and its confrontation with God's sovereignty: the beast who exercises authority and makes war on the saints (chapter 13), the kings of the earth who commit immorality with Babylon (17:2; 18:3, 9), the kings assembled at Armageddon (16:14), and the kings who bring their glory into the new Jerusalem (21:24). The inclusion of "kings" in John's commission signals that the prophecy to come will engage directly with political power -- with emperors, with the structures of imperial authority, with the question of who truly reigns. This is not a private, spiritual message but a public, political proclamation that addresses the powers that govern the earth.

The preposition epi can mean "concerning, about" (prophesying about these groups), "over, upon" (prophesying to or over these groups), or "against" (prophesying against them). The ambiguity may be intentional. The prophetic word in what follows will be simultaneously about the nations and their rulers, directed toward them, and at times pronounced against them. The preposition encompasses all three dimensions of the prophetic vocation.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. **Chapter 10 is the first half of the interlude between the sixth and seventh trumpets, structurally parallel to the interlude between the sixth and seventh seals (chapter 7).** The seal-interlude answered the question "Who can stand?" (6:17) by showing the sealed and the great multitude. The trumpet-interlude addresses the crisis of 9:20-21 -- the earth-dwellers' refusal to repent despite devastating judgment -- by re-equipping the prophet with an internalized word and re-commissioning him to prophesy to all peoples and kings. The mechanism for completing God's purpose is not judgment alone but the prophetic word proclaimed alongside and within judgment.

2. **The strong angel bears the accumulated attributes of divine presence from across Israel's scriptures and from this document's own earlier visions.** Cloud (Exodus 13:21; 19:9; Ezekiel 1:4), rainbow (Genesis 9:12-17; Ezekiel 1:28; Revelation 4:3), sun-face (Revelation 1:16; Daniel 10:6), fire-pillar feet (Exodus 13:21; Revelation 1:15) -- each attribute has deep theophanic roots. The angel is not identified as the risen Christ but bears the insignia of divine authority. As the angel of the LORD in the burning bush spoke God's words while remaining distinct from God (Exodus 3:2-4), so this angel represents the divine commission without being the divine person himself.

3. **The little scroll (biblaridion) is distinguished from the great scroll (biblion) of chapter 5 by its size and by its condition: it is open.** The great scroll was sealed with seven seals and required the Lamb to open it. The little scroll is already open -- the seals have been broken, and the content is accessible. The little scroll represents the prophetic message that John must internalize and proclaim in the second half of the document. The scroll-eating scene transitions John from observer of judgments to active prophetic participant in what follows.

4. **The lion-roar (v. 3) activates the prophetic tradition of Amos 3:7-8 and Hosea 11:10.** In Amos, the lion's roar is God's voice that compels prophetic response: "The Lord GOD has spoken! Who can do anything but prophesy?" The sequence in Revelation 10 follows the same pattern: the lion-roar (v. 3), the divine speech (the thunders, the oath), and the commission to prophesy (v. 11). The roar announces that God is speaking and that prophetic proclamation must follow.

5. **The seven thunders (v. 3) speak intelligible content that John is prohibited from recording.** Drawing on Psalm 29, where "the voice of the LORD" appears seven times in connection with thunder, the seven thunders represent the complete divine voice. The command to seal their words creates a boundary around the revelation: the audience receives a vast and detailed disclosure of God's purposes, but not everything. The sealing reminds the audience that God's purposes exceed what any human document can contain. The contrast with Daniel 12:4 ("seal up the book until the end of time") is instructive: Daniel's book was sealed because the time had not come; the thunders' words are sealed even though the time has come. Some dimensions of divine communication are simply withheld.

6. **The angel's oath (vv. 5-7) directly reenacts Daniel 12:7, with a critical difference in content.** In Daniel, the man in linen swore that the appointed time would be "a time, times, and half a time" -- a specific duration before fulfillment. In Revelation, the angel swears that "there will be no more delay" -- the duration has expired. What Daniel was told to wait for is now announced as imminent. The oracle that was sealed in Daniel's book is being unsealed in John's prophecy. The oath also answers the martyrs' cry of 6:10 ("How long?") by declaring that the chronos (delay, waiting period) imposed in 6:11 is over. The trajectory from "How long?" (6:10) through "a little while longer" (6:11) to "no more delay" (10:6) forms one of the document's most carefully sustained arcs.

7. **"The mystery of God is finished" (v. 7) identifies the content of the seventh trumpet.** The mystery (mysterion) -- God's hidden but progressively revealed plan -- will reach its completion when the seventh angel sounds. That sounding occurs in 11:15, where the announcement comes: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ." The mystery of God is the full establishment of divine sovereignty over all creation. This is what the prophets announced (Amos 3:7) and what the trumpet sequence has been driving toward. The verb euengelizo ("to announce good news") characterizes the mystery's completion as gospel -- even though it includes judgment, it is fundamentally the good news that God reigns.

8. **The scroll-eating scene (vv. 8-10) reenacts Ezekiel's prophetic commissioning (Ezekiel 2:8-3:3, 14).** The parallels are extensive: a scroll is presented to the prophet, the prophet is told to eat it, the scroll is sweet as honey in the mouth. The difference is the explicit bitterness in the stomach, which Ezekiel experienced only afterward ("I went embittered in the rage of my spirit," Ezekiel 3:14). The sweetness and bitterness represent the dual nature of the prophetic message: God's word is inherently sweet (Psalm 19:10; 119:103; Jeremiah 15:16), but the specific content -- judgment on peoples and kings, destruction of Babylon, the cost of faithful witness -- is devastating. The prophet carries within himself the full weight of what he must proclaim. He is not a detached messenger but one who has consumed the message and bears it in his body.

9. **The re-commissioning in verse 11 pivots the document from its first major movement (chapters 1-9) to its second (chapters 11-22).** The command "You must prophesy again" marks a literary and theological turning point. The word dei ("it is necessary") places John's prophesying within the framework of divine necessity that governs the entire document. The scope is universal -- "peoples, nations, languages, and kings" -- and the substitution of "kings" for the more common "tribes" signals that the prophecy to come will engage directly with political power. Chapters 13 (the beasts), 17-18 (Babylon and the kings of the earth), and 19 (the rider and the armies) fulfill this commission. The prophet who has eaten the scroll now speaks from within himself the word that will confront the nations and their rulers.

10. **The chapter's placement between 9:20-21 (the refusal to repent) and 11:13 (the giving of glory to God) illuminates the role of prophetic witness in the document's theology of judgment.** Judgment alone does not accomplish God's purpose -- the earth-dwellers experience six trumpet-plagues and do not repent. What the interlude introduces is the prophetic word: the scroll eaten, the prophet re-commissioned, the witnesses who prophesy and die and are raised (11:3-12). The earthquake following the witnesses' vindication produces a different result: "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). The author's argument is that God's full purpose requires not only judgment but the prophetic word -- the testimony of those who have internalized God's message and proclaim it at the cost of their own lives. The chapter commissions John for precisely this role.
