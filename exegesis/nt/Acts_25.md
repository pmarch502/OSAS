# Acts 25 -- First-Century Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 24):** Paul has been in Caesarea in Roman custody since the tribune Claudius Lysias transferred him from Jerusalem under heavy military escort to escape an assassination plot by more than forty Jews who had bound themselves by oath not to eat or drink until they killed him (23:12-35). The chapter opened with the high priest Ananias, certain elders, and a professional attorney named Tertullus traveling from Jerusalem to Caesarea to bring formal charges before the governor Felix. Tertullus's accusation contained three elements: Paul is "a public menace and one who stirs up dissensions among all the Jews throughout the world" (a political charge of sedition), he is "a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes" (an identification of Paul's movement), and "he even tried to desecrate the temple" (a religious charge, 24:5-6). Paul's defense addressed each point: he did not argue or cause a riot in the temple, the synagogues, or the city (24:12); his accusers cannot prove their charges (24:13); he serves "the God of our fathers" according to "the Way, which they call a sect," believing everything in the Law and the Prophets (24:14); he shares with his accusers the hope of "a resurrection of both the righteous and the wicked" (24:15); he came to Jerusalem to bring charitable gifts and present offerings and was found purified in the temple without any crowd or uproar (24:17-18); the Jews from Asia who actually started the disturbance are absent and should be the ones testifying (24:19); and the only declaration he made before the Sanhedrin was "For the resurrection of the dead I am on trial before you today" (24:21). Felix, who had "quite accurate knowledge about the Way" (24:22), adjourned the proceedings without rendering a verdict, claiming he would decide the case when the tribune Lysias came down. He kept Paul in loose custody, allowing his friends to provide for him. Felix and his Jewish wife Drusilla heard Paul speak about "faith in Christ Jesus," and when Paul discussed "righteousness, self-control, and the judgment to come," Felix became frightened and dismissed him (24:24-25). Felix repeatedly summoned Paul, hoping for a bribe (24:26). After two years of this, Felix was succeeded by Porcius Festus, and "wanting to do the Jews a favor" (charin... katathesthai tois Ioudaiois), Felix left Paul imprisoned (24:27). The chapter thus established two critical patterns: Paul's accusers cannot prove their charges in a Roman court, and the Roman governor compromises justice to maintain political relations with the Jewish leadership.

**What the author argues immediately after (Acts 26):** Agrippa grants Paul permission to speak, and Paul delivers the most detailed and rhetorically polished of his three defense speeches in Acts (the others being Acts 22 before the Jerusalem crowd and Acts 24 before Felix). Paul addresses Agrippa as one who is "an expert in all customs and questions among the Jews" (26:3) -- a very different audience from Festus. Paul identifies the core issue of his trial: "I am standing trial for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers; the promise to which our twelve tribes hope to attain, as they earnestly serve God night and day" (26:6-7). He then asks the decisive question: "Why is it considered incredible among you people if God raises the dead?" (26:8). He recounts his former life as a Pharisee, his violent persecution of the believers, and his encounter with the risen Jesus on the Damascus road, including details not present in the earlier accounts -- that the voice spoke "in the Hebrew dialect" and that Jesus said "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (26:14), a Greek proverb describing futile resistance against a superior power. The commission Paul received was "to open their eyes so that they may turn from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins and an inheritance among those who have been sanctified by faith in Me" (26:18). Paul reports that he responded by proclaiming "to those in Damascus first, and in Jerusalem, and then all the region of Judea, and even to the Gentiles, that they are to repent and turn to God, performing deeds consistent with repentance" (26:20). Festus interrupts, calling Paul insane; Paul insists he is speaking "truthful and rational words" and appeals to Agrippa's knowledge of the prophets. Agrippa responds ambiguously: "In a short time you are going to persuade me to make a Christian of myself" (26:28). Paul expresses the wish that all present might become as he is, "except for these chains" (26:29). The hearing ends with Agrippa, Festus, Bernice, and the assembled dignitaries withdrawing and agreeing among themselves: "This man is not doing anything deserving death or imprisonment" (26:31). Agrippa delivers the chapter's final verdict to Festus: "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:32).

**How chapter 25 connects these:** Acts 24 ended with a governor who knew Paul was innocent but refused to release him for political reasons, leaving him in prison for two years and then bequeathing the unresolved case to his successor as a political favor to the Jewish leadership. Acts 26 will present Paul's most comprehensive defense speech and the most explicit judicial verdict of innocence in the entire narrative. Acts 25 is the mechanism that connects these two endpoints. It answers three questions that the narrative requires: How does the new governor handle the inherited case? How does Paul end up before a king? And what sets Paul irrevocably on the road to Rome? The chapter answers the first question by showing Festus repeating Felix's pattern -- a Roman governor who finds no criminal wrongdoing but who is willing to compromise the case to please the Jewish leadership. It answers the second by introducing Agrippa and Bernice and narrating Festus's own need: he has a prisoner he must send to the Emperor but no coherent charges to write. It answers the third through the legal act that dominates the center of the chapter: Paul's appeal to Caesar. The appeal is the chapter's pivot. Everything before it (vv. 1-9) creates the conditions that make the appeal necessary -- the persistent Jewish charges, the governor's willingness to trade justice for political favor. Everything after it (vv. 13-27) deals with the consequences of the appeal -- Festus must now send Paul to Rome, but he has nothing to write. The chapter thus functions as the narrative fulcrum that tilts Paul's trajectory from Caesarea to Rome, fulfilling the Lord's declaration in 23:11: "as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome."

## Verses 1-5: The New Governor and the Renewed Conspiracy

"Festus, then, after arriving in the province, went up to Jerusalem from Caesarea three days later" (25:1).

The transition of power from Felix to Festus is the event that reopens Paul's case. Porcius Festus is known from Josephus (Antiquities 20.8.9-11; War 2.14.1) as the successor to Felix, arriving in Judea around 59-60 CE. His first act upon arriving in the province was to travel from Caesarea -- the Roman administrative capital of Judea, where the governor's official residence (the praetorium) was located -- to Jerusalem, the religious and political center of the Jewish population. The phrase "went up" (anebe) reflects both geography (Jerusalem sits at a higher elevation than the coastal city of Caesarea) and the conventional language of pilgrimage and diplomatic respect: one always "goes up" to Jerusalem. That Festus made this visit within three days of his arrival indicates his awareness that Jerusalem was the political center of gravity for governing Judea. A new governor needed to establish working relations with the Jewish leadership immediately.

"And the chief priests and the leading men of the Jews brought charges against Paul, and they were pleading with him, requesting a concession against Paul, that he might have him brought to Jerusalem (at the same time, setting an ambush to kill him on the way)" (25:2-3).

The phrase "the chief priests and the leading men of the Jews" (hoi archiereis kai hoi protoi ton Ioudaion) identifies the same coalition that prosecuted Paul before Felix. The word protoi ("first men, leading men, principal men") denotes the aristocratic elite of Judean society -- members of prominent priestly and lay families who held informal but decisive influence over public affairs. That they approach Festus during his inaugural visit to Jerusalem indicates that Paul's case has not been forgotten during the two years of Felix's inaction. It remains a priority for the leadership.

Their approach takes two forms. First, they "brought charges" (enephanisan -- from emphanizo, "to make visible, to present formally, to lay information before a magistrate"). This is the same verb used in 24:1 for the formal presentation of charges before Felix. Second, they "were pleading with him" (parakalountes auton), making a personal request beyond the formal charges. What they request is a "concession" (charin) -- literally a "favor" or "grace" -- that Paul be transferred to Jerusalem for trial.

The word charin here is the same root that appeared in 24:27 when Felix left Paul imprisoned "wanting to do the Jews a favor" (charin... katathesthai tois Ioudaiois). The author is establishing a vocabulary of political exchange. The Jewish leadership trades in favors (charites) with Roman governors; governance in Judea runs on this currency. What they are requesting from Festus is not justice but a political concession -- they want the case moved from the Roman tribunal in Caesarea to Jerusalem, where their influence is greater.

The author then provides the audience with information the characters do not all share: "at the same time, setting an ambush to kill him on the way" (enedran poiountes anelein auton kata ten hodon). The word enedra ("ambush") is the same word used in 23:16 when Paul's nephew discovered the earlier conspiracy: "he heard of their ambush" (akousas... ten enedran). The verb anelein ("to kill, to do away with") is the same verb used in 23:15 for the plotters' intention. The author is drawing a direct line between the conspiracy of Acts 23 and this new plot. The forty men who swore an oath to kill Paul may or may not be the same individuals involved now -- the text does not specify -- but the pattern is identical: a request to transfer Paul from Roman custody, coupled with a plan to murder him during the transfer. The earlier plot was foiled by Paul's nephew reporting it to the tribune Lysias. This time, the plot is foiled by Festus's own decision not to grant the request.

The author's parenthetical disclosure of the ambush plan serves the narrative in two ways. First, it demonstrates the persistent lethal hostility that Paul faces from the Jewish leadership -- this is not a good-faith legal dispute but a campaign to kill him. Second, it vindicates Paul's later refusal to go to Jerusalem (25:10-11) by showing the reader that the transfer would have been a death sentence. Paul may or may not have known about the specific ambush plan, but his instinct to stay within the Roman legal system rather than submit to Jerusalem proceedings is shown to be a matter of survival.

"Festus then answered that Paul was being kept in custody in Caesarea, and that he himself was about to leave shortly. 'Therefore,' he said, 'have the influential men among you go there with me, and if there is anything wrong about the man, have them bring charges against him'" (25:4-5).

Festus's response is procedurally correct. Paul is already in custody in Caesarea, where the governor's tribunal is located; there is no legal reason to move him. Festus invites the Jewish leaders to send their "influential men" (hoi en hymin dynatoi -- literally "the powerful ones among you") to Caesarea to present their charges before the proper tribunal. The conditional "if there is anything wrong about the man" (ei ti estin en to andri atopon) uses the word atopon, meaning "out of place, improper, wrong, criminal." The word carries a note of skepticism: Festus does not assume guilt. He says "if" there is something wrong, not "since" or "because." The conditional construction preserves judicial neutrality -- at least in form.

The author does not tell us why Festus refused the transfer request. He may have been following standard Roman procedure, he may have been suspicious of the urgency, or he may simply have preferred to handle the case in his own tribunal rather than in the politically charged atmosphere of Jerusalem. Whatever his reasons, the refusal saves Paul's life -- a fact the author has ensured the reader knows, even though Festus himself may not.

## Verses 6-8: The Trial Before Festus

"After Festus had spent no more than eight or ten days among them, he went down to Caesarea, and on the next day he took his seat on the tribunal and ordered that Paul be brought" (25:6).

The imprecise time note -- "no more than eight or ten days" (hemeras ou pleious okto e deka) -- is characteristic of the author's style when giving approximate durations. What matters is the sequence: Festus completed his introductory visit to Jerusalem, returned to Caesarea, and immediately (the next day) convened a formal hearing. The phrase "took his seat on the tribunal" (kathisas epi tou bematos) describes a formal judicial proceeding. The bema is the raised platform from which a Roman magistrate rendered judgments. In Caesarea, this was located in the praetorium originally built by Herod the Great, the same tribunal where Pilate had sat (cf. Matthew 27:19; John 19:13, where Pilate "sat down on the judgment seat," epi bematos). The verb kathisas ("having sat down") is the technical term for a magistrate opening a formal session. This is not a casual hearing; it is a Roman trial.

"After Paul arrived, the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing many, and serious, charges against him which they could not prove" (25:7).

The accusers are described as having "stood around him" (periestesan auton) -- the verb periistemi means "to stand in a circle around, to surround." The image is of Paul encircled by his accusers, physically embodying the hostility directed at him. The charges are described with two qualifications: they are "many" (polla) and "serious" (barea -- literally "heavy, weighty, grave"). But the author immediately undercuts the gravity of the charges with a single clause: "which they could not prove" (ha ouk ischyon apodeixai). The verb ischyo means "to have strength, to be able, to have the power"; apodeiknymi means "to demonstrate, to prove, to show by evidence." The combination means: they lacked the ability to substantiate their accusations. The pattern from Acts 24 repeats: serious-sounding charges with no evidence to support them.

The author does not enumerate the specific charges here, having already detailed them through Tertullus in 24:5-6. Instead, Paul's response in the next verse reveals their content indirectly.

"While Paul said in his own defense, 'I have not done anything wrong either against the Law of the Jews, or against the temple, or against Caesar'" (25:8).

Paul's defense is a triple denial that corresponds to three categories of accusation. The three areas -- the Law of the Jews (ton nomon ton Ioudaion), the temple (to hieron), and Caesar (Kaisara) -- represent the three spheres in which Paul's opponents have tried to locate his offense. Against the Law: the accusation from 21:21 and 21:28 that Paul teaches Jews to forsake Moses. Against the temple: the accusation from 21:28 that Paul brought Gentiles into the temple and defiled it. Against Caesar: the political dimension of the charge -- that Paul stirs up sedition among Jews throughout the world (24:5), which would constitute a disturbance of the Pax Romana and thus an offense against imperial order.

The third category -- "against Caesar" -- is new in Paul's explicit defense. Before Felix, Paul addressed only the Jewish religious charges. The addition of Caesar here suggests that the accusers may have intensified their political framing of the case for the new governor. If Paul could be presented not merely as a religious deviant but as a political threat to Roman order, a Roman governor would have stronger motivation to act against him. Paul's denial addresses all three fronts: he has violated neither Jewish religious law, nor the sanctity of the temple, nor Roman political order.

The verb hamartano ("to sin, to do wrong") used here (ouden hemarton -- "I have in no way sinned/done wrong") carries both legal and moral weight. Paul is not merely entering a technical legal plea; he is declaring that he has committed no offense in any of these domains.

## Verses 9-12: The Favor, the Refusal, and the Appeal to Caesar

"But Festus, wanting to do the Jews a favor, replied to Paul and said, 'Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem and stand trial before me on these charges?'" (25:9).

The phrase "wanting to do the Jews a favor" (thelion tois Ioudaiois charin katathesthai) is verbally identical to the phrase used of Felix in 24:27 (charin... katathesthai tois Ioudaiois). The author has chosen the same words deliberately. The repetition establishes a pattern: two successive Roman governors, both finding no criminal wrongdoing, both willing to compromise Paul's case for political advantage with the Jewish leadership. The word charin ("favor, grace") appears again -- the same currency of political exchange noted in 25:3 when the Jewish leaders requested a "concession." Roman governance in Judea operates through an economy of favors exchanged between the governor and the local elite. Paul's freedom is the coin being spent.

Festus's proposal is superficially reasonable: he offers to hold the trial in Jerusalem rather than Caesarea, still with himself presiding ("before me," ep' emou). The change would be in venue only, not in jurisdiction -- Festus would still be the judge. But the offer itself reveals a willingness to accommodate the accusers at the expense of the accused. Paul has been held in Caesarea for over two years precisely because the tribune Lysias determined that Jerusalem was too dangerous for him. The transfer to Caesarea was motivated by an assassination plot. The Jewish leaders have just renewed their request for a Jerusalem transfer, again accompanied by an ambush plan (25:3). Festus may not know about the ambush, but the reader does. The author has arranged the narrative so that the reader understands what Festus does not: the proposal to move Paul to Jerusalem is not a neutral change of venue.

"But Paul said, 'I am standing before Caesar's tribunal, where I ought to be tried. I have done nothing wrong to the Jews, as you also very well know'" (25:10).

Paul's response is a legal argument, not a personal complaint. He begins with a statement of jurisdictional principle: "I am standing before Caesar's tribunal" (epi tou bematos Kaisaros hestos eimi). The bema in Caesarea is Caesar's tribunal -- the governor sits as the Emperor's representative, and his court is an extension of imperial authority. Paul argues that this is the proper venue: "where I ought to be tried" (hou me dei krinesthai). The verb dei ("it is necessary, it is fitting, one ought") indicates obligation -- not merely preference but juridical propriety. A Roman citizen's case properly belongs before a Roman magistrate sitting on the imperial tribunal, not before a proceeding influenced by the local population's political interests.

The second clause -- "I have done nothing wrong to the Jews, as you also very well know" (Ioudaious ouden edikesa, hos kai sy kallion epiginoskes) -- goes further. Paul directly addresses Festus and asserts that the governor himself recognizes Paul's innocence. The comparative adverb kallion ("quite well, very well, better") suggests that Festus understands the situation better than his proposal implies. Paul is saying: you know I am innocent; your offer to move the trial to Jerusalem is not motivated by justice but by politics. The verb epiginosko ("to recognize, to know fully") is stronger than simple ginosko; it implies thorough understanding. Paul credits Festus with full awareness of the facts while challenging him to act on that awareness.

"If, therefore, I am in the wrong and have committed something deserving death, I am not trying to avoid execution; but if there is nothing to the accusations which these men are bringing against me, no one can hand me over to them. I appeal to Caesar" (25:11).

Paul constructs a formal conditional argument with two branches. The first branch: "If I am in the wrong" (ei men oun adiko) and "have committed something deserving death" (axion thanatou pepracha ti), "I am not trying to avoid execution" (ou paraitoumai to apothanein). The verb paraiteomai means "to beg off, to ask to be excused, to seek to avoid." Paul explicitly accepts the legitimacy of capital punishment for genuine crimes. He is not claiming immunity from justice; he is claiming innocence. If he has done something that warrants death, he will accept death. This statement preempts any suggestion that his resistance to the Jerusalem transfer is mere self-preservation. His objection is not to punishment but to injustice.

The second branch: "if there is nothing to the accusations" (ei de ouden estin hon houtoi kategorousin mou -- literally "if there is nothing of which these men accuse me"), "no one can hand me over to them" (oudeis me dynatai autois charisasthai). The verb charisasthai is crucial. It is from charizomai, meaning "to grant as a favor, to give freely, to hand over as a gift." The verb is cognate with charis ("grace, favor") and charin ("a favor"), the same word that described what the Jewish leaders requested (25:3) and what Festus wanted to give them (25:9). Paul uses the legal language of favor-exchange against the favor itself: no one has the authority to "gift" me to my accusers. The verb transforms the proposed Jerusalem transfer from a neutral procedural adjustment into what it actually is -- a politician handing a prisoner over as a political gift. Paul refuses to be the currency of this transaction.

"I appeal to Caesar" (Kaisara epikaloumai). The verb epikaleo in the middle voice means "to call upon, to invoke, to appeal to." This is the formal legal act of provocatio ad Caesarem -- the right of a Roman citizen to have his case heard by the Emperor himself when he believes he cannot receive justice from the local magistrate. The right of appeal (provocatio) was one of the most ancient protections of Roman citizenship, established by the Lex Valeria of 509 BCE and reinforced by subsequent legislation. By the first century, the provocatio allowed any Roman citizen to bypass the provincial governor's judgment and have the case transferred to the Emperor's personal jurisdiction. Once invoked, the appeal could not be revoked by the governor -- the case must go to Rome.

The verb epikaloumai is the same verb used throughout Acts for "calling on the name of the Lord" (Acts 2:21, quoting Joel 2:32; Acts 9:14, 21; Acts 22:16). The verbal coincidence may not be accidental on the part of the author. Paul "calls upon" Caesar in the same grammatical construction that believers "call upon" the Lord. The parallel is not theological (the author is not equating Caesar with Christ) but structural: Paul's survival depends on invoking an authority higher than the one immediately before him, and the language of invocation is the same in both cases.

"Then when Festus had conferred with his council, he answered, 'You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go'" (25:12).

Festus consults his consilium -- the advisory council of assessors (symboulion) that typically accompanied a Roman governor and assisted in judicial deliberations. The consultation is procedurally proper; a governor did not make major judicial decisions without consulting his advisors. The response is formulaic and final: "Kaisara epikekliesai, epi Kaisara poreuse" -- "You have called upon Caesar; to Caesar you shall go." The perfect tense (epikekliesai, "you have appealed") indicates a completed legal act that cannot be undone. The future tense (poreuse, "you shall go") states the inevitable consequence. The appeal has been made; the transfer is now a matter of law, not discretion.

The word poreuse ("you shall go") echoes the Lord's command in 22:21: "Go (poreuou), for I will send you far away to the Gentiles." In 22:21, the command was divine; in 25:12, the mechanism is Roman legal procedure. The destination in both cases is the same: away from Jerusalem, toward the nations. The Lord's directive, delivered in a temple vision, is being fulfilled through the machinery of Roman imperial justice. Paul's appeal to Caesar is simultaneously a legal act and, within the author's narrative, the instrument through which God's declared purpose is accomplished.

## Verses 13-22: Festus Presents the Case to King Agrippa

"Now when several days had passed, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived in Caesarea, paying their respects to Festus" (25:13).

The author introduces two new figures. "King Agrippa" is Herod Agrippa II, the son of Herod Agrippa I (whose death the author narrated in Acts 12:20-23) and the great-grandson of Herod the Great. Agrippa II held the title of king over territories in the northeast of Palestine (parts of the former tetrarchies of Philip and Lysanias), though Judea itself was under direct Roman governance through the procurator. He also held a unique privilege: the Roman authorities had granted him custodianship of the Jerusalem temple, including the right to appoint the high priest (Josephus, Antiquities 20.1.3; 20.9.4). This meant Agrippa was intimately familiar with Jewish religious affairs -- a qualification that will prove essential to the narrative.

"Bernice" (Bernike) was Agrippa's sister, the eldest daughter of Herod Agrippa I. She had been married to her uncle Herod of Chalcis (who died in 48 CE) and later briefly to Polemon II of Cilicia, but by this time she was living with her brother Agrippa. Josephus reports widespread rumors of an incestuous relationship between Agrippa and Bernice (Antiquities 20.7.3), and the Roman satirist Juvenal later alluded to the same (Satires 6.156-160). The author of Acts does not comment on the relationship but consistently pairs the two names -- "Agrippa and Bernice" -- throughout chapters 25-26, presenting them as a unit. A first-century audience familiar with the Herodian family's reputation would bring their own knowledge to this pairing without needing the author to editorialize.

The visit is described as "paying their respects" (aspasamenoi ton Pheston -- literally "having greeted Festus") -- a diplomatic courtesy call on the new governor. It was customary for client kings to pay their respects to a new provincial governor, affirming the working relationship between Roman administration and the local client monarchy.

"And while they were spending many days there, Festus presented Paul's case to the king" (25:14a).

The phrase "many days" (pleious hemeras) indicates an extended visit, during which Festus raised Paul's case in conversation. The verb anetheto (from anatithemai, "to lay before, to set forth, to communicate") suggests a deliberate presentation -- Festus is not making casual conversation but seeking counsel from someone who understands Jewish affairs better than he does.

"Saying, 'There is a man who was left as a prisoner by Felix; and when I was in Jerusalem, the chief priests and the elders of the Jews brought charges against him, asking for a sentence of condemnation against him'" (25:14b-15).

Festus begins his account. The phrase "left as a prisoner by Felix" (kataleleimmenos hypo Phelikos desmios) uses the perfect passive participle, indicating Paul's ongoing status as an inherited problem. The word desmios ("prisoner, one in bonds") describes Paul's condition. Festus inherited Paul along with the office. The accusers sought "a sentence of condemnation" (katadiken -- literally "a judgment against"). The word katadike appears only here in the New Testament and means a negative verdict, a condemnation. The Jewish leaders did not merely bring charges; they came seeking a predetermined outcome.

"I replied to them that it is not the custom of the Romans to hand over any person before the accused meets his accusers face to face, and has an opportunity to make his defense against the charges" (25:16).

Festus presents himself to Agrippa as a champion of Roman legal procedure. The word ethos ("custom, practice") here refers to the established principles of Roman justice. Two specific principles are named: the right of confrontation (the accused must "meet his accusers face to face," kata prosopon echoi tous kategorountas -- literally "have the accusers before his face") and the right of defense (the accused must have "an opportunity to make his defense," topon te apologias laboi -- literally "receive a place of defense"). Both principles were fundamental to Roman law. Cicero articulated them: a defendant must be present at his own trial, must know the charges against him, and must be permitted to respond (Pro Quinctio 8.30; In Verrem 2.1.9). Festus portrays his refusal to grant the Jewish leaders' initial request (25:3-5) as an act of Roman legal principle rather than mere administrative convenience.

The verb charisasthai ("to hand over as a favor") appears again, now in Festus's own mouth: "it is not the custom of the Romans to hand over (charisasthai) any person." The same verb Paul used to protest Festus's proposal (25:11, "no one can hand me over to them") is now being used by Festus himself to describe what he did not do for the Jewish leaders in Jerusalem. The irony is that Festus describes his past refusal to hand Paul over while having just attempted to do exactly that by proposing the Jerusalem transfer (25:9). Festus's account to Agrippa omits his own offer to move the trial to Jerusalem. He presents a cleaner version of events to the king than what actually transpired.

"So after they had assembled here, I did not delay, but on the next day took my seat on the tribunal and ordered that the man be brought. When the accusers stood up, they did not begin bringing any charges against him of crimes that I suspected, but they simply had some points of disagreement with him about their own religion and about a dead man, Jesus, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:17-19).

Festus describes the Caesarea trial and confesses his surprise at its content. The phrase "crimes that I suspected" (pon[eron] hon ego hypeno[oun] -- literally "of the evil things which I was supposing") reveals Festus's expectations. Given the intensity of the Jewish leadership's pursuit of Paul -- traveling from Jerusalem, enlisting leading men, pressing charges repeatedly -- Festus expected serious criminal allegations: sedition, violence, or some form of political conspiracy that would justify Roman judicial involvement. What he found instead was something quite different.

"Some points of disagreement about their own religion" (zetemata... peri tes idias deisidaimonias). The word zetemata (from zetema, "a question, a point of inquiry, a disputed matter") reduces the controversy to an academic dispute. The word deisidaimonia is carefully chosen and deliberately ambiguous. In Greek usage, it can mean either "religion" (a neutral or respectful sense, as in Acts 17:22 where Paul tells the Athenians "I observe that you are very religious," kata panta hos deisidaimonesterous) or "superstition" (a dismissive sense, as in the common Greco-Roman characterization of foreign religions). Speaking to Agrippa, who is Jewish, Festus likely intends the neutral sense -- "their own religious practice" -- though a Roman audience might hear the word differently. The qualifier idias ("their own") distances Festus from the content: this is their internal affair, not a matter that naturally falls within Roman judicial competence.

"And about a dead man, Jesus, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (peri tinos Iesou tethnekotes, hon ephasken ho Paulos zen). This sentence is one of the most remarkable in Acts, because it is the entire controversy -- the resurrection of Jesus -- as perceived by an uninvolved Roman official. Festus has distilled weeks of hearings, multiple accusers, and elaborate legal proceedings down to a single factual dispute: there is a dead man named Jesus, and Paul claims this dead man is alive. The word tethnekotes (perfect participle of thnesko, "to die") means "one who has died and remains dead" -- the natural state of a dead person. The verb ephasken (imperfect of phasko, "to assert, to claim, to declare") has a slightly skeptical tone -- Paul "was asserting" or "kept claiming." The verb zen (present infinitive of zao, "to live") describes present, ongoing life. The contrast is stark: the dead one versus the living one, as Paul claimed.

Festus has arrived, without theological training or scriptural background, at the exact center of the dispute. The entire controversy between Paul and the Jewish leadership, once stripped of its layers of legal accusation and political maneuvering, reduces to one question: is Jesus dead or alive? Everything else -- the Law, the temple, Caesar, the Nazarenes, sedition, defilement -- is scaffolding around this single point. Festus sees it clearly precisely because he has no stake in the answer. For the Jewish leaders, the question of Jesus's resurrection is inseparable from questions of messianic authority, temple legitimacy, and the validity of Torah interpretation. For Festus, it is simply a bizarre factual claim about a specific individual.

"And being at a loss how to investigate such matters, I asked whether he was willing to go to Jerusalem and stand trial there on these matters" (25:20).

The verb aporoumenos (from aporeo, "to be at a loss, to be perplexed, to not know what to do") describes Festus's genuine intellectual and procedural confusion. Roman legal training did not equip a governor to adjudicate disputes about whether dead men were alive. Festus has no legal framework, no precedent, and no expertise for this kind of case. His proposal to move the trial to Jerusalem was, in his own telling, motivated not by political calculation but by helplessness -- let the case be tried where the expertise exists. The author has already told us (25:9) that Festus's real motivation was political favor-seeking, but Festus presents a more flattering version of his reasoning to Agrippa.

"But when Paul appealed to be held in custody for the Emperor's decision, I ordered that he be kept in custody until I send him to Caesar" (25:21).

Festus summarizes the appeal. The phrase "the Emperor's decision" (ten tou Sebastou diagnosen) uses the title Sebastos -- the Greek equivalent of the Latin Augustus, the official title of the reigning Emperor (at this time, Nero). The word diagnosen (from diagnosis, "an examination, a decision, a judicial determination") is a technical legal term for an authoritative ruling. Paul has requested that the final determination of his case be made by the Emperor himself. Festus complied, and Paul remains in custody pending the transfer.

"Then Agrippa said to Festus, 'I also would like to hear the man myself.' 'Tomorrow,' he said, 'you shall hear him'" (25:22).

Agrippa's interest is expressed with the imperfect tense: eboulomen ("I was wanting" or "I have been wanting") -- suggesting that his curiosity predates this conversation. The word kai ("also") may imply that Agrippa's interest is comparable to Festus's own: just as Festus found the case puzzling, Agrippa also wants to hear Paul for himself. Alternatively, the "also" may echo the reader's own desire -- everyone, it seems, wants to hear what Paul has to say.

Festus's immediate response -- "Tomorrow you shall hear him" -- indicates both the governor's eagerness to have Agrippa's assistance and the practical convenience of the king's presence. The promise is fulfilled promptly; there is no delay.

The exchange also echoes a distant precedent within this narrative. When Herod Antipas heard about Jesus's fame, "he kept trying to see him" (Luke 9:9), and when Jesus was finally sent to him by Pilate, Antipas "was very glad, for he had long desired to see him" (Luke 23:8). Now another Herod -- Agrippa II, Antipas's grand-nephew -- wants to hear Paul. The Herodian dynasty encounters the message about Jesus across generations. Antipas heard about Jesus and wanted to see him; Agrippa hears about Paul and wants to hear him. The parallel is structural: in both cases, a Herodian ruler is drawn to examine the matter personally; in both cases, the ruler finds no guilt (Luke 23:15; Acts 26:31-32).

## Verses 23-27: The Formal Hearing and Festus's Dilemma

"So, on the next day when Agrippa and Bernice came amid great pomp and entered the auditorium, accompanied by the commanders and the prominent men of the city, at the command of Festus, Paul was brought before them" (25:23).

The scene is set with deliberate contrast. The word phantasia ("pomp, pageantry, display") appears only here in the entire New Testament. In Greek usage, the word means "a making visible, a display, an appearance" -- and it carries a connotation of surface show, of appearance as opposed to reality. The cognate verb phantazo means "to make appear" and is related to phantasma ("apparition, phantom"). The author's word choice is quietly ironic: the entrance of Agrippa and Bernice is described with a word that suggests spectacle rather than substance -- things made to appear impressive.

The "auditorium" (akroaterion, from akroaomai, "to listen, to hear") is literally a "hearing room" -- a formal chamber designed for audiences and hearings. The setting is filled with chiliarchoi ("commanders" -- the equivalent of military tribunes, suggesting that several were present, indicating a significant military garrison) and the "prominent men of the city" (andres hoi kat' exochen tes poleos -- literally "the men who are preeminent of the city"). The audience is the military and civilian elite of Caesarea.

Into this display of earthly power, Paul is brought. The verb agein ("to lead, to bring") is used in the passive (achthenta, "having been brought") -- Paul does not enter on his own authority; he is brought, a prisoner led before the powerful. The contrast between the phantasia of the royal entrance and the prisoner in chains is the visual center of the scene. The entire assembly of power -- king, governor, sister-consort, military commanders, civic notables -- exists in this moment as an audience for a man who is in custody. The one who has no power in this room is the one everyone has assembled to hear.

"And Festus said, 'King Agrippa, and all you gentlemen present with us, you see this man about whom all the people of the Jews appealed to me, both in Jerusalem and here, shouting that he ought not to live any longer'" (25:24).

Festus opens the hearing with a formal address. He describes Paul as the man "about whom all the people of the Jews" (pan to plethos ton Ioudaion) have appealed -- the word plethos ("multitude, mass, entire body") generalizes the opposition from the chief priests and elders to the whole Jewish community. This is likely an exaggeration -- the author has not described the entire Jewish population as hostile to Paul, only the leadership -- but Festus is presenting the case to dignitaries and has reason to emphasize the scale of the opposition.

The word enetychon ("appealed to," from entynchano, "to meet with, to petition, to appeal to") is the same verb used in Romans 11:2 for Elijah's appeal to God against Israel: "he appeals to God against Israel" (entynchanei to theo kata tou Israel). The parallel is coincidental at the level of vocabulary but illuminating at the level of irony: in Romans, Elijah appeals to God against Israel; here, Israel appeals to Rome against Paul.

The description of the Jewish demand is visceral: they were "shouting that he ought not to live any longer" (botontes me dein auton zen meketi -- literally "crying out that it is not necessary for him to live any longer"). The verb boao means "to shout, to cry aloud"; the phrasing echoes the crowd's demand in 22:22: "Away with such a fellow from the earth! For he should not be allowed to live." Festus is characterizing the Jewish opposition to Paul as a demand for his death.

"But I found that he had committed nothing deserving death; and since he himself appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him" (25:25).

The conjunction de ("but") marks the contrast between the Jewish demand and Festus's finding. The verb katelabomen (from katalambano, "to seize, to comprehend, to find out, to determine") means Festus has examined the case and reached a conclusion: Paul has committed "nothing deserving death" (meden axion auton thanatou peprachota -- literally "nothing worthy of death has been done by him"). The word axion ("worthy, deserving") is the same word Paul used in 25:11 when he said "if I have committed something deserving death" (axion thanatou). Festus confirms Paul's own conditional: there is nothing that warrants execution.

This is the third explicit declaration of Paul's innocence by a Roman official in the narrative. The tribune Lysias wrote to Felix that Paul was "charged with nothing deserving death or imprisonment" (23:29). Felix found no basis for judgment and delayed indefinitely (24:22-27). Now Festus finds "nothing deserving death." The author is building an accumulating record of Roman judicial findings in Paul's favor.

Yet Festus simultaneously explains why Paul is not simply released: "since he himself appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him" (autou de toutou epikalesamenou ton Sebaston, ekrina pempein). Once the appeal to Caesar has been invoked, the governor no longer has discretion to release the prisoner. The appeal transfers jurisdiction; the case now belongs to the Emperor. Festus presents this as a straightforward procedural consequence: Paul appealed, so Paul must be sent. But the author has shown the reader that the appeal was itself a consequence of Festus's willingness to trade Paul's case for political favor (25:9). The chain of causation runs: Festus wanted to do the Jews a favor, Paul saw the danger, Paul appealed to Caesar, and now Festus must send him. The governor's political maneuvering produced the very outcome that removes the case from his control.

"Yet, I have nothing definite about him to write to my lord. Therefore, I have brought him before you all and especially before you, King Agrippa, so that after the investigation has taken place, I may have something to write" (25:26).

Festus now reveals the practical problem that motivates the hearing. The word asphales ("definite, certain, secure") is the same word the tribune Lysias sought when he could not determine "the certain facts" of Paul's case (21:34; 22:30). Festus needs something concrete to write in the elogium -- the formal statement of charges that must accompany a prisoner being sent to the Emperor for trial. Without a clear statement of charges, the transfer would be procedurally deficient and potentially embarrassing for Festus.

The phrase "my lord" (to kyrio mou) is Festus's reference to the Emperor -- at this time, Nero (reigned 54-68 CE). The title kyrios ("lord") applied to the Emperor was a matter of usage that varied over time. Earlier emperors like Augustus and Tiberius reportedly refused the title (Suetonius, Augustus 53; Tiberius 27), considering it too servile for a princeps among citizens. By the time of Nero, the usage was becoming more common, though not yet the formal imperial title it would become under Domitian. Festus's casual use of "my lord" for the Emperor reflects the evolving practice of his era.

The phrase "especially before you, King Agrippa" (malista epi sou, basileu Agrippa) explains why Agrippa is essential to this hearing. Agrippa is, as Paul will acknowledge in 26:3, "an expert in all customs and questions among the Jews." His custodianship of the temple and his authority to appoint the high priest give him a depth of understanding of Jewish religious affairs that no Roman governor could possess. Festus needs Agrippa's expertise to translate the religious dispute into terms that can be communicated to the Emperor in a legal document.

"For it seems absurd to me in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges against him as well" (25:27).

The word alogon ("absurd, unreasonable" -- literally "without logos," without reason) describes Festus's assessment of his own situation. He is about to send a prisoner to the Emperor without being able to articulate what the prisoner is charged with. The word alogon carries philosophical weight in Greek: to act without logos is to act irrationally, against the ordering principle of reason. Festus, a Roman administrator trained in law and governance, recognizes the irrationality of his position. He has a prisoner whom the Jewish leadership wants dead, whom he has found innocent of any capital crime, who has appealed to Caesar, and whose case he cannot even summarize coherently for the Emperor. The absurdity is total.

The word aitias ("charges, reasons, causes" -- from aitia, "a cause, a charge, a reason") is the technical legal term for the formal statement of charges. Festus cannot formulate the aitiai because the actual dispute -- whether a dead man named Jesus is alive -- is not a category within Roman criminal law. There is no statute under which to charge Paul. The accusations of sedition and temple defilement have not been proven. What remains is a theological disagreement about resurrection, and Festus has no legal language for it.

## The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 25 is organized around three scenes, each involving a different configuration of characters, and each advancing the narrative toward Rome through the intersection of Jewish hostility, Roman political calculation, and Paul's exercise of his legal rights.

The first scene (vv. 1-12) presents the problem and its solution. The problem is the same one that has defined Paul's situation since his arrest in Acts 21: the Jewish leadership wants him dead, and the Roman authorities find no criminal basis for the charges but are unwilling to release him for political reasons. The solution is Paul's appeal to Caesar, which removes the case from the cycle of political favor-trading between governors and the Jewish elite. The appeal is simultaneously an act of self-preservation and, within the author's narrative framework, the mechanism of divine purpose. The Lord declared in 23:11 that Paul must testify in Rome; the appeal to Caesar is how that declaration becomes reality. The author does not present these as competing explanations -- human legal action on one hand, divine purpose on the other -- but as the same event viewed from two angles.

The second scene (vv. 13-22) functions as a summary and transition. Festus retells the case to Agrippa, and in doing so, the author achieves several purposes. First, the retelling allows the reader to see the case through Roman eyes. Festus's summary strips away the theological complexity and reduces the dispute to its simplest form: a disagreement about religion and a claim about a dead man being alive. This outsider perspective highlights what the accumulated narrative of Acts has been building toward: the entire controversy over Paul is ultimately about the resurrection of Jesus. Every charge -- sedition, apostasy from Moses, temple defilement -- is a covering for the real issue, which the accusers cannot articulate in Roman legal terms because it is theological, not criminal.

Second, the retelling reveals Festus's self-serving revision of events. He presents himself to Agrippa as a defender of Roman legal principle (25:16) while omitting his attempt to trade Paul's case for a political favor (25:9). The author has given the reader both versions, allowing us to see the gap between what Festus did and what Festus says he did. This gap characterizes not just Festus but the entire system of provincial Roman justice as the author portrays it: procedurally principled in theory, politically compromised in practice.

Third, the scene introduces Agrippa's interest and expertise as the instrument through which Festus hopes to formulate his report. The hearing that follows in Acts 26 exists not as a trial (Paul has already appealed; no verdict can be rendered) but as an investigation (anakriseos, 25:26) designed to produce a written summary of charges for the Emperor. This is a critical distinction: the hearing before Agrippa has no judicial power. It is an advisory proceeding. The irony is that an advisory hearing with no legal authority will produce the most explicit declaration of Paul's innocence in the entire narrative: "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:32).

The third scene (vv. 23-27) sets the stage for Acts 26 while making a visual argument about power and vulnerability. The author describes the phantasia -- the pomp, the spectacle -- of Agrippa and Bernice's entrance, surrounded by military commanders and civic dignitaries. Into this theater of power, a prisoner is brought. The entire apparatus of provincial authority -- governor, king, military, aristocracy -- assembles to hear one man in chains. The author does not moralize about the contrast, but first-century readers acquainted with Israel's prophetic tradition would recognize the pattern. Joseph stood before Pharaoh (Genesis 41). Daniel stood before Nebuchadnezzar (Daniel 2) and before Belshazzar (Daniel 5). Jeremiah stood before King Zedekiah (Jeremiah 38). In each case, the prisoner or the powerless one held the truth that the powerful needed. Festus has assembled the greatest concentration of authority available to him in Caesarea, and the reason is that he needs Paul -- the prisoner -- to help him understand what to write to the Emperor. The powerful need the prisoner's words because the prisoner possesses something they do not: a coherent account of why he is being prosecuted.

The chapter also continues the pattern of the "favor" motif (charis/charin/charisasthai) that runs from Acts 24 through Acts 25. Felix wanted to do the Jews a favor (24:27); the Jewish leaders requested a favor from Festus (25:3); Festus wanted to grant the Jews a favor (25:9); Festus told Agrippa he refused to hand Paul over as a favor (25:16, using charisasthai); Paul insisted no one has the authority to hand him over as a favor (25:11, using charisasthai). The vocabulary of favor, grace, and gift -- words that in other contexts carry positive connotations -- is here consistently associated with the corruption of justice. The "favor" being exchanged is a human life. The author uses the language of charis ironically: the "grace" being sought and offered is the abandonment of a man to those who want to kill him.

Finally, the chapter demonstrates how the author handles the relationship between human agency and divine direction. No angel appears. No miraculous intervention occurs. No prophetic word is spoken. Yet the chapter is saturated with fulfillment. The Lord told Paul in 23:11 that he must testify in Rome. In chapter 25, that testimony in Rome becomes inevitable through entirely human mechanisms: a new governor's political calculations, the Jewish leadership's persistent hostility, Paul's legal knowledge, and the procedural requirements of Roman criminal law. The author presents a world in which God's declared purposes are accomplished through the ordinary operation of human institutions -- even compromised, self-serving, politically motivated ones. Festus's willingness to trade justice for political favor, which could have destroyed Paul, instead produces the appeal that sends Paul to Rome. The instrument of potential injustice becomes the vehicle of divine purpose. The author does not explain this tension; he narrates it, allowing the reader to observe how the machinery of empire, operated by men pursuing their own interests, carries out the plan that the Lord announced in a nocturnal vision in the barracks of the Antonia fortress.
