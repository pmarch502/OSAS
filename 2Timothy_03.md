# 2 Timothy 3 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**The document's genre and occasion:** This is a letter from Paul to Timothy, written from imprisonment (1:8, 16; 2:9), under the shadow of Paul's approaching death (4:6-8). The letter functions as a final testament -- the passing of responsibility, warning, and encouragement from Paul to his spiritual son and successor. The crisis of false teaching in Ephesus, which prompted 1 Timothy, has worsened. Hymenaeus, who was handed over to Satan in 1 Timothy 1:20, has reappeared in 2 Timothy 2:17 alongside a new companion, Philetus, teaching that the resurrection has already happened and upsetting the faith of some (2:18). The mass desertion Paul reported in 1:15 -- "all who are in Asia turned away from me" -- indicates that the social cost of remaining aligned with Paul and his gospel has become too high for most. Paul writes from isolation, with only Luke remaining with him (4:11), Demas having deserted (4:10), and others dispersed to various locations (4:10-12). The letter is personal, urgent, and suffused with the awareness that Paul's time is short and the work must continue without him.

**What the author was arguing immediately before (2 Timothy 2):** Chapter 2 addressed the practical requirements and theological foundations for Timothy's ministry in a community under threat from false teaching. It opened with the imperative to transmit the gospel through a chain of faithful people (2:1-2), employed three metaphors -- soldier, athlete, farmer -- to describe the character required for faithful service (2:3-7), and grounded everything in the gospel of the risen Davidic Messiah for which Paul suffers in chains (2:8-10). The faithful saying of 2:11-13 established a comprehensive framework of reliability: dying with Christ leads to living with him, enduring leads to reigning with him, denying him leads to being denied by him, and Christ remains faithful to all his stated terms because he cannot deny himself. Paul then identified the specific threat: Hymenaeus and Philetus, whose teaching that the resurrection has already happened spreads like gangrene and upsets the faith of some (2:14-18). Against this threat, God's firm foundation stands, bearing a double seal drawn from the Korah rebellion in Numbers 16: "The Lord knows those who are his" and "Let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity" (2:19). The vessel metaphor of 2:20-21 established that status within the household is not fixed but changeable through cleansing. And the chapter closed with instructions for the Lord's servant to correct opponents with gentleness, in the hope that God might grant them repentance and escape from the devil's snare (2:22-26).

The closing of chapter 2 is critical for understanding chapter 3's opening. Chapter 2 ended on a note of qualified hope: the opponents are captured by the devil, doing his will (2:26), but God "may perhaps" grant them repentance (2:25). The opponents are real, their error is dangerous, their captivity is genuine -- but the door to restoration has not been slammed shut. Chapter 3 will now intensify the description of these opponents, extend their behavior into an eschatological framework, and provide Timothy with the resources to stand against them.

**What the author argues immediately after (2 Timothy 4):** Chapter 4 opens with what may be the most solemn charge in the entire letter: "I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom: preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching" (4:1-2). The charge is eschatological in orientation -- it invokes the judgment of Christ and his kingdom. The reason for the charge follows immediately: "For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions, and will turn away from listening to the truth and wander off into myths" (4:3-4). This prediction directly extends the description of the false teachers in 3:1-9 and 3:13. Timothy is commanded: "As for you, always be sober-minded, endure suffering, do the work of an evangelist, fulfill your ministry" (4:5). Then comes Paul's farewell: "For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing" (4:6-8). Paul's life is the model of what chapter 3 has described: faithful endurance through suffering, grounded in the scriptures, leading to the crown on "that day."

**How chapter 3 connects the two:** Chapter 2 ended by describing the opponents' condition (captured by the devil) and the hope of their recovery (God may grant repentance). Chapter 3 opens by placing these opponents within a broader eschatological framework: the "last days" bring "times of difficulty" (3:1), and the people described in the vice list of 3:2-5 are the same kind of people Paul has been warning about throughout the letter. The movement from chapter 2 to chapter 3 is not a change of subject but an escalation of the same argument. Chapter 2 dealt with specific false teachers (Hymenaeus and Philetus) and their specific error (the resurrection has already happened). Chapter 3 broadens the lens to describe the character type that produces such teachers, locates them within the eschatological pattern of the "last days," compares them to the Egyptian magicians who opposed Moses, and then pivots sharply to Timothy: "You, however" (3:10, sy de). The contrast between the opponents (3:1-9, 13) and Timothy (3:10-12, 14-17) is the chapter's structural backbone. Chapter 3 then provides Timothy with his ultimate resource -- the sacred writings that have been his foundation since childhood (3:15-17) -- which leads directly into the solemn charge of 4:1-2 to preach the word. The chapter is a bridge: it explains why the charge of chapter 4 is so urgent (because the opposition is so dangerous) and equips Timothy for the charge (because Scripture is so sufficient).

## Verses 1-5: The Character of the "Last Days" -- People Within the Community

"But understand this, that in the last days there will come times of difficulty. For people will be lovers of self, lovers of money, proud, arrogant, abusive, disobedient to their parents, ungrateful, unholy, heartless, unappeasable, slanderous, without self-control, brutal, not loving good, treacherous, reckless, swollen with conceit, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God, having the appearance of godliness but denying its power. Avoid such people."

**"But understand this, that in the last days there will come times of difficulty"** (touto de ginōske, hoti en eschatais hēmerais enstēsontai kairoi chalepoi). The imperative ginōske ("know, understand, recognize") commands Timothy to grasp what follows -- not as speculation but as certain knowledge. The demonstrative touto ("this") points forward to the content of the warning.

The phrase en eschatais hēmerais ("in the last days") requires careful attention to its first-century meaning. The expression has deep roots in the LXX. In Isaiah 2:2 (LXX), "in the last days" (en tais eschatais hēmerais) introduces the vision of the mountain of the Lord being established and the nations streaming to it. In Micah 4:1 (LXX), the identical phrase opens the parallel vision. In Hosea 3:5 (LXX), "in the last days" (ep' eschatou tōn hēmerōn) describes Israel returning to seek the Lord their God and David their king. In Daniel 2:28 (LXX, Theodotion), Nebuchadnezzar's dream concerns "what will be in the last days" (en eschatais hēmerais). In Genesis 49:1 (LXX), Jacob gathers his sons to tell them "what will happen to you in the last days" (ep' eschatōn tōn hēmerōn). The phrase in the LXX consistently points to a decisive future period when God's purposes come to their climax -- not necessarily the final moments of cosmic history, but the age of fulfillment toward which Israel's story has been moving.

Crucially, the early communities understood themselves as already living in the last days. In Acts 2:17, Peter quotes Joel 2:28-32 (LXX 3:1-5) and applies it to the present: "In the last days (en tais eschatais hēmerais), God declares, I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh." Peter's quotation reframes Joel's "after these things" (meta tauta) as "in the last days," and declares that what is happening at Pentecost is the fulfillment of that prophecy. The last days have begun. In Hebrews 1:2, God has spoken "in these last days" (ep' eschatou tōn hēmerōn toutōn) through his Son -- the author places the present moment within the last days. In 1 Peter 1:20, Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world but "was made manifest in the last times" (ep' eschatou tōn chronōn) for the sake of believers. In 1 John 2:18, "it is the last hour" (eschatē hōra estin).

Paul's use of "in the last days" here, therefore, does not describe a remote future period that has no bearing on Timothy's present situation. The last days are the present age -- the age inaugurated by Christ's death and resurrection, the age in which the community now lives. The "times of difficulty" (kairoi chalepoi) that Paul describes are not distant predictions but descriptions of what Timothy is already encountering or will shortly encounter. The verb enstēsontai (from enistanai, "to be present, to stand in, to set in") in the future tense can describe what is about to begin or what is already setting in. In 2 Timothy 3:1-5, the vice list that follows describes people Paul tells Timothy to "avoid" (3:5) -- a command that only makes sense if such people are present or imminent, not centuries away. The connection to the false teachers of chapter 2 is immediate: these are not abstract future figures but the very kind of people Timothy is already dealing with.

The noun kairos ("time, season, decisive moment, opportunity") is distinct from chronos ("duration, period, span of time"). A kairos is a significant moment, a time charged with meaning and consequence. The adjective chalepos ("difficult, hard, harsh, fierce, dangerous") appears only here and in Matthew 8:28 in the New Testament, where it describes the Gadarene demoniacs as "so fierce that no one could pass that way." The word conveys genuine danger -- these are not merely inconvenient times but genuinely threatening ones.

**The vice list (vv. 2-4):** "For people will be lovers of self, lovers of money, proud, arrogant, abusive, disobedient to their parents, ungrateful, unholy, heartless, unappeasable, slanderous, without self-control, brutal, not loving good, treacherous, reckless, swollen with conceit, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God."

The conjunction gar ("for") explains why the times will be difficult: they will be difficult because of the character of the people who populate them. The subject is hoi anthrōpoi ("the people, the human beings") -- not a specific named group but a character type. The list contains nineteen descriptors, arranged with rhetorical care. The list opens with two compound words beginning with phil- ("lover of"): philautoi ("lovers of self") and philargyroi ("lovers of money"). It closes with another phil- compound: philēdonoi ("lovers of pleasure") set in explicit contrast with philotheoi ("lovers of God"). The framing is architectural: the list begins and ends with what people love, and the wrong loves (self, money, pleasure) have displaced the right love (God). The entire vice list is a catalog of what happens when the orientation of love is disordered.

The individual terms:

(1) **Philautoi** ("lovers of self") -- self-love as the organizing principle of life, the orientation that makes the self the center around which everything else revolves. This is the root from which the other vices grow.

(2) **Philargyroi** ("lovers of money, avaricious") -- the same word appeared in 1 Timothy 6:10 in its noun form (philargyria, "love of money"), where Paul declared it "a root of all kinds of evils" through which "some have wandered away from the faith." The connection to the false teachers of 1 Timothy, who saw godliness as a "means of gain" (1 Tim 6:5), is direct.

(3) **Alazonēs** ("boasters, braggarts, pretentious people") -- in Romans 1:30, Paul included alazōn in the vice list describing humanity that has exchanged God's truth for a lie. The alazon is one who claims more than is warranted, who inflates the self beyond reality.

(4) **Hyperēphanoi** ("proud, arrogant, haughty") -- in the LXX, this word consistently describes those who set themselves against God. In Proverbs 3:34 (LXX): "The Lord opposes the arrogant (hyperēphanois) but gives grace to the humble." James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 both quote this proverb. In Luke 1:51, Mary sings that God "has scattered the proud (hyperēphanous) in the thoughts of their hearts." The proud person is not merely socially obnoxious but positioned against God's own order.

(5) **Blasphēmoi** ("blasphemous, revilers, slanderers") -- the word can refer to blasphemy against God or abusive speech against other people. In 1 Timothy 1:13, Paul described himself as formerly a "blasphemer" (blasphēmon), and in 1 Timothy 1:20, Hymenaeus and Alexander were handed over to Satan "that they may learn not to blaspheme" (mē blasphēmein). The reappearance of this word ties the vice list to the specific false teachers of the letter.

(6) **Goneusin apeitheis** ("disobedient to parents") -- the breakdown of the household order. In the LXX, the command to honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16) is foundational. In Deuteronomy 21:18-21, the "stubborn and rebellious son" who will not obey his parents is subject to severe judgment. In Romans 1:30, disobedience to parents appears in the list of behaviors characterizing humanity that has turned from God. The inclusion of this domestic vice among theological and social ones indicates that the corruption is comprehensive -- it penetrates even the most basic human relationships.

(7) **Acharistoi** ("ungrateful, thankless") -- the failure to acknowledge what one has received. Ingratitude is the refusal to recognize dependence, the denial of the gift-nature of existence. In Luke 6:35, God is "kind to the ungrateful (acharistous) and the evil." In Romans 1:21, humanity "did not honor God or give thanks (ēucharistēsan) to him" -- the failure of gratitude is near the beginning of the descent into sin.

(8) **Anosioi** ("unholy, impious") -- the alpha-privative of hosios ("holy, devout, pious"). In 1 Timothy 1:9, the anosioi appeared in the list of those for whom the law was given: "the lawless and disobedient, the ungodly and sinners, the unholy and profane." The repetition across both letters binds the vice lists together.

(9) **Astorgoi** ("heartless, without natural affection") -- the alpha-privative of storge ("natural family affection"). The word describes the absence of the most basic human bond: the love between parents and children, between family members. It appeared in Romans 1:31 in the same position in Paul's list there. The breakdown extends beyond the household order of disobedience to parents to the utter absence of the affection that makes family life possible.

(10) **Aspondoi** ("unappeasable, implacable, irreconcilable") -- the alpha-privative of spondē ("a libation poured to ratify a treaty, a truce"). The aspondos is one who refuses to make peace, who will not enter into treaty, who remains in permanent hostility. No reconciliation is possible with such a person because they refuse the very concept of reconciliation.

(11) **Diaboloi** ("slanderers, false accusers") -- the plural of diabolos. The word means literally "one who throws across" (dia + ballein) -- one who throws accusations, who creates division through false charges. In 1 Timothy 3:11, the deacons' wives (or female deacons) must not be diabolous ("slanderers"). The word is also the title used for the devil himself (ho diabolos, "the slanderer, the accuser"), and its use here for human beings who engage in the devil's characteristic activity is pointed: these people do the devil's work of accusation and division.

(12) **Akrateis** ("without self-control, intemperate") -- the alpha-privative of kratos ("strength, mastery, control"). The akratēs is one who lacks mastery over impulses, who is ruled by desires rather than ruling them. This stands in direct contrast to the sōphronismos ("self-discipline, sound-mindedness") that God's Spirit produces (1:7).

(13) **Anēmeroi** ("brutal, savage, untamed") -- the alpha-privative of hēmeros ("tame, gentle, civilized"). The word describes behavior that has crossed the line from human conduct into something feral. It appears only here in the New Testament.

(14) **Aphilagathoi** ("not loving good, hostile to goodness") -- the alpha-privative of philagathos ("lover of good"). In Titus 1:8, the overseer must be philagathos ("a lover of what is good"). The people described here are the exact opposite of what community leadership requires.

(15) **Prodotai** ("traitors, betrayers") -- from prodidonai ("to give over beforehand, to betray"). In Luke 6:16, Judas Iscariot is called prodotēs ("traitor"). In Acts 7:52, Stephen accuses the council: "you have now betrayed (prodotai) and murdered" the Righteous One. The word carries the weight of deliberate treachery -- not accidental failure but purposeful betrayal of trust.

(16) **Propeteis** ("reckless, headlong, rash") -- from propiptein ("to fall forward"). The propetēs rushes forward without thought, acts without consideration, speaks without weighing consequences. In Acts 19:36, the town clerk of Ephesus urges the crowd not to be propeteis ("reckless") -- the same city where Timothy now serves.

(17) **Tetyphōmenoi** ("swollen with conceit, blinded by pride, puffed up") -- the perfect passive participle of typhoun ("to wrap in smoke, to blind with conceit, to puff up"). The same verb appeared in 1 Timothy 3:6, where a new convert might become tetyphōtheis ("puffed up with conceit") and fall into the judgment of the devil. In 1 Timothy 6:4, the false teacher is tetyphōtai ("puffed up with conceit"), understanding nothing. The repetition is deliberate: the vice list describes the same character trait that has already been identified as the hallmark of the false teachers in both letters.

(18-19) **Philēdonoi mallon ē philotheoi** ("lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God") -- the final pair creates the climactic contrast. The comparative mallon ē ("rather than, more than") sets the two loves in opposition: pleasure versus God. These are not people who have no religious identity at all; they are people whose love for pleasure has displaced their love for God. The fact that they are "lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God" presupposes that they are in a position where love of God might be expected of them. They are not pagans who have never heard; they are people within or adjacent to the community who have chosen the wrong love.

**"Having the appearance of godliness but denying its power"** (echontes morphōsin eusebeias tēn de dynamin autēs ērnēmenoi). This is the defining characteristic that locates these people within the community, not outside it. The noun morphōsis ("outward form, appearance, semblance, embodiment") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Romans 2:20, where Paul describes the Jewish teacher who has "the embodiment of knowledge and truth" (tēn morphōsin tēs gnōseōs kai tēs alētheias) in the law -- the external structure that contains the reality. Here the morphōsis is of eusebeia ("godliness, piety, proper reverence toward God"), the same word that has been a key term throughout both letters. In 1 Timothy 2:2, the community was to pray for a peaceful life so they could live in "godliness" (eusebeia). In 1 Timothy 3:16, the "mystery of godliness" (to tēs eusebeias mystērion) was confessed in a hymnic fragment about Christ. In 1 Timothy 4:7-8, Timothy was to train himself "for godliness" (pros eusebeian), which "holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come." In 1 Timothy 6:3, sound teaching is teaching that "accords with godliness" (kata eusebeian). In 1 Timothy 6:5-6, the false teachers imagined godliness to be a "means of gain" (porismos), while genuine "godliness with contentment is great gain."

These people possess the morphōsis -- the external form, the visible shape, the religious surface -- of eusebeia. They look godly. They participate in the community's life. They have the outward markers of devotion. But they have denied (ērnēmenoi, perfect middle participle of arneisthai, "to deny, to disown, to repudiate") its power (dynamin). The perfect tense indicates a settled, completed act with ongoing results: they denied the power at some point and continue in that denial. The verb arneisthai is the same verb used in the faithful saying of 2:12-13: "if we deny (arnesometha) him, he also will deny us." The echo is structural: denying the power of godliness is a form of denying Christ himself, because the power of godliness is the dynamis that God gives (1:7, "God gave us a spirit not of cowardice but of power [dynameōs]"). The power (dynamis) they deny is the same power Paul attributed to God's Spirit in 1:7 -- the power that enables courage, self-discipline, and faithfulness under pressure. To deny this power while maintaining the outward form is to retain religion as a social identity while gutting it of its transformative reality.

**"Avoid such people"** (kai toutous apotrepou). The verb apotrepesthai ("to turn away from, to avoid, to shun") in the middle voice means to turn oneself away. The present imperative commands ongoing practice: keep avoiding them, make it habitual. The demonstrative toutous ("these people") refers to the entire list just described. The command to avoid them presupposes that they are present and accessible -- one cannot be told to avoid people who do not yet exist. This confirms that the "last days" are not a remote future but a present reality in which Timothy must make practical decisions about association.

The command connects to 2:19, where the second seal on God's foundation read: "Let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity." The same logic operates here: those who belong to God must separate from those who corrupt the community. The command also connects to 2:22, where Timothy was told to flee youthful passions and pursue righteousness "along with those who call on the Lord from a pure heart." The community Timothy is to cultivate is defined both positively (the pure-hearted) and negatively (avoiding those with the form but not the power of godliness).

## Verses 6-9: The Infiltrators and Their Precedent -- Jannes and Jambres

"For among them are those who creep into households and capture weak women, burdened with sins and led astray by various passions, always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth. Just as Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses, so these people also oppose the truth, people corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith. But they will not get very far, for their folly will be plain to all, as was that of those two."

**"For among them are those who creep into households"** (ek toutōn gar eisin hoi endynontes eis tas oikias). The conjunction gar ("for") explains what makes these people particularly dangerous: their method is infiltration. The preposition ek ("out of, from among") identifies the infiltrators as a subset of the people described in the vice list -- not all of them creep into households, but this is the activity of some among them. The verb endynein ("to creep in, to worm one's way in, to enter by stealth") appears only here in the New Testament. The word connotes covert, surreptitious entry -- not a bold frontal approach but a worming inward through deception. The noun oikia ("house, household") describes the domestic setting. In the first-century world, the household was the basic unit of community life. The ekklēsia met in houses (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2). To creep into households was to infiltrate the very spaces where the community gathered, taught, and worshiped.

**"And capture weak women"** (kai aichmalōtizontes gynaikaria). The verb aichmalōtizein ("to take captive, to capture as a prisoner of war, to lead into captivity") is a military term. The verb appears in Luke 21:24 for the Jews being "led captive" (aichmalōtisthēsontai) among all nations, and in Romans 7:23 for the law of sin "making me captive" (aichmalōtizonta me). In 2 Corinthians 10:5, Paul uses the same verb positively: "taking every thought captive (aichmalōtizontes) to obey Christ." The metaphor is imprisonment: the infiltrators take captives.

The noun gynaikarion (diminutive of gynē, "woman") is literally "little women" or "weak women." The diminutive form does not necessarily carry contempt for women as such; it describes vulnerability -- women who are susceptible because of their specific condition, which is immediately specified. The description that follows explains why these particular women are vulnerable, not why women in general are inherently weak.

**"Burdened with sins and led astray by various passions"** (sesōreumena hamartiais, agomena epithymiais poikilais). Two participles describe the condition that creates the vulnerability. First, sesōreumena (from sōreuein, "to heap up, to pile on, to load") -- they are "loaded up" with sins (hamartiais). The perfect passive participle indicates a cumulative condition: sins have been piled on them over time and they carry the weight. Second, agomena (from agein, "to lead, to drive, to conduct") -- they are "being led" by various (poikilais, "manifold, diverse, variegated") desires (epithymiais). The present passive participle describes an ongoing condition: they are continually driven by shifting desires. The combination of guilt (loaded with sins) and instability (driven by desires) makes them susceptible to teachers who promise new revelation, secret knowledge, or alternative paths to resolution. The false teachers exploit precisely this vulnerability.

**"Always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth"** (pantote manthanonta kai mēdepote eis epignōsin alētheias elthein dynamena). This is one of the chapter's most penetrating descriptions. The adverb pantote ("always, at all times") paired with mēdepote ("never, at no time") creates a devastating contrast: perpetual motion without arrival. The verb manthanein ("to learn, to study, to be taught") in the present participle describes ongoing, continuous learning. These captive women are not indifferent to religious instruction; they are voracious consumers of it. They are always in the posture of learning. But the learning never produces the result it should.

The noun epignōsis ("full knowledge, deep recognition, thorough understanding") is the strengthened form of gnosis ("knowledge"). In 2:25, Paul expressed the hope that God might grant the opponents "repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth" (metanoian eis epignōsin alētheias). The identical phrase here -- eis epignōsin alētheias -- describes the destination these women can never reach. The contrast with 2:25 is structural: the opponents might receive repentance leading to knowledge of the truth, but the women they have captured are stuck in perpetual learning that never arrives at that knowledge. The problem is not intellectual capacity but spiritual orientation: driven by desires rather than by genuine seeking, loaded with unresolved guilt rather than cleansed by repentance, they mistake the accumulation of information for the attainment of truth.

The verb dynamena (from dynasthai, "to be able, to have the capacity") states inability: they are "not able" to arrive. The inability is not constitutional -- it is not that God made them incapable -- but conditional: their current state of being burdened with sins and led by desires renders them unable to reach the truth. The possibility of change is implicit: if the burden were lifted and the desires reoriented, the capacity to arrive might be restored. This connects to the vessel metaphor of 2:20-21, where cleansing from what is dishonorable changes a vessel's status.

**"Just as Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses"** (hon tropon de Iannēs kai Iambrēs antestēsan Mōysei). The comparative phrase hon tropon ("in the way that, just as") establishes a parallel between the current opponents and a specific pair of opponents from Israel's history. The names Jannes and Jambres do not appear in the canonical Hebrew scriptures or the LXX text of Exodus. They come from Jewish tradition that identified the Egyptian magicians who opposed Moses in Exodus 7:11, 22; 8:7, 18-19; 9:11. The names were widely known in Second Temple Judaism: they appear in the Damascus Document (CD 5:17-19), which warns that "Jannes and his brother" were raised up by Belial to oppose Moses and Aaron. They appear in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Exodus 1:15; 7:11. Pliny the Elder (Natural History 30.2.11) mentions "Iannes" as a famous magician. The tradition was well established and would have been recognized by Timothy and by any Jewish or Jewish-educated hearer.

The verb anthistanai ("to oppose, to resist, to set oneself against, to withstand") in the aorist (antestēsan) describes a completed act of opposition. The dative Mōysei ("Moses") identifies the object of opposition. The parallel is precise: as Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses -- God's authorized agent for delivering Israel -- so these false teachers oppose the truth. The comparison carries several implications drawn from the Exodus narrative:

(1) Jannes and Jambres initially appeared to replicate what Moses did. In Exodus 7:11-12, Pharaoh's magicians "did the same things by their secret arts" -- they turned their staffs into snakes, turned water to blood, and produced frogs. Their imitation was real enough to give Pharaoh a reason to dismiss Moses' authority. The false teachers similarly produce a convincing imitation of genuine teaching -- they have the "appearance of godliness" (3:5) and engage in constant learning activity (3:7).

(2) The imitation had limits. In Exodus 8:18-19, the magicians could not produce gnats: "the magicians tried by their secret arts to produce gnats, but they could not." They confessed to Pharaoh: "This is the finger of God." The imitation broke down when the reality exceeded the capacity of the counterfeit. Paul will make the same point in verse 9: the false teachers "will not get very far."

(3) The magicians suffered judgment. In Exodus 9:11, "the magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils, for the boils came upon the magicians as well as upon all the Egyptians." The opponents of God's authorized messenger were struck with the same plagues as the rest of the disobedient. The implication is that the false teachers who oppose the truth face a similar fate.

**"So these people also oppose the truth"** (houtōs kai houtoi anthistantai tē alētheia). The present tense anthistantai ("are opposing") describes ongoing, current activity -- these people are presently in active opposition to the truth. The dative tē alētheia ("the truth") identifies what they resist: not merely Paul's authority or Timothy's teaching, but the truth itself. In 2:15, Timothy was to "rightly handle the word of truth" (ton logon tēs alētheias). In 2:18, Hymenaeus and Philetus had "swerved from the truth" (peri tēn alētheian ēstochēsan). In 2:25, the hope was repentance leading to "knowledge of the truth" (epignōsin alētheias). The truth is the consistent reference point throughout the letter; the false teachers are consistently defined by their deviation from it.

**"People corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith"** (anthrōpoi katephtharmenoi ton noun, adokimoi peri tēn pistin). Two descriptions characterize these opponents:

First, katephtharmenoi ton noun ("corrupted in mind, ruined in their thinking"). The verb kataphtheirein ("to corrupt thoroughly, to destroy, to ruin") in the perfect passive participle describes a completed state of corruption -- their minds (nous, "mind, understanding, capacity for rational thought") have been corrupted and remain corrupted. In 1 Timothy 6:5, the same concept appeared with a different compound: diephtharmenōn ton noun ("people of corrupted mind"), describing the false teachers who imagine godliness to be a means of gain. The corruption is intellectual and moral simultaneously: these people cannot think straight about the truth because their mental capacity has been ruined.

Second, adokimoi peri tēn pistin ("disqualified regarding the faith, failing the test concerning the faith"). The adjective adokimos ("unapproved, failing the test, disqualified, counterfeit") is the exact opposite of dokimos ("approved, tested, proven genuine") from 2:15, where Timothy was to present himself to God as one "approved" (dokimon). The word was used for metals that failed the assay, for coins that proved counterfeit. These people have been tested regarding the faith (peri tēn pistin, "concerning the faith, in the area of the faith") and have failed. The faith here is not merely personal trust but the faith -- the body of apostolic teaching, the deposit that Timothy guards (1:14). When measured against this standard, the false teachers are found to be counterfeit.

The contrast with Timothy is precise and deliberate: Timothy is to be dokimos (2:15); these people are adokimoi (3:8). Timothy rightly handles the word of truth (2:15); these people oppose the truth (3:8). Timothy has a sound mind through God's Spirit of sōphronismos (1:7); these people have corrupted minds (3:8). The antithesis runs throughout the letter.

**"But they will not get very far, for their folly will be plain to all, as was that of those two"** (all' ou prokopsousin epi pleion, hē gar anoia autōn ekdēlos estai pasin, hōs kai hē ekeinōn egeneto). The verb prokoptein ("to advance, to make progress, to go forward") appeared in 2:16 to describe the "progress" of the empty talkers -- their progress in ungodliness. Here the same verb is negated: they will not advance further (ou prokopsousin epi pleion). The irony is that in 3:13, Paul will say that "evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse" (prokopsousin epi to cheiron) -- they will "advance" in the wrong direction. The two uses of prokoptein in this chapter create a paradox: the false teachers will not advance in their deceptive project (v. 9), yet they will advance in their own wickedness (v. 13). Their influence will be checked, but their personal corruption will continue.

The reason their project will fail is that their anoia ("folly, mindlessness, lack of understanding") will become ekdēlos ("clearly visible, evident to all, perfectly obvious") to all (pasin). The noun anoia is composed of the alpha-privative + nous ("mind") -- it means "mindlessness, irrationality." This is the end point of the mental corruption described above: their minds, having been corrupted (katephtharmenoi ton noun), produce folly (anoia) that eventually becomes visible to everyone.

The comparison hōs kai hē ekeinōn egeneto ("as was that of those two") returns to Jannes and Jambres. Their folly became plain -- when they could not produce gnats (Exodus 8:18-19), when boils covered them (Exodus 9:11), when the plagues distinguished between Israel and Egypt in ways their magic could not replicate. The tradition that Jannes and Jambres were eventually exposed and destroyed was well known. Paul assures Timothy that the same exposure will occur with the current opponents: time will reveal the counterfeit for what it is.

## Verses 10-12: The Contrast -- Timothy's Following of Paul

"You, however, have followed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life, my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness, my persecutions and sufferings that happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra -- which persecutions I endured, yet from them all the Lord rescued me. Indeed, all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted."

**"You, however, have followed my teaching"** (sy de parēkolouthēsas mou tē didaskalia). The pronoun sy ("you," emphatic, placed first) combined with the adversative particle de ("however, but, on the other hand") creates the same contrastive structure as 2:1 (sy oun, "you then") -- Timothy is set in sharp contrast with the people just described. They are lovers of self; Timothy follows Paul. They have the form of godliness but deny its power; Timothy has observed Paul's teaching, conduct, and suffering firsthand.

The verb parakolouthein ("to follow closely, to accompany, to trace carefully, to follow as a disciple") means more than casual acquaintance. The word describes someone who has walked alongside another person closely enough to have observed every aspect of their life. In Luke 1:3, the author uses the same verb to describe having "followed all things closely" (parēkolouthēkoti) -- careful, first-hand investigation. Timothy has not merely heard Paul's teaching from a distance; he has accompanied Paul, observed his life, and internalized his example.

What Timothy has followed is presented as a list of nine items, all governed by the possessive mou ("my, of me"). Each item is an aspect of Paul's life that Timothy has had opportunity to observe:

(1) **Didaskalia** ("teaching, instruction, doctrine") -- what Paul taught. This is the content of the deposit (1:14; 2:2), the pattern of sound words (1:13), the word of truth (2:15). Timothy has followed Paul's teaching not in the sense of being a student in a classroom but in the sense of being an apprentice who has learned by proximity.

(2) **Agōgē** ("conduct, way of life, manner of living") -- how Paul behaved. The word appears only here in the New Testament. It describes the total pattern of someone's life -- their habits, decisions, relationships, daily behavior. Paul's teaching was authenticated by his conduct; Timothy observed both.

(3) **Prothesis** ("purpose, aim, intention, resolve") -- what Paul was aiming at. The same word appeared in 1:9 for God's own "purpose" (prothesin) in saving and calling. Paul has a purpose that governs his life, and Timothy has observed it: the faithful transmission of the gospel, the building of communities, the service of the elect.

(4) **Pistis** ("faith, trust, faithfulness, fidelity") -- Paul's personal trust in God and his faithfulness to the commission. Timothy has seen Paul believe under pressure, maintain trust when circumstances argued against it.

(5) **Makrothymia** ("patience, long-suffering, forbearance") -- the capacity to bear with people and situations over extended periods without losing composure. The word describes the patience of God himself (Romans 2:4; 9:22) and is listed as a fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22).

(6) **Agapē** ("love") -- the same love that is the aim of sound teaching (1 Tim 1:5), part of the spirit God gives (1:7), and the pursuit Timothy was commanded to follow (2:22). Timothy has observed Paul loving people -- even difficult people, even opponents.

(7) **Hypomonē** ("steadfastness, endurance, perseverance") -- the same word used in the faithful saying of 2:12 (ei hypomenomen, "if we endure"). Paul does not merely commend endurance; he practices it, and Timothy has watched him practice it.

(8) **Diōgmois** ("persecutions") -- the external attacks Paul has suffered for the gospel's sake.

(9) **Pathēmasin** ("sufferings") -- the broader category of what Paul has experienced, encompassing but not limited to the persecutions.

The list moves from the interior (teaching, conduct, purpose, faith) through relational qualities (patience, love, steadfastness) to external experiences (persecutions, sufferings). Timothy has observed the whole person -- not just Paul's public ministry but his private character, not just his triumphs but his sufferings.

**"That happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra"** (hoia moi egeneto en Antiocheia, en Ikoniō, en Lystrois). The relative pronoun hoia ("such as, the kind that") introduces specific examples. Three cities are named, and all three belong to Paul's first missionary journey as recorded in Acts 13-14:

(1) **Antioch** (of Pisidia, not the Antioch of Syria) -- Acts 13:50 records that in Pisidian Antioch, "the Jews incited the devout women of high standing and the leading men of the city, stirred up persecution (diōgmon) against Paul and Barnabas, and drove them out of their district."

(2) **Iconium** -- Acts 14:1-6 describes how in Iconium, "an attempt was made by both Gentiles and Jews, with their rulers, to mistreat them and to stone them," and Paul and Barnabas fled.

(3) **Lystra** -- Acts 14:19-20 records that in Lystra, "Jews came from Antioch and Iconium, and having persuaded the crowds, they stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing him to be dead."

The significance of these three cities for Timothy is personal and geographic. According to Acts 16:1, Timothy was from Lystra (or Derbe, the next city in the sequence). The persecutions Paul experienced at Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra were the persecutions that occurred in Timothy's home region, the events that would have been local history for Timothy and his family. When Paul says "you have followed my persecutions and sufferings that happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra," he is reminding Timothy of events that Timothy's own community witnessed -- events that occurred before or around the time Timothy joined Paul's mission. Timothy's grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (1:5) may have been among those who saw Paul stoned and dragged out of Lystra, supposing him dead. Timothy's decision to follow Paul (Acts 16:3) was made with full knowledge of what that following would cost -- he had seen the cost with his own eyes.

**"Which persecutions I endured, yet from them all the Lord rescued me"** (hoious diōgmous hypēnegka, kai ek pantōn me errysato ho kyrios). The verb hypopherein ("to bear, to endure, to undergo") describes carrying a weight underneath -- sustaining it, not collapsing under it. Paul bore the persecutions; they did not break him. The verb rhyesthai ("to rescue, to deliver, to save from danger") in the aorist (errysato) describes specific, completed acts of deliverance. The agent of rescue is ho kyrios ("the Lord").

The language echoes the Psalms. Psalm 34:19 (LXX 33:20): "Many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the Lord delivers him out of them all" (ek pasōn autōn rhysetai auton ho kyrios). The verbal parallel is nearly exact. Paul's experience of deliverance from persecution is presented as the fulfillment of the psalmist's confidence: the Lord delivers the righteous from their afflictions. The statement does not mean that Paul was never harmed (he was stoned and left for dead at Lystra); it means that the Lord brought him through every trial without the destruction of his mission or his faith. The rescue is comprehensive -- ek pantōn ("from all of them") -- not selective.

**"Indeed, all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted"** (kai pantes de hoi thelontes eusebōs zēn en Christō Iēsou diōchthēsontai). The particle kai... de ("and indeed, yes and") introduces a generalizing statement. Paul moves from his own specific experience to a universal principle. The scope is stated without qualification: pantes ("all") who possess a particular desire will face a particular outcome.

The desire is specified: hoi thelontes eusebōs zēn en Christō Iēsou -- "those who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus." The verb thelein ("to will, to wish, to desire, to purpose") describes not a passing inclination but a settled intention. The adverb eusebōs ("godly, piously, with proper reverence") is the adverbial form of eusebeia, the key term discussed above. The phrase en Christō Iēsou ("in Christ Jesus") specifies the sphere in which this godly living occurs -- not generic piety but godliness rooted in and expressed through Christ.

The outcome is stated as a future certainty: diōchthēsontai ("they will be persecuted"). The passive voice leaves the agent of persecution unspecified -- it will come from various sources. The statement is absolute: all who desire this, will experience this. There are no exceptions. Godly living in Christ Jesus does not lead to social acceptance; it leads to persecution.

This verse connects directly to the preceding description of the false teachers. Those who have the "appearance of godliness but deny its power" (3:5) escape persecution precisely because they have hollowed out their godliness. Their form without power does not threaten the world's arrangements. But genuine godliness -- godliness with power, the kind animated by the Spirit of power, love, and self-discipline (1:7) -- provokes resistance. The false teachers' avoidance of persecution is itself evidence of their inauthenticity: if all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will be persecuted, then those who are not persecuted have either not desired to live godly or are not doing so in Christ Jesus.

## Verse 13: The Trajectory of Deception

"While evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse, deceiving and being deceived."

**"While evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse"** (ponēroi de anthrōpoi kai goētes prokopsousin epi to cheiron). The adversative de ("but, while, on the other hand") sets the trajectory of the evil against the trajectory of the faithful. The adjective ponēros ("evil, wicked, bad, malicious") describes moral corruption, not merely error. The noun goēs ("impostor, sorcerer, charlatan, juggler") appears only here in the New Testament. In classical Greek, the goēs was originally a sorcerer or enchanter -- one who used incantations and spells. By the first century, the word had broadened to mean a deceiver, a fraud, someone who uses trickery to mislead. The connection to the Jannes and Jambres comparison is deliberate: Jannes and Jambres were the paradigmatic goētes -- sorcerers who used their craft to oppose God's truth. The current opponents are their successors.

The verb prokoptein ("to advance, to make progress") appeared in 2:16 for the false teachers' "progress" toward ungodliness, and in 3:9 for their limited advance (they will not get very far). Here the paradox is stated explicitly: they will advance (prokopsousin), but their advance is epi to cheiron ("toward the worse, from bad to worse"). Their progress is deterioration. The more they advance, the worse they become. This is the opposite of the genuine progress (prokopē) that Paul hoped for in the communities (cf. Philippians 1:25, "your progress and joy in the faith"). The false teachers' progress is regress -- a deepening of corruption, not an ascent toward truth.

**"Deceiving and being deceived"** (planōntes kai planōmenoi). The verb planein ("to lead astray, to deceive, to cause to wander") appears in both the active participle (planōntes, "deceiving, leading others astray") and the passive participle (planōmenoi, "being deceived, being led astray themselves"). The double use creates a picture of mutual and self-reinforcing deception. The false teachers are not merely cynical manipulators who know the truth and deliberately hide it; they are themselves deceived. They lead others astray because they have themselves been led astray. The deception is both outward-directed (they deceive others) and inward-experienced (they are themselves in the grip of deception). This connects to 2:26, where the opponents were described as "captured by the devil to do his will" -- they are instruments of a deception larger than themselves.

The combination of active and passive deception also explains why the women they capture are "always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (3:7): the teachers themselves do not possess the truth, so no amount of learning from them can produce it in their students. The blind lead the blind, and both end in the ditch (cf. Matthew 15:14; Luke 6:39).

## Verses 14-15: Timothy's Foundation -- What He Learned and From Whom

"But as for you, continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it, and how from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus."

**"But as for you, continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed"** (sy de mene en hois emathes kai epistōthēs). The pronoun sy ("you," emphatic) combined with the adversative de ("but, however") creates the second major contrast of the chapter (the first was 3:10, sy de parēkolouthēsas). The evil and the impostors advance from bad to worse (3:13); Timothy, by contrast, is to continue (mene, present imperative of menein, "to remain, to abide, to stay, to continue, to stand firm"). The verb menein is one of the most important words in the New Testament for the concept of perseverance. In John 15:4-7, Jesus commands: "Abide (meinate) in me, and I in you... If you abide in me, and my words abide in you..." In 1 John 2:24, "Let what you heard from the beginning abide in you." In Hebrews 13:1, "Let brotherly love continue (menetō)." The verb describes remaining in a position already occupied, not arriving at a new position. Timothy is not told to discover something new but to stay where he is.

What Timothy is to continue in is specified by two verbs: emathes ("you learned," from manthanein, "to learn") and epistōthēs ("you were firmly convinced, you were assured," from pistousthai, "to be made faithful, to be confirmed, to be made certain"). The first verb describes the reception of information; the second describes the inner conviction that the information is true. Timothy both learned and was persuaded. The two verbs together describe a faith that is both informed and convicted -- not ignorant credulity but knowledge accompanied by settled confidence.

**"Knowing from whom you learned it"** (eidōs para tinōn emathes). The participle eidōs ("knowing," from oida, "to know with certainty") modifies the imperative mene: Timothy is to continue in what he learned, knowing from whom he learned it. The preposition para ("from, from beside") with the genitive tinōn ("whom") identifies the source of Timothy's learning. The plural tinōn ("from whom," plural) indicates multiple sources. Who are these sources?

In the immediate context, two groups have been identified: (1) Paul himself, from whom Timothy has "followed my teaching" (3:10) and who transmitted the deposit (1:13-14; 2:2); (2) Timothy's grandmother Lois and mother Eunice, in whom sincere faith first dwelt (1:5). The next verse will identify the childhood instruction Timothy received, pointing to the maternal and grandmaternal influence. The plural "from whom" thus encompasses both family and apostolic instruction -- the two channels through which Timothy received his formation.

The emphasis on source is deliberate and strategic. In a community where false teachers are active, the question "from whom did you learn?" becomes a test of authenticity. The false teachers' doctrine comes from corrupted minds (3:8); Timothy's learning comes from Paul, from Lois, from Eunice -- from sources whose faithfulness has been demonstrated. The chain of transmission that 2:2 described (Paul to Timothy to faithful people to others) has its roots here: the reliability of the source determines the reliability of the teaching.

**"And how from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings"** (kai hoti apo brephous ta hiera grammata oidas). The conjunction kai hoti ("and that") introduces a further specification of what Timothy knows. The noun brephos ("infant, baby, small child") with the preposition apo ("from") identifies the starting point: from infancy, from the earliest possible age, Timothy has known the sacred writings. The phrase ta hiera grammata ("the sacred writings, the holy letters") is a standard designation for the Jewish scriptures -- what we would call the Hebrew Bible or the Old Testament, in their Greek translation (the LXX). The adjective hieros ("sacred, holy, devoted to the gods") marks these writings as belonging to the divine sphere. The noun gramma ("letter, writing, document") in the plural refers to the written texts themselves.

Timothy's acquaintance with the scriptures began in infancy. This confirms the picture from 1:5: his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice, Jewish women of faith, raised him in the knowledge of Israel's scriptures. Acts 16:1 confirms that Timothy's mother was a Jewish believer; his father was Greek. The household was mixed, but the maternal instruction was Jewish, and it included thorough grounding in the sacred writings. This childhood formation is now invoked as the foundation that will sustain Timothy against the false teachers. They are "always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (3:7); Timothy has known the truth-bearing writings since he was an infant.

**"Which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus"** (ta dynamena se sophisai eis sōtērian dia pisteōs tēs en Christō Iēsou). The relative pronoun ta ("which") refers to the sacred writings. Their capacity is stated: dynamena ("being able, having the power to") -- the scriptures possess inherent capacity. The verb sophizein ("to make wise, to instruct, to give wisdom") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in 2 Peter 1:16, where Peter distinguishes true testimony from "cleverly devised myths" (sesophismenois mythois). In the LXX, the word family is prominent: Psalm 19:7 (LXX 18:8), "the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise (sophizousa) the simple." Psalm 119:98 (LXX 118:98), "Your commandment makes me wiser (esophisas me) than my enemies." The sacred writings make Timothy wise -- not merely informed but wise, possessing the practical discernment that comes from understanding God's ways.

The wisdom the scriptures produce has a specific direction: eis sōtērian ("for salvation, toward salvation, unto salvation"). The preposition eis ("into, toward, for the purpose of") indicates the goal toward which the wisdom moves. The scriptures make Timothy wise in a way that leads to salvation. The noun sōtēria ("salvation, deliverance, rescue") in the LXX consistently refers to God's acts of rescue -- from enemies, from exile, from death (cf. the discussion at 2:10). Here the salvation is not limited to a single moment but describes the comprehensive deliverance that God accomplishes -- the "salvation with eternal glory" of 2:10 toward which the elect are moving.

The means by which the scriptures' wisdom leads to salvation is specified: dia pisteōs tēs en Christō Iēsou ("through faith that is in Christ Jesus"). The preposition dia ("through, by means of") identifies faith as the channel. The faith is located en Christō Iēsou ("in Christ Jesus") -- it is not generic religious faith but specific trust in and fidelity to Christ Jesus. The structure of the verse is layered: the sacred writings produce wisdom, the wisdom is directed toward salvation, and the salvation is accessed through faith in Christ Jesus. The scriptures do not save automatically; they save by producing wisdom that leads to a faith that is in Christ.

This verse makes an extraordinary claim about the Jewish scriptures -- written centuries before Christ's incarnation -- and their relationship to Christ. The sacred writings that Timothy's grandmother taught him from infancy are "able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus." The writings of Moses, the Psalms, the Prophets point toward and find their fulfillment in Christ. This is the same claim Paul made in Romans 1:2 (the gospel was "promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy scriptures") and in 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 ("Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures... he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures"). The sacred writings are not superseded by the gospel but are the foundational wisdom that leads to the gospel's reception.

## Verses 16-17: The Nature and Purpose of Scripture

"All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work."

**"All Scripture is breathed out by God"** (pasa graphē theopneustos). The adjective pasa ("all, every") with the noun graphē ("writing, scripture") covers the totality of the sacred writings just mentioned in verse 15. The noun graphē in the singular often refers to a specific passage of scripture ("the scripture says," as in Romans 4:3; 9:17; Galatians 4:30), but pasa graphē -- "every scripture" or "all scripture" -- extends the claim to the whole collection. The immediate referent, given the context of verse 15 ("the sacred writings which you have known from childhood"), is the Jewish scriptures -- the Torah, the Prophets, and the Writings that constituted the scriptures available to Timothy from infancy. The claim covers whatever falls within the category of "sacred writings" (ta hiera grammata).

The adjective theopneustos ("God-breathed, breathed out by God, inspired by God") appears only here in the New Testament and is extremely rare in Greek literature before this text. It is a compound of theos ("God") + pneustos (from pnein, "to breathe, to blow"). The word could theoretically be read as either active ("breathing God, filled with God's breath") or passive ("breathed by God, produced by God's breathing"). The passive reading is overwhelmingly more natural in context: the scriptures were produced by God's breath, they originate from God's own exhalation.

The concept of God's breath as the agent of creation and communication has deep roots in Israel's scriptures. In Genesis 2:7 (LXX), God "breathed" (enephysēsen) into Adam's nostrils the breath of life (pnoēn zōēs). In Psalm 33:6 (LXX 32:6), "By the word of the Lord the heavens were made, and by the breath (pneumati) of his mouth all their host." In Job 32:8 (LXX), "But it is the spirit (pneuma) in man, the breath (pnoē) of the Almighty, that makes him understand." In 2 Samuel 23:2, David says: "The Spirit of the Lord speaks through me; his word is on my tongue." In Isaiah 59:21: "My Spirit that is upon you, and my words that I have put in your mouth." The breath/spirit of God is the agent through which God communicates his word to and through human authors.

To say that scripture is theopneustos is to say that its origin lies in God's own breath -- God breathed it out, God is its ultimate source. The claim does not specify the mechanism (dictation, inspiration, illumination, or some other mode) but identifies the source: the scriptures came from God. This is not a new claim in Jewish thought; it is the standard understanding of the prophetic and scriptural writings. The novelty, if any, is the use of a single adjective to compress this understanding into a word.

The grammatical question of whether theopneustos is predicative ("all scripture is God-breathed and [is] profitable") or attributive ("all God-breathed scripture is also profitable") has been debated, but both readings yield essentially the same meaning in context. If predicative, Paul asserts two things about all scripture: it is God-breathed and it is profitable. If attributive, Paul says that all God-breathed scripture -- which is all scripture, since the identification of the sacred writings as God-breathed is assumed rather than argued -- is also profitable. In either reading, the practical conclusion is the same: the scriptures that Timothy has known since childhood are both God-breathed and useful.

**"And profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness"** (kai ōphelimos pros didaskalian, pros elegmon, pros epanorthōsin, pros paideian tēn en dikaiosynē). The adjective ōphelimos ("profitable, useful, beneficial, advantageous") appeared in 1 Timothy 4:8, where Paul said that "bodily training is of some value (ōphelimon), but godliness is profitable (ōphelimos) in every way." The word describes practical usefulness -- the scriptures are not merely venerable or interesting but useful, functional, productive of results.

The usefulness is specified in four directions, each introduced by the preposition pros ("for, toward, for the purpose of"):

(1) **Didaskalian** ("teaching, instruction, doctrine") -- the communication of truth, the establishment of right understanding. The noun didaskalia has appeared throughout both letters as a central concern: "sound teaching" (hygiainousa didaskalia, 1 Tim 1:10; 4:6; 2 Tim 4:3; Titus 1:9; 2:1), "teaching that accords with godliness" (1 Tim 6:3), the teaching from which some will depart (1 Tim 4:1). Scripture is the source and standard for this teaching.

(2) **Elegmon** ("reproof, refutation, conviction, rebuke") -- the exposure and correction of error. The noun elegmos appears only here in the New Testament. The related verb elegchein ("to convict, to reprove, to expose") appeared in 1 Timothy 5:20 ("rebuke [those who persist in sin] in the presence of all") and in Titus 1:9 (the overseer must be "able to rebuke those who contradict"). Scripture provides the basis for refuting falsehood -- it is the standard against which error is measured and exposed.

(3) **Epanorthōsin** ("correction, restoration, setting right, amendment") -- the positive counterpart to reproof. Where reproof identifies what is wrong, correction establishes what is right. The noun epanorthōsis appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of epi ("upon") + ana ("up, again") + orthoun ("to set upright, to make straight"). The word describes the act of setting something upright again that has fallen over, restoring something to its proper position. Scripture not only identifies error but provides the path back to truth.

(4) **Paideian tēn en dikaiosynē** ("training in righteousness, discipline in right conduct") -- the ongoing formation of character. The noun paideia ("training, education, discipline, instruction") is a comprehensive word in Greek culture, covering the entire process by which a person is formed from childhood into a mature, competent adult. In the LXX, paideia is consistently associated with God's disciplining activity. Proverbs 3:11 (LXX): "My son, do not despise the Lord's discipline (paideias kyriou) or be weary of his reproof." Hebrews 12:5-11 develops the concept of divine paideia at length: "The Lord disciplines (paideuei) the one he loves." The phrase en dikaiosynē ("in righteousness") specifies the domain of the training: the scripture trains people in dikaiosynē -- right conduct, right relationship with God, conformity to God's character and standards.

The four purposes together describe a comprehensive pedagogical function. Teaching establishes truth. Reproof exposes error. Correction restores the erring to the right path. Training in righteousness forms the character that sustains faithfulness over time. The sequence moves from the cognitive (teaching and reproof) to the practical (correction and training). Scripture addresses both what is believed and how life is lived.

**"That the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work"** (hina artios ē ho tou theou anthrōpos, pros pan ergon agathon exērtismenos). The conjunction hina ("in order that, so that") introduces the purpose for which scripture's fourfold function exists. The phrase ho tou theou anthrōpos ("the man of God, the person belonging to God") is the title used for Timothy. In 1 Timothy 6:11, Paul addressed Timothy directly: "But as for you, O man of God, flee these things." The phrase has deep roots in the LXX, where it designates individuals who serve as God's authorized representatives. Moses is "the man of God" (anthrōpos tou theou) in Deuteronomy 33:1; Joshua 14:6; 1 Chronicles 23:14; Ezra 3:2. Elijah is "a man of God" in 1 Kings 17:18, 24. Elisha is "the man of God" in 2 Kings 4:7, 9, 16, 21, 22, 25, 27, and many other verses. David is "the man of God" in 2 Chronicles 8:14; Nehemiah 12:24, 36. The title identifies a person who belongs to God, who serves God, who speaks and acts on God's behalf. Timothy stands in this lineage.

The adjective artios ("complete, capable, proficient, fit for a task") appears only here in the New Testament. It describes someone who is fully formed for their function -- not lacking anything essential, not deficient in any necessary quality. A tool that is artios is one that is fit for its purpose; a person who is artios is one who can fulfill their role completely.

The participle exērtismenos (from exartizein, "to complete, to finish, to equip thoroughly, to furnish completely") reinforces the concept with an intensified form. The prefix ex- ("out, thoroughly, completely") intensifies the base artizein ("to prepare, to equip"). The word describes thorough, comprehensive equipping -- not partial preparation but complete readiness. The phrase pros pan ergon agathon ("for every good work") specifies what the equipping is for: every good work, without exception or limitation. The expression pan ergon agathon ("every good work") appeared in 2:21, where the cleansed vessel was described as "prepared for every good work" (eis pan ergon agathon hetoimasmenon). The verbal echo binds the two passages: just as the cleansed vessel is prepared for every good work, so the man of God who is formed by scripture is equipped for every good work. The difference is that 2:21 described the preparation through cleansing from dishonor, while 3:17 describes the preparation through the formative work of God-breathed scripture. Both processes contribute to the same result: a person ready for comprehensive service.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

2 Timothy 3 is structured around two sharp contrasts -- between the false teachers and Timothy, between the corrupting influence of deception and the formative power of scripture -- and moves the letter from the specific warnings of chapter 2 to the solemn charge of chapter 4.

(1) **Verses 1-5** describe the character of people who will mark the "last days" -- an era that the early communities understood as already present. The vice list of nineteen descriptors catalogs a comprehensive corruption that begins with disordered love (self, money, pleasure rather than God) and culminates in the defining trait: having the outward form of godliness while denying its power. These are not outsiders but people within or adjacent to the community who maintain religious appearances while rejecting the transformative reality. Timothy is commanded to avoid them.

(2) **Verses 6-9** describe the method and precedent of the false teachers. They infiltrate households, take captive vulnerable people who are burdened with guilt and driven by shifting desires, and produce students who are always learning but never arriving at truth. The comparison to Jannes and Jambres -- the Egyptian magicians who opposed Moses -- locates the current opponents within a scriptural pattern: they imitate the genuine article, they briefly succeed, but their folly eventually becomes evident to everyone. Their opposition to the truth identifies them as corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith -- the exact opposite of Timothy, who is to present himself as approved (2:15).

(3) **Verses 10-12** pivot to Timothy with the emphatic contrast "You, however." Timothy has followed Paul's teaching, conduct, purpose, faith, patience, love, and steadfastness through persecutions and sufferings -- specifically the persecutions at Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra, in Timothy's own home region. The Lord rescued Paul from all of them. The section concludes with a universal principle: all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted. The false teachers' avoidance of persecution is itself evidence that their godliness lacks power.

(4) **Verse 13** states the trajectory of the wicked: evil people and impostors will advance from bad to worse, deceiving others and being deceived themselves. The mutual, self-reinforcing nature of the deception explains why their students can never arrive at truth: the teachers themselves do not possess it.

(5) **Verses 14-15** ground Timothy's resistance in two foundations: the sources from whom he learned (Paul, Lois, Eunice -- people whose faithfulness has been demonstrated) and the sacred writings he has known since infancy. These writings are "able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" -- the Jewish scriptures, read in light of Christ, provide the wisdom that leads to salvation. The faith that mediates this salvation is specifically faith in Christ Jesus.

(6) **Verses 16-17** make the chapter's climactic claim about the nature and function of scripture. All scripture is God-breathed -- its origin lies in God's own breath -- and it is useful for four purposes: teaching (establishing truth), reproof (exposing error), correction (restoring the erring), and training in righteousness (forming character). The goal of this fourfold function is that the man of God may be complete and equipped for every good work. Scripture is not merely an archive of ancient texts but a living, functional instrument by which God forms his servants for comprehensive service.

The chapter's overall architecture serves the letter's central concern: the faithful transmission and defense of the gospel across generations. The false teachers represent the threat -- they are corrupted, corrupting, and escalating in their wickedness. Timothy represents the alternative -- grounded in Paul's example, rooted in the sacred writings, formed by God-breathed scripture for every good work. The chapter does not promise that the false teachers will disappear or that the difficulty will ease. It promises that their folly will eventually become evident (3:9) and that the man of God who is formed by scripture will be complete and equipped (3:17). The resource for standing against the corruption of the last days is not superior intellect, institutional power, or strategic brilliance but the ancient scriptures, known since childhood, breathed out by God, and made wise for salvation through faith in the risen Davidic Messiah. This is the foundation from which the solemn charge of 4:1-2 -- "preach the word" -- will issue. Timothy is to preach because he has been equipped by scripture; he has been equipped by scripture because God breathed it; and the word he preaches is the word of the truth from which the false teachers have swerved but which remains, like God's firm foundation (2:19), unshaken.
