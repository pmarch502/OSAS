Acts 15 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 14): Paul and Barnabas continued the pattern established in Acts 13 across multiple cities in the interior of Asia Minor. At Iconium, they entered the synagogue and "spoke in such a way that a great number of both Jews and Greeks believed" (14:1), but "unbelieving Jews stirred up the Gentiles and poisoned their minds against the brothers" (14:2). The Iconium pattern replicated Pisidian Antioch precisely: synagogue reception, then Jewish opposition recruiting Gentile hostility. At Lystra, Paul healed a man crippled from birth (14:8-10), and the pagan crowd attempted to offer sacrifice to Barnabas and Paul as Zeus and Hermes (14:11-13) — a misidentification that Paul and Barnabas urgently rejected, tearing their garments and directing the crowd to "a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them" (14:15). Then Jews arrived from Antioch and Iconium — the very cities where Paul had preached — and "having persuaded the crowds, they stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing him to be dead" (14:19). The persecution followed the messengers from city to city. Paul survived, and he and Barnabas retraced their route through Lystra, Iconium, and Pisidian Antioch, "strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith, and saying that through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God" (14:22). They appointed elders in every community (14:23). The return journey took them back to Antioch in Syria, where "they declared all that God had done with them, and how he had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles" (14:27). The summary is precise: the central accomplishment of the first missionary journey was Gentile inclusion. They remained in Antioch "no little time" (14:28) — a period of stability and teaching before the crisis of chapter 15 erupts.

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 16): Paul proposes a second journey to visit the communities established on the first. A sharp disagreement with Barnabas over John Mark — who had deserted them in Pamphylia (13:13; 15:38) — results in their separation. Barnabas takes Mark and sails to Cyprus; Paul chooses Silas and departs through Syria and Cilicia. In Lystra, Paul encounters Timothy, "the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek" (16:1). Paul circumcises Timothy "because of the Jews who were in those places, for they all knew that his father was a Greek" (16:3) — a practical accommodation that does not contradict the Jerusalem decision, since Timothy's Jewish mother made him Jewish by birth and the circumcision addresses his standing within the Jewish community, not his salvation. Paul and his companions deliver the Jerusalem council's decisions (ta dogmata ta kekrimena hypo ton apostolon kai presbyteron, 16:4) to the churches, which are "strengthened in the faith" and "increased in numbers daily" (16:5). The Spirit then redirects their travel — preventing them from entering Asia, blocking them from Bithynia — until a vision at Troas sends them across to Macedonia (16:6-10). The narrative moves decisively westward into Europe. The Jerusalem council's decision has freed the mission: Gentile communities can grow without the obstacle of mandatory circumcision, and the messengers can carry the council's letter as authorization from the apostles and elders.

How chapter 15 connects these: Acts 14 ended with Paul and Barnabas in Antioch, having completed a journey that demonstrated the reality of Gentile conversion on a large scale. They reported that God "had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles" (14:27). Acts 16 shows the mission expanding westward into Europe, carrying the Jerusalem council's decisions as formal authorization. Acts 15 is the crisis and resolution that stands between the accomplished fact (Gentiles are believing) and the authorized future (the mission can proceed without requiring circumcision). The question that chapter 15 must resolve is not whether Gentiles can believe — Peter's experience with Cornelius (Acts 10-11) and Paul's first journey (Acts 13-14) have demonstrated that they do. The question is what is required of them once they have believed. Must Gentile believers become Jews through circumcision and Torah observance in order to be saved? Or is faith in Christ sufficient? The answer to this question will determine whether the "door of faith" that God opened for the Gentiles (14:27) remains open or is narrowed to a gate that only circumcision can unlock. Everything that happens in Acts 16-28 depends on the decision reached in Acts 15.

Verses 1-2: The Crisis at Antioch

"But some men came down from Judea and were teaching the brothers, 'Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved'" (15:1).

The conjunction "but" (kai tines) marks a disruption. The period of stability in Antioch (14:28) is broken by the arrival of men from Judea — not identified by name, not described as apostles or elders, simply "some men" (tines). They are teaching (edidaskon — imperfect tense, indicating ongoing, sustained instruction, not a single statement). Their teaching is stated with precision: "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved" (ean me peritmethrthete to ethei to Mouseos, ou dynasthe sothenai). The claim has two components. First, circumcision is necessary. Second, without it, salvation is impossible. The phrase "the custom of Moses" (to ethei to Mouseos) grounds the requirement in Mosaic authority — this is not a human tradition but a divine command (Genesis 17:9-14, where God tells Abraham: "Every male among you shall be circumcised... Any uncircumcised male who is not circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant"). The men from Judea are not inventing a requirement; they are insisting on one that is embedded in the foundational covenant with Abraham and codified by Moses.

The word "saved" (sothenai) is the crux. The men from Judea are not merely recommending circumcision as a helpful practice or a mark of deeper commitment. They are asserting it as a condition of salvation. Without it, the Gentile believers in Antioch "cannot be saved" — their faith in Christ is insufficient without this additional act. This turns circumcision from a covenantal marker into a soteriological prerequisite.

The location of this confrontation — Antioch — is significant. Antioch is the community where "the disciples were first called Christians" (11:26), where Barnabas and Saul taught for a full year, where the Gentile mission was launched by the Spirit's command (13:1-3). It is the most established, most vibrant, most ethnically mixed community outside Jerusalem. If Gentile believers in Antioch can be told they "cannot be saved" without circumcision, then the Gentile mission itself is at stake. The men from Judea are not attacking a fringe community; they are challenging the base of operations for the entire Gentile outreach.

"And after Paul and Barnabas had no small dissension and debate with them, Paul and Barnabas and some of the others were appointed to go up to Jerusalem to the apostles and the elders about this question" (15:2).

The response is "no small dissension and debate" (staseos kai zeteseos ouk oliges). The word stasis means "standing apart, faction, dissension, strife" — it carries the connotation of division within a community, not merely intellectual disagreement. The word zetesis means "questioning, inquiry, dispute." The litotes ("no small" meaning "very great") emphasizes the severity. This is a community-splitting crisis. Paul and Barnabas resist the teaching vigorously, but the matter cannot be settled locally. The Antioch community recognizes that a question of this magnitude — whether salvation requires circumcision — must be decided by the apostles and elders in Jerusalem. They "appoint" (etaxan) Paul and Barnabas and "some of the others" (tinas allous) to travel to Jerusalem for a formal resolution.

The verb etaxan is from tasso — "to arrange, to appoint, to assign to a position" — the same root used in Acts 13:48 ("as many as were appointed to eternal life believed"). The appointment is a corporate decision: the Antioch community sends its representatives to the source of apostolic authority. The question will not be resolved by Paul and Barnabas's personal authority, formidable as it is. It requires the agreement of the entire leadership.

Verse 3: The Journey Through Phoenicia and Samaria

"So, being sent on their way by the church, they passed through both Phoenicia and Samaria, describing in detail the conversion of the Gentiles, and brought great joy to all the brothers" (15:3).

The journey from Antioch to Jerusalem runs through Phoenicia and Samaria — regions where the gospel had already taken root (Acts 8:4-25 for Samaria; 11:19 for Phoenicia). As Paul and Barnabas pass through, they describe "in detail" (ekdiegoumenoi — from ekdiegeomai, "to narrate fully, to tell at length") the "conversion of the Gentiles" (ten epistrophen ton ethnon). The word epistrophe means "turning, conversion" — a turning from one direction to another. The Gentiles have turned — from idols, from pagan worship, from life without Israel's God — to faith in Christ.

The response is "great joy" (charan megalen) among "all the brothers" (pasin tois adelphois). The communities in Phoenicia and Samaria — themselves composed of people who had crossed boundaries (Samaritans in chapter 8, scattered Hellenists in chapter 11) — rejoice at the news of further Gentile conversion. There is no controversy here. The joy is universal. The opposition to Gentile inclusion comes not from the broader believing community but from a specific faction within it, as the next verse will reveal.

Verses 4-5: Arrival in Jerusalem and the Pharisee Believers' Demand

"When they came to Jerusalem, they were welcomed by the church and the apostles and the elders, and they declared all that God had done with them" (15:4).

The reception in Jerusalem is warm: they are "welcomed" (paredechthesan — from paradechomai, "to receive, to welcome"). Paul and Barnabas report "all that God had done with them" (hosa ho theos epoiesen met' auton). The phrasing exactly matches Acts 14:27, where they reported the same thing to the Antioch community. The emphasis is on divine agency: not what Paul and Barnabas accomplished, but what God did with them. God is the subject; the missionaries are his instruments.

"But some believers who belonged to the party of the Pharisees rose up and said, 'It is necessary to circumcise them and to order them to keep the law of Moses'" (15:5).

The opposition in Jerusalem is now identified with specificity. These are not outsiders or opponents of the movement. They are "believers" (pepisteukotos — perfect participle of pisteuo, "having believed"; these are people who have come to faith in Jesus). And they belong to "the party of the Pharisees" (tes haireseos ton Pharisaion). The word hairesis means "sect, party, school of thought" — it is used in Acts for the Sadducees (5:17) and the Pharisees (26:5) as distinct schools within Judaism. These are Pharisees who have believed in Jesus but who understand that belief as an addition to, not a replacement of, Pharisaic Torah observance.

Their demand goes beyond what the men in Antioch had said. In verse 1, the requirement was circumcision for salvation. Here the requirement is twofold: circumcise them (peritemnein autous) and "order them to keep the law of Moses" (parangelein te terein ton nomon Mouseos). The verb parangelein means "to command, to charge, to give orders" — it is authoritative language, not advisory. And "the law of Moses" (ton nomon Mouseos) encompasses the entire Mosaic legislation, not merely circumcision. The Pharisee believers are insisting that Gentile believers must become full proselytes: circumcised, Torah-observant, subject to the entire covenantal framework of Israel. This is the position in its maximum form.

The logic of this position has internal coherence. If Jesus is the Jewish Messiah, and if the Messiah's role is to restore Israel, then belonging to the Messiah's community means belonging to Israel, and belonging to Israel means circumcision and Torah observance. From a Pharisaic perspective, this is not legalism but consistency: the covenant that God made with Abraham included circumcision as its sign (Genesis 17:10-14), and the God who sent the Messiah is the same God who gave that covenant. To suggest that Gentiles can belong to the Messiah without entering the covenant would seem, from this perspective, to separate the Messiah from the God who sent him.

The author presents both positions without editorializing. The text does not call the Pharisee believers wrong; it records their position and lets the speeches that follow address it.

Verse 6: The Assembly

"The apostles and the elders were gathered together to consider this matter" (15:6).

The deliberation is formal. "The apostles and the elders" (hoi apostoloi kai hoi presbyteroi) convene as a body — the recognized leadership of the Jerusalem community. The verb synechthesan ("were gathered together") indicates a purposeful assembly, not a casual conversation. The matter under consideration is a question (tou logou toutou — literally "this word/matter") that requires corporate discernment. The structure mirrors the pattern of communal decision-making that runs through Acts: the selection of Matthias (1:15-26), the response to the Hellenist widows' complaint (6:1-6), the reception of Peter's Cornelius report (11:1-18). At critical junctures, the community deliberates together.

Verses 7-11: Peter's Speech — God Already Settled This

"After there had been much debate, Peter stood up and said to them, 'Brothers, you know that in the early days God made a choice among you, that through my mouth the Gentiles would hear the word of the gospel and believe'" (15:7).

The assembly does not begin with speeches; it begins with "much debate" (polles zeteseos — the same word zetesis from verse 2). The community argues. The author does not report the content of this debate; he summarizes it as extensive and then presents three speeches that carry the argument: Peter's, the report of Barnabas and Paul, and James's.

Peter speaks first — the same Peter who was the primary figure in Acts 1-12 and who was specifically chosen by God for the Cornelius event. His authority on this question is not theoretical but experiential: he was there. He was the one God used.

Peter's opening claim is that "God made a choice" (ho theos en hemin exelexato). The verb exelexato is from eklegomai — "to choose, to select." This is the same verb used in the LXX for God's choice of Israel: "The Lord your God has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession" (Deuteronomy 7:6 LXX, exelexato). Peter is using election language — the vocabulary of God's sovereign selection. But the object of God's choice here is not Israel as a nation; it is Peter as an instrument. God chose "among you" (en hemin — "among us") that "through my mouth" (dia tou stomatos mou) the Gentiles would hear the gospel and believe. Peter is claiming that the Cornelius event was not his initiative but God's prior election. God chose him for this specific purpose.

The phrase "in the early days" (aph' hemeron archaion — literally "from ancient days" or "from the early days") is notable. Peter is speaking of the Cornelius event, which occurred many years before this council. The phrase places the event in the category of established precedent, not recent innovation. What Peter did at Cornelius's house is not a novelty being debated; it is an established act of God that the community has already accepted (cf. Acts 11:18, where the Jerusalem community acknowledged: "Then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance that leads to life").

"And God, who knows the heart, bore witness to them, by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us, and he made no distinction between us and them, having cleansed their hearts by faith" (15:8-9).

Peter's argument rests on three assertions, each grounded in what God did, not in what Peter decided.

First: God is "the one who knows the heart" (ho kardiognostes theos). The compound word kardiognostes ("heart-knower") appears in the New Testament only here and in Acts 1:24, where the community prayed before selecting Matthias: "You, Lord, who know the hearts of all, show which one of these two you have chosen." In both uses, the word identifies God as the one who sees what humans cannot — internal reality, as opposed to external markers. The relevance is pointed: circumcision is an external, physical marker. God, who knows hearts, has already rendered his verdict on the Gentiles' internal state, and his verdict is expressed not through an external sign but through the gift of his Spirit.

Second: God "bore witness to them" (emartyrasen autois) by giving them the Holy Spirit "just as he did to us" (kathos kai hemin). The verb martyreo ("to bear witness, to testify") presents the gift of the Spirit as God's own testimony — God's own act of witness-bearing — in favor of the Gentile believers. The standard "just as he did to us" (kathos kai hemin) repeats Peter's earlier argument from Acts 11:15 ("the Holy Spirit fell on them just as on us at the beginning") and Acts 10:47 ("who received the Holy Spirit just as we have"). The equality of the gift is the equality of the recipients. If God gave the same Spirit to uncircumcised Gentiles as to circumcised Jews, then God himself has declared the two groups equal.

Third: God "made no distinction between us and them" (outhen diekrinen metaxy hemon te kai auton). The verb diekrinen is from diakrino — "to distinguish, to separate, to make a difference between." This is the verb that has tracked through the entire Cornelius narrative: the Spirit told Peter to go "making no distinction" (meden diakrinanta, 10:20; 11:12); the Jerusalem believers were "distinguishing" (diekrinonto, 11:2) against Peter. Now Peter declares that God himself made no distinction. The ultimate arbiter has spoken: there is no difference between "us" (Jewish believers) and "them" (Gentile believers). The basis for this lack of distinction is stated: God cleansed "their hearts by faith" (te pistei katharisas tas kardias auton). The cleansing is internal (hearts, not flesh), and its instrument is faith (te pistei — dative of means), not circumcision. God purified the Gentiles through the same means by which he purified the Jews: faith. The verb katharizo ("to cleanse, to purify") is the same verb used in Peter's vision: "What God has made clean (ekatharisen), do not call common" (10:15). The cleansing that God performed on the animals in the vision — which Peter understood as referring to persons (10:28) — is now stated in its final form: God cleansed Gentile hearts by faith.

"Now, therefore, why are you putting God to the test by placing a yoke on the neck of the disciples that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear?" (15:10).

Peter now turns from narration to challenge. The verb peirazete ("you are testing, you are putting to the test") carries serious weight. In the LXX, "testing God" is a grave offense. At Massah and Meribah, the Israelites "tested the Lord" (Exodus 17:2, 7; Numbers 14:22; Deuteronomy 6:16), and that testing provoked divine anger. Psalm 78:18, 41, 56 recounts Israel's wilderness failures in terms of "testing God." Peter is saying that requiring circumcision for Gentile salvation is not merely an error of judgment; it is an act of testing God — of challenging God's own declared verdict on the Gentiles.

The metaphor of the "yoke" (zygon) requires careful reading. In the LXX and in rabbinic usage, a "yoke" can refer to submission — the yoke of a king, the yoke of foreign domination (1 Kings 12:4, 9-11, where the people ask Rehoboam to lighten the yoke his father Solomon had placed on them; Isaiah 9:4; 10:27; 14:25, where God breaks the yoke of Assyria). The phrase "yoke on the neck" (zygon epi ton trachelon) echoes Jeremiah 27:8 (LXX 34:8) and 28:14, where submission to Babylon is described as a yoke placed on the necks of the nations. Peter is characterizing the demand to impose full Torah observance on Gentile believers as a form of oppressive burden.

Peter's most striking claim is in the final clause: "neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear" (hon oute hoi pateres hemon oute hemeis ischysamen bastasai). This is not a statement about the Law's inherent deficiency but an honest assessment of Israel's historical experience. The prophets consistently testified that Israel failed to keep the covenant. Jeremiah 31:32: "my covenant that they broke, though I was their husband." Ezekiel 20:13: "the house of Israel rebelled against me in the wilderness; they did not walk in my statutes." Nehemiah 9:26-29 catalogs Israel's repeated failure to obey the Law despite God's repeated patience. The wilderness generation, the period of the judges, the monarchy, the exile — Israel's history, as told by Israel's own prophets, is a history of inability to bear the yoke of the Law fully. Peter is not dismissing the Law; he is acknowledging what Israel's own scriptures acknowledge: the Law exposed sin more than it eliminated it. To place this same yoke on Gentiles — when Israel itself could not bear it — is to impose a requirement that demonstrably does not produce the salvation it is claimed to secure.

"But we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (15:11).

Peter's concluding statement is remarkable for its directional reversal. The Pharisee believers' position was: Gentiles must become like us (circumcised, Torah-observant) in order to be saved. Peter's position inverts the comparison: "we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (alla dia tes charitos tou kyriou Iesou pisteouomen sothenai kath' hon tropon kakeinoi). The phrase "just as they will" (kath' hon tropon kakeinoi — "in the same manner as those also") does not say the Gentiles are saved as Jews are. It says Jews are saved as Gentiles are — through grace, not through Torah observance. The comparison runs in the unexpected direction. The assumed baseline is not Jewish covenant standing but grace. Grace is the common ground; everything else is additional. Jews are not saved by the Law plus Christ; Jews, like Gentiles, are saved by the grace of the Lord Jesus. The Law is not the standard to which Gentiles must be raised; grace is the standard by which all — Jews and Gentiles alike — are saved.

The word "grace" (charitos) here connects directly to Acts 13:43, where Paul and Barnabas in Pisidian Antioch "urged them to continue in the grace of God." It also connects to Acts 14:26, where Paul and Barnabas returned to Antioch, "where they had been commended to the grace of God for the work that they had fulfilled." Grace is the basis of the mission, the basis of the community, and now, in Peter's declaration, the basis of salvation for everyone.

Verse 12: Barnabas and Paul's Testimony

"And all the assembly fell silent, and they listened to Barnabas and Paul as they related what signs and wonders God had done through them among the Gentiles" (15:12).

The silence (esigesen — from sigao, "to be silent, to cease speaking") is the assembly's response to Peter's speech. The debate stops. The same silence fell after Peter's defense of the Cornelius event in Acts 11:18 ("they fell silent"). Peter's argument, grounded in God's own action, has quieted the opposition.

Into this silence, Barnabas and Paul speak. The author reverses the usual ordering — not "Paul and Barnabas" (the post-Acts-13 standard) but "Barnabas and Paul" (Barnaba kai Paulou). This may reflect the Jerusalem setting, where Barnabas was known first (4:36-37; 9:27; 11:22) and had deeper relationships with the Jerusalem community. Barnabas's credibility in Jerusalem exceeds Paul's; he is listed first because this is his home audience.

They report "signs and wonders" (semeia kai terata) — the same phrase used for the apostles' activity in Jerusalem (2:43; 5:12). The signs and wonders were done by God "through them" (di' auton) "among the Gentiles" (en tois ethnesin). The evidence is not theological argumentation but narrated divine action. God performed the same kinds of authenticating works among Gentiles that he had performed through the apostles among Jews. The signs and wonders are God's endorsement of the Gentile mission.

The structure of the three testimonies is deliberate: Peter appeals to past divine action (the Cornelius precedent), Barnabas and Paul appeal to present divine action (the first missionary journey), and James will appeal to prophetic scripture. Past experience, present experience, and scriptural authority converge on the same conclusion.

Verses 13-18: James's Speech — Scripture Confirms What God Has Done

"After they finished speaking, James replied, 'Brothers, listen to me. Simeon has related how God first visited the Gentiles, to take from them a people for his name'" (15:13-14).

James speaks last and will render the judgment. This is the James identified in Acts 12:17, where Peter, after his prison escape, said "report these things to James and the brothers." He is the brother of Jesus (Galatians 1:19), the recognized leader of the Jerusalem community. His authority in this assembly is evident from the structure: he speaks after all others, and his verdict becomes the decision.

James refers to Peter by his Hebrew name, "Simeon" (Symeon) — a form closer to the original Hebrew Shimon than the Hellenized Petros. The choice of the Semitic name, unique in Acts, may signal James's orientation toward the Hebrew scriptural tradition and the Jewish character of the Jerusalem community.

James summarizes Peter's testimony in a single, compressed sentence that contains a phrase of enormous significance: God "visited the Gentiles to take from them a people for his name" (epeskepsato labein ex ethnon laon to onomati autou). Each term requires attention.

"Visited" (epeskepsato) — from episkeptomai, "to look upon, to visit, to inspect." In the LXX, this verb is used for God's saving intervention in Israel's history. Genesis 50:24-25: "God will surely visit you (episkepsetai) and bring you up out of this land." Exodus 4:31: the people "believed... that the Lord had visited (epeskeptai) the sons of Israel." Ruth 1:6: "the Lord had visited (epeskepsato) his people by giving them food." Luke 1:68: Zechariah's Song — "Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has visited (epeskepsato) and redeemed his people." The word consistently describes God's active, saving intervention. James uses this word for what God did among the Gentiles. God "visited" the Gentiles — the same language used for God's saving visits to Israel. The Gentile inclusion is not an afterthought but a divine visitation of the same kind that characterized God's dealings with his own people.

"To take from them a people for his name" (labein ex ethnon laon to onomati autou). The key term is laos — "people." In the LXX, laos is the standard word for Israel as God's covenant people. Deuteronomy 7:6: "The Lord your God has chosen you to be a people (laon) for his own possession." Deuteronomy 14:2: "you are a people (laos) holy to the Lord your God." The word ethnos (plural ethne, "nations, Gentiles") describes the other nations — those who are not laos. James is using both terms in a single phrase: from the ethne ("nations/Gentiles"), God is taking a laos ("a people"). The nations are producing a people. Those who were not laos are becoming laos. This echoes Hosea 2:23 (LXX 2:25): "I will say to Not-My-People (ou laon mou), 'You are my people (laos mou).'" The prophet's declaration that those who were not God's people would become God's people is being fulfilled, in James's reading, through the Gentile conversions that Peter, Barnabas, and Paul have reported.

"And with this the words of the prophets agree, as it is written, 'After this I will return, and I will rebuild the tent of David that has fallen; I will rebuild its ruins, and I will restore it, that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles who are called by my name, says the Lord, who makes these things known from of old'" (15:15-18).

James declares that the prophets "agree" (symphonousin — from symphoneoo, "to sound together, to be in harmony") with what has happened. The verb is musical: the prophetic voices harmonize with the experienced reality. James is not proof-texting — pulling an isolated verse to support a predetermined conclusion. He is declaring that the prophetic tradition as a whole ("the words of the prophets," hoi logoi ton propheton, plural) resonates with what God has done.

The specific text James quotes is Amos 9:11-12, but the form he quotes is the LXX, which differs significantly from the Hebrew. This difference is not incidental; it is the foundation of James's argument, and a first-century hearer using the Greek scriptures would have heard the text exactly as James quotes it.

The Hebrew of Amos 9:11-12 reads: "In that day I will raise up the booth of David that is fallen... that they may possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name." The key phrase in Hebrew is yirshu et she'erit Edom — "they may possess the remnant of Edom." This describes Israel's conquest and dominion over Edom and the nations.

The LXX of Amos 9:11-12 reads differently: hopos an ekzetesoosin hoi kataloipoi ton anthropon ton kyrion, kai panta ta ethne eph' hous epikekletai to onoma mou ep' autous — "that the remnant of humanity may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles upon whom my name is called." The LXX translators apparently read the Hebrew differently: instead of yirshu ("they may possess") they read yidreshu ("they may seek"); instead of Edom they read adam ("humanity/mankind"). The result is a text that speaks not of Israel conquering Edom but of the nations seeking the Lord — not military dominion but spiritual inclusion.

James quotes the LXX form. For his argument, this matters decisively. The LXX Amos does not describe Israel dominating the Gentiles; it describes the Gentiles seeking the Lord. The text envisions a time when "all the Gentiles upon whom my name is called" (panta ta ethne eph' hous epikekletai to onoma mou ep' autous) will be included in the restored people of God. The Gentiles are "called by my name" — they bear God's name, they belong to God, they are God's people — without any reference to their becoming Israelites through circumcision. Their inclusion is accomplished not by their entering Israel's covenant but by God's name being placed upon them.

The phrase "the tent of David that has fallen" (ten skenen Dauid ten peptokuian — literally "the tabernacle/booth of David that has fallen") refers to the Davidic dynasty, which "fell" with the Babylonian exile. The "tent" (skene) is the humble word — a booth, a temporary shelter, not a palace or a temple. David's royal house has become a collapsed tent. God's promise is to rebuild it. James reads this rebuilding as having begun in Jesus, the descendant of David. The resurrection and exaltation of Jesus is the raising of David's fallen tent. And the purpose of this rebuilding is stated: "that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles who are called by my name." The rebuilding of David's house is for the inclusion of the nations. The restoration of Israel is not an end in itself; it is the means by which the Gentiles are brought in.

The final phrase — "says the Lord, who makes these things known from of old" (legei kyrios poion tauta gnosta ap' aionos) — asserts that Gentile inclusion was always part of God's plan, known "from of old" (ap' aionos — "from the age," i.e., from ancient times). This is not a new development; it is an ancient purpose now being realized. The men from Judea and the Pharisee believers are treating Gentile inclusion without circumcision as an innovation; James treats it as the fulfillment of what God declared through the prophets long ago.

Verses 19-21: James's Judgment — The Four Prohibitions

"Therefore my judgment is that we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God, but should write to them to abstain from the things polluted by idols, and from sexual immorality, and from what has been strangled, and from blood" (15:19-20).

James issues his verdict: "my judgment" (ego krino — literally "I judge, I decide"). The verb krino carries judicial weight — this is a ruling, not a suggestion. The first part is negative: "we should not trouble" (me parenochleein — from parenochleo, "to cause additional difficulty, to burden beyond what is necessary") "those of the Gentiles who turn to God" (tois apo ton ethnon epistrephousin epi ton theon). The verb epistrephein ("to turn, to turn around") is the standard word for conversion, used throughout Acts (3:19; 9:35; 11:21; 14:15; 26:18, 20). The Gentiles who are "turning to God" — the same turning that Paul and Barnabas have been reporting — should not be burdened with additional requirements beyond what James is about to specify.

The second part is positive: four specific prohibitions. The Gentile believers are to "abstain from" (apechesthai — "to hold oneself away from, to avoid") four things:

1. "Things polluted by idols" (ton alisgamaton ton eidolon). The word alisgemata (from alisgeoo, "to pollute, to contaminate") appears nowhere else in the New Testament. In Daniel 1:8 (Theodotion), Daniel resolved not to "defile himself" (alisgeethenai) with the king's food. The "pollutions of idols" refers to food that has been offered to pagan deities in their temples — meat sacrificed on idol altars and then sold in the market or consumed at pagan feasts. In verse 29, this is restated as "food sacrificed to idols" (eidolothyton). For a Gentile living in a Greco-Roman city, eating sacrificial meat was an ordinary social activity; temple banquets were part of civic and commercial life. Abstaining from idol-food meant a visible, costly break with pagan religious practice.

2. "Sexual immorality" (porneia). The word porneia has a wide semantic range: prostitution, fornication, incest, and any sexual practice forbidden by the Torah. In Leviticus 18, a comprehensive list of prohibited sexual relations is given, and Leviticus 18:24-30 warns that these practices defiled the nations who formerly inhabited the land. The inclusion of porneia in this list addresses sexual practices that were commonplace in Greco-Roman culture but prohibited by Israel's scriptures — particularly temple prostitution and forms of sexual union that Leviticus classifies as abominations. This is not a novel prohibition but the assertion that the moral standards of Leviticus 18 apply to Gentile believers as well.

3. "What has been strangled" (tou pniktou). An animal that has been strangled — killed without draining its blood — retains its blood in the meat. This prohibition connects directly to the next item and to the foundational command of Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 17:13-14: the blood must be drained from any animal killed for food.

4. "Blood" (tou haimatos). The prohibition of blood consumption goes back to the earliest post-flood legislation: "But you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood" (Genesis 9:4). This command was given to Noah — before the Abrahamic covenant, before circumcision, before the Law of Moses. Leviticus 17:10-12 repeats and expands it: "If any one of the house of Israel or of the strangers who sojourn among them eats any blood, I will set my face against that person... For the life of the flesh is in the blood." The Leviticus passage explicitly includes "the strangers who sojourn among them" (ton proselytion ton paroikounton en hymin in the LXX) — Gentiles living among Israel were already bound by the blood prohibition. The prohibition of blood was never restricted to the circumcised; it was imposed on all who lived within Israel's community.

The four prohibitions, taken together, have a discernible logic. They correspond closely to the requirements placed on the "stranger sojourning among Israel" (ger) in Leviticus 17-18. Leviticus 17:8-9 prohibits the sojourner from offering sacrifice outside the designated place (cf. idol food). Leviticus 17:10-12 prohibits the sojourner from consuming blood. Leviticus 17:13 requires that blood be drained from hunted game (cf. things strangled). Leviticus 18:6-26 prohibits the sojourner from the sexual practices listed there (cf. porneia). These chapters in Leviticus define the minimum requirements for Gentiles living among God's people — not full Torah observance, not circumcision, but the avoidance of idolatry, blood consumption, improperly slaughtered meat, and sexual immorality.

James's prohibitions thus follow a known scriptural pattern: the Gentile in Israel's midst was always held to certain standards without being required to become an Israelite. The Torah itself distinguished between what was required of the ger (the resident alien) and what was required of the native-born Israelite. James applies this existing scriptural distinction to the new situation: Gentile believers are like the sojourners in Israel — part of the community, bound by the community's core standards, but not required to become Israelites through circumcision.

"For from ancient generations Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him, for he is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" (15:21).

This final statement of James's speech has been read in multiple ways, but in context, its function appears to be an explanation of why the four prohibitions are appropriate. Moses "is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" — the Torah is publicly accessible, regularly recited, in every city where there is a Jewish community. James is observing that these prohibitions are not arbitrary but are rooted in Torah commands that are well known and publicly read. The Gentile believers will encounter these standards whenever they interact with the Jewish community, since the synagogue readings will make Moses's commands audible. The four prohibitions are also, in practical terms, the requirements that make table fellowship between Jewish and Gentile believers possible. A Jewish believer who observes the food laws cannot eat with a Gentile believer who consumes blood, eats strangled meat, or partakes of idol food. The four prohibitions create the conditions under which the unified community — Jewish and Gentile together — can share meals, worship together, and live as one body.

Verses 22-29: The Letter

"Then it seemed good to the apostles and the elders, with the whole church, to choose men from among them and send them to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas. They sent Judas called Barsabbas, and Silas, leading men among the brothers" (15:22).

The decision is corporate: the apostles, the elders, and "the whole church" (hole te ekklesia) agree. This is not James's private ruling; it is ratified by the entire community. The verb edoxen ("it seemed good") is the standard term for official decrees in Greek civic assemblies — it appears in inscriptions introducing governmental decisions. The Jerusalem community acts as a deliberative body issuing a formal resolution.

Two envoys are chosen to accompany Paul and Barnabas: Judas Barsabbas (possibly related to the Joseph Barsabbas of Acts 1:23, who was a candidate to replace Judas Iscariot) and Silas (also known by the Latin form Silvanus, who will become Paul's companion in Acts 15:40-18:5 and is co-sender of 1 Thessalonians 1:1 and 2 Thessalonians 1:1). They are described as "leading men among the brothers" (andras hegoumenous en tois adelphois) — their authority lends weight to the letter they carry. The decision to send Jerusalem's own representatives alongside Paul and Barnabas ensures that the Antioch community hears the verdict not only from those who opposed the requirement of circumcision but from Jerusalem's own chosen delegates.

"With the following letter: 'The brothers, both the apostles and the elders, to the brothers who are of the Gentiles in Antioch and Syria and Cilicia, greetings'" (15:23).

The letter is addressed to Gentile believers (tois... adelphois tois ex ethnon — "to the brothers who are from the Gentiles") in three regions: Antioch (where the crisis originated), Syria (the broader region around Antioch), and Cilicia (Paul's home region, where communities also existed). The salutation "greetings" (chairein) is the standard Greek epistolary opening.

"Since we have heard that some persons have gone out from us and troubled you with words, unsettling your minds, although we gave them no instructions, it has seemed good to us, having come to one accord, to choose men and send them to you with our beloved Barnabas and Paul, men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. We have therefore sent Judas and Silas, who themselves will tell you the same things by word of mouth" (15:24-27).

The letter contains a significant disavowal: the men who went to Antioch and taught that circumcision was necessary for salvation were "not authorized" (hois ou diesteilametha — "we gave them no instructions/commands"). The verb diastellomai means "to give orders, to command, to charge." The Jerusalem leadership explicitly disclaims responsibility for the teaching that provoked the crisis. Those men acted on their own, without apostolic or elder sanction. The letter calls their effect "troubling" (etaraxan — from tarasso, "to agitate, to disturb, to throw into confusion") and "unsettling" (anaskeuazontes — literally "dismantling, tearing down" — a word used for the demolition of buildings or the dismantling of fortifications). The teaching did not merely cause confusion; it tore down what had been built in Antioch.

The commendation of "our beloved Barnabas and Paul" (tois agapetois hemon Barnaba kai Paulo) — beloved is agapetois, a term of deep affection and esteem — and the description of them as "men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (anthropois paradedokosi tas psychas auton hyper tou onomatos tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou) serve to validate their authority. The verb paradedokosi (from paradidomi, "to hand over, to deliver up") used for their lives means they have "handed over their lives" — they have put their lives at risk. The stonings, the flights, the persecutions of Acts 13-14 are in view. Jerusalem honors their sacrifice and implicitly endorses their work.

"For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements: that you abstain from what has been sacrificed to idols, and from blood, and from what has been strangled, and from sexual immorality. If you keep yourselves from these, you will do well. Farewell" (15:28-29).

The letter's most remarkable claim is in its first phrase: "It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (edoxen gar to hagio pneumati kai hemin). The community claims that its decision is not merely human consensus but the Spirit's verdict. The conjunction "and" (kai) places the Spirit's authority alongside the community's discernment. The Spirit who fell on Cornelius's household (Acts 10:44), who sent Barnabas and Saul on the first mission (Acts 13:2), who directed the entire movement from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth, is now identified as the co-author of this decision. The community does not simply announce its own judgment; it claims to have discerned the Spirit's mind.

The prohibitions are restated in slightly different form. "Things polluted by idols" (alisgamaton ton eidolon, v. 20) is now "what has been sacrificed to idols" (eidolothyton). The four items are the same; the language is clarified. The total requirement is described as "no greater burden" (meden pleon... baros — "no additional weight/burden"). The word baros ("burden, weight") connects to Peter's "yoke" language in verse 10. The council has determined that these four prohibitions are burden enough; nothing additional — not circumcision, not full Torah observance — is to be imposed.

The final clause — "If you keep yourselves from these, you will do well" (ex hon diatirountes heautous eu praxete) — is conditional. The benefits of the decision are contingent on the Gentile believers' actual observance of the four prohibitions. The formula eu praxete ("you will do well, you will prosper, you will act rightly") is a standard epistolary closing phrase in Greek letters, but in context it also carries practical weight: abstaining from these things will position the Gentile believers rightly within the community of God's people.

Verses 30-35: The Letter's Reception in Antioch

"So when they were sent off, they went down to Antioch, and having gathered the congregation together, they delivered the letter. And when they had read it, they rejoiced because of its encouragement" (15:30-31).

The delegation arrives in Antioch and convenes the community. The letter is read publicly. The response is "joy" (echaresan) at the "encouragement" (paraklesis). The word paraklesis — "comfort, encouragement, exhortation" — is the same word used in Acts 13:15 for the synagogue rulers' invitation to Paul: "if you have any word of encouragement." The Jerusalem council's letter is received as a paraklesis — a word that comforts, that encourages, that builds up. The contrast with the effect of the unauthorized teachers is stark: those men "troubled" and "unsettled" (v. 24); the letter brings joy and encouragement.

"And Judas and Silas, who were themselves prophets, encouraged and strengthened the brothers with many words" (15:32).

Judas and Silas are identified as "prophets" (prophetai) — they are not merely letter-carriers but Spirit-gifted speakers who can expound and apply the letter's significance. They "encouraged" (parekalesan — from parakaleo, the verb corresponding to the noun paraklesis) and "strengthened" (epesterixan — from episterizo, "to establish firmly, to buttress, to make stable") the brothers "with many words" (dia logou pollou). The word epesterixan is the same verb used in Acts 14:22 for what Paul and Barnabas did on their return journey: "strengthening the souls of the disciples." The Antioch community, destabilized by the unauthorized teaching, is now re-established and re-fortified by both the letter's authority and the prophetic ministry of Jerusalem's delegates.

"And after they had spent some time, they were sent off in peace by the brothers to those who had sent them" (15:33).

Judas and Silas are "sent off in peace" (apelythesan met' eirenes). The phrase "in peace" (met' eirenes) indicates that the relationship between Antioch and Jerusalem is reconciled. The crisis that began with "no small dissension" (v. 2) ends with peace. The community that was troubled is now at rest.

"But Paul and Barnabas remained in Antioch, teaching and preaching the word of the Lord, with many others also" (15:35).

Paul and Barnabas stay and resume their work. The verbs are paired: "teaching" (didaskontes) and "preaching the good news" (euangelizomenoi) — instruction for believers and proclamation for new hearers. The phrase "with many others also" (kai met' heteron pollon) indicates that the Antioch community has a substantial body of teachers and preachers, not just two. The community is mature, gifted, and active.

Verses 36-41: The Separation of Paul and Barnabas

"And after some days Paul said to Barnabas, 'Let us return and visit the brothers in every city where we proclaimed the word of the Lord, and see how they are'" (15:36).

Paul proposes a second journey — not a new mission to unreached areas but a pastoral visit to the communities established on the first journey. The concern is for the welfare of existing believers: "see how they are" (pos echousin — "how they are doing, what their condition is"). This is pastoral care, not evangelistic expansion. The communities in Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe were left in the hands of newly appointed elders (14:23) in an environment of active persecution (14:19-22). Paul wants to know if they have survived, if they have held fast, if they need further teaching or strengthening.

"And Barnabas wanted to take with them John called Mark. But Paul thought best not to take with them one who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia and had not gone with them to the work" (15:37-38).

The disagreement concerns John Mark, who "withdrew" (apostanta — from aphistemi, "to depart, to withdraw, to fall away") from them in Pamphylia during the first journey (13:13). Paul's assessment of this withdrawal is sharp: Mark "had not gone with them to the work" (me synelthonta autois eis to ergon). The word "the work" (to ergon) echoes the Spirit's commissioning language in Acts 13:2: "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work (to ergon) to which I have called them." Mark's departure was not merely a personal decision; it was a withdrawal from the Spirit-commissioned work. Paul regards this as disqualifying.

Barnabas, characteristically, wants to give Mark another chance. The text does not report Barnabas's reasoning, but his history is relevant. Barnabas was the one who vouched for the converted Saul when the Jerusalem disciples were afraid of him (9:27). Barnabas saw potential in people that others dismissed. His instinct is restorative. Mark is his cousin (Colossians 4:10), which adds a familial dimension but does not reduce the issue to nepotism — Barnabas's entire history in Acts is one of extending trust and opportunity.

"And there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other. Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus, and Paul chose Silas and departed, having been commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord" (15:39-40).

The word for the disagreement is paroxysmos — "a sharp irritation, a provocation, a paroxysm." It is a strong word, denoting not polite disagreement but heated conflict. The author does not soften it or explain it away. The two men who were co-commissioned by the Spirit (13:2), who endured persecution together (13:50; 14:5, 19), who stood together against the circumcision party (15:2, 12), and who were jointly commended by the Jerusalem letter as "men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (15:26) — these two men cannot agree and must part.

The narrative records the result without rendering a verdict on who was right. Barnabas takes Mark to Cyprus — his homeland (4:36), where the first missionary journey began (13:4). Paul takes Silas — one of the Jerusalem delegates, a prophet (15:32), a Roman citizen (16:37) — and heads through Syria and Cilicia. The single mission becomes two. The author notes that Paul was "commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord" (paradotheis te chariti tou kyriou hypo ton adelphon) — the Antioch community's blessing rests on Paul's departure. Whether the same commendation was given to Barnabas, the text does not say. The silence may be simply a matter of the narrative following Paul's trajectory rather than Barnabas's, since Paul will be the protagonist for the remainder of Acts.

"And he went through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches" (15:41).

Paul's journey begins with the strengthening (episterizon — the same verb from 14:22 and 15:32) of existing communities. The mission that will eventually carry Paul to Macedonia, Achaia, Asia, and Rome begins with the quiet, unglamorous work of pastoral care. The Jerusalem council's decision accompanies him: the letter authorizing Gentile freedom from circumcision will be delivered to every community he visits (16:4).

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 15 is the structural center of the book of Acts. Everything before it builds toward the question it resolves; everything after it proceeds from the answer it gives.

The question is stated in verse 1: "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved." Must Gentile believers become Jews in order to be saved?

The answer comes from three converging lines of evidence:

Peter (vv. 7-11) appeals to God's demonstrated action. God gave the Holy Spirit to uncircumcised Gentiles at Cornelius's house. God made no distinction between Jewish and Gentile believers. God cleansed Gentile hearts by faith, not by circumcision. Therefore, requiring circumcision is testing God — challenging his own verdict.

Barnabas and Paul (v. 12) appeal to God's ongoing action. God performed signs and wonders among the Gentiles on the first missionary journey. The same divine authentication that attended the apostles' work in Jerusalem attended the Gentile mission in Asia Minor.

James (vv. 13-21) appeals to God's declared purpose. The prophet Amos foretold that the restoration of David's fallen tent would result in the Gentiles seeking the Lord — Gentiles who bear God's name, included without becoming Israelites. This was known "from of old" — it was always the plan.

The convergence is complete: what God did (Peter), what God is doing (Barnabas and Paul), and what God said he would do (James/Amos) all point to the same conclusion. Gentile believers are not to be burdened with circumcision or full Torah observance. Their salvation is through grace, the same grace by which Jewish believers are saved (v. 11). The four prohibitions define the boundaries of community life — prohibitions rooted in Torah's own provisions for the sojourner in Israel — without imposing the full yoke of the Law.

The chapter's deeper logic follows the pattern of the entire book. Acts has narrated a series of boundary crossings, each initiated by God and each initially resisted by the community: the Samaritans (chapter 8), the Ethiopian eunuch (chapter 8), Cornelius (chapters 10-11), the Antioch community (chapter 11), the first missionary journey (chapters 13-14). At each stage, God acted first, and the community caught up to what God had done. Acts 15 is the moment of formal acknowledgment — the community's collective recognition that what God has been doing all along is consistent, intentional, and prophetically grounded. The council does not create a new policy; it ratifies what God has already enacted.

The chapter also marks a transition. Peter speaks for the last time in Acts. Barnabas, after the separation in verses 39-40, disappears from the narrative. James emerges as the leader of the Jerusalem community. Paul, with Silas as his new companion, carries the council's decision into the western world. The cast of characters shifts. The question that dominated Acts 1-15 — how the movement would relate to the Gentile world — has been formally answered. The question that will dominate Acts 16-28 is different: how will the message advance through the Roman world, and what will it cost the messengers who carry it?

The separation of Paul and Barnabas (vv. 36-41) is the chapter's most human moment. The men who together endured stoning, persecution, theological controversy, and a cross-continental journey cannot agree on a personnel decision. The author does not moralize. He records the paroxysm, the separation, and the result: two missions instead of one, and the narrative follows Paul. The effect is multiplication through conflict — a pattern that echoes the scattering after Stephen's death (8:1-4), where persecution produced geographic expansion. Here, personal disagreement produces strategic multiplication. Barnabas goes to Cyprus; Paul goes to Syria, Cilicia, and beyond. The word goes further because the messengers divide.

The letter's claim — "It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (15:28) — is the chapter's most audacious assertion. The community declares that its deliberation was not merely human wisdom but the Spirit's own guidance. This claim is grounded in the entire narrative of Acts: the Spirit who spoke at Pentecost (chapter 2), who directed Philip to the Ethiopian (8:29), who told Peter to go with Cornelius's men (10:19-20), who commissioned Barnabas and Saul (13:2), who confirmed the Gentile mission with signs and wonders (14:3) — this same Spirit has been present in the deliberations of the council and has guided the community to its conclusion. The Spirit's agency in the decision is consistent with the Spirit's agency throughout Acts: the Spirit acts, and the community recognizes and follows.

The chapter's final word on the soteriological question is Peter's statement in verse 11: "We believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will." The direction of comparison is decisive. The standard of salvation is not Jewish practice into which Gentiles must be absorbed. The standard is grace, through which all — Jew and Gentile alike — are saved. Peter does not argue that Gentiles can be saved as Jews are, which would maintain the Jewish experience as the norm. He argues that Jews are saved as Gentiles are — by grace alone, through faith. This inversion of expected priority does not diminish Israel's history or the Law's significance; it identifies the deeper ground on which both Israel's story and the Gentile mission stand. That ground is grace. The Law served its purpose within Israel's history, but the ground beneath the Law was always grace, and grace is what has now been revealed as the universal basis of salvation for everyone who believes.
