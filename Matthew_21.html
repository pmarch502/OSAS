Matthew 21

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 20): The author continued the thread begun at 19:30 ("many who are first will be last, and the last first") with the parable of the workers in the vineyard (20:1-16), in which those hired at the eleventh hour received the same wage as those who labored all day. The parable ended with the identical statement in reverse order: "So the last will be first, and the first last" (20:16). The landowner's response to the grumbling workers was sharp: "Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?" (20:15). The parable thus established that the kingdom operates on the principle of the owner's sovereign generosity, not the workers' calculation of merit — and that those who consider themselves first by virtue of longer service may find themselves resentful of the grace extended to latecomers. Jesus then delivered the third and most detailed passion prediction (20:17-19): "Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem. And the Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him over to the Gentiles to be mocked and flogged and crucified, and he will be raised on the third day." This is the first time crucifixion is explicitly named, and the first time the role of the Gentiles is specified. The mother of James and John then asked Jesus to grant her sons seats at his right and left in his kingdom (20:20-21), exposing the disciples' persistent inability to understand the nature of Jesus's kingship. Jesus responded: "You do not know what you are asking" (20:22), and taught that greatness in his community is defined by servanthood: "Whoever would be great among you must be your servant (diakonos), and whoever would be first among you must be your slave (doulos), even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (20:26-28). The chapter closed at Jericho, where two blind men cried out, "Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!" (20:30-31). Despite the crowd's attempt to silence them, Jesus stopped, was "moved with compassion" (splagchnistheis, 20:34), and healed them. They followed him. The chapter thus moved from sovereign generosity (the vineyard parable) to the explicit prediction of death in Jerusalem, to the redefinition of greatness as servanthood, to the healing of the blind who recognized Jesus as Son of David — and the road now leads directly to Jerusalem.

What comes after (Matthew 22): The confrontation with the religious leadership that dominates chapter 21 intensifies in chapter 22. Jesus tells the parable of the wedding feast (22:1-14), in which a king sends invitations to a marriage feast for his son, but the invited guests refuse to come — some making light of it, others killing the messengers. The king destroys those murderers and burns their city (22:7), then sends servants into the streets to gather "both bad and good" (22:10). When the king finds a guest without a wedding garment, that man is cast into "the outer darkness" where there is "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (22:13), and Jesus concludes: "For many are called, but few are chosen" (22:14). The parable extends the vineyard tenants parable of 21:33-46 by moving from Israel's leaders who rejected and killed the prophets to the broader question of who will actually participate in the kingdom and on what terms. The Pharisees then attempt to trap Jesus with a question about paying taxes to Caesar (22:15-22); the Sadducees test him on the resurrection (22:23-33); a lawyer asks about the greatest commandment (22:34-40); and Jesus himself poses a question about David's Son and David's Lord (22:41-46) that silences them all: "No one was able to answer him a word, nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions" (22:46). Chapter 22 thus completes the pattern begun in chapter 21: Jesus enters Jerusalem as the one to whom the kingdom's authority belongs, confronts the religious leadership's failure, tells parables that announce the transfer of the kingdom from those who rejected the owner's messengers to those who will produce its fruit, and then answers and silences every challenge the leadership can mount.

How chapter 21 connects them: Chapter 20 ended on the road from Jericho to Jerusalem, with the third passion prediction fresh and the blind men's acclamation of Jesus as "Son of David" still in the air. Chapter 21 is the arrival. Everything that has been building since 16:21 — the passion predictions, the teaching about servanthood, the journey to Jerusalem — now reaches the city itself. Jesus enters Jerusalem in a manner that deliberately enacts the prophecy of Zechariah 9:9, presenting himself publicly as Israel's king. But the kingship he enacts is the paradoxical one the author has been constructing: he comes "humble" (praus), mounted on a donkey, not a warhorse. The "Son of David" acclamation that the blind men at Jericho spoke privately now erupts from the crowds in the streets of Jerusalem. Once inside the city, Jesus goes immediately to the temple and overturns the tables — an act of prophetic judgment that draws on Jeremiah 7 and Isaiah 56 and asserts his authority over the center of Israel's religious life. The rest of the chapter consists of a sustained confrontation between Jesus and the chief priests and elders, conducted through the question of authority (vv. 23-27) and two parables (the two sons, vv. 28-32; the tenants, vv. 33-46) that together constitute an indictment: Israel's leaders have failed to respond to God's messengers, failed to produce the fruit God requires, and will see the kingdom transferred from them to others. The movement from chapter 20's road to Jerusalem through chapter 21's arrival and confrontation to chapter 22's escalating tests is continuous — it is the single sustained narrative of Jesus's final days in Jerusalem, where the clash between his authority and the religious establishment's authority comes to its decisive point.

The Entry into Jerusalem: Zechariah's King Enacted (vv. 1-11)

"And when they drew near to Jerusalem and came to Bethphage, to the Mount of Olives, then Jesus sent two disciples, saying to them, 'Go into the village in front of you, and immediately you will find a donkey tied, and a colt with her (onon dedemenon kai polon met' autes). Untie them and bring them to me. If anyone says anything to you, you shall say, "The Lord needs them," and he will send them at once.'"

The geography is precise. Bethphage is a village on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, the last settlement before Jerusalem on the road from Jericho. The Mount of Olives carries its own prophetic weight: Zechariah 14:4 declares that "on that day his feet shall stand on the Mount of Olives that lies before Jerusalem on the east," followed by the LORD's coming with all his holy ones and the establishment of his universal kingship (Zechariah 14:9). The author places Jesus at precisely the location where Zechariah located the LORD's eschatological arrival.

The instruction to find a donkey and a colt is distinctive. The author specifies both animals — "a donkey tied, and a colt with her" — where the parallel accounts in Mark (11:2) and Luke (19:30) mention only the colt. This doubling corresponds to the author's reading of Zechariah 9:9, which he is about to cite.

"This took place to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet, saying, 'Say to the daughter of Zion, "Behold, your king is coming to you, humble (praus), and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a beast of burden (epi onon kai epi polon huion hypozugiou)."'"

The citation combines Isaiah 62:11 ("Say to the daughter of Zion") with Zechariah 9:9. The Zechariah text in Hebrew uses parallelism — "humble and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey" — where the two lines describe the same animal in parallel. The author appears to read the text as describing two animals, and accordingly has Jesus ride with both the donkey and the colt present. Whether the author intends a literal two-animal reading or is simply preserving the wording of the prophecy with both terms present, the theological point is the same: Jesus is enacting the arrival of Zechariah's king.

The critical word in the citation is praus — "humble" or "gentle." This is the same word Jesus used to describe himself at 11:29: "I am gentle (praus) and lowly (tapeinos) in heart." It is the same word from the third beatitude: "Blessed are the meek (praeis), for they shall inherit the earth" (5:5). The king who enters Jerusalem is defined by the same quality that defines his followers. He does not come on a warhorse, as a conquering general would, but on a donkey — the animal of peace, of the working peasant, of humble daily labor. The entry is a deliberate contrast to every expectation of military triumph. Zechariah 9:9 itself makes this explicit: the verse continues (in the Hebrew text, though the author does not cite this portion) with "I will cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the war horse from Jerusalem; and the battle bow shall be cut off, and he shall speak peace to the nations." The king who comes on a donkey is the king who abolishes the instruments of war.

"The disciples went and did as Jesus had directed them. They brought the donkey and the colt and put on them their cloaks (epetheken epano auton ta himatia), and he sat on them."

The placing of cloaks on the animals echoes 2 Kings 9:13, where Jehu's officers spread their garments under him on the steps when he was proclaimed king. The garments function as an improvised royal covering — the disciples are preparing a throne, however humble, for the one they recognize as king.

"Most of the crowd (ho de pleistos ochlos) spread their cloaks on the road, and others cut branches from the trees and spread them on the road."

The spreading of garments on the road is again royal language, as in the Jehu episode. The cutting of branches recalls the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot), when palm and other branches were waved in celebration (Leviticus 23:40). In 1 Maccabees 13:51, when Simon Maccabeus entered the citadel of Jerusalem after expelling the Gentile garrison, the people entered "with praise and palm branches, and with harps and cymbals and stringed instruments, and with hymns and songs." The crowd is greeting Jesus with the gestures reserved for a king entering his city.

"And the crowds that went before him and that followed him were shouting, 'Hosanna to the Son of David (hosanna to huio Dauid)! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord (eulogemenos ho erchomenos en onomati kyriou)! Hosanna in the highest (hosanna en tois hypsistois)!'"

"Hosanna" (hosanna) is a transliteration of the Hebrew hoshi'a na, meaning "Save, please!" or "Save now!" It comes from Psalm 118:25: "Save us, we pray, O LORD! O LORD, we pray, give us success!" Psalm 118 is a processional psalm used at major festivals, particularly Tabernacles and Passover. The next verse of the psalm — "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD" (Psalm 118:26) — is what the crowd cites. In its original setting, this blessing was spoken by the priests to the worshippers entering the temple. Here it is directed at Jesus as a messianic acclamation. The phrase "he who comes" (ho erchomenos) carries eschatological weight: at 11:3, John the Baptist from prison asked, "Are you the one who is to come (ho erchomenos), or shall we look for another?" The crowd answers the question John asked.

The title "Son of David" (huios Dauid) has appeared at critical moments: the genealogy's opening (1:1), the blind men's cry (9:27, 20:30-31), the Canaanite woman's appeal (15:22), and the crowd's astonishment at 12:23 ("Can this be the Son of David?"). Now it is publicly proclaimed as Jesus enters the capital. The Davidic claim is being made in the city of David, within sight of David's throne.

"And when he entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred (eseisthe pasa he polis), saying, 'Who is this (tis estin houtos)?' And the crowds said, 'This is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee (houtos estin ho prophetes Iesous ho apo Nazareth tes Galilaias).'"

The verb seio (to shake, to quake) is striking. The author uses this word for earthquakes — it will appear at 27:51 when the earth shakes at Jesus's death and at 28:2 when the earth shakes at his resurrection. The city "was shaken" — the arrival of the king is seismic. The same word appeared at 2:3, when "all Jerusalem was troubled" (etarachthe) at the report of the magi; now at the other end of the narrative, the city shakes again at Jesus's entry. The two responses form a frame: Jerusalem was disturbed by news of the king's birth, and Jerusalem quakes at the king's arrival. Both times, the city asks who this is, and both times, the identification carries threat.

The crowd identifies Jesus as "the prophet ... from Nazareth of Galilee." The identification as "the prophet" (ho prophetes) with the definite article may allude to the prophet-like-Moses of Deuteronomy 18:15, the same text echoed at the transfiguration ("listen to him," 17:5). But the qualifier "from Nazareth of Galilee" also underscores the irony: Galilee is not where the authorities expect the Messiah to originate (cf. John 7:41, 52), and Nazareth is the obscure village from which Nathanael asked, "Can anything good come?" (John 1:46). The one who shakes Jerusalem comes from the margins.

The Temple Cleansed: Prophetic Judgment on Israel's Worship (vv. 12-17)

"And Jesus entered the temple (to hieron) and drove out all who sold and bought in the temple, and he overturned the tables of the money-changers (ton kollybiston) and the seats of those who sold pigeons (ton polounton tas peristeras)."

The term hieron refers to the entire temple complex, not just the inner sanctuary (naos). The commercial activity Jesus disrupts was located in the Court of the Gentiles — the outermost court of the temple, the only area where non-Jews could come to pray. Money-changers (kollybistai) were necessary because the temple tax had to be paid in Tyrian shekels (the only currency accepted for temple payments, due to its silver purity), so pilgrims carrying Roman or other coinage needed to exchange it. The pigeon sellers provided the sacrificial animals required by Torah for the purification offerings of the poor (Leviticus 5:7, 12:8). These commercial activities were not inherently illegitimate — they served the temple's ritual functions. Jesus's action is not directed against commerce as such but against what the commerce has become, as the next verse explains.

"He said to them, 'It is written, "My house shall be called a house of prayer (oikos proseuchis klethesetai)," but you make it a den of robbers (spelaion leston).'"

The statement combines two prophetic texts. The first is Isaiah 56:7: "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (LXX: ho gar oikos mou oikos proseuches klethesetai pasin tois ethnesin). The full text of Isaiah 56:7 includes "for all peoples" (pasin tois ethnesin) — the house of prayer is specifically for the nations, for the Gentiles. This is significant because the commercial activity occupied the Court of the Gentiles — the very space where non-Jews were supposed to be able to approach God in prayer. The commerce had colonized the space designated for Gentile worship.

The second text is Jeremiah 7:11: "Has this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers (spelaion leston) in your eyes?" Jeremiah 7 is the prophet's temple sermon, delivered at the gate of the temple, warning Israel not to trust in the words "the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD" (Jeremiah 7:4) while practicing injustice, oppression, and idolatry. Jeremiah's point was that the temple had become a refuge for those who practiced wickedness — a "den" (spelaion, cave) to which robbers retreat after committing their crimes, feeling safe because they are in sacred space. The temple does not sanctify those who misuse it; it indicts them. Jesus is placing himself in the role of Jeremiah, pronouncing the same judgment: the temple has become a place where those who exploit and oppress take refuge under the cover of religious legitimacy.

The word lestai (robbers, bandits) is stronger than "thieves" (kleptai). A lestes is a violent criminal, a bandit — the word used for those crucified alongside Jesus (27:38, 44) and for Barabbas in some traditions. The accusation is not of petty theft but of systemic exploitation carried out under the protection of sacred space.

"And the blind and the lame came to him in the temple, and he healed them (kai proselthon auto typhloi kai choloi en to hiero, kai etherapeusen autous)."

This detail, unique to the author's account, is theologically charged. In 2 Samuel 5:8, after David captured Jerusalem, a saying arose: "The blind and the lame shall not come into the house." This was understood to exclude the physically disabled from the temple. Jesus, having just cleared the temple of its commercial operations, immediately fills the space with the very people who were excluded. The Son of David's temple is not a marketplace for the privileged but a place of healing for the marginalized. The action demonstrates in concrete terms what the temple was meant to be: a house of prayer where God's restorative power is available to all, including those whom the existing order excluded.

"But when the chief priests and the scribes saw the wonderful things that he did (tas thaumasias ha epoiesen), and the children crying out in the temple, 'Hosanna to the Son of David!' they were indignant (eganaktesan)."

The chief priests and scribes respond to two things: the healings and the children's acclamation. The children in the temple are shouting the same "Hosanna to the Son of David" that the crowds shouted at the entry. The leadership is "indignant" (aganakteo — the same word used at 20:24 when the ten disciples were indignant at James and John's request for positions of honor). Their indignation is provoked by the messianic title being used in the temple — the claim that this Galilean is the Son of David, spoken in the very precinct where David's heir exercises authority. The Davidic claim, made in the temple, is a direct challenge to the authority of the chief priests who administer it.

"And they said to him, 'Do you hear what these are saying?' And Jesus said to them, 'Yes; have you never read, "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise (ek stomatos nepion kai thelazonton katertiso ainon)"?'"

Jesus responds with Psalm 8:2 (LXX 8:3): "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise." In the original psalm, this is addressed to God — it is God who has ordained praise from the youngest and weakest to silence the enemy and the avenger. Jesus applies this text to himself: the praise directed at him by children is the praise that God himself has prepared. The implication is that to silence the children's Hosannas would be to oppose what God has ordained. The chief priests, who should recognize the fulfillment of scripture when it occurs in their own temple, are instead offended by it. The contrast is sharp: children and the blind and lame recognize who Jesus is; the religious experts do not.

"And leaving them, he went out of the city to Bethany (Bethanian) and lodged there."

Jesus withdraws to Bethany, about two miles east of Jerusalem on the far side of the Mount of Olives. This pattern of entering Jerusalem by day and withdrawing at night continues through the passion week. Bethany is the home of Lazarus, Martha, and Mary (John 11:1; cf. Matthew 26:6, "the house of Simon the leper" in Bethany). The withdrawal may reflect practical safety — Jerusalem is hostile territory — but it also creates a daily rhythm of approach and departure that frames the confrontations of the following days.

The Fig Tree Cursed: Enacted Judgment on Fruitlessness (vv. 18-22)

"In the morning, as he was returning to the city, he became hungry. And seeing a fig tree by the wayside, he went to it and found nothing on it but only leaves (ei me phylla monon). And he said to it, 'May no fruit ever come from you again (ou meketi ek sou karpos genetai eis ton aiona)!' And the fig tree withered at once (exeranthe parachrema he syke)."

This action must be read as a prophetic sign-act, not a personal expression of frustration. The prophets of Israel frequently performed symbolic actions that enacted the reality they proclaimed: Isaiah walked naked and barefoot for three years as a sign against Egypt and Cush (Isaiah 20:2-4); Jeremiah smashed a potter's flask at the Valley of Hinnom (Jeremiah 19:1-13); Ezekiel lay on his side for 390 days (Ezekiel 4:4-6). The cursing of the fig tree belongs to this tradition of enacted prophecy.

The fig tree in Israel's prophetic literature consistently represents Israel itself or its leadership. Jeremiah 8:13: "When I would gather them, declares the LORD, there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree; even the leaves are withered." Hosea 9:10: "Like grapes in the wilderness, I found Israel. Like the first fruit on the fig tree in its first season, I saw your fathers." Micah 7:1: "Woe is me! For I have become as when the summer fruit has been gathered, as when the grapes have been gleaned: there is no cluster to eat, no first-ripe fig that my soul desires." Joel 1:7: "It has laid waste my vine and splintered my fig tree." In each case, the absence of fruit on the fig tree signifies Israel's failure to produce the response God sought.

The phrase "nothing on it but only leaves" is the critical detail. The tree has the appearance of fruitfulness — leaves suggest life and health — but produces no actual fruit. This is precisely the accusation the author has been building through the preceding episodes. The temple has all the external apparatus of worship — the commerce, the ritual, the institutional structure — but has become a "den of robbers" rather than a "house of prayer." The religious leadership has the credentials and authority but does not recognize the one whom God has sent. The fig tree with leaves but no fruit is an enacted parable of the temple establishment: impressive in appearance, barren in substance.

The immediate withering (exeranthe parachrema — "dried up at once") intensifies the sign. In the parallel account in Mark (11:12-14, 20-21), the disciples do not notice the withering until the next morning. The author compresses the timeline so that the consequence is instantaneous, emphasizing the immediacy of the judgment.

"When the disciples saw it, they marveled, saying, 'How did the fig tree wither at once (pos parachrema exeranthe he syke)?' And Jesus answered them, 'Truly, I say to you, if you have faith and do not doubt (ean echete pistin kai me diakrithete), you will not only do what has been done to the fig tree, but even if you say to this mountain, "Be taken up and thrown into the sea," it will happen. And whatever you ask in prayer, you will receive, if you have faith (ean pisteuete).'"

The disciples ask about mechanism — how did the tree wither so quickly? Jesus redirects to the principle: faith that does not doubt (me diakrithete — the verb diakrino means to waver, to be divided in one's mind) can accomplish even greater things. The reference to "this mountain" (to orei touto) is not generic. Jesus is standing between the Mount of Olives and Jerusalem, within sight of the temple mount. The mountain cast into the sea may echo the prophetic tradition of Zechariah 14:10, where the land around Jerusalem is leveled, and it may anticipate Jesus's own prophecy of the temple's destruction (24:1-2). In context, the saying addresses the disciples' capacity to participate in God's acts of judgment and restoration through faith and prayer.

The connection to the immediately preceding sign-act is important. The fig tree's judgment was an act of divine power channeled through Jesus's word. Jesus tells the disciples that they, through faith, have access to the same power. The community formed around Jesus is not merely an audience to his prophetic acts but a participant in them. The condition, however, is faith without internal division — a condition that recalls the "little faith" (oligopistia) the author has repeatedly diagnosed in the disciples (6:30, 8:26, 14:31, 16:8, 17:20).

The Question of Authority: John and Jesus (vv. 23-27)

"And when he entered the temple, the chief priests and the elders of the people came up to him as he was teaching, and said, 'By what authority are you doing these things (en poia exousia tauta poieis), and who gave you this authority (kai tis soi edoken ten exousian tauten)?'"

The question comes from the two groups who hold official power in Jerusalem: the chief priests (archiereis), who control the temple, and the elders of the people (presbyteroi tou laou), who form the lay aristocracy of the Sanhedrin. Together they represent the institutional authority of Israel. Their question is pointed: "By what authority?" (en poia exousia — literally, "in what kind of authority"). The word exousia (authority, right, power) has been a central term in the document. At 7:29, the crowds recognized that Jesus taught "as one having authority" (hos exousian echon) in contrast to their scribes. At 9:6, Jesus claimed "authority on earth to forgive sins." At 10:1, he gave the twelve "authority over unclean spirits." At 28:18, the risen Jesus will declare, "All authority (pasa exousia) in heaven and on earth has been given to me." The chief priests' question is whether Jesus possesses the kind of authority that would justify his actions in the temple — the overturning of tables, the healings, the teaching. The implicit accusation is that he does not; he has no institutional sanction, no appointment, no official standing.

"These things" (tauta) refers not merely to his current teaching but to the entirety of what he has done since entering Jerusalem: the public entry as messianic king, the disruption of the temple commerce, the healings, the acceptance of the Hosanna acclamations. All of these are acts that claim an authority the leadership has not conferred.

"Jesus answered them, 'I also will ask you one question (eroteso hymas kago logon hena), and if you tell me the answer, then I also will tell you by what authority I do these things. The baptism of John, from where did it come (to baptisma to Ioannou pothen en)? From heaven or from man (ex ouranou e ex anthropon)?'"

Jesus does not refuse to answer; he poses a counter-question that exposes the structure of the problem. The question about John's baptism is not a deflection but a diagnostic: if the chief priests and elders can correctly identify the source of John's authority, they will have the answer to their own question about Jesus's authority. John announced Jesus (3:11-12); John baptized Jesus (3:13-17); Jesus himself identified John as the Elijah who was to come (11:14, 17:12-13). To acknowledge that John's baptism was "from heaven" (ex ouranou) — that is, authorized by God — is to acknowledge the divine authority behind the one John pointed to. The question about John's authority and the question about Jesus's authority are the same question.

"And they discussed it among themselves, saying, 'If we say, "From heaven," he will say to us, "Why then did you not believe him?" But if we say, "From man," we are afraid of the crowd, for they all hold that John was a prophet.'"

The leaders' deliberation reveals that their question was not sincere inquiry but political calculation. They are not seeking truth; they are managing risk. If they affirm John's divine authorization, they indict themselves for not responding to his message — which would mean accepting Jesus. If they deny John's divine authorization, they face the crowd, who regard John as a prophet. Their inability to answer is not ignorance but willful evasion. They know the implications of both answers and refuse to accept either.

"So they answered Jesus, 'We do not know (ouk oidamen).' And he said to them, 'Neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things (oude ego lego hymin en poia exousia tauta poio).'"

The leaders' "we do not know" is not an honest confession of uncertainty but a strategic refusal to commit. Jesus's response matches their evasion: if they will not engage honestly with the evidence already before them (John's ministry, which pointed to Jesus), then further declaration from Jesus would serve no purpose. The exchange demonstrates a principle the author has established from the parables discourse onward (13:10-17): understanding is given to those who respond to what they have already received, and it is withheld from those who refuse to engage. The chief priests' refusal to answer about John disqualifies them from receiving a direct answer about Jesus.

The Parable of the Two Sons: Doing versus Saying (vv. 28-32)

"'What do you think (ti de hymin dokei)? A man had two sons (anthropos eichen tekna duo). And he went to the first and said, "Son, go and work in the vineyard today (teknon, hypage semeron ergazou en to ampeloni)." And he answered, "I will not (ou thelo)," but afterward he changed his mind and went (hysteron metameletheis apelthen). And he went to the other son and said the same. And he answered, "I go, sir (ego, kyrie)," but did not go.'"

The parable is addressed directly to the chief priests and elders (v. 23) — the same group that asked about authority. The vineyard (ampelon) is an immediately recognizable image for Israel in the prophetic tradition. Isaiah 5:1-7 is the controlling text: "The vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah are his pleasant planting." When the author uses "vineyard" in a parable directed at Israel's leadership, the first-century hearer knows the referent.

The first son initially refuses — "I will not" (ou thelo) — but then metameletheis (having changed his mind, having felt regret) goes to work. The verb metamelomai is distinct from metanoeo (to repent). Metamelomai denotes a change of feeling or purpose, a regret about one's previous decision, leading to a reversal of action. It does not carry the same theological weight as metanoeo but describes the practical reality: the son reversed course and did what the father asked.

The second son says "I go, sir" (ego, kyrie) — the address is respectful, the promise is immediate — but he does not go. His response has every mark of compliance: verbal agreement, honorific address, no objection. But it produces no action. The parable isolates the difference between speech and conduct, between profession and performance.

"'Which of the two did the will of his father (tis ek ton duo epoiesen to thelema tou patros)?' They said, 'The first.' Jesus said to them, 'Truly, I say to you, the tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you (hoi telonai kai hai pornai proagousin hymas eis ten basileian tou theou).'"

The chief priests and elders are forced to answer their own indictment. The one who "did the will of the father" (epoiesen to thelema tou patros) is not the one who said the right words but the one who ultimately acted. The phrase "the will of the father" echoes 7:21: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven." The parallel is exact: saying "Lord, Lord" without doing the Father's will is the second son. The verbal profession without corresponding action is precisely what Jesus condemned at the climax of the Sermon on the Mount.

The identification of the two sons is then made explicit. The tax collectors and prostitutes are the first son: they initially lived in open defiance of God's will, but when John came preaching repentance, they believed and changed course. The chief priests and elders are the second son: they profess obedience, hold the positions of religious authority, say the right words — but they did not respond to John's message, and they are not responding to Jesus.

"'For John came to you in the way of righteousness (en hodo dikaiosynes), and you did not believe him, but the tax collectors and the prostitutes believed him. And even when you saw it (kai hymeis idontes), you did not afterward change your minds and believe him (oude metemelethesan hysteron tou pisteusai auto).'"

The phrase "the way of righteousness" (en hodo dikaiosynes) describes John's ministry. In LXX usage, "the way of righteousness" is a path of conduct consistent with God's requirements (Proverbs 8:20, 12:28, 16:31). John came embodying and proclaiming righteous conduct — specifically, repentance and the fruits worthy of repentance (3:8). The leadership did not believe him. Tax collectors and prostitutes — the very categories that represent the most despised members of society, those furthest from institutional religion — believed and responded. And even after seeing these transformations, the leadership still did not change their minds (metemelethesan, the same verb used for the first son's change of direction). The indictment is layered: they rejected John, they rejected the evidence of transformed lives, and they are now rejecting Jesus. Each layer deepens their culpability. Seeing and not responding is worse than not seeing at all — a principle the author established in 11:20-24, where the cities that saw Jesus's mighty works and did not repent would face harsher judgment than Sodom.

The Parable of the Tenants: The Vineyard Transferred (vv. 33-46)

"'Hear another parable (allen parabolen akousate). There was a master of a house (anthropos oikodespotes) who planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower, and leased it to tenants (georgois), and went into another country.'"

The language is drawn directly and unmistakably from Isaiah 5:1-2 (LXX): "My beloved had a vineyard on a very fertile hill. He dug it and cleared it of stones, and planted it with choice vines; he built a watchtower in the midst of it, and hewed out a winepress in it." Every detail — the planting, the fence, the winepress, the tower — recapitulates Isaiah's Song of the Vineyard. Any first-century Jewish listener steeped in the prophetic writings would recognize the allusion immediately. In Isaiah 5:7, the identification is explicit: "For the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel." The author is telling a story about Israel, using Israel's own self-description.

The innovation in Jesus's version is the tenants (georgoi — farmers, vine-dressers). In Isaiah 5, the vineyard itself was the problem — "he looked for justice, but behold, bloodshed; for righteousness, but behold, an outcry" (Isaiah 5:7). In Jesus's parable, the vineyard is not defective; the tenants are. The vineyard (Israel, or more precisely, the kingdom of God's purposes for Israel) is good. The problem lies with those entrusted to manage it — the leadership.

"'When the season for fruit drew near (hote de engisen ho kairos ton karpon), he sent his servants (tous doulous autou) to the tenants to get his fruit (labein tous karpous autou). And the tenants took his servants and beat one, killed another, and stoned another. Again he sent other servants, more than the first (pleionis ton proton). And they did the same to them.'"

The owner's expectation is fruit (karpos). The word karpos has been a governing term since 3:8, where John the Baptist demanded "fruit worthy of repentance" (karpon axion tes metanoias). At 7:16-20, Jesus established the principle: "You will recognize them by their fruits" (apo ton karpon auton epignosesthe autous), and "every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire." At 12:33, "the tree is known by its fruit." The fig tree that was just cursed (vv. 18-19) bore no fruit. Throughout the document, fruit is the visible evidence of genuine response to God. The owner's sending of servants to collect fruit is God's sending of prophets to Israel to receive the response due to him.

The servants (douloi) represent the prophets. The treatment they receive — beaten, killed, stoned — recapitulates Israel's history of rejecting and killing its prophets. The tradition of Israel's violence against the prophets was well established: 2 Chronicles 24:20-21 records the stoning of Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the court of the temple; 1 Kings 19:10 has Elijah lamenting, "The people of Israel have forsaken your covenant, thrown down your altars, and killed your prophets with the sword"; Jeremiah was beaten and imprisoned (Jeremiah 20:2, 37:15); tradition held that Isaiah was sawn in two. Jesus himself will invoke this pattern explicitly at 23:29-37: "You build the tombs of the prophets and decorate the monuments of the righteous... so you witness against yourselves that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets."

The detail that the owner sends "more than the first" (pleionis ton proton) shows patience and escalation — God did not give up after the first rejection but sent more messengers. The tenants' response remained the same.

"'Finally he sent his son to them (hysteron de apesteilen pros autous ton huion autou), saying, "They will respect my son (entrapesontai ton huion mou)." But when the tenants saw the son, they said to themselves, "This is the heir (houtos estin ho kleronomos). Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance (kai schomen ten kleronomian autou)." And they took him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him (kai labontes auton exebalon exo tou ampelonos kai apekteinan).'"

The escalation from servants to son is the theological climax. The owner's decision to send his son represents a qualitative shift: the son is not merely another messenger but the heir (kleronomos), the one who has a relationship to the vineyard that no servant has. The father's expectation — "they will respect my son" (entrapesontai, from entrepo, to turn toward, to regard with respect, to feel shame before) — expresses the reasonable hope that the heir's status will elicit a different response than the servants received.

The tenants' reasoning is nakedly self-interested: "This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance." They recognize the son's identity (houtos estin ho kleronomos — "this is the heir") and respond not with respect but with calculated murder. Their goal is to seize ownership — to convert what was entrusted to them into their own possession. The accusation is that Israel's leadership recognizes who Jesus is (or at minimum, understands his claim) and rejects him not out of ignorance but out of a desire to maintain their own hold on what belongs to God.

The detail that "they threw him out of the vineyard and killed him" (exebalon exo tou ampelonos kai apekteinan) places the killing outside the vineyard. Jesus was crucified outside the walls of Jerusalem (Hebrews 13:12; cf. John 19:17, "he went out"). The sequence — expulsion first, then killing — matches the passion narrative's movement: Jesus will be led out of Jerusalem to Golgotha.

"'When therefore the owner of the vineyard comes (hotan oun elthe ho kyrios tou ampelonos), what will he do to those tenants?' They said to him, 'He will put those wretches to a miserable death (kakous kakos apolesei autous) and let out the vineyard to other tenants (kai ton ampelona ekdosetai allois georgois) who will give him the fruits in their seasons (hoitines apodosousin auto tous karpous en tois kairois auton).'"

Again, as in the parable of the two sons, Jesus forces his audience to pronounce their own judgment. The chief priests and elders declare the appropriate consequence: the owner will destroy the wicked tenants and transfer the vineyard to others who will produce the required fruit. The phrase kakous kakos apolesei autous is emphatic — "he will destroy those evil ones evilly," a wordplay that intensifies the judgment. They do not yet realize they are sentencing themselves.

The transfer to "other tenants" (allois georgois) is critical. The vineyard itself is not destroyed — the vineyard is the project of God's kingdom, his purposes for bearing fruit in the world. What changes is the management. The tenants who failed to produce fruit are removed; new tenants who will produce fruit replace them.

"Jesus said to them, 'Have you never read in the Scriptures (oudepote anegnote en tais graphais): "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone (lithon hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes, houtos egenethe eis kephalen gonias); this was the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes (para kyriou egeneto haute, kai estin thaumasten en ophthalmois hemon)"?'"

The citation is Psalm 118:22-23 — the same psalm from which the crowds drew their "Hosanna" and "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" at the entry (v. 9). The psalm is now used in a different mode: the crowds celebrated the processional blessing, but now Jesus invokes the psalm's statement about rejection. The "stone" (lithos) that "the builders" (hoi oikodomountes) rejected has become the head of the corner (kephale gonias) — the most important structural element, the cornerstone or capstone that holds the structure together.

The builders are those responsible for constructing — that is, those entrusted with the work of building Israel's life as God's people. The chief priests, elders, and scribes are the builders. They have rejected the very stone that God has made the foundation of his building. The rejection is not accidental or unavoidable; it is the deliberate act of those who had the responsibility to build rightly. The passive voice — "this was the Lord's doing" (para kyriou egeneto haute) — indicates that the rejected stone's exaltation is God's own action. The builders rejected it; God made it the cornerstone. Human rejection and divine vindication stand in direct opposition.

"'Therefore I tell you, the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits (dio lego hymin hoti arthesetai aph' hymon he basileia tou theou kai dothesetai ethnei poiounti tous karpous autes).'"

This is one of the most direct statements in the entire document. The author uses "the kingdom of God" (he basileia tou theou) rather than his usual "the kingdom of heaven" (he basileia ton ouranon) — a rare variation in this document that may serve to emphasize the divine ownership of what is being discussed. The kingdom will be taken (arthesetai — passive, indicating God's action) from the present leadership and given (dothesetai — another divine passive) to a people (ethnos) who produce its fruits.

The word ethnos (nation, people) is significant. In the singular, it does not automatically mean "Gentiles" (the plural ethne is the standard term for the nations/Gentiles). It means "a people" — a new community defined not by ethnic identity but by the criterion of fruit-bearing. This is consistent with John the Baptist's warning at 3:9: "Do not presume to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father,' for I tell you, God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham." The transfer is from those who hold the position but produce no fruit to those who actually bear the fruit the owner requires. The criterion is performance, not pedigree.

"'And the one who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces; and when it falls on anyone, it will crush him (kai ho peson epi ton lithon touton synthlasthesetai; eph' hon d' an pese likmeset auton).'"

This saying extends the stone imagery with two additional scriptural allusions. The first — "the one who falls on this stone will be broken" — echoes Isaiah 8:14-15, where the LORD himself becomes "a stone of offense and a rock of stumbling to both houses of Israel... and many shall stumble on it. They shall fall and be broken." The second — "when it falls on anyone, it will crush him" — echoes Daniel 2:34-35, 44-45, where a stone "cut out by no human hand" strikes the great statue and grinds it to powder, and the stone becomes a great mountain filling the earth. The stone in Daniel represents God's kingdom that destroys all human empires. Jesus identifies himself as this stone: those who stumble over him will be broken, and those upon whom his judgment falls will be crushed. The two verbs — synthlao (to shatter) and likmao (to winnow, to scatter like chaff, to crush to powder) — depict total destruction. The rejected cornerstone is also the stone of judgment.

"When the chief priests and the Pharisees heard his parables, they perceived that he was speaking about them (egnosan hoti peri auton legei). And although they were seeking to arrest him, they feared the crowds (ephobethesan tous ochlous), because they held him to be a prophet (epei eis propheten auton eichon)."

The author confirms what the hearers already recognized: the parables were directed at them. The chief priests and Pharisees (now mentioned alongside the chief priests, broadening the indictment beyond the initial questioners) understood perfectly that they were the tenants who killed the servants and the son, the second son who said "I go" and did not go. Their response is not repentance but a desire to arrest Jesus — the very action the parable predicted. The tenants in the parable said, "Let us kill him," and now the real-life tenants are "seeking to arrest him." The parable is being fulfilled in the act of hearing it.

Their restraint is not conscience but fear of the crowds, who regard Jesus as a prophet. The irony is thick: the leadership recognizes the parable's accusation and responds by seeking to do exactly what the parable accused them of doing, restrained only by political calculation — the same political calculation that governed their non-answer about John's baptism (vv. 25-26). At every turn in this chapter, the leadership's responses are governed by fear and strategy rather than by truth and repentance.

The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. It presents Jesus's public messianic claim in Jerusalem. Throughout the document, Jesus has operated under what scholars sometimes call a "messianic secret" — instructing those he heals not to tell anyone (8:4, 9:30, 12:16, 16:20), withdrawing from confrontation (12:15, 14:13), and speaking in parables that conceal as much as they reveal (13:10-17). Chapter 21 marks the end of that pattern. Jesus enters Jerusalem in a manner calculated to fulfill Zechariah 9:9, accepts the crowd's "Hosanna to the Son of David" without correction, cleanses the temple as an act of royal and prophetic authority, and allows children to continue the acclamation in the temple itself. The public claim is unmistakable: he is the king of Israel, the Son of David, the one the prophets announced. The remaining chapters of the document unfold the consequences of this public claim.

2. It enacts judgment on the temple and its leadership as a prophetic sign. The cleansing of the temple is not reform but indictment. By invoking Jeremiah 7 ("den of robbers"), Jesus places the current temple administration under the same judgment Jeremiah pronounced against the temple of his day — a judgment that resulted in the temple's destruction by Babylon. The cursing of the fig tree, bracketing the temple action, provides the interpretive key: the tree with leaves but no fruit represents the temple system with all its institutional apparatus but no genuine fruit of worship and justice. The healing of the blind and lame inside the temple shows what the temple should be: a place of God's restorative power available to those the existing order has marginalized.

3. It establishes the criterion of fruit-bearing as the basis for participation in the kingdom. From John the Baptist's demand for "fruit worthy of repentance" (3:8) through the Sermon on the Mount's teaching that "every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down" (7:19) to the sower parable's emphasis on the soil that produces a harvest (13:23), the author has been building toward this point. In chapter 21, the fig tree that bears no fruit is cursed and withers. The first son who ultimately works in the vineyard does the father's will; the second son who speaks but does not act does not. The tenants who refuse to give the owner his fruit lose the vineyard. The kingdom will be given to a people producing its fruits. The principle is stated in multiple forms, from multiple angles, all converging on the same point: what God requires is not profession, position, or pedigree, but fruit.

4. It announces the transfer of the kingdom from Israel's current leadership to a new people. The statement at verse 43 — "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" — is one of the most consequential declarations in the document. It does not announce the rejection of Israel as such (the vineyard remains; it is not destroyed), but the removal of the present tenants and the installation of new ones. The criterion for the new tenancy is fruit-bearing. This is consistent with 8:11-12, where Jesus predicted that "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, while the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness." The author presents the kingdom as God's project, advanced through human agents, but not owned by them — and when the agents fail to produce what the owner requires, new agents are appointed.

5. It demonstrates the leadership's self-condemning response to Jesus's claims. At every point in the chapter, the chief priests and elders are offered the opportunity to respond and at every point they refuse. They cannot answer the question about John's baptism because both answers are politically dangerous (vv. 25-27). They correctly identify the first son as the one who did the father's will, but they are the second son (vv. 28-32). They pronounce judgment on the wicked tenants without recognizing themselves in the verdict (vv. 40-41). When they finally understand that the parables are about them (v. 45), they seek to arrest Jesus rather than repent — becoming, in that very moment, the tenants who seize the son to kill him. The chapter constructs a pattern of increasing clarity and decreasing excuse: the leadership understands and rejects, which is the condition Jesus identified at 13:14-15 as the fulfillment of Isaiah 6:9-10 — seeing but not perceiving, hearing but not understanding.

6. It continues the author's inversion of expected categories. The ones who enter the kingdom are not those with religious credentials but tax collectors and prostitutes who repented (v. 31). The ones healed in the temple are the blind and lame, traditionally excluded from its precincts (v. 14). The ones whose praise God has ordained are not the chief priests but children (v. 16). The king who enters Jerusalem comes not on a warhorse but on a donkey (v. 5). The stone that the expert builders rejected becomes the cornerstone of God's entire structure (v. 42). At every turn, the author presents God's action as overturning the assessments of those who hold institutional power. This is the same inversion that has operated since the beatitudes (5:3-12) and that will reach its climax in the crucifixion, where the one who appears most powerless exercises the greatest authority.
