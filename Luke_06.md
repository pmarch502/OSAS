# Luke 6 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 5):** Luke 5 narrated the calling of Jesus's first disciples through a miraculous catch of fish, where Simon Peter, overwhelmed by the display of authority over creation, responded with "Go away from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man!" (5:8). Jesus then healed a leper, commanding him to show himself to the priest and make the offering Moses prescribed -- working within Israel's institutional structures even while transcending them. The author then introduced the first controversy with the religious authorities: the healing of a paralytic, where Jesus declared "your sins are forgiven" and the scribes and Pharisees charged him with blasphemy ("Who can forgive sins but God alone?" 5:21). Jesus healed the man as evidence that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (5:24). The controversy intensified when Jesus called Levi the tax collector and ate with "tax collectors and sinners" (5:29-30), provoking the Pharisees' objection. Jesus answered with the analogy of the physician who comes not for the healthy but the sick (5:31-32), and concluded with two parables about the incompatibility of the new with the old -- new patches on old garments, new wine in old wineskins (5:36-39). The chapter thus established Jesus's authority (over nature, disease, sin, and social boundaries) while simultaneously generating the religious conflict that will intensify in chapter 6.

**What the author argues immediately after (Luke 7):** Luke 7 opens with the healing of the centurion's servant, where a Gentile military officer displays a faith that astonishes Jesus: "I say to you, not even in Israel have I found such great faith" (7:9). Jesus then raises the widow of Nain's son from the dead, and the people respond by declaring "a great prophet has arisen among us" and "God has visited His people" (7:16). John the Baptist, now imprisoned, sends messengers to ask whether Jesus is "the Expected One" (7:19-20), and Jesus answers by pointing to his actions -- the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news preached to them (7:22) -- a direct echo of the Isaiah 61 program announced at Nazareth (4:18-19) and of Isaiah 35:5-6. The chapter then contrasts the response of the people and tax collectors (who acknowledged God's justice, having been baptized by John) with the Pharisees and lawyers (who "rejected God's purpose for themselves," 7:30). It concludes with the anointing by a sinful woman, where Jesus declares that the one who has been forgiven much loves much, while the one forgiven little loves little (7:47). Chapter 7 thus extends the themes of faith, response, and division that chapter 6 sets up: who truly hears and acts on Jesus's words?

**How chapter 6 connects these:** Luke 6 is the pivot between the emerging conflict with the religious authorities (chapter 5) and the demonstration of who responds rightly to Jesus (chapter 7). The chapter accomplishes this through three movements. First, it brings the Sabbath controversy to a climax: two Sabbath incidents (grain-plucking, healing of the withered hand) in which Jesus asserts authority over the Sabbath itself and the Pharisees begin planning their response. Second, Jesus selects twelve from among his disciples and names them apostles -- forming a new community out of the larger group that has been following him. Third, he delivers extended teaching to this community that redefines what faithfulness looks like: not conventional piety or social respectability, but radical generosity, enemy-love, refusal to condemn, and the building of one's life on obedient action rather than mere hearing. The chapter thus transitions from controversy about authority (who has the right to define what God's law requires?) to community formation (who are the people of this new movement?) to the ethical character of that community (what does their life look like?). This sets up chapter 7's demonstrations: the centurion who acts on faith, the sinful woman who loves much, and the Pharisee who does neither.

## Verses 1-5: Authority Over the Sabbath -- The Grain Incident

"Now it happened that He was passing through some grainfields on a Sabbath; and His disciples were picking the heads of grain, rubbing them in their hands, and eating the grain. But some of the Pharisees said, 'Why are you doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?' And Jesus answered and said to them, 'Have you not even read what David did when he was hungry, he and those who were with him, how he entered the house of God, and took and ate the consecrated bread, which is not lawful for any to eat except the priests alone, and gave it to his companions?' And He was saying to them, 'The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.'"

This is the first of two Sabbath controversies that open chapter 6, continuing the conflict that began in chapter 5 but now centering it specifically on the Sabbath -- the most visible marker of Jewish covenant identity.

**The action itself.** The disciples pick grain, rub it to remove the husks, and eat it. Under the Torah, this was explicitly permitted: "When you enter your neighbor's standing grain, then you may pluck the heads with your hand" (Deuteronomy 23:25). The act of taking grain from a neighbor's field by hand was not theft; it was a provision of the law for the hungry. The Pharisees' objection is not about the taking of grain but about the day on which it is done. Their charge is that the disciples are violating the Sabbath.

The Sabbath prohibition derives from the fourth commandment (Exodus 20:8-11, Deuteronomy 5:12-15): "You shall not do any work" (kol melacha). What constitutes "work" became a matter of extensive interpretation. The Pharisaic tradition (later codified in the Mishnah, Shabbat 7:2) identified thirty-nine categories of prohibited labor, including reaping and threshing. Plucking grain could be classified as reaping; rubbing it in the hands could be classified as threshing. The disciples' casual act thus fell within the sphere of Sabbath regulation as the Pharisees understood it.

**Jesus's response -- the David precedent.** Jesus does not argue from legal technicality or from a general principle of Sabbath relaxation. He argues from a specific scriptural precedent: "Have you not even read" (oude touto anegnote) -- a rhetorical challenge implying that their reading of Scripture is insufficient.

The incident Jesus cites is 1 Samuel 21:1-6. David, fleeing from Saul, arrived at the tabernacle at Nob and asked the priest Ahimelech for bread. The only bread available was the "bread of the Presence" (lechem hapanim, "showbread"), the twelve loaves set before the LORD in the tabernacle and reserved exclusively for the priests (Leviticus 24:5-9: "it shall be for Aaron and his sons, and they shall eat it in a holy place"). Ahimelech gave the bread to David and his companions, on the condition that they had kept themselves from women (a purity regulation).

The precedent operates on several levels. First, it establishes that Israel's own scriptures record an authoritative figure (David, the anointed king) setting aside a specific provision of the Torah (the exclusive priestly right to the showbread) in the face of human need, without the text condemning him for it. If David could set aside the restriction on the bread of the Presence, the argument runs, then the disciples' minor Sabbath violation for the sake of hunger is at least equally defensible.

Second, and more significantly, the choice of David is not arbitrary. David was Israel's anointed king, the mashiach. By citing David's action as precedent for his own disciples' behavior, Jesus implicitly places himself in the position of the anointed one -- the one whose authority is analogous to (or exceeds) David's. The comparison is between anointed kings and their companions: what David's men could do under David's authority, Jesus's disciples can do under his.

**"The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath."** The concluding declaration moves from precedent to principle. "The Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthropou) is the self-designation Jesus uses throughout the Synoptic Gospels. The phrase has background in two directions. In Ezekiel, God repeatedly addresses the prophet as "son of man" (ben adam), simply meaning "human being" (Ezekiel 2:1, 3, 6, 8, etc.). In Daniel 7:13-14, "one like a son of man" comes on the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and receives "dominion, glory, and a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations, and languages might serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion which will not pass away."

The claim that the Son of Man is "Lord" (kyrios) of the Sabbath is extraordinary. The Sabbath was instituted by God at creation (Genesis 2:2-3) and given as a covenant sign between God and Israel (Exodus 31:13-17: "it is a sign between Me and you throughout your generations"). To claim lordship over the Sabbath is to claim authority over something that belongs to God's own ordering of time. The claim does not abolish the Sabbath; it asserts that the Son of Man has the right to determine what the Sabbath requires -- a right that the Pharisees claimed for their interpretive tradition and that Jesus claims for himself.

## Verses 6-11: Authority Over the Sabbath -- The Withered Hand

"On another Sabbath He entered the synagogue and was teaching; and there was a man there whose right hand was withered. The scribes and the Pharisees were watching Him closely to see if He healed on the Sabbath, so that they might find reason to accuse Him. But He knew what they were thinking, and He said to the man with the withered hand, 'Get up and come forward!' And he got up and came forward. And Jesus said to them, 'I ask you, is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath or to do harm, to save a life or to destroy it?' And after looking around at them all, He said to him, 'Stretch out your hand!' And he did so; and his hand was restored. But they themselves were filled with rage, and discussed together what they might do to Jesus."

This second Sabbath incident raises the stakes. The first was about the disciples' casual act; this is about a deliberate healing in the synagogue, under the direct scrutiny of opponents who have come looking for grounds to accuse Jesus.

**The setting and the surveillance.** The author notes that the scribes and Pharisees "were watching Him closely" (pareterounto auton) -- the imperfect tense indicates sustained, attentive observation. The verb paratereo carries the connotation of hostile watching, surveillance with the intent of finding fault. They already know what Jesus is likely to do; they are waiting to catch him doing it. The man with the withered hand is, for them, a test case -- not a person in need but an opportunity for accusation.

**Jesus's response.** Jesus "knew what they were thinking" (autos de edei tous dialogismous auton) -- the word dialogismos means "reasoning," "deliberation," "inner thought." Jesus perceives the calculation behind their observation. Rather than avoiding the confrontation, he escalates it. He calls the man forward, bringing the case into the center of public attention. He will not heal quietly or privately; he forces the question into the open.

**The question.** "Is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath or to do harm, to save a life or to destroy it?" (ei exestin to sabbato agathopoiesai e kakopoiesai, psychen sosai e apolesai). This is not a question about the permissibility of healing on the Sabbath; it is a question about the nature of the Sabbath itself. Jesus reframes the issue: the choice on the Sabbath is not between acting and refraining from action. The choice is between doing good and doing harm. To refrain from healing when one has the power to heal is not neutrality; it is harm. The Sabbath, which was given for Israel's benefit (Deuteronomy 5:14-15 grounds the Sabbath in liberation from Egypt: "you shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out of there"), cannot be a day when the capacity to do good is suspended.

The phrase "to save a life or to destroy it" (psychen sosai e apolesai) uses the word psyche, which can mean "life," "soul," or "self." The parallelism between "do good / do harm" and "save life / destroy life" suggests that Jesus sees the withered hand not as a trivial condition but as a matter connected to the wholeness of a person's life. The word apolesai ("to destroy") is the same word the demon used in 4:34 ("Have You come to destroy us?"). To leave a person in a diminished state when restoration is possible is, in Jesus's framing, an act aligned with destruction rather than with the saving purpose of the Sabbath.

**The healing.** Jesus commands "stretch out your hand" (exteinon ten cheira sou), and the hand is restored. The healing involves no physical touch, no visible "work" -- only a spoken word and the man's obedient response. The author may intend the irony: the Pharisees are looking for a violation of the Sabbath work prohibition, but Jesus performs the healing through speech alone. There is nothing to classify as labor under any Sabbath framework.

**The opponents' response.** "They were filled with rage" (eplesthesan anoias) -- the word anoia literally means "mindlessness," "irrationality," "fury." It is a strong word: they are not merely displeased but enraged to the point of irrationality. The same verb plesthemi ("to be filled") was used positively of the Holy Spirit filling various figures in Luke 1-2 (Elizabeth in 1:41, Zechariah in 1:67). Here it is used of the Pharisees being filled with rage. The contrast is implicit: to be filled with the Spirit produces prophecy and praise; to be filled with anoia produces plotting against the one who does good.

They "discussed together what they might do to Jesus" (dielaloum pros allelous ti an poieseian to Iesou). The discussion about "what to do" with Jesus is ominous. In the context of Luke's narrative arc, this marks the point at which passive opposition becomes active plotting. The healing on the Sabbath -- the act of restoring a man's hand -- is what triggers it. The author presents this as deeply ironic: the act of doing good and saving life on the Sabbath produces in Jesus's opponents the desire to do harm and destroy life.

## Verses 12-16: The Selection of the Twelve

"It was at this time that He went off to the mountain to pray, and He spent the whole night in prayer to God. And when day came, He called His disciples to Him and chose twelve of them, whom He also named as apostles: Simon, whom He also named Peter, and Andrew his brother; and James and John; and Philip and Bartholomew; and Matthew and Thomas; and James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon who was called the Zealot; and Judas the son of James, and Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor."

**The prayer.** The author notes that Jesus prayed through the entire night (en dianyktereuo en te proseuche tou theou) before making this selection. The verb dianykteruo means "to spend the night through" -- it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. The all-night prayer underscores the gravity of the decision: from the larger body of disciples, twelve are being selected for a specific role. Luke consistently places prayer before pivotal moments in Jesus's ministry (3:21 before the baptism, 5:16 after the healings, 9:18 before Peter's confession, 9:28 before the transfiguration, 22:41-44 before the arrest). The pattern suggests that the major turning points of the narrative are understood as proceeding from communion with God, not from strategic calculation.

**"He chose twelve."** The number twelve is deliberate and deeply rooted in Israel's symbolic world. Israel consisted of twelve tribes, descended from the twelve sons of Jacob/Israel (Genesis 35:22-26, 49:1-28). The twelve tribes constituted the whole people of God. By selecting twelve, Jesus is performing a symbolic reconstitution: he is forming the nucleus of a renewed Israel. This is not a replacement of Israel but a re-gathering -- the prophetic hope of the reunification of the scattered tribes (Isaiah 49:6, Ezekiel 37:15-28, Jeremiah 31:1). The twelve apostles correspond to the twelve patriarchs as the foundation of the community Jesus is assembling.

**"Whom He also named as apostles."** The word apostolos means "one who is sent," from apostellō ("to send"). In non-biblical Greek, the word could refer to a naval expedition or an envoy. In the LXX, the verb apostellō is used for God's commissioning of prophets (Isaiah 6:8: "Whom shall I send?"; Jeremiah 1:7: "to all whom I send you, you shall go"). The act of naming them "apostles" defines their role: they are sent ones, commissioned agents who bear the authority of the one who sends them. The parallel with Israel's commissioning is evident -- Israel was called to be a "kingdom of priests" (Exodus 19:6), a people sent to serve God's purposes among the nations.

**The list itself.** Several names merit attention:

Simon is given the name Peter (Petros, from petra, "rock") by Jesus -- a renaming that implies a new identity and function, recalling God's renaming of Abram to Abraham (Genesis 17:5) and Jacob to Israel (Genesis 32:28).

Matthew is the tax collector called in 5:27-28 (named Levi in that account). His inclusion alongside Simon called the Zealot is striking: a tax collector (who collaborated with Rome) and a Zealot (who opposed Roman rule with potentially violent resistance) are brought into the same community. The twelve are not ideologically uniform; they are held together by their relationship to Jesus, not by their prior political commitments.

Judas Iscariot is identified proleptically as the one "who became a traitor" (hos egeneto prodotes). The word prodotes means "betrayer" or "traitor." The author signals from the beginning that one of the chosen twelve will betray Jesus. The verb egeneto ("became") indicates that this was not his identity from the start but something he became -- a transformation in the wrong direction, a falling away from the community he was chosen to be part of.

## Verses 17-19: Descent and Healing

"Jesus came down with them and stood on a level place; and there was a large crowd of His disciples, and a great throng of people from all Judea and Jerusalem and the coastal region of Tyre and Sidon, who had come to hear Him and to be healed of their diseases; and those who were troubled with unclean spirits were being cured. And all the people were trying to touch Him, because power was coming from Him and healing them all."

Jesus descends from the mountain where he chose the twelve and stands on "a level place" (epi topou pedinou) -- a flat area, contrasting with the mountain (oros) where he had been praying. The audience is identified with care: "a large crowd of His disciples" (ochlos polys matheton autou) -- indicating that the twelve are selected from within a much larger body of followers -- and "a great throng of people" (plethos poly tou laou) from a wide geographic range.

The geographic range is significant: "all Judea and Jerusalem and the coastal region of Tyre and Sidon." Judea and Jerusalem represent the Jewish heartland. Tyre and Sidon are Gentile coastal cities in Phoenicia -- the same region from which the widow of Zarephath came, to whom Elijah was sent when Israel rejected his message (4:26, citing 1 Kings 17). The mention of Tyre and Sidon anticipates the universalizing trajectory that Luke has been building since the genealogy traced Jesus to Adam (3:38) and Isaiah 40:5 declared "all flesh will see the salvation of God" (3:6).

The description of "power coming from Him" (dynamis par' autou exercheto) and "healing them all" (iato pantas) presents Jesus's healing activity as the direct enactment of the Isaiah 61 program announced at Nazareth. The healing is comprehensive and undiscriminating -- it extends to all who come.

## Verses 20-23: The Blessings

"And turning His gaze toward His disciples, He began to say, 'Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be satisfied. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh. Blessed are you when people hate you, and when they exclude you, and insult you, and scorn your name as evil, on account of the Son of Man.'"

**The audience.** The author specifies that Jesus directs his gaze "toward His disciples" (tous ophthalmous autou eis tous mathetas autou). The teaching is addressed to the disciples in the hearing of the larger crowd. The second-person pronoun "you" (hymeis) is used throughout, making these declarations directly personal rather than general or abstract. This matters: Jesus is not describing a universal principle about poverty and hunger but addressing the specific community of people who have chosen to follow him.

**The literary form.** The word "blessed" (makarios) in the LXX introduces a declaration of the current or future state of the person described. It does not mean "happy" in the modern emotional sense. In the Psalms, makarios marks the person whose way of life is aligned with God's purposes: "Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked" (Psalm 1:1 LXX). In Isaiah, it marks the one who trusts in the LORD: "Blessed are all those who wait for Him" (Isaiah 30:18). The makarism declares that the person described is in the right position before God, regardless of present appearances.

**"Blessed are you who are poor."** Luke's version says "poor" (hoi ptochoi) without Matthew's qualifier "in spirit" (Matthew 5:3). The word ptochos in the LXX describes those who are materially destitute and socially marginalized. The ptochoi in the Psalms are those who cry out to God because they have no other resource: "This poor man cried, and the LORD heard him" (Psalm 34:6 [33:7 LXX]). In Isaiah 61:1 -- the passage Jesus read at Nazareth -- the good news is specifically "to the poor" (ptochois). The declaration "yours is the kingdom of God" (hymetera estin he basileia tou theou) uses the present tense: the kingdom belongs to them now, not merely in the future. The logic connects to the Nazareth announcement: if the kingdom of God is breaking in through Jesus's ministry, and if that ministry is defined by Isaiah 61 as good news to the poor, then the poor are the first recipients of the kingdom's reality.

**"Blessed are you who hunger now."** Again, the language is direct and physical. The word peinontes describes bodily hunger. The qualifier "now" (nyn) introduces a temporal contrast: the present hunger stands against a future satisfaction. "You will be satisfied" (chortasthesesthe) -- the verb chortazo means to feed to the full, to satiate. In the LXX, this verb appears in descriptions of God's provision: "He satisfies you with the finest of the wheat" (Psalm 147:14 [146:14 LXX]). The promise is that the present deprivation is not permanent; God's provision will reverse it.

**"Blessed are you who weep now."** The verb klaiontes describes audible, grieving weeping -- not quiet sadness but the weeping of lamentation. "You will laugh" (gelasete) -- the verb gelao appears rarely in the LXX but carries the connotation of vindication and joy after suffering. Sarah "laughed" (Genesis 18:12-15, 21:6) at the birth of Isaac, whose name itself means "he laughs." The laughter promised here is the laughter of reversal: what was cause for grief will become cause for joy.

**"Blessed are you when people hate you."** The fourth declaration specifies persecution: hatred (miseo), exclusion (aphorizo -- "to separate from," to excommunicate from the community), insult (oneido), and the casting out of the disciples' name as evil (ekballo to onoma hymon hos poneron). The phrase "cast out your name as evil" likely refers to formal exclusion from the synagogue and community, with the person's reputation destroyed. The cause of this persecution is specified: "on account of the Son of Man" (heneka tou huiou tou anthropou). The persecution is not random but targeted at those who identify with Jesus.

## Verse 23: The Prophetic Precedent

"Rejoice on that day and leap for joy, for behold, your reward is great in heaven. For their fathers used to treat the prophets the same way."

The response to persecution is not stoic endurance but joy -- "rejoice" (charete) and "leap for joy" (skirtesate, the same verb used of John leaping in Elizabeth's womb when Mary arrived, 1:41, 44). The reason for joy is twofold. First, "your reward is great in heaven" (ho misthos hymon polys en to ourano) -- the word misthos means "wages," "payment," what is owed for labor rendered. The reward is not described as future entrance into heaven but as a reward stored in heaven, secured by God. Second, the treatment they receive places them in a lineage: "their fathers used to treat the prophets the same way" (kata ta auta gar epoioun tois prophetais hoi pateres auton). The persecuted disciples are being compared to the prophets of Israel who were rejected, persecuted, and killed (1 Kings 19:10, 2 Chronicles 36:15-16, Jeremiah 38:6, Nehemiah 9:26). To be hated and excluded for the sake of the Son of Man is to stand in the succession of those who spoke God's word and were rejected by those who claimed to be God's people.

The pronoun "their" (auton) is significant: "their fathers" distinguishes the persecutors' lineage from the disciples' lineage. Those who persecute the disciples are the spiritual descendants of those who persecuted the prophets.

## Verses 24-26: The Corresponding Warnings

"But woe to you who are rich, for you are receiving your comfort in full. Woe to you who are well-fed now, for you will be hungry. Woe to you who laugh now, for you will mourn and weep. Woe to you when all people speak well of you, for their fathers used to treat the false prophets the same way."

The four woes correspond precisely to the four blessings, creating a parallel structure of reversal:

| Blessing | Woe |
|---|---|
| Poor -- kingdom is yours | Rich -- you have received your comfort |
| Hungry now -- will be satisfied | Well-fed now -- will be hungry |
| Weeping now -- will laugh | Laughing now -- will mourn and weep |
| Hated on account of the Son of Man -- prophets' lineage | Spoken well of by all -- false prophets' lineage |

The structure is the same reversal that Mary proclaimed in the Magnificat: "He has filled the hungry with good things, and sent away the rich empty-handed" (1:53). The author is connecting Jesus's teaching directly to the prophetic vision articulated by his mother before his birth.

**"Woe to you who are rich, for you are receiving your comfort in full."** The word "woe" (ouai) in the prophetic tradition is not a curse but a lament -- an announcement of disaster or the recognition that someone's situation, despite appearances, is actually dire. The rich (plousioi) are receiving their "comfort" (paraklesin) "in full" (apechete). The verb apecho is a commercial term meaning "to receive in full," "to be paid in full" -- it was used on receipts to indicate that payment had been completed. The implication is stark: what the rich have now is all they will receive. There is no further comfort coming. Simeon described Jesus's coming as "the consolation [paraklesin] of Israel" (2:25) -- the rich have received a different consolation, one that consists of present material abundance, and it is already paid out in full.

**"Woe to you who are well-fed now."** The reversal of the second blessing: those who are satiated now will hunger. The same temporal marker "now" (nyn) appears: the present state is not permanent.

**"Woe to you who laugh now."** Those whose present condition gives them cause for laughter will find themselves mourning and weeping. The reversal of present circumstances is total.

**"Woe to you when all people speak well of you."** Universal popularity is the counterpart of persecution. Where the blessed are hated and excluded on account of the Son of Man, those under woe are praised by everyone. The comparison is devastating: "their fathers used to treat the false prophets the same way" (kata ta auta gar epoioun tois pseudoprophetais hoi pateres auton). If being persecuted places one in the lineage of the true prophets, then being universally praised places one in the lineage of the false prophets -- those who told the people what they wanted to hear (1 Kings 22:6-12, where four hundred prophets told Ahab what he desired; Jeremiah 6:14, 8:11, where false prophets proclaimed "Peace, peace," when there was no peace; Ezekiel 13:10, where they "plastered over" the wall with whitewash).

The pronoun pattern in the woes mirrors the blessings: "their fathers" (hoi pateres auton) again distinguishes the audience of the woes from the audience of the blessings. The woes are not addressed to outsiders or to the absent rich as a general class; they are spoken to those within hearing who are in the position described. The author's placement of these woes immediately after the blessings creates a choice: every hearer must determine which category describes them.

## Verses 27-31: The Command to Love Enemies

"But I say to you who hear, love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you. Whoever hits you on the cheek, offer him the other also; and whoever takes away your cloak, do not withhold your tunic from him either. Give to everyone who asks of you, and whoever takes away what is yours, do not demand it back. Treat others the same way you want them to treat you."

**"But I say to you who hear."** The phrase "to you who hear" (tois akouousin) distinguishes the responsive hearers from the general audience. The same distinction will reappear at the chapter's close, where Jesus differentiates the one who "hears My words and acts on them" from the one who "has heard and has not acted" (6:47, 49). The teaching that follows is for those who are willing to receive it.

**"Love your enemies."** The command agapate tous echthrous hymon uses the verb agapao, which in the LXX describes God's love for Israel (Deuteronomy 7:8: "because the LORD loved you") and the commanded love of the neighbor (Leviticus 19:18: "you shall love your neighbor as yourself"). The extension of this love to enemies goes beyond Leviticus 19:18, which specified "neighbor" (plesion) and "the sons of your people." However, it does not contradict the Torah; Exodus 23:4-5 already required action on behalf of an enemy's stray ox or collapsed donkey. Proverbs 25:21-22 commanded feeding a hungry enemy. The command to love enemies intensifies a trajectory already present in Israel's scriptures rather than creating something wholly new.

The four commands that follow define what enemy-love looks like in practice: "do good" (kalos poieite) to those who hate, "bless" (eulogeite) those who curse, "pray" (proseuchesthe) for those who mistreat. Each command names a hostile action from the enemy and responds with a positive action from the disciple. The pattern is consistent: the disciple's response is not determined by the enemy's behavior but by the disciple's commitment to do good regardless.

**The specific examples.** Being struck on the cheek and having one's cloak taken represent personal affront and economic loss respectively. The instruction to offer the other cheek and to give the tunic as well does not address the question of whether resistance to evil is always wrong in every circumstance; it addresses the disciple's posture toward personal retaliation. The point is the refusal to respond in kind -- to break the cycle of retaliatory escalation. In a first-century context of Roman occupation, where honor culture made retaliation a social obligation, these instructions are radically counter-cultural.

**"Give to everyone who asks of you."** The command is unqualified in its scope: "everyone" (panti to aitounti se). The instruction does not calculate whether the asker is deserving or whether giving is prudent; it describes a posture of open-handed generosity that mirrors God's own generosity (as verse 35 will make explicit).

**"Treat others the same way you want them to treat you."** This principle (kathos thelete hina poiosin hymin hoi anthropoi, poieite autois homoios) establishes reciprocity as the standard, but a particular kind of reciprocity: it is self-referential, asking the disciple to use their own desire for good treatment as the measure of how they treat others. The logic assumes that all people know what they would want to receive, and commands them to give that to others first.

## Verses 32-36: The Logic of Gratuitous Generosity

"If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners love those who love them. If you do good to those who do good to you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners do the same. If you lend to those from whom you expect to receive, what credit is that to you? Even sinners lend to sinners in order to receive back the same amount. But love your enemies, and do good, and lend, expecting nothing in return; and your reward will be great, and you will be sons of the Most High; for He Himself is kind to ungrateful and evil people. Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful."

Jesus now explains the logic behind the enemy-love commands. The argument proceeds through three parallel examples (loving, doing good, lending), each followed by the same conclusion: "what credit is that to you? For even sinners do the same."

**The word "credit."** The Greek word charis is used here, which ordinarily means "grace," "favor," or "thanks." In this context it functions as "what kind of credit/favor/reward is that to you?" The point is that loving those who love you back, doing good to those who reciprocate, and lending to those who will repay are acts of simple self-interest. They operate on the principle of exchange. Even "sinners" (hamartoloi) -- those outside the community of the faithful -- practice this kind of reciprocity. There is nothing distinctive about it; it does not reflect the character of God's people because it does not reflect the character of God.

**"Expecting nothing in return."** The phrase meden apelpizontes has been translated variously. The verb apelpizo can mean "to despair" or "to expect back." In this context, the instruction is to lend without expecting repayment -- a radical economic instruction that echoes the Jubilee legislation (Leviticus 25) and the Sabbatical year provisions for debt release (Deuteronomy 15:1-11). Deuteronomy 15:9 specifically warns against the hard-hearted response of withholding loans because the year of release is near. Jesus pushes the principle further: do not merely lend despite the year of release, but lend without expectation of return at all.

**"You will be sons of the Most High."** The phrase huioi hypsistou esesthe does not describe a biological relationship but a character resemblance. In Hebrew and Aramaic idiom, "son of" indicates participation in the character or quality of something: "sons of light," "sons of thunder," "sons of disobedience." To be "sons of the Most High" is to act in a way that reflects the character of the Most High. The title "Most High" (hypsistos) has appeared in Luke's narrative at 1:32 (the angel says Jesus "will be called the Son of the Most High") and 1:35 ("the power of the Most High will overshadow you") and 1:76 ("you will be called the prophet of the Most High"). The disciples are being told that by practicing enemy-love and gratuitous generosity, they become recognizable as children of the same God whose character these acts reflect.

**"For He Himself is kind to ungrateful and evil people."** This is the theological foundation of the entire teaching. God's own character is one of kindness (chrestos) extended to the "ungrateful" (acharistous) and the "evil" (ponerois). God does not limit his kindness to those who respond well or deserve it. If God's generosity were reciprocal -- given only to those who return thanks and act rightly -- it would not be grace. The disciples are called to mirror this divine pattern: to love enemies, do good without reciprocation, and lend without expecting return, because this is how God acts.

**"Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful."** The summary command uses the word oiktirmones ("merciful," "compassionate"), from oiktirmos, which in the LXX translates the Hebrew rachamim (tender compassion, mercy from the gut). In Exodus 34:6, when God revealed His character to Moses, He declared Himself "compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in lovingkindness and truth." The command is not to achieve divine perfection but to reflect divine character: the standard of behavior is derived from who God is, not from what one's enemies deserve.

## Verses 37-38: On Judging and Giving

"Do not judge, and you will not be judged; and do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Pardon, and you will be pardoned. Give, and it will be given to you. They will pour into your lap a good measure -- pressed down, shaken together, and running over. For by your standard of measure it will be measured to you in return."

The instructions here follow directly from the mercy principle of verse 36. The structure is a series of parallel imperatives with corresponding consequences:

- Do not judge (me krinete) --> you will not be judged
- Do not condemn (me katadikadzete) --> you will not be condemned
- Pardon (apolyete) --> you will be pardoned
- Give (didote) --> it will be given to you

The verbs "judge" (krino) and "condemn" (katadikazo) are legal terms. Krino means to render a verdict; katadikazo means to pronounce a sentence of condemnation. The prohibition is not against discernment or evaluation (the very teaching Jesus is giving requires his hearers to evaluate their own lives). It is against assuming the position of judge and pronouncing condemnation on others. This connects to the mercy theme: the merciful person does not stand over others in judgment because they recognize their own need for mercy.

The giving metaphor is drawn from the marketplace: grain measured into one's lap (kolpos, the fold of the garment used as a pouch), pressed down (pepiesmenon), shaken together (sesaleumenon), and running over (hyperekchynnomenon). The imagery describes a merchant filling a measure to overflowing generosity -- far more than the minimum required. The principle is that the measure one uses toward others becomes the measure God uses toward oneself. This is not a mechanical law of karma but an expression of the coherence between human action and divine response: the merciful receive mercy; the generous receive generosity; the judgmental receive judgment.

## Verses 39-42: The Blind Guide, the Student, and the Plank

"And He also spoke a parable to them: 'A blind man cannot guide a blind man, can he? Will they not both fall into a pit? A student is not above his teacher; but everyone, after he has been fully trained, will be like his teacher. Why do you look at the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye? Or how can you say to your brother, "Brother, let me take out the speck that is in your eye," when you yourself do not see the log that is in your own eye? You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take out the speck that is in your brother's eye.'"

These short illustrations continue the argument about judging. They are not disconnected sayings but extensions of the same point.

**The blind guide.** The image makes a simple point: one who cannot see cannot lead another who cannot see. In the context of the preceding instructions about not judging, the implication is that the person who assumes the role of judge over others may themselves be blind -- lacking the self-awareness and mercy that would qualify them to guide anyone. The Pharisees, who have been watching Jesus "closely" (6:7) to find fault, are implicitly in view: they claim the authority to judge what is lawful, but they cannot see that their opposition to doing good on the Sabbath is a form of blindness.

**The student and teacher.** "A student is not above his teacher" (ouk estin mathetes hyper ton didaskalon autou). The principle establishes the relationship of formation: the student is shaped by the teacher. "Everyone, after he has been fully trained, will be like his teacher" (katertismenos de pas estai hos ho didaskalos autou). The verb katartizo means to complete, to equip, to bring to maturity. The implication is that disciples become like their teacher -- in character, in practice, in the way they see. If the teacher is blind, the fully trained student will also be blind. If the teacher is merciful and generous, the student will become so. This places a weight on the question of whom one follows and from whom one learns.

**The speck and the log.** The hyperbole is deliberate and comic: a karphos (splinter, small piece of straw) compared to a dokos (a beam, a structural timber used in building). The absurdity of someone with a building beam in their eye attempting to perform delicate surgery on someone else's splinter is the point. The illustration does not forbid helping others with their faults; it demands that self-examination precede the attempt: "first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly" (tote diablepseis). The word diablepo means to see through clearly, to see with restored vision. The sequence matters: self-correction restores the sight necessary for genuinely helping others. Without it, the attempted help is an act of blindness dressed up as service.

The word "hypocrite" (hypokrita) comes from the Greek theater, where it referred to an actor who wore a mask -- one who plays a role that does not correspond to the reality behind it. The person who judges others while ignoring their own greater fault is performing a role: the role of the morally clear-sighted, the qualified corrector. But the performance is a mask; the reality is a beam in the eye.

## Verses 43-45: The Character of the Tree

"For there is no good tree that produces bad fruit, nor, on the other hand, a bad tree that produces good fruit. For each tree is known by its own fruit. For people do not gather figs from thorns, nor do they pick grapes from a briar bush. The good person out of the good treasure of his heart produces what is good; and the evil person out of the evil treasure produces what is evil; for his mouth speaks from that which fills his heart."

This section provides the principle underlying everything Jesus has been teaching. The argument is from nature: trees produce fruit consistent with their own nature. A good tree does not and cannot produce bad fruit; a bad tree does not and cannot produce good fruit. Figs do not grow on thornbushes; grapes do not come from briars. The identification of a tree's nature comes from its fruit, not from its appearance or its claims about itself.

The application moves from trees to people: "the good person out of the good treasure of his heart produces what is good" (ho agathos anthropos ek tou agathou thesaurou tes kardias properei to agathon). The word thesauros ("treasure," "storehouse") indicates what is accumulated and stored within. The heart (kardia) in Jewish anthropology is not the seat of emotion (as in modern usage) but the center of the will, the mind, and moral character -- the core of the person from which decisions, words, and actions flow. What comes out of a person -- in speech, in action, in the treatment of others -- reveals what is stored within.

"For his mouth speaks from that which fills his heart" (ek gar perisseumatous kardias lalei to stoma autou). The word perisseuma means "overflow," "abundance." Speech is the overflow of the heart's content. This has direct bearing on everything preceding: the one who judges and condemns has a heart that overflows with condemnation. The one who blesses and gives has a heart that overflows with generosity. The Pharisees whose response to a healing was rage (6:11) revealed the content of their hearts. The enemy-loving, non-judging disciple reveals a different content.

This tree-and-fruit principle also connects backward to John the Baptist's preaching in 3:8-9: "produce fruits consistent with repentance... every tree that does not produce good fruit is being cut down and thrown into the fire." The same organic logic applies: genuine repentance produces visible fruit, and the absence of fruit reveals the absence of the reality it claims to represent.

## Verses 46-49: The Two Builders

"Why do you call Me, 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say? Everyone who comes to Me and hears My words and acts on them, I will show you whom he is like: he is like a man building a house, who dug deep and laid a foundation on the rock; and when a flood occurred, the torrent burst against that house and could not shake it, because it had been well built. But the one who has heard and has not acted accordingly, is like a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation; and the torrent burst against it and immediately it collapsed, and the ruin of that house was great."

**"Why do you call Me, 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say?"** The word kyrios ("Lord") is a title of authority and submission. To call someone kyrios is to acknowledge their authority over oneself. Jesus's question exposes the contradiction between verbal acknowledgment and practical obedience. The repetition "Lord, Lord" (kyrie, kyrie) intensifies the address -- it is emphatic, fervent, possibly liturgical -- but the emphasis of the title only deepens the contradiction when it is not accompanied by obedience.

This question establishes the interpretive frame for the parable that follows: the distinction is not between hearing and not hearing, or between acknowledging and not acknowledging, but between hearing-and-doing and hearing-without-doing.

**The two builders.** Both builders build houses. Both houses face the same flood (plemmyras, a sudden inundation). The difference is entirely in the foundation. The first builder "dug deep" (eskapsen kai ebathynen) and laid a foundation on the rock (etheken themelion epi ten petran). The labor is emphasized: digging deep is costly and time-consuming. The result is stability: the flood could not shake the house because it was "well built" (dia to kalos oikodomesthai auten).

The second builder constructed directly on the ground without a foundation (choris themeliou). The construction was presumably faster and easier. But when the same torrent struck, the collapse was immediate (euthys synepesen) and the ruin was "great" (mega).

The metaphor is transparent from the preceding context. The "rock" foundation is hearing Jesus's words and acting on them. Building without a foundation is hearing without acting. The flood is not specified as a particular event -- it could represent persecution (the hatred and exclusion of verse 22), judgment, the pressures of life, or eschatological testing. The point is that the test will come regardless; the only question is whether one's life has been built on the foundation of obedience.

The image of building on rock has scriptural background. In Isaiah 28:16, God declares: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a tested stone, a costly cornerstone for the foundation, firmly placed. He who believes in it will not be disturbed." In Psalm 18:2 [17:3 LXX]: "The LORD is my rock and my fortress." The metaphorical connection between God (or God's word) and rock is deeply rooted in Israel's tradition. Jesus's instruction to build on the rock of his words thus places his teaching in the position that the Torah and God's word occupied in Israel's life: the foundation on which all else depends.

## Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 6 is structured as a single continuous argument that moves from the question of authority, through the formation of a community, to the ethical character that community must embody, and finally to the decisive distinction between hearing and doing.

**The Sabbath controversies (vv. 1-11)** establish the question that drives the chapter: Who has the authority to define what God requires? The Pharisees claim that authority through their interpretive tradition. Jesus claims it through his identity as the Son of Man who is Lord of the Sabbath. The two incidents demonstrate that the Sabbath, rightly understood, is for doing good and saving life -- not for the enforcement of restrictions that prevent restoration. The Pharisees' response -- rage and plotting -- reveals that their understanding of the Sabbath has become an instrument that opposes the very purposes of God.

**The selection of the twelve (vv. 12-16)** is the author's transition from controversy to community. After demonstrating his authority through conflict with the religious establishment, Jesus forms his own community -- twelve apostles corresponding to the twelve tribes, the nucleus of a renewed Israel. The selection follows all-night prayer, indicating that this new community is constituted not by human strategy but by divine direction.

**The blessings and woes (vv. 20-26)** define the identity of this new community by reversal: the poor, hungry, weeping, and persecuted are the blessed ones; the rich, full, laughing, and universally praised are under woe. The structure mirrors the Magnificat (1:46-55) and places the disciples in the lineage of the true prophets, while placing those who enjoy universal approval in the lineage of the false prophets. The community Jesus is forming is one that will be characterized by present suffering and future vindication, not by present comfort and future loss.

**The enemy-love teaching (vv. 27-36)** is the ethical core of the chapter. It defines what life in this community looks like: not retaliation but generosity, not reciprocal exchange but gratuitous giving, not self-interested relationship but love directed toward those who can offer nothing in return. The theological foundation is the character of God: God is kind to the ungrateful and evil, and the disciples are to be merciful as their Father is merciful. The distinctiveness of the community is not that they love those who love them (everyone does that) but that they love those who hate them -- because that is what God does.

**The instructions about judging (vv. 37-42)** apply the mercy principle to internal community relationships: do not judge, do not condemn, pardon, give. Self-examination must precede any attempt to correct others. The blind cannot lead the blind; the person with a beam in their eye cannot remove a splinter from another's.

**The tree-and-fruit principle (vv. 43-45)** provides the diagnostic: the heart's content is revealed by what it produces. Speech and action are the overflow of what is stored within. This principle simultaneously validates the teaching (those who act mercifully reveal merciful hearts) and warns against hypocrisy (those who call Jesus "Lord" while practicing judgment and retaliation reveal hearts at odds with their words).

**The two builders (vv. 46-49)** bring the chapter to its conclusion with a stark either/or: hearing Jesus's words and acting on them builds a life that withstands the coming test; hearing without acting builds a life that collapses when the test arrives. The entire chapter has been building to this distinction. The Pharisees heard the Torah but responded to its fulfillment with rage. The disciples hear Jesus's words -- but will they act? The question is left open, to be answered by the narrative that follows in chapter 7, where a Gentile centurion, a grieving widow, a sinful woman, and the imprisoned John will each demonstrate different modes of hearing and responding.

The chapter's central claim is that the authority Jesus exercises over the Sabbath, over disease, over the community he forms, is exercised through a specific kind of character -- mercy, generosity, enemy-love, non-retaliation -- and that this character is not merely recommended but required, because it is the character of God Himself. To follow Jesus is to be formed into people who act like God acts: kind to the ungrateful, merciful to the evil, giving without expectation of return, building on the rock of obedience rather than the sand of mere acknowledgment.
