Titus 2 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Titus 1:10-16): The closing section of chapter 1 identified and condemned the false teachers operating in the Cretan communities. These are "especially those of the circumcision party" (1:10), who teach "Jewish myths and the commands of people who turn away from the truth" (1:14). Their teaching is characterized as empty talk and deception (1:10), their motive as shameful gain (1:11), and their effect as the overturning of whole households (1:11). The section concluded with a devastating verdict: "They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works. They are detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work" (1:16). The contrast between verbal profession and actual conduct — between claiming to know God and denying him through behavior — was established as the diagnostic test for genuine versus counterfeit faith.

What the author argues immediately after (Titus 3:1-8): Chapter 3 opens by extending the behavioral instructions outward, from the internal life of the community to its relationship with the surrounding civic order: "Remind them to be submissive to rulers and authorities, to be obedient, to be ready for every good work, to speak evil of no one, to avoid quarreling, to be gentle, and to show perfect courtesy toward all people" (3:1-2). This is followed by a theological statement (3:3-7) that parallels the one in 2:11-14 — both describe the appearing of God's saving action and its transformative effect on believers. Where 2:11-14 speaks of "the grace of God" appearing and "training us," 3:4-7 speaks of "the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior" appearing and saving us "by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit." Both theological statements ground behavioral instruction in God's prior action. And 3:8 draws the conclusion: "so that those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works."

How chapter 2 connects both: Chapter 2 is the hinge of the entire letter. It answers the question that chapter 1 posed: if the false teachers' program of external regulation and verbal profession produces detestable, disobedient people unfit for any good work (1:16), what does sound teaching produce? The answer is chapter 2: sound teaching produces specific, observable, transformed conduct across every social category — older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and slaves — all ordered toward the same qualities of self-control, dignity, and visible goodness. The chapter then provides the theological foundation for this transformed conduct (2:11-14): the grace of God itself is the agent of transformation, training believers to live in a specific way while they await a specific hope. Chapter 3 will then extend this same pattern outward to civic life and ground it in a second theological statement about God's saving mercy. The structure is: false teaching produces bad works (ch. 1) --> sound teaching produces good works (ch. 2) --> the theological ground of the transformation (2:11-14; 3:3-7) --> the extension to all of life (ch. 3). Chapter 2 supplies both the practical content and the theological engine.

Verse 1: The Pivot — From False Teaching to Sound Doctrine

"But as for you, teach what accords with sound doctrine."

(Sy de lalei ha prepei te hygiainouse didaskalia.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The false teachers profess to know God but deny him by their works (1:16). What the author argues immediately after: The specific content of sound doctrine is spelled out in the instructions to older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and slaves (2:2-10). How this verse connects the two: The emphatic pronoun sy ("you") and the adversative conjunction de ("but") create a sharp pivot. The preceding section described what the false teachers do and are. Now: "But you" — Titus, in contrast to them. The structure is identical to 1 Timothy 6:11, where after describing the false teachers' love of money, Paul wrote: "But as for you, O man of God, flee these things" (sy de, o anthrope theou, tauta pheuge). The sy de construction draws a line between Titus and the false teachers and places Titus on the opposite side.

The verb lalein ("to speak, to talk, to say, to utter") is a broader word than didaskein ("to teach") — it covers any kind of speaking, including informal instruction, conversation, and exhortation. The choice of lalein rather than didaskein may reflect the diverse contexts in which Titus will deliver this instruction: not only in formal teaching settings but in personal conversations, household visits, and community gatherings.

The relative clause ha prepei te hygiainouse didaskalia — "what is fitting for sound doctrine" or "what accords with healthy teaching" — specifies the content. The verb prepein ("to be fitting, to be suitable, to be proper, to be becoming") describes appropriateness: what Titus teaches must be suitable to, consistent with, and produced by sound doctrine. The implication is that doctrine has practical consequences — sound doctrine produces fitting behavior, and fitting behavior is the visible evidence of sound doctrine. The word prepein will reappear in verse 10, where slaves are to "adorn the doctrine of God our Savior" — the same logic of doctrine expressing itself in visible, fitting conduct.

The adjective hygiainousa ("sound, healthy, in good condition"), the present active participle of hygiainein ("to be healthy"), returns the medical metaphor that has run throughout the letter. In 1:9, the elder must be able to instruct in "sound doctrine" (te didaskalia te hygiainouse). In 1:13, the sharp rebuke aims at restoring the community so they may be "sound in the faith" (hygiainosin en te pistei). The participle is in the present tense, indicating ongoing health: the teaching is continually sound, perpetually healthy. It is not a teaching that was once healthy and has since declined; it is a teaching that maintains its health because it comes from the trustworthy word (1:9). The medical metaphor implies that false teaching is a disease and sound teaching is both the cure and the condition of health. The instructions that follow (2:2-10) are what healthy teaching looks like when it enters the life of a community.

Verses 2-6: Behavioral Instructions by Social Category

Verse 2: Older Men

"Older men are to be sober-minded, dignified, self-controlled, sound in faith, in love, and in steadfastness."

(Presbyteras nephalious einai, semnous, sophronas, hygiainontas te pistei, te agape, te hypomone.)

The noun presbyteras ("older men") here is not the same word as presbyterous ("elders") in 1:5, though both derive from the same root presbyteros ("older, elder"). The context determines the difference: in 1:5, presbyterous designated an office to be filled by appointment (katasteses, "appoint"); here, presbyteras designates a demographic category — the older men of the community, regardless of whether they hold the office of elder. The instruction addresses their character as community members, not their qualifications for office.

Six qualities are prescribed:

(1) Sober-minded (nephalious). The adjective nephalios ("sober, temperate, clear-headed") in its primary sense describes abstention from excessive wine. In 1 Timothy 3:2, it appeared among the overseer qualifications; in 1 Timothy 3:11, it was required of the women (whether deaconesses or deacons' wives). The word carries both a literal and a metaphorical sense: the nephalios person is both free from intoxication and clear in judgment. He is not muddled by excess. In a community where the false teachers are described as "evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12), the older men are to exhibit the opposite: clarity and restraint.

(2) Dignified (semnous). The adjective semnos ("worthy of respect, dignified, honorable, serious, reverent") describes a person whose conduct commands respect. In 1 Timothy 3:8, deacons must be semnous; in 1 Timothy 3:11, women must be semnas. In Philippians 4:8, whatever is semnon is among the things the community should think about. The word implies gravity without grimness — a life that carries weight, that is taken seriously, that commands esteem not by demanding it but by deserving it. The older men are to be the kind of people whose presence in the community lends it stability and authority.

(3) Self-controlled (sophronas). The adjective sophron ("of sound mind, self-controlled, temperate, prudent, moderate") appeared in 1:8 among the positive elder qualifications. It is a keynote term of this chapter, appearing or being echoed in nearly every social category: sophronas here for older men; sophronizein in verse 4 for what older women teach younger women; sophronein in verse 6 for younger men; sophronos in verse 12 for how the grace of God trains believers to live. The word family (sophron, sophronein, sophroneo, sophrosyne, sophronismos, sophronizo) saturates this chapter more densely than any other section of the Pauline corpus. The concentration is deliberate: self-control — the mastery of one's own impulses, desires, and reactions — is the central behavioral theme of Paul's instruction for Crete. Given the portrait of the Cretan character drawn in 1:12 ("always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons"), self-control is precisely the antidote: the capacity to govern oneself against the cultural currents of deception, brutishness, and appetite.

(4-6) Sound in faith, in love, and in steadfastness (hygiainontas te pistei, te agape, te hypomone). The participle hygiainontas ("being sound, being healthy") applies the medical metaphor directly to the older men's inner life. They are to be healthy — not merely appearing healthy but actually being healthy — in three domains:

Faith (pistis) — their trust in God and in the gospel. Love (agape) — their active care for others, the self-giving orientation that defines the community's life. Steadfastness (hypomone) — their capacity to endure, to persist, to remain under pressure without breaking. The noun hypomone ("endurance, patient endurance, perseverance, steadfastness") is composed of hypo ("under") + menein ("to remain, to stay, to abide"). It describes the quality of remaining under a burden or trial without abandoning one's position. In Romans 5:3-4, Paul writes: "suffering produces endurance (hypomonen), and endurance produces character (dokimen), and character produces hope." In Romans 15:4-5: "through endurance (tes hypomones) and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope." In James 1:3-4: "the testing of your faith produces steadfastness (hypomonen). And let steadfastness (hypomone) have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing."

The triad of faith, love, and steadfastness parallels the well-known Pauline triad of faith, love, and hope (1 Thessalonians 1:3; 1 Corinthians 13:13; Colossians 1:4-5), but with hypomone ("steadfastness/endurance") replacing elpis ("hope"). The substitution is not accidental. Hope is the future orientation; steadfastness is the present practice that hope makes possible. The older men are called to endurance — the daily, sustained, unglamorous persistence that keeps faith and love active over time. In a community facing the disruption of false teachers and the pressures of a hostile cultural environment, the steadfastness of the older men provides an anchor.

The fact that the older men must be "sound" (hygiainontas) in these three qualities implies that unsoundness is possible. Faith, love, and steadfastness can become diseased — weakened by false teaching, eroded by cultural compromise, or simply worn down by time and difficulty. The medical metaphor treats these as conditions requiring ongoing health, not achievements to be accomplished once and forgotten.

Verses 3-5: Older Women and Their Ministry to Younger Women

"Older women likewise are to be reverent in behavior, not slanderers or slaves to much wine. They are to teach what is good, and so train the young women to love their husbands and children, to be self-controlled, pure, working at home, kind, and submissive to their own husbands, that the word of God may not be reviled."

(Presbyteridas hosautos en katastemati hieroprepeis, me diabolous, me oino pollo dedoulomenas, kalodidaskalous, hina sophronizousin tas neas philandrous einai, philoteknous, sophronas, hagnas, oikourgous, agathas, hypotassomenas tois idiois andrasin, hina me ho logos tou theou blasphemetai.)

Older women (presbyteridas). The feminine form of presbyteros in its demographic sense: older women in the community, not holders of an office.

Likewise (hosautos). The adverb hosautos ("in the same way, likewise, similarly") connects the instruction for older women to that for older men: they too are held to a standard of conduct befitting sound doctrine, just as the older men are.

Reverent in behavior (en katastemati hieroprepeis). The noun katastema ("deportment, demeanor, bearing, outward appearance and conduct") describes the overall manner in which a person carries herself — her composure, bearing, and visible conduct taken as a whole. The adjective hieroprepes ("befitting a sacred person, reverent, suitable to a holy place") is composed of hieros ("sacred, holy, belonging to the divine") + prepein ("to be fitting, to be proper"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. It describes conduct that would be appropriate in a sacred context — the bearing of a person who lives as though she inhabits holy space. The compound implies that the older woman's entire life is a sacred sphere: her behavior in everyday settings should have the quality of behavior appropriate to a temple. This does not mean artificial solemnity but the kind of seriousness, dignity, and purposefulness that reflects an awareness of God's presence in all of life.

Not slanderers (me diabolous). The adjective diabolos ("slanderous, given to malicious gossip") is the same word used as a noun for "the devil" or "the accuser" (ho diabolos). In its adjectival use, it describes a person who accuses others falsely, who spreads destructive talk, who damages reputations through speech. In 1 Timothy 3:11, the same qualification appeared for women: me diabolous ("not slanderers"). In 2 Timothy 3:3, the word appears in the vice list of the last days. The older women's speech must not be destructive. In a community where false teachers are already spreading harmful talk (1:10-11), the older women must not compound the damage with malicious gossip. Their speech, like their behavior, must be hieroprepeis — fitting for the sacred.

Not slaves to much wine (me oino pollo dedoulomenas). The perfect passive participle dedoulomenas (from douloun, "to enslave, to make a slave of, to bring into bondage") describes a settled condition of enslavement: they have been enslaved and remain enslaved to much wine. The metaphor is strong — wine is not merely consumed but has become a master. The dative oino pollo ("to much wine") specifies the enslaver. The qualification echoes the elder's requirement of not being paroinos ("given to wine," 1:7) but uses a stronger image: not merely given to wine but enslaved by it. The qualification is practical: in the Greco-Roman world, wine was consumed at virtually every meal and social gathering, and the line between moderate use and excess was easily crossed. For the older women, who are about to be charged with training younger women, the requirement has particular force: a person enslaved to wine cannot be trusted with the formation of others.

Teachers of what is good (kalodidaskalous). The compound adjective kalodidaskalos ("teacher of good things, teacher of what is noble and excellent") is composed of kalos ("good, noble, beautiful, fine, excellent") + didaskalos ("teacher"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. It designates a specific role for the older women: they are to be teachers, and the content of their teaching is to kalon — what is good, noble, and excellent. This is not teaching in the formal sense of doctrinal instruction in the assembly but the formation of character through personal example, mentoring, and practical guidance. The older women have a teaching ministry specifically directed toward the younger women (verse 4), and the content of this teaching is specified in what follows.

So that they may train the young women (hina sophronizousin tas neas). The verb sophronizein ("to bring to one's senses, to train in self-control, to teach moderation, to encourage, to advise") is a causative form from the sophron root: to make someone sophron, to produce self-control in another person. The word appears only here in the New Testament. The hina ("so that, in order that") clause makes this the purpose of the older women's character and teaching role: they are to possess the qualities listed above so that they can form the younger women in self-control and wisdom. The younger women are tas neas — "the young women," using the general term neos ("young, new, fresh") rather than a more specific designation.

The content of the training is specified in a list of seven qualities:

(1) To love their husbands (philandrous einai). The adjective philandros ("husband-loving, affectionate to one's husband") is composed of philos ("loving, fond of") + aner ("husband, man"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. In Greek usage, philandros described the devoted wife, the woman whose primary relational commitment was to her husband. The quality is listed first, establishing the marital relationship as the primary sphere of the young woman's relational life.

(2) To love their children (philoteknous). The adjective philoteknos ("child-loving, loving one's children") is composed of philos ("loving") + teknon ("child"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. The quality extends the relational commitment from husband to children. The order is deliberate: husband first, then children — the same order as the household structure in which the family operates.

(3) Self-controlled (sophronas). The adjective sophron returns for the younger women, just as it appeared for the older men (v. 2) and the elders (1:8). The repetition reinforces the centrality of this quality across every category. The younger women need the same self-mastery as every other group.

(4) Pure (hagnas). The adjective hagnos ("pure, chaste, innocent, morally pure") describes moral and sexual purity. In 2 Corinthians 11:2, Paul writes: "I betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a pure virgin (parthenon hagnen) to Christ." In Philippians 4:8, hagna is among the qualities believers are to contemplate. In 1 Timothy 5:22: "Keep yourself pure (hagnon)." The adjective describes a life free from moral contamination, particularly in the sphere of sexual conduct. For young married women, the qualification points to marital faithfulness and the avoidance of the sexual laxity that characterized much of Greco-Roman society.

(5) Working at home (oikourgous). The adjective oikourgos ("working at home, caring for the household, domestic") is composed of oikos ("house, household") + ergon ("work"). Some manuscripts read oikourous ("keeper of the home," from oikos + ouros, "guardian, keeper"). In either reading, the quality describes active engagement with the domestic sphere — not idleness but productive work within the household. The ancient household was an economic unit, not merely a residence: the wife's management of the household was a substantial economic and social contribution. Proverbs 31:10-31 describes the valiant woman whose household management includes commerce, textile production, agriculture, and charitable activity — all conducted from and through the household. The qualification is not about confinement to a physical space but about industrious engagement with the sphere of responsibility entrusted to the young wife.

(6) Kind (agathas). The adjective agathos ("good, kind, generous, beneficial") in this context describes a disposition of kindness and goodness toward others. The young women are to be characterized by active benevolence, not merely by the absence of malice.

(7) Submissive to their own husbands (hypotassomenas tois idiois andrasin). The present middle/passive participle hypotassomenas (from hypotassein, "to place under, to arrange under, to subordinate") describes a voluntary ordering — the placement of oneself under the authority structure of the household. The reflexive pronoun idiois ("their own") specifies that this submission is within the particular marriage relationship, not a general submission of all women to all men. The verb hypotassein in the middle voice carries the sense of voluntary arrangement: the young wife places herself within the household order. The same instruction appears in Ephesians 5:22 and Colossians 3:18. In each case, the context is the ordered household as a unit within the larger community.

The instruction is given within the first-century household structure (the Greco-Roman oikos) where the household was the basic building block of society, and the relationship between husband and wife was embedded within a larger framework of mutual obligations. Paul's parallel instructions to husbands in Ephesians 5:25-33 (to love their wives as Christ loved the church) and Colossians 3:19 (to love their wives and not be harsh with them) indicate that the submission is not one-directional servitude but part of a mutual structure. In the present context, however, the instruction is given only to the wives through the older women's training, and the purpose clause that follows will explain why.

"That the word of God may not be reviled" (hina me ho logos tou theou blasphemetai). The hina me ("in order that not, lest") clause provides the purpose for the entire set of instructions to the younger women. The verb blasphemein ("to slander, to revile, to speak evil of, to defame, to blaspheme") describes hostile, contemptuous speech directed against something sacred. The subject of this blasphemy is ho logos tou theou — "the word of God." The concern is missionary and apologetic: the conduct of the community's members either commends or discredits the gospel message. If young women in the community behave in ways that the surrounding society regards as dishonorable, shameful, or subversive of social order, the result will be that outsiders speak evil of the word of God itself. The gospel will be blamed for producing bad behavior.

This concern has precedent. In Isaiah 52:5 (LXX): "Because of you my name is continually blasphemed (blasphemeitai) among the nations." In Romans 2:24, Paul quotes this same passage: "The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you." In 2 Samuel 12:14, Nathan tells David: "Because by this deed you have utterly scorned the LORD, the child who is born to you shall die" — David's conduct gave occasion for the enemies of YHWH to blaspheme. The pattern is consistent: the conduct of God's people either honors or dishonors God's name among those who observe them. The purpose clause reveals that Paul's behavioral instructions are not arbitrary social conservatism but strategic: the community's conduct is its primary testimony to the watching world, and conduct that provokes contempt from outsiders undermines the gospel's credibility. The younger women's faithfulness in their domestic roles serves the larger mission by keeping the word of God from being reviled.

Verses 6-8: Younger Men and Titus as Model

"Likewise, urge the younger men to be self-controlled. Show yourself in all respects to be a model of good works, and in your teaching show integrity, dignity, and sound speech that cannot be condemned, so that an opponent may be put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us."

(Tous neoterous hosautos parakalei sophronein, peri panta seauton parechomenos typon kalon ergon, en te didaskalia aphthorian, semnodeta, logon hygie akatagnodston, hina ho ex enantias entrape meden echon legein peri hemon phaulon.)

Younger men (tous neoterous). The comparative adjective neoteros ("younger") designates the younger men of the community, the demographic counterpart to the presbyteras ("older men") of verse 2.

Likewise (hosautos). Again the connecting adverb: the younger men, like the older men, older women, and younger women, are subject to instruction that accords with sound doctrine.

Urge (parakalei). The verb parakalein ("to exhort, to urge, to encourage, to appeal to, to comfort") appeared in 1:9 as part of the elder's dual task: "to exhort with sound doctrine and to refute those who contradict." Titus is now doing what the elders will eventually be responsible for: exhorting with sound doctrine. The present imperative indicates ongoing action: Titus is to keep urging them.

To be self-controlled (sophronein). The infinitive of sophronein ("to be of sound mind, to be self-controlled, to think soberly, to exercise moderation") is the verbal form of the adjective sophron that has appeared in every preceding category. The instruction to the younger men is strikingly compressed: where the older men received six qualities and the younger women received seven, the younger men receive one — self-control. This compression may reflect the fact that self-control encompasses all the other qualities: the young man who masters himself will naturally exhibit the sobriety, dignity, and soundness that characterize the older men. It may also reflect the particular vulnerability of younger men to impulsive, excessive, or reckless behavior — the single quality they most need is the one that governs all others.

The concentration of the sophron word family in this chapter is now fully visible: sophronas for older men (v. 2); sophronizein for what older women do for younger women (v. 4); sophronas for younger women (v. 5); sophronein for younger men (v. 6); and sophronos for how the grace of God trains believers to live (v. 12). Self-control is the thread that runs through every social category and culminates in the theological statement that attributes it to divine grace. It is not merely a human virtue to be cultivated through willpower; it is the product of God's grace operating in the community through sound teaching.

"Show yourself in all respects to be a model of good works" (peri panta seauton parechomenos typon kalon ergon). The verb parechesthai ("to show, to present, to furnish, to provide, to offer") in the middle voice means "to present yourself, to show yourself, to make yourself available as." The reflexive pronoun seauton ("yourself") and the universal qualifier peri panta ("in all things, in every respect") make the instruction comprehensive: Titus is to present himself in every area of life as a typon — "a model, a pattern, a type, an example."

The noun typos ("type, pattern, model, example, impression") in its primary sense is the mark left by a stamp or a blow — the impression in wax or clay that reproduces the shape of the die. By extension, it came to mean "pattern, model" — the thing that others reproduce by imitation. In Philippians 3:17, Paul writes: "Brothers, join in imitating me, and keep your eyes on those who walk according to the example (typon) you have in us." In 1 Thessalonians 1:7: "so that you became an example (typon) to all the believers in Macedonia and in Achaia." In 1 Timothy 4:12: "set the believers an example (typos) in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity." The typos is not merely a verbal instructor but a living demonstration — a person whose life reproduces the pattern that others are to follow.

The model is specified as kalon ergon — "good works" or "noble deeds." The adjective kalos ("beautiful, fine, noble, good, excellent") in the Pastoral Epistles describes works that are not merely morally correct but visibly admirable — works that can be seen and appreciated by observers. In 1 Timothy 3:1, the overseer's task is called kalon ergon. In 1 Timothy 5:10, a widow is evaluated by her kala erga. In 1 Timothy 5:25, good works (ta erga ta kala) "are conspicuous, and even those that are not cannot remain hidden." In 1 Timothy 6:18, the rich are to "be rich in good works" (ergois kalois). The phrase kalon ergon connects directly to the verdict on the false teachers in 1:16: they are "unfit for any good work" (pros pan ergon agathon adokimoi). Titus is to be the living opposite: a model of the very good works for which the false teachers are disqualified.

"And in your teaching show integrity, dignity" (en te didaskalia aphthorian, semnodeta). Two qualities are required of Titus's teaching:

(1) Integrity (aphthorian). The noun aphthoreia (or aphthoreia/aphthoria, "incorruptness, purity, integrity") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and phtheirein ("to corrupt, to destroy, to ruin"). Teaching that has aphthoreia is teaching that has not been corrupted — not adulterated, not mixed with impurities, not diluted with false elements. The word implies that teaching can be corrupted — and that the false teachers' teaching has been corrupted. Titus's teaching must be the uncorrupted original, free from the contamination of Jewish myths and human commands (1:14).

(2) Dignity (semnodeta). The noun semnoteta (semnotes, "dignity, seriousness, gravity, respectability") is the abstract noun from semnos ("worthy of respect, dignified"), which appeared in verse 2 for older men. The teaching itself must carry weight and gravity — it must be delivered with the seriousness that its content demands. Frivolous, casual, or careless teaching dishonors the word it conveys.

"And sound speech that cannot be condemned" (logon hygie akatagnodston). The noun logos ("word, speech, message") here refers to Titus's actual discourse — the words he uses when he teaches. The adjective hygies ("sound, healthy, whole") is the adjective form of the verb hygiainein that has characterized "sound doctrine" throughout the letter. The word connects Titus's speech to the medical metaphor: his words are to be healthy, free from disease. The adjective akatagnostos ("not able to be condemned, beyond reproach, unable to be censured") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) + katagignoskein ("to condemn, to find fault with, to pass judgment against"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. Titus's speech is to be of such quality that no legitimate criticism can be brought against it — no factual errors, no logical fallacies, no ethical failures, no contradictions with practice.

"So that an opponent may be put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us" (hina ho ex enantias entrape meden echon legein peri hemon phaulon). The purpose clause reveals the strategic dimension of Titus's modeling and teaching. The phrase ho ex enantias ("the one from the opposite side, the opponent, the adversary") describes anyone who opposes the community and its message — whether the false teachers within or hostile observers without. The verb entrepein ("to put to shame, to make ashamed, to cause to turn in upon oneself") in the passive describes the opponent's intended experience: he is to be shamed, turned back on himself, made to realize the emptiness of his criticism. And the means of his shaming is specified: meden echon legein peri hemon phaulon — "having nothing evil to say about us." The adjective phaulon ("evil, base, worthless, bad") describes speech that is harmful or contemptible. The opponent is silenced not by argument alone but by the absence of material for accusation. When Titus's life and teaching are beyond reproach, the opponent finds nothing to attack.

The pronoun hemon ("us") is worth noting. Paul shifts from second person singular (you, Titus) to first person plural (us). The shift may indicate that Paul includes himself and the broader apostolic circle in the scope of what the opponent might criticize: Titus's conduct reflects not only on himself but on the entire mission. Alternatively, "us" may encompass the whole community: the opponent's inability to find fault with Titus's teaching removes a potential weapon against the entire community.

Verses 9-10: Slaves — Conduct That Adorns the Doctrine

"Bondservants are to be submissive to their own masters in everything; they are to be well-pleasing, not argumentative, not pilfering, but showing all good faith, so that in everything they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior."

(Doulous idiois despotais hypotassesthai, en pasin euarestous einai, me antilegontas, me nosphizomenous, alla pasan pistin endeiknumenous agathen, hina ten didaskleian ten tou soteros hemon theou kosmousin en pasin.)

Bondservants (doulous). The noun doulos ("slave, bondservant, servant") designates persons in the condition of slavery — a fundamental and pervasive institution in the first-century Greco-Roman world. Estimates suggest that slaves constituted between 15% and 35% of the population of Roman provinces. Slavery was not racially based in the Roman system; persons became slaves through war, debt, birth to enslaved parents, exposure of infants, or self-sale. Slaves served in every capacity from agricultural labor to household management, from tutoring to accounting. The early communities included both slaves and masters (Philemon 10-16; 1 Corinthians 7:21-22; Colossians 3:22-4:1; Ephesians 6:5-9; 1 Timothy 6:1-2). The inclusion of slaves among the social categories receiving instruction indicates that they are full members of the community whose conduct matters as much as anyone else's.

To be submissive to their own masters (idiois despotais hypotassesthai). The verb hypotassesthai ("to submit, to be subject to, to place oneself under") in the middle/passive infinitive describes the same voluntary ordering that appeared for the young wives (v. 5). The dative idiois despotais ("to their own masters") specifies the relationship. The noun despotes ("master, lord, owner") designates the slaveholder — the person who has legal authority over the slave. The adjective idiois ("their own") specifies: each slave to his or her own master, not a general submission to all masters. The same instruction appeared in 1 Timothy 6:1: "Let all who are under a yoke as bondservants regard their own masters (tous idious despotas) as worthy of all honor." The instruction is practical: the slave's daily life is lived under the authority of the despotes, and the conduct of the slave in that relationship is the primary sphere in which his or her faith becomes visible.

In everything (en pasin). The comprehensive scope: the submission covers all areas of the slave's service, not merely selected duties.

Well-pleasing (euarestous). The adjective euarestos ("well-pleasing, acceptable, satisfying") is composed of eu ("well, good") + arestos ("pleasing, acceptable"). In Paul's usage, the word frequently describes what is pleasing to God: Romans 12:1, "present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable (euareston) to God"; Romans 14:18, "whoever thus serves Christ is acceptable (euarestos) to God and approved by men"; Ephesians 5:10, "try to discern what is pleasing (euareston) to the Lord"; Colossians 3:20, "Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases (euareston) the Lord." Here the word describes the slave's relationship to the human master, but the echoes of divine-pleasing conduct are audible: the slave who serves the earthly master well is simultaneously serving in a way that pleases God.

Not argumentative (me antilegontas). The present participle of antilegein ("to speak against, to contradict, to argue, to oppose verbally") describes a disposition of verbal resistance. The same verb appeared in 1:9 (tous antilegontas, "those who contradict" — the false teachers). The slave is not to exhibit the same argumentative spirit that characterizes the opponents of sound doctrine. The word does not prohibit all speech but describes the habitual disposition of talking back, contradicting, challenging every instruction.

Not pilfering (me nosphizomenous). The present middle participle of nosphizein ("to misappropriate, to embezzle, to steal by setting aside for oneself, to pilfer") describes the practice of secretly taking from the master's goods for oneself. The verb appears in Acts 5:2-3, where Ananias and Sapphira "kept back" (enosphisato) part of the proceeds from the sale of their property — the same verb for secret misappropriation. In the LXX, the verb appears in Joshua 7:1 for Achan's taking of devoted things (enosphisanto apo tou anathematos). The instruction is practical: slaves had daily access to their masters' property and the temptation to take small amounts was constant. Pilfering damages the master, violates trust, and — in the context of this passage — discredits the gospel.

But showing all good faith (alla pasan pistin endeiknumenous agathen). The adversative alla ("but, rather, on the contrary") introduces the positive counterpart. The verb endeiknysthai ("to show, to demonstrate, to display, to prove") in the middle voice means "to show forth from oneself, to demonstrate by one's own conduct." What is demonstrated is pasan pistin agathen — "all good faith" or "complete good faithfulness." The noun pistis here functions in its sense of "faithfulness, trustworthiness, fidelity" rather than "belief." The adjective agathen ("good") and the adjective pasan ("all, complete, total") together describe a comprehensive, thorough trustworthiness: the slave is to be demonstrably reliable in every respect. The master who employs a believing slave should find that slave more trustworthy, not less — more reliable, not less — more faithful in every duty, not less.

"So that in everything they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior" (hina ten didaskalian ten tou soteros hemon theou kosmousin en pasin). The purpose clause for the slaves' conduct matches the purpose clause for the younger women (v. 5, "that the word of God may not be reviled") but states the purpose positively rather than negatively. Where verse 5 aimed to prevent blasphemy, verse 10 aims to produce adornment.

The verb kosmein ("to arrange, to put in order, to decorate, to adorn, to make beautiful") is the verb from which the noun kosmos ("order, adornment, world, universe") derives. The word describes the act of making something beautiful through arrangement, decoration, or embellishment. In Matthew 23:29, the Pharisees "adorn (kosmeite) the tombs of the righteous." In Matthew 25:7, the virgins "trimmed (ekosmesan) their lamps." In 1 Peter 3:5, "the holy women... used to adorn (ekosmoun) themselves." In 1 Timothy 2:9, women are to "adorn (kosmein) themselves in respectable apparel." The image is striking: the slave's conduct adorns the doctrine. Doctrine is treated as something visible that can be either adorned or disfigured by the conduct of those who profess it. The slave who is submissive, well-pleasing, non-argumentative, honest, and thoroughly faithful makes the doctrine look beautiful — puts it on display in its best possible form.

The object adorned is ten didaskalian ten tou soteros hemon theou — "the doctrine of God our Savior." The full title tou soteros hemon theou ("of God our Savior") reappears from 1:3. The doctrine that the slaves adorn is not a human philosophy or social program but the teaching that comes from God himself — the God who saves. The effect is that the slave, occupying the lowest position in the social hierarchy, becomes the bearer of the gospel's public beauty. The most powerful apologetic for the doctrine is not the eloquence of its teachers but the transformed conduct of its most socially invisible adherents. When a slave who has every reason to be resentful, dishonest, and resistant instead demonstrates complete faithfulness, kindness, and willing service, the doctrine that produced that transformation is adorned.

The phrase en pasin ("in everything, in all things") bookends the instruction: slaves are to submit "in everything" (en pasin, v. 9) and to adorn the doctrine "in everything" (en pasin, v. 10). The comprehensiveness of the scope matches the comprehensiveness of the claim: the doctrine shapes all of life, and the slave's entire conduct serves the doctrine's display.

Verses 11-14: The Theological Ground — The Grace of God Has Appeared

"For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age, waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works."

(Epephane gar he charis tou theou soterios pasin anthropois, paideuousa hemas hina arnesamenoi ten asebeian kai tas kosmikas epithymias sophronos kai dikaios kai eusebos zesomen en to nyn aioni, prosdechomenoi ten makarian elpida kai epiphaneian tes doxes tou megalou theou kai soteros hemon Iesou Christou, hos edoken heauton hyper hemon hina lytrousetai hemas apo pases anomias kai katharise heauto laon periousion zelouten kalon ergon.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The behavioral instructions for every social category (2:2-10), each oriented toward visible conduct that commends the gospel. What the author argues immediately after: Titus is to "declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority" (2:15), then chapter 3 extends the behavioral instructions to civic life (3:1-2) and provides a second theological grounding statement (3:3-7). How this section connects the two: Verses 11-14 provide the theological engine for the behavioral instructions that precede them and the continued instructions that follow. The conjunction gar ("for") at the beginning makes this explicit: the reason the community members should live as described in 2:2-10 is that the grace of God has appeared and is actively training them. The behavioral instructions are not arbitrary moral requirements imposed from outside but the natural product of God's grace operating from within. The theological statement transforms the preceding social instructions from human ethics into the fruit of divine action.

Verse 11: The Appearing of Grace

"For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people" (Epephane gar he charis tou theou soterios pasin anthropois).

The verb epiphainein ("to show forth, to appear, to become visible, to manifest") in the aorist passive (epephane) describes a past event: something that was not previously visible has now come into view. The verb shares its root with the noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation"), which will appear in verse 13 for the future appearing of Christ. The two uses create a frame: there has been a first appearing (the grace of God has appeared) and there will be a second appearing (the glory of our great God and Savior, v. 13). The community lives between these two epiphanies.

The concept of divine epiphany was well known in the first-century world. In Greek religion, an epiphaneia was the visible manifestation of a deity — a god appearing to humans in perceptible form. In the LXX, the language of appearing is used for God's self-disclosure: Exodus 3:2, the angel of the LORD "appeared" (ophthe) to Moses in the burning bush; Genesis 12:7, "The LORD appeared (ophthe) to Abram." The Feast of Hanukkah was called the feast of the Epiphaneia because it celebrated God's manifest intervention through the Maccabees (2 Maccabees 15:27). In the Roman imperial cult, the emperor's visit to a city was an epiphaneia — a visitation of the divine ruler. Paul's use of the term operates on multiple levels: it draws on Israel's tradition of divine visitation, and it implicitly contrasts the appearing of God's grace with the epiphanies claimed for emperors and pagan deities.

What has appeared is he charis tou theou — "the grace of God." The noun charis ("grace, favor, kindness, gift, goodwill") in Paul's usage consistently describes God's unmerited favor — the disposition of God toward humanity that gives what humanity has not earned and cannot deserve. Romans 3:24: "being justified by his grace (te autou chariti) as a gift." Romans 5:15: "the free gift in the grace (te chariti) of the one man Jesus Christ." Ephesians 2:8-9: "For by grace (te chariti) you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works." Grace in Paul is not merely God's attitude but God's action — the concrete expression of divine favor in the events of salvation.

The grace is described as soterios — "saving, bringing salvation, having the power to save." The adjective soterios ("saving, delivering, preservative") is related to soter ("savior") and soteria ("salvation"). The grace that has appeared is characterized by its saving purpose: it comes to deliver, to rescue, to bring soteria.

The scope is specified: pasin anthropois — "for all people" or "to all humanity." The dative pasin ("all, every") with anthropois ("humans, people") indicates the universality of the grace's reach. The grace of God has appeared not for one ethnic group, one social class, or one demographic category but for all people. This is precisely the point of the preceding social instructions: older men, older women, younger women, younger men, slaves — the grace reaches every category because it is for all people. The universal scope of grace is also the theological refutation of the false teachers' narrowing program: they impose circumcision and Jewish regulations on the community, restricting access to God's favor; but the grace of God has appeared for all people, without such restrictions.

The same universality appeared in 1 Timothy 2:4: God "desires all people (pantas anthropous) to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth." And in 1 Timothy 4:10: God "is the Savior of all people (panton anthropon), especially of those who believe." The consistency is deliberate: the grace of God is universally available, extending to every person without ethnic or social precondition.

Verse 12: The Training Function of Grace

"Training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age" (paideuousa hemas hina arnesamenoi ten asebeian kai tas kosmikas epithymias sophronos kai dikaios kai eusebos zesomen en to nyn aioni).

The present active participle paideuousa ("training, educating, instructing, disciplining") from paideuein ("to train a child, to educate, to discipline, to instruct, to correct") describes the ongoing activity of grace. The verb paideuein has a rich background. In Greek usage, it described the education and formation of a child (pais) — the comprehensive process of shaping character, instilling knowledge, and training in proper behavior. In the LXX, paideuein takes on the additional connotation of divine discipline: Proverbs 3:11-12 (LXX): "My son, do not despise the discipline (paideias) of the Lord... for the Lord disciplines (paideuei) the one he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights." Deuteronomy 8:5: "Know then in your heart that, as a man disciplines (paideuei) his son, the LORD your God disciplines (paideusei) you." The paideia of the Lord is formative: it shapes, corrects, and trains the person toward maturity and right conduct.

The subject of paideuousa is he charis — "grace." This is a remarkable statement. Grace is not merely the initial act of forgiveness or the entry point into relationship with God. Grace is an active, ongoing trainer. It educates. It disciplines. It shapes behavior. The grace that "appeared" (v. 11) in a past event continues to operate in the present as a formative force in the lives of believers. The present tense of the participle (paideuousa, "training") indicates continuous action: grace keeps training, keeps educating, keeps forming.

The object of the training is hemas — "us." The pronoun is first person plural, including Paul, Titus, and the entire community. Grace trains all of them, not just the leaders or the spiritually mature. Every social category addressed in 2:2-10 is included in the "us" whom grace is training.

The content of the training is twofold — a negative renunciation and a positive manner of life:

(A) Renunciation: "to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions" (arnesamenoi ten asebeian kai tas kosmikas epithymias). The aorist middle participle arnesamenoi (from arneisthai, "to deny, to renounce, to refuse, to repudiate") describes a decisive act of denial — not gradual reduction but definitive renunciation. The same verb appeared in 1:16, where the false teachers "deny" (arnountai) God by their works. Now the believers are called to deny something: asebeian and kosmikas epithymias. The verb creates a deliberate contrast: the false teachers deny God; the believers deny ungodliness. The act of denial that the false teachers direct toward God, the believers redirect toward what is contrary to God.

The noun asebeia ("ungodliness, impiety, irreverence, lack of proper worship") is the direct opposite of eusebeia ("godliness, piety"), which was the letter's goal from the greeting (1:1, "the truth that accords with godliness"). What is renounced is the entire orientation of life away from God — the fundamental irreverence that treats God as irrelevant, unimportant, or nonexistent in practical terms. The false teachers exemplify asebeia: they profess to know God but live as though God's actual requirements do not apply to them.

The phrase tas kosmikas epithymias ("worldly passions, worldly desires") identifies the specific manifestations of ungodliness. The adjective kosmikos ("belonging to the world, worldly, of this present order") describes desires that are shaped by and oriented toward the present world-order — the system of values, priorities, and appetites that operates apart from God's purposes. The noun epithymia ("desire, longing, craving, lust, passion") in Paul's usage typically describes desires that pull a person away from God's purposes: Romans 1:24, "God gave them up in the lusts (epithymiais) of their hearts"; Galatians 5:16, "do not gratify the desires (epithymian) of the flesh"; Galatians 5:24, "those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires (epithymiais)." The phrase does not condemn all desire but identifies desires that are kosmikai — shaped by the world-system rather than by God's purpose.

(B) Positive manner of life: "to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age" (sophronos kai dikaios kai eusebos zesomen en to nyn aioni). The aorist subjunctive zesomen ("we might live") describes the manner of life that grace produces. Three adverbs specify its character:

(1) Self-controlled (sophronos). The adverb of sophron — the word that has dominated this entire chapter. Self-control is now revealed as not merely a human achievement but the product of grace's training. When grace educates, the result is sophrosyne — the mastery of one's own impulses and desires.

(2) Upright (dikaios). The adverb of dikaios ("just, righteous, fair"). Living justly — treating others rightly, honoring obligations, acting with fairness.

(3) Godly (eusebos). The adverb of eusebes ("godly, pious, devout"). Living with proper reverence toward God — the eusebeia that was the letter's goal from 1:1. The three adverbs address three relationships: sophronos governs the relationship to oneself (self-mastery); dikaios governs the relationship to others (justice); eusebos governs the relationship to God (reverence). Together they constitute a complete ethic — a life properly ordered in every direction.

The phrase en to nyn aioni ("in the present age") specifies the temporal context. The noun aion ("age, era, epoch") in Jewish and early Christian usage describes a period of time characterized by certain conditions. The "present age" (ho nyn aion) is the age before the final consummation — the time in which the community lives after the first appearing of grace but before the second appearing of glory (v. 13). The phrase "in the present age" accomplishes two things: it acknowledges that the present conditions are not final (there is an age to come), and it insists that the present conditions are not irrelevant (godly living is required now, in this age, not deferred to the future). The community does not wait passively for the age to come; it lives actively in the present age according to the pattern grace has taught.

Verse 13: The Blessed Hope — Awaiting the Second Appearing

"Waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (prosdechomenoi ten makarian elpida kai epiphaneian tes doxes tou megalou theou kai soteros hemon Iesou Christou).

The present middle participle prosdechomenoi ("waiting for, expecting, looking forward to, welcoming") from prosdechesthai ("to receive, to wait for, to expect, to look forward to") describes the community's posture as it lives in the present age: it is a community in active expectation. The participle is present tense, indicating ongoing waiting — not a one-time act of hope but a sustained orientation toward a future event. In Mark 15:43, Joseph of Arimathea was "waiting for the kingdom of God" (prosdechomenos ten basileian tou theou). In Luke 2:25, Simeon was "waiting for the consolation of Israel" (prosdechomenos paraklesin tou Israel). In Luke 2:38, Anna spoke of Jesus to "all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem" (tois prosdechomenois lutrosin Ierousalem). The verb describes the posture of faithful Israel — the patient, expectant waiting for God to fulfill his promises. The community in Crete shares this posture: they live in the present age, but their orientation is toward what God will do next.

What they await is described with two nouns joined by kai ("and"):

(1) The blessed hope (ten makarian elpida). The noun elpis ("hope, expectation, confident anticipation") describes not a vague wish but a confident expectation grounded in God's promise. In the greeting (1:2), Paul's apostleship rests ep' elpidi zoes aioniou — "on the hope of eternal life." The hope of the greeting is now revisited: the community's daily life is oriented toward this same hope. The adjective makarios ("blessed, happy, fortunate, to be envied") describes the hope as blessed — it is a source of deep happiness, a ground of congratulation. The elpis is makaria because what it expects is certain: it rests on the promise of the God who cannot lie (1:2).

(2) The appearing of the glory (epiphaneian tes doxes). The noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation, visible display") echoes the verb epephane ("appeared") in verse 11, creating the framework of two appearances. The first epiphaneia was the appearing of grace (v. 11, past). The second epiphaneia is the appearing of glory (v. 13, future). Between these two appearances, the community lives, trains, renounces, and waits. The noun doxa ("glory, splendor, brightness, radiance, magnificence, honor") in the LXX translates the Hebrew kavod ("weight, glory, honor") and describes the visible manifestation of God's presence and power. The kavod of YHWH appeared as fire on Sinai (Exodus 24:17), filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34-35), and filled the temple (1 Kings 8:10-11). The glory of God is God's presence made visible. The community awaits the moment when the glory that is presently hidden will become visible — the full, public manifestation of God's splendor.

"Of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (tou megalou theou kai soteros hemon Iesou Christou). This phrase is among the most theologically dense in the letter. The grammatical structure — a single article (tou) governing both megalou theou ("great God") and soteros hemon ("our Savior"), with Iesou Christou ("Jesus Christ") standing in apposition to the whole — most naturally reads as attributing both titles to one person: Jesus Christ is "our great God and Savior." This reading follows a well-established Greek grammatical pattern (the Granville Sharp rule): when two substantives of the same case are connected by kai and governed by a single article, they typically refer to the same person. This is how a first-century Greek speaker would most naturally parse the phrase.

The title megalou theou ("great God") echoes the LXX and its descriptions of Israel's God. Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God (ho theos ho megas), mighty and awesome." Psalm 95:3 (LXX 94:3): "For the LORD is a great God (theos megas), and a great King above all gods." Daniel 2:45 (LXX): "The great God (ho theos ho megas) has made known to the king what shall be after this." Nehemiah 8:6: "And Ezra blessed the LORD, the great God (ton theon ton megan)." The application of this language to Jesus Christ continues the pattern established in the greeting (1:3-4), where the title soter ("savior") was applied to both God and Christ. Throughout the letter, the titles of Israel's God flow to Christ: Savior (1:4; 2:13; 3:6), and now great God (2:13). The alternation between "God our Savior" (1:3; 2:10; 3:4) and "Jesus Christ our Savior" (1:4; 3:6) or "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13) creates a theological unity in which Christ participates in the identity and activity of Israel's God.

Verse 14: The Purpose of Christ's Self-Giving

"Who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works" (hos edoken heauton hyper hemon hina lytrousetai hemas apo pases anomias kai katharise heauto laon periousion zelouten kalon ergon).

The relative pronoun hos ("who") refers back to Jesus Christ. The verb edoken ("he gave") is the aorist of didonai ("to give"). The reflexive pronoun heauton ("himself") specifies what was given: Christ gave himself. The preposition hyper ("for, on behalf of, for the sake of") with the genitive hemon ("us") specifies the beneficiaries: the giving was for us, on our behalf. In Galatians 1:4, Paul writes: "who gave himself (tou dontos heauton) for our sins to deliver us from the present evil age." In Galatians 2:20: "the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself (paradondos heauton) for me." In Ephesians 5:25: "Christ loved the church and gave himself up (heauton paredoken) for her." In 1 Timothy 2:6: "who gave himself (ho dous heauton) as a ransom for all." The language of self-giving is consistent across the Pauline corpus: Christ's death is understood as a voluntary act of giving himself on behalf of others.

The purpose of this self-giving is stated with a hina clause containing two infinitives:

(1) "To redeem us from all lawlessness" (lytrousetai hemas apo pases anomias). The verb lytrousthai ("to redeem, to ransom, to set free by paying a price, to liberate") in the middle voice means "to ransom for himself." The word carries profound resonance from the LXX and Israel's history. The verb lytrousthai and the related noun lytrosis ("redemption, ransom, deliverance") are the primary vocabulary for God's liberation of Israel from Egypt. Deuteronomy 7:8: "the LORD redeemed you (elytrosato hymas) out of the house of slavery." Deuteronomy 13:5 (LXX 13:6): "the LORD your God, who redeemed you (ton lytrousamemon se) out of the land of Egypt." Psalm 130:8 (LXX 129:8): "And he will redeem (lytrosetai) Israel from all his iniquities (ek pason ton anomion autou)." The parallel between Psalm 130:8 (LXX 129:8) and this verse is striking: the Psalm says God will redeem Israel from all his iniquities; Paul says Christ gave himself to redeem us from all lawlessness. The language of redemption from Egypt is applied to redemption from anomia.

The noun anomia ("lawlessness, iniquity, wickedness, transgression of law") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) + nomos ("law"). Anomia describes the condition of living without law — not merely breaking specific commands but existing in a state of fundamental disregard for God's ordering of life. In Matthew 7:23, Jesus says to those who claim to have prophesied and cast out demons in his name: "Depart from me, you workers of lawlessness (hoi ergazomenoi ten anomian)." In Matthew 24:12: "because lawlessness (anomian) will be increased, the love of many will grow cold." In Romans 6:19: "just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to lawlessness (te anomia) leading to more lawlessness (anomian), so now present your members as slaves to righteousness leading to sanctification." In 2 Thessalonians 2:7: "the mystery of lawlessness (tes anomias) is already at work."

The adjective pases ("all, every") makes the redemption comprehensive: not from some lawlessness but from all lawlessness. The redemption is total. Christ's self-giving addresses the entire condition of anomia, not merely its individual manifestations. This connects to the false teachers' situation: they profess to know God but are "disobedient" (apeitheis, 1:16) and "unfit for any good work" (1:16) — they exhibit the very anomia from which Christ's self-giving redeems. Redemption from lawlessness is not merely forensic (declared free from penalty) but transformative (actually delivered from the condition of lawlessness into a new pattern of life).

(2) "And to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works" (kai katharise heauto laon periousion zelouten kalon ergon). The second purpose of Christ's self-giving is purification. The verb katharizein ("to cleanse, to purify, to make clean") in the LXX describes both ritual purification (Leviticus 14:7, the cleansing of a leper; Numbers 8:6-7, the purification of the Levites) and moral cleansing (Psalm 51:2 LXX [50:4]: "Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me [katharison me] from my sin"). The verb connects to the purity theme of 1:15: "To the pure, all things are pure." Where the false teachers have defiled minds and consciences (1:15) and attempt to achieve purity through external regulations, Christ's self-giving accomplishes actual purification. The cleansing is real, not ceremonial; it reaches the nous and the syneidesis that the false teachers' program cannot touch.

The reflexive dative heauto ("for himself") specifies the beneficiary: Christ purifies this people for himself. The people belong to Christ as a result of the purification. This echoes the covenantal language of the LXX, where God takes Israel as his own people.

The phrase laon periousion — "a people for his own possession" or "a special people" — is one of the most important allusions in the letter. The adjective periousios ("chosen, special, one's own particular possession, set apart as property") appears in the LXX at Exodus 19:5: "you shall be to me a people periousios (laos periousios) from all the nations" — the same phrase. Deuteronomy 7:6: "For you are a people holy to the LORD your God. The LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession (laon periousion), out of all the peoples who are on the face of the earth." Deuteronomy 14:2: "For you are a people holy to the LORD your God, and the LORD has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession (laon periousion), out of all the peoples who are on the face of the earth." Deuteronomy 26:18: "the LORD has declared today that you are a people for his treasured possession (laon periousion), as he has promised you."

The phrase laon periousion is a signature designation for Israel's covenant identity: Israel is God's special, treasured, chosen people, set apart from all nations as his own. By applying this phrase to the community of believers in Christ — a community that includes Gentile Cretans, slaves, and members of every social category — Paul makes an extraordinary claim: the community formed by Christ's self-giving is the laos periousios, the people of God's own possession. The identity that belonged to Israel at Sinai now belongs to those whom Christ has redeemed and purified. This is not the replacement of Israel but the fulfillment of the promise contained in the Exodus covenant: God said he would have a people for his own, and through Christ's self-giving, he does. The inclusion of Gentiles in this people is precisely what the circumcision party (1:10) resists, and precisely what the gospel, as Paul understands it, accomplishes.

The final qualification of this people is zelouten kalon ergon — "zealous for good works." The adjective zelotes ("zealot, zealous person, one who burns with zeal") describes passionate, active commitment. In Acts 21:20, the Jewish believers in Jerusalem are described as "all zealous for the law" (zelotai tou nomou). In Acts 22:3, Paul describes himself as "zealous for God" (zelotes hyparchon tou theou). In Galatians 1:14, Paul was "extremely zealous for the traditions of my fathers" (perissoteros zelotes hyparchon ton patrikon mou paradoseon). The noun carries the sense of burning, passionate devotion — not tepid approval but active, eager pursuit. And the object of this zeal is kalon ergon — "good works," the same phrase that appeared in verse 7 (Titus as a model of "good works") and that contrasts with the false teachers' disqualification from "every good work" (1:16).

The entire purpose statement of verse 14 thus answers the question the letter has been building toward: what does Christ's self-giving accomplish? It accomplishes two things. First, it redeems from lawlessness — it delivers people from the condition of living apart from God's purposes. Second, it purifies a people for Christ's own possession — a people whose identity echoes Israel's covenant identity at Sinai, and whose character is defined by zealous pursuit of good works. The false teachers' program of external regulation and verbal profession cannot produce this people; only Christ's self-giving can. The behavioral instructions of 2:2-10 are not moral bootstrapping; they are the visible manifestation of what Christ's redemption and purification have produced — a people who live the way they live because they have been redeemed from lawlessness and purified for Christ's own possession.

Verse 15: The Charge to Titus

"Declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you."

(Tauta lalei kai parakalei kai elenche meta pases epitages; medeis sou periphroneitō.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The theological ground of transformed living — Christ's redemptive self-giving (2:11-14). What the author argues immediately after: The extension of behavioral instruction to civic life — submission to rulers, readiness for every good work, gentleness toward all people (3:1-2). How this verse connects the two: Verse 15 is a hinge, closing the instructions of chapter 2 and authorizing Titus to continue with what follows in chapter 3. The verse does not introduce new content but empowers the delivery of all the content that has preceded and will follow.

"Declare these things" (tauta lalei). The demonstrative pronoun tauta ("these things") refers back to everything in the chapter: the behavioral instructions (2:2-10) and the theological ground (2:11-14). The verb lalein ("to speak, to say, to talk") returns from verse 1: there, Titus was to "speak what accords with sound doctrine"; here, the instruction is to keep speaking "these things" — the specific content that accords with sound doctrine has now been supplied, and Titus is to deliver it.

"Exhort and rebuke with all authority" (parakalei kai elenche meta pases epitages). Two verbs specify the modes of Titus's speaking:

(1) Exhort (parakalei). The verb parakalein ("to encourage, to urge, to exhort, to appeal to") describes the positive dimension: Titus is to encourage the community toward the behaviors and attitudes described in the chapter.

(2) Rebuke (elenche). The verb elenchein ("to refute, to reprove, to convict, to expose") describes the confrontational dimension: Titus is to confront those who resist or contradict. The same verb appeared in 1:9 (the elder must "rebuke those who contradict") and 1:13 ("rebuke them sharply"). The combination of parakalei and elenche — encouragement and confrontation — captures the full scope of Titus's pastoral responsibility. Sound doctrine requires both positive instruction for the willing and firm correction for the resistant.

The phrase meta pases epitages ("with all authority") specifies the manner of Titus's speech. The noun epitage ("command, order, decree, authority, injunction") appeared in 1:3, where Paul's preaching was entrusted to him kat' epitagen tou soteros hemon theou — "by the command of God our Savior." The connection is deliberate: the authority with which Titus speaks is not his own but derives from the same chain of command that authorized Paul. God commanded Paul; Paul commissioned Titus; Titus speaks with the full weight of that delegated authority. The adjective pases ("all, full, complete") indicates that Titus is not to hold back or dilute his authority: he is to exercise the full authority that has been given to him.

"Let no one disregard you" (medeis sou periphroneitō). The verb periphronein ("to look down on, to despise, to disregard, to think around or past") is composed of peri ("around") + phronein ("to think, to have in mind"). The word appears only here in the New Testament. It describes the act of thinking past someone, looking around them as though they were not there — dismissal through disregard rather than active opposition. The present imperative with medeis ("let no one") is a prohibition: no one in the community is to treat Titus with this kind of contemptuous dismissal.

The parallel in 1 Timothy 4:12 is instructive: "Let no one despise (kataphroneitō) you for your youth." Timothy's potential liability was his youth; the verb used there, kataphronein ("to look down on, to think against"), describes active contempt. For Titus, the verb periphronein suggests a different dynamic: the risk is not contempt but dismissal — the community might simply ignore Titus's authority, looking past him rather than opposing him directly. The instruction authorizes Titus to insist on being heard: his message carries the authority of the apostolic commission, and the community cannot afford to look past it.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Titus 2 answers the question that chapter 1 posed: if false teaching produces people who are "detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work" (1:16), what does sound teaching produce? The answer unfolds in three movements:

(1) Verses 1-10: Sound teaching produces transformed conduct in every social category. The chapter opens with a sharp pivot from the false teachers to Titus: "But as for you, teach what accords with sound doctrine" (2:1). What follows is specific behavioral instruction for every category of the community: older men are to be sober-minded, dignified, self-controlled, sound in faith, love, and steadfastness (2:2); older women are to be reverent, not slanderous, not enslaved to wine, but teachers of good who train the younger women (2:3-4); younger women are to love their husbands and children, be self-controlled, pure, industrious, kind, and submissive to their own husbands (2:4-5); younger men are to be self-controlled (2:6); Titus himself is to be a model of good works with teaching marked by integrity, dignity, and sound speech (2:7-8); slaves are to be submissive, well-pleasing, honest, and demonstrably faithful (2:9-10). Two purpose clauses frame the instructions: the younger women's conduct prevents the word of God from being reviled (2:5), and the slaves' conduct adorns the doctrine of God our Savior (2:10). The community's behavior is its primary testimony to the watching world.

The dominant quality across every category is self-control (sophrosyne in its various forms), which appears in connection with older men, older women's teaching, younger women, younger men, and the theological statement. Self-control is the antidote to the Cretan cultural tendencies described by their own prophet (1:12) and the defining mark of the community formed by sound doctrine.

(2) Verses 11-14: The theological ground — the grace of God has appeared and is training believers. The behavioral instructions are not arbitrary moral demands but the product of divine action. The grace of God has appeared (past event, first epiphany), bringing salvation for all people (2:11). This grace actively trains believers to renounce ungodliness and worldly desires and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age (2:12). Meanwhile, the community waits for the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ (2:13, second epiphany). Christ gave himself for us with a twofold purpose: to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession — the laos periousios of the Exodus covenant — who are zealous for good works (2:14). The theological statement transforms the behavioral instructions from human ethics into the fruit of grace, and it connects the community's identity to Israel's covenant identity as God's treasured people.

The two epiphanies — the appearing of grace (past) and the appearing of glory (future) — create the temporal framework within which the community lives. The present age is not a waiting room but a training ground, where the grace that has already appeared continues to form the people who will greet the glory when it appears.

(3) Verse 15: Titus is charged to declare, exhort, and rebuke with full authority. The closing verse authorizes the delivery of everything the chapter contains. Titus is not offering suggestions; he speaks with the authority of the apostolic commission, which derives from God's own command (1:3). No one is to disregard him.

The chapter's architecture reveals a careful logic: behavior flows from doctrine (2:1), doctrine is grounded in God's action in Christ (2:11-14), and God's action creates a people whose very identity is defined by zealous pursuit of good works (2:14). The false teachers of chapter 1 imposed external regulations while their minds and consciences remained defiled (1:15). The sound teaching of chapter 2 works from the inside out: grace trains, Christ redeems and purifies, and the result is a people who live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives — not because they have been commanded to by human authorities, but because the grace of the God who saves has appeared and is actively forming them into the people he purified for his own.
