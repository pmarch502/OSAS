# 1 John 4 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (1 John 3):** Chapter 3 opened with an exclamation about the community's identity as children of God: "See how great a love the Father has given us, that we would be called children of God; and in fact we are" (3:1). The language of divine parentage introduced at the end of chapter 2 ("everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him," 2:29) was developed into a full exploration of what it means to be God's children versus the devil's children. The criteria for distinguishing the two groups are behavioral: "Everyone who practices sin also practices lawlessness" (3:4); "No one who remains in him sins continually; no one who sins continually has seen him or known him" (3:6); "The one who practices sin is of the devil" (3:8). The Son of God appeared to take away sins (3:5) and to destroy the works of the devil (3:8). By 3:10, the author stated the summary test: "By this the children of God and the children of the devil are obvious: everyone who does not practice righteousness is not of God, nor the one who does not love his brother."

From 3:10 onward, the chapter pivoted to love as the defining mark. "This is the message which you have heard from the beginning, that we are to love one another" (3:11) -- the second use of the word angelia ("message") in the letter, the first being the declaration that "God is light" (1:5). The example of Cain, who "was of the evil one and murdered his brother" because "his own deeds were evil, but his brother's were righteous" (3:12), served as the negative illustration. The author then stated the present application: "We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers and sisters. The one who does not love remains in death" (3:14). Love was defined not as word or tongue but as deed and truth (3:18), and its supreme model was Christ: "We know love by this, that He laid down His life for us; and we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers and sisters" (3:16). The chapter concluded with a statement that directly triggers the opening of chapter 4: "The one who keeps His commandments remains in Him, and He in him. We know by this that He remains in us, by the Spirit whom He has given us" (3:24).

This final clause is critical. The author has stated that the evidence of mutual indwelling -- God remaining in the believer and the believer remaining in God -- is "the Spirit whom He has given us." The Spirit is introduced as the marker of genuine relationship with God. But if the Spirit is the evidence, a question immediately arises: how does one distinguish the true Spirit from a false one? If anyone can claim to have "the Spirit," how do the recipients of the letter know which spirits are genuinely from God? Chapter 4 opens by answering precisely this question.

**What the author argues immediately after (1 John 5:1-5):** Chapter 5 opens with a declaration that gathers up the two themes chapter 4 has been weaving together -- right confession and love: "Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the Father loves the child born of Him" (5:1). The christological confession that chapter 4 tested in vv. 1-6 and the love that chapter 4 expounded in vv. 7-21 are now united in a single statement about divine parentage. "By this we know that we love the children of God, when we love God and follow His commandments" (5:2). The commandments are not burdensome (5:3). Then the theme of overcoming returns: "For whoever has been born of God overcomes the world; and this is the victory that has overcome the world: our faith. Who is the one who overcomes the world, but the one who believes that Jesus is the Son of God?" (5:4-5). The verb nikao ("to overcome"), which appeared in 4:4 ("you have overcome them"), now receives its full explanation: the victory over the world is achieved through faith in Jesus as the Son of God -- the very confession that 4:2-3 identified as the test of the Spirit of God and that 4:15 identified as the condition for mutual indwelling with God.

**How chapter 4 connects these:** Chapter 3 ended with the Spirit as the evidence of God's indwelling. Chapter 5 will unite right confession with love as the two marks of being born of God. Chapter 4 is the bridge: it opens by explaining how to test spirits (4:1-6), pivots to the nature and practice of love (4:7-21), and in doing so demonstrates that these two themes -- right confession about Jesus and love for the brothers -- are not separate subjects but two expressions of the same reality. The confession that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:2) and the practice of love for one another (4:7-12) are both evidence that one is "from God" (ek tou theou). The chapter's structure mirrors the letter's two great declarations about God's nature: "God is light" (1:5) governed the diagnostic tests of chapters 1-3; "God is love" (4:8, 16) governs the exposition of love in this chapter. Together they describe the single, undivided character of God and the corresponding single, undivided life required of those who claim fellowship with him.

## Verses 1-3: Testing the Spirits -- The Criterion of the Incarnation

"Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world. By this you know the Spirit of God: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God; and every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not from God; this is the spirit of the antichrist, which you have heard is coming, and now it is already in the world."

**"Beloved" (agapetoi).** The author addresses the community with the same term of affection he used in 2:7 and 3:2, 21. The word agapetos ("beloved, dear one") comes from the same root as agape ("love"), and its use here, at the opening of a section that will ultimately develop into the letter's fullest treatment of love, is significant. The community is addressed as those who are loved before they are instructed about love. The author does not stand over the community as a judge but alongside it as one who shares their identity as God's beloved.

**"Do not believe every spirit" (me panti pneumati pisteuete).** The present imperative with the negative me functions as a standing prohibition: "stop believing every spirit" or "do not make it your habit to believe every spirit." The word pneuma ("spirit") here does not refer to disembodied supernatural beings in the abstract but to the spiritual influence or teaching that stands behind human teachers. In the first-century context, prophetic speech was understood as the expression of a spirit working through a human speaker. The spirit was known by its message. The community is not told to reject all spiritual claims but to evaluate them -- not all spirits are from God.

The connection to 3:24 is immediate. The author has just stated that the Spirit given by God is the evidence of mutual indwelling. Now he warns that not every claim to have or speak by "spirit" is valid. The transition is seamless: if the Spirit is the marker of genuine relationship with God, then testing whether a given spirit is truly from God is an urgent practical necessity.

**"But test the spirits to see whether they are from God" (alla dokimazete ta pneumata ei ek tou theou estin).** The verb dokimazo ("to test, to examine, to prove, to approve after testing") belongs to the vocabulary of metallurgy and craftsmanship -- it was used for testing metals to determine their purity. In the LXX, God tests hearts (Psalm 17:3 [16:3 LXX]: "You have tested [edokimasas] my heart"); the Psalmist asks God to test him (Psalm 26:2 [25:2 LXX]: "Test me [dokimason me], O LORD, and try me; examine my mind and my heart"); and Israel tested God in the wilderness (Psalm 95:9 [94:9 LXX]: "your fathers tested me [edokimasan me]"). Paul uses the word for the testing of faith (Romans 5:4), the testing of what is God's will (Romans 12:2: "so that you may prove [dokimazein] what the will of God is"), and the self-examination before the Lord's Supper (1 Corinthians 11:28: "let a person examine [dokimazeto] himself"). The author instructs the community to apply this same critical examination to spiritual claims. The phrase "whether they are from God" (ei ek tou theou estin) uses the preposition ek ("from, out of") to indicate origin -- the test determines the source. A spirit is either from God or not from God; there is no third category.

**"Because many false prophets have gone out into the world" (hoti polloi pseudoprophetai exelelythasin eis ton kosmon).** The reason for the testing is stated: many false prophets are already active. The word pseudoprophetes ("false prophet") has deep roots in Israel's scriptures. Moses warned Israel: "If a prophet or a dreamer of dreams arises among you and gives you a sign or a wonder, and the sign or the wonder comes true, of which he spoke to you, saying, 'Let us follow other gods and let us serve them,' you shall not listen to the words of that prophet" (Deuteronomy 13:1-3). The false prophet in Deuteronomy is not necessarily one who fails to produce signs but one whose message leads away from the true God -- the test is the direction of the teaching, not the spectacle of the sign. Jeremiah warned: "Do not let your prophets who are in your midst or your diviners deceive you, and do not listen to their interpretations of your dreams which you dream. For they prophesy falsely to you in My name; I have not sent them" (Jeremiah 29:8-9). Ezekiel condemned "foolish prophets who are following their own spirit and have seen nothing" (Ezekiel 13:3). The false prophet in Israel's tradition is one who claims to speak for God but whose message originates in human invention or in a spirit other than God's.

The verb exelelythasin ("have gone out," perfect tense of exerchomai) indicates completed action with ongoing relevance -- the false prophets went out and are still out there. The language echoes the description of the departed community members in 2:19: "They went out from us" (ex hemon exelthan). The false prophets and the departed schismatics are the same group, or at least overlapping groups. They have gone out "into the world" (eis ton kosmon) -- they operate in the sphere that the author has already characterized as the domain opposed to God (2:15-17).

**"By this you know the Spirit of God" (en touto ginoskete to pneuma tou theou).** The formula "by this you know" (en touto ginoskete) recurs throughout the letter as the marker of a diagnostic test (2:3, 5; 3:16, 19, 24; 4:2, 13; 5:2). The verb ginoskete may be either indicative ("you know") or imperative ("know!"). In context, it functions as both: the author is telling them the criterion and assuming they can apply it. "The Spirit of God" (to pneuma tou theou) is the genuine Spirit, the one whose presence confirms mutual indwelling (3:24).

**"Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (pan pneuma ho homologei Iesoun Christon en sarki elelythota ek tou theou estin).** The test is christological and specific. The verb homologeo ("to confess, to acknowledge openly, to profess") has appeared in the letter for confession of sin (1:9) and confession of the Son (2:23: "the one who confesses the Son has the Father also"). Here, the content of the confession is precisely stated: "Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" (Iesoun Christon en sarki elelythota). The perfect participle elelythota ("having come and remaining") is significant -- it describes not merely a past event but a continuing reality. Jesus Christ came in the flesh and remains the one who came in the flesh; the incarnation is not a temporary episode but a permanent reality.

The phrase "in the flesh" (en sarki) is the crux. The word sarx ("flesh") in this author's vocabulary denotes genuine, physical, material human existence. The prologue of the Fourth Gospel declared: "the Word became flesh" (ho logos sarx egeneto, John 1:14). The author of 1 John opened his letter by testifying to having heard, seen, gazed upon, and touched with his hands the word of life (1:1). The insistence on the bodily, physical reality of Jesus's existence has been present from the document's first sentence. Now it becomes the explicit test: does the spirit-behind-the-teacher affirm that the Messiah Jesus came as a real human being, in real flesh? Any teaching that separates the divine Christ from the human Jesus -- that treats the incarnation as partial, temporary, or illusory -- fails this test.

The letter's opening prologue (1:1-4) was written against this background. The cascade of physical verbs -- "heard," "seen with our eyes," "looked at," "our hands have touched" -- was not ornamental but polemical: it asserted, against those who denied it, that the word of life had a tangible body. Chapter 4 now formalizes what the prologue enacted: the confession of Jesus Christ in the flesh is the dividing line between truth and error.

**"And every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not from God" (kai pan pneuma ho me homologei ton Iesoun ek tou theou ouk estin).** The negative counterpart. Some manuscripts read "does not confess Jesus Christ as having come in the flesh" (expanding the shorter reading), while others have the shorter "does not confess Jesus" (me homologei ton Iesoun). The shorter reading may be original, with the longer reading being a harmonization with verse 2. The shorter reading is more stark: any spirit that does not confess Jesus -- that refuses to acknowledge the person of Jesus in his full identity as the Christ who came in the flesh -- is not from God. The name "Jesus" (ton Iesoun) without further qualification points to the whole person: the historical man Jesus of Nazareth, who is the Christ, who came in the flesh. To not confess "Jesus" is to deny the entire reality that the name represents.

**"This is the spirit of the antichrist" (touto estin to tou antichristou).** The neuter demonstrative touto ("this") refers back to the spirit that does not confess Jesus. This spirit is identified as "the [spirit] of the antichrist" (to tou antichristou -- the article tou with the genitive indicates "the one belonging to the antichrist"). The term antichristos appeared in 2:18 ("antichrist is coming, even now many antichrists have appeared") and 2:22 ("This is the antichrist, the one who denies the Father and the Son"). In 2:18, the expected singular antichrist was realized in multiple antichrists; here, the spirit of the antichrist is the spiritual force animating those multiple false prophets. The author is not describing a single eschatological villain but a present, active spiritual influence that works through human teachers who deny the incarnation.

**"Which you have heard is coming, and now it is already in the world" (ho akeekoate hoti erchetai, kai nyn en to kosmo estin ede).** This precisely echoes 2:18: the expected future figure is already present. The community was taught to expect a coming antichrist; the author informs them that the spirit of the antichrist is not a future threat but a present reality. It is "already" (ede) in the world. The perfect tense akeekoate ("you have heard and continue to know") indicates that this was part of their foundational instruction. The author is not introducing new information but drawing out the implications of what they already know.

## Verses 4-6: Two Origins, Two Audiences -- The Community's Victory Over the False Prophets

"You are from God, little children, and have overcome them; because greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world. They are from the world, therefore they speak as from the world, and the world listens to them. We are from God. The one who knows God listens to us; the one who is not from God does not listen to us. By this we know the spirit of truth and the spirit of error."

**"You are from God, little children" (hymeis ek tou theou este, teknia).** The emphatic pronoun hymeis ("you") contrasts the community with the false prophets. The preposition ek ("from, out of") identifies origin and source, as it has throughout the passage: the spirits are tested to see if they are "from God" (ek tou theou, v. 1); the spirit that confesses Jesus is "from God" (ek tou theou, v. 2); now the community itself is declared to be "from God." The community's origin determines its allegiance, its capacity to recognize truth, and its ultimate destiny. The address teknia ("little children") returns to the author's characteristic pastoral term (2:1, 12, 28; 3:7, 18).

**"And have overcome them" (kai nenikekate autous).** The verb nikao ("to overcome, to conquer") in the perfect tense (nenikekate) indicates completed victory with ongoing results: the community has overcome and stands in the state of having overcome. The pronoun autous ("them") refers to the false prophets / antichrists of verses 1-3. The victory is not future; it is accomplished. The community has already triumphed over the false teachers. In 2:13-14, the young men were said to have "overcome the evil one" (nenikekate ton poneron); here, the entire community has overcome the evil one's agents. The victory will be expanded in 5:4-5, where it is identified with faith: "this is the victory that has overcome the world -- our faith."

**"Because greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world" (hoti meizon estin ho en hymin e ho en to kosmo).** The reason for the community's victory is stated in terms of comparative power. "He who is in you" (ho en hymin) is the Spirit given by God (3:24) -- the anointing (chrisma) that teaches and abides (2:27). "He who is in the world" (ho en to kosmo) is the spirit of the antichrist (v. 3), which is "already in the world." The contest is between two indwelling powers: the Spirit of God within the community and the spirit of error within the world. The community's victory is not achieved by its own strength, wisdom, or discernment but by the superior power of the one who indwells them. This echoes Jesus's declaration in the farewell discourse: "In the world you have tribulation, but take courage; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). The community's victory derives from Jesus's victory.

**"They are from the world" (autoi ek tou kosmou eisin).** The pronoun autoi ("they") refers to the false prophets. Their origin is "from the world" (ek tou kosmou) -- the system of values and allegiances opposed to God (as defined in 2:15-17). Three consequences follow from this origin:

**"Therefore they speak as from the world" (dia touto ek tou kosmou lalousin).** Their speech is determined by their origin. Those who are from the world speak the world's language, advance the world's concerns, and articulate the world's perspective. The preposition ek governs both their being and their speaking. The content of their teaching bears the marks of its source.

**"And the world listens to them" (kai ho kosmos auton akouei).** The world recognizes its own. The world listens to those who speak its language because the message resonates with the world's values. This provides a diagnostic criterion: popularity with the world is not evidence of truth but evidence of worldly origin. The reception a message receives is shaped by the audience's origin; the world receives what comes from the world.

**"We are from God" (hemeis ek tou theou esmen).** The pronoun hemeis ("we") shifts the focus to the author and the eyewitness community. The "we" here is the apostolic circle -- those who saw, heard, and touched the word of life (1:1-4). Their origin is from God, and therefore their message bears the marks of that origin.

**"The one who knows God listens to us" (ho ginoskon ton theon akouei hemon).** The criterion now runs in the other direction: just as the world listens to its own, so those who know God listen to the apostolic community. The verb ginosko ("to know") in the present participle (ginoskon) indicates ongoing, experiential knowledge -- not mere awareness but relational acquaintance with God. The logic is circular by design: those who know God recognize the voice of God's messengers; those who do not know God do not. This is not an arbitrary gatekeeping but a description of how spiritual recognition works. Jesus expressed the same principle in the Fourth Gospel: "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me" (John 10:27); "He who is of God hears the words of God; for this reason you do not hear them, because you are not of God" (John 8:47). Recognition of the true message is itself evidence of one's origin.

**"The one who is not from God does not listen to us" (hos ouk estin ek tou theou ouk akouei hemon).** The negative counterpart completes the criterion. Non-reception of the apostolic message is evidence that the hearer is not from God. The departed group has demonstrated this: they left the community and rejected the apostolic teaching, thereby revealing their origin.

**"By this we know the spirit of truth and the spirit of error" (ek toutou ginoskomen to pneuma tes aletheias kai to pneuma tes planes).** The two spirits are now named: "the spirit of truth" (to pneuma tes aletheias) and "the spirit of error" (to pneuma tes planes). The phrase "spirit of truth" (pneuma tes aletheias) is the precise term Jesus used in the farewell discourse for the Holy Spirit: "the Spirit of truth (to pneuma tes aletheias), whom the world cannot receive, because it does not see him or know him; you know him because he abides with you and will be in you" (John 14:17; also 15:26, 16:13). The Fourth Gospel's description of the world's inability to receive the Spirit of truth directly parallels the statement here that the world listens to those from the world and does not listen to those from God.

The phrase "spirit of error" or "spirit of deception" (to pneuma tes planes) has no direct parallel in the Fourth Gospel but connects to the broader first-century Jewish vocabulary of two spirits or two ways. The noun plane ("error, wandering, deception") derives from the verb planao ("to lead astray, to deceive"), which the author has already used in 1:8 (self-deception), 2:26 (those trying to deceive the community), and 3:7 ("let no one deceive you"). The concept of two opposed spirits -- one of truth and one of deception -- operating in the world and contending for human allegiance is attested in the Rule of the Community from the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QS 3:18-4:26), where the "spirit of truth" and the "spirit of deceit" are set over humanity until the time of God's visitation. While the author of 1 John is not directly dependent on the Qumran texts, both draw from a common Jewish tradition of moral dualism in which the world is a battleground between truth and falsehood, light and darkness, God and the adversary.

The criterion the author provides is relational and communal, not individual and subjective. The test is not "does this spirit make me feel a certain way?" but "does this spirit confess Jesus Christ come in the flesh, and does it align with the apostolic testimony?" The community discerns the spirits by reference to the christological confession (v. 2) and by noting who listens to the apostolic witness (v. 6). Both tests are external and verifiable.

## Verses 7-12: Love, the Nature of God, and the Incarnation as Love's Supreme Expression

"Beloved, let us love one another; for love is from God, and everyone who loves has been born of God and knows God. The one who does not love does not know God, because God is love. By this the love of God was revealed in us, that God has sent His only Son into the world so that we may live through Him. In this is love, not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son to be the propitiation for our sins. Beloved, if God so loved us, we also ought to love one another. No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God remains in us, and His love is perfected in us."

**"Beloved, let us love one another" (agapetoi, agapomen allelous).** The address agapetoi ("beloved") appears for the second time in the chapter (after v. 1), now opening a new section. The hortatory subjunctive agapomen ("let us love") includes the author in the exhortation -- this is not a command from above but an invitation to shared practice. The shift from the christological test of vv. 1-6 to the love exhortation of vv. 7-21 is not a change of subject but a deepening of the same argument. The test of the spirits asked whether a teacher confesses Jesus Christ come in the flesh; the love exhortation now asks whether the community lives out the implications of that confession. If Jesus Christ came in the flesh because of God's love (as vv. 9-10 will state), then the community that confesses this must embody the same love. Confession without love is as empty as love without confession.

The connection to the preceding chapters is also direct. The commandment to love one another was introduced in 2:7-11 as both old and new, was declared "the message you have heard from the beginning" in 3:11, and was elaborated in 3:14-18 with the example of Christ laying down his life. Now, in chapter 4, the author grounds the commandment not merely in Jesus's example or in the community's founding instruction but in the very nature of God.

**"For love is from God" (hoti he agape ek tou theou estin).** The preposition ek ("from, out of") again indicates origin. Love does not originate in human effort, emotion, or decision; it comes from God. Love is not a human virtue that pleases God; it is a divine reality that enters human life from its source in God. This claim prepares for the declaration in verse 8.

**"And everyone who loves has been born of God and knows God" (kai pas ho agapon ek tou theou gegennnetai kai ginoskei ton theon).** Two results follow from loving: divine parentage and knowledge of God. The perfect tense gegennnetai ("has been born and remains in that state") describes the new birth as a past event with continuing reality. The present tense ginoskei ("knows") describes an ongoing relationship. The author does not say "everyone who loves will be born of God" (as though love earns the new birth) but states a present correspondence: the one who loves is the one who has been born of God. Love is the evidence of divine parentage, just as practicing righteousness was the evidence in 2:29 and 3:9-10. The test is not whether one claims to be born of God but whether the family resemblance -- love -- is visible.

**"The one who does not love does not know God" (ho me agapon ouk egno ton theon).** The negative counterpart uses the aorist egno ("did not come to know, never knew") rather than the present "does not know." The aorist is more absolute: the person who does not love never arrived at knowledge of God in the first place. It is not that they knew God and then lost that knowledge; they never had it. Their claim to know God was always empty.

**"Because God is love" (hoti ho theos agape estin).** This is the chapter's second great declaration about God's nature, following "God is light" in 1:5. The structure is identical: subject (ho theos) + predicate nominative without article (agape) + verb (estin). As with "God is light," the anarthrous predicate indicates quality or nature: love is what God is, describing his essential character. The declaration does not say God is "loving" (an attribute among others) but God is love (love defines his being). The statement is not reversible -- the author does not say "love is God," which would deify a human emotion. Rather, the nature of God determines the nature of genuine love: to know what love is, one must look at God; to see love in action, one must see what God does.

The two great declarations -- "God is light" and "God is love" -- are not contradictory but complementary. Light describes God's moral purity, his total absence of darkness, his self-revealing transparency. Love describes God's self-giving action toward others, his movement outward toward the world he has made. Light is God's character as it relates to truth and holiness; love is God's character as it relates to relationship and redemption. Both are equally fundamental, and neither can be understood without the other. Light without love would be mere judgment; love without light would be mere sentiment. The God of this letter is both.

**"By this the love of God was revealed in us" (en touto ephanerothe he agape tou theou en hemin).** The verb phaneroo ("to reveal, to make visible, to manifest") has been used throughout the letter for divine acts of making known: the life was manifested (1:2), the Son of God was manifested to take away sins (3:5) and to destroy the works of the devil (3:8). Now God's love is said to have been manifested. Love is not an abstract quality; it was made visible in a specific historical event. The phrase "in us" (en hemin) may mean "among us" (in our midst) or "toward us" (directed at us) -- probably both: God's love was displayed in the community's midst and directed toward the community.

**"That God has sent His only Son into the world so that we may live through Him" (hoti ton huion autou ton monogene apestalken ho theos eis ton kosmon hina zesomen di' autou).** The content of the manifestation is the sending of the Son. The word monogenes (rendered "only" or "only begotten") requires attention. In the LXX, monogenes translates the Hebrew yachid ("only, unique, solitary"). Abraham's sacrifice of Isaac is described with this word: "Take now your son, your only son (ton monogene), whom you love -- Isaac" (Genesis 22:2 LXX). The Psalmist pleads: "Rescue my soul from destruction, my only life (ten monogene mou) from the lions" (Psalm 22:20 [21:21 LXX]; cf. Psalm 25:16 [24:16 LXX]; 35:17 [34:17 LXX]). Judges 11:34 describes Jephthah's daughter as his "only child" (monogenes). The word denotes uniqueness and special status rather than a biological process of generation. In the Fourth Gospel, monogenes appears in 1:14 ("the glory as of the only [monogenous] from the Father"), 1:18 ("the only [monogenes] God who is in the bosom of the Father"), and 3:16, 18 ("He gave His only [monogene] Son"). The author of 1 John uses the same term to identify the Son's unique relationship to the Father -- he is the one-of-a-kind Son, the Son with no parallel.

The verb apestalken ("has sent," perfect tense of apostello) indicates a past sending with continuing effect: God sent the Son, and the effects of that sending persist. The purpose is stated with hina ("so that, in order that"): "so that we may live through him" (hina zesomen di' autou). The subjunctive zesomen ("we may live") uses the verb zao ("to live"), connecting to the letter's opening declaration about "the eternal life" (ten zoen ten aionion, 1:2) and to the promise of 2:25 ("this is the promise which He Himself made to us: eternal life"). The preposition dia ("through") with the genitive autou ("him") identifies the Son as the means, the channel, the agent through whom life comes to the community. Life is not self-generated; it comes through the sent Son.

**"In this is love, not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son to be the propitiation for our sins" (en touto estin he agape, ouch hoti hemeis egapekamen ton theon, all' hoti autos egapesen hemas kai apesteilen ton huion autou hilasmon peri ton hamartion hemon).** The author now defines love by correcting a potential misunderstanding. Love is not defined by the human movement toward God ("not that we loved God") but by God's movement toward humanity ("that He loved us"). The direction of genuine love is established: it flows from God to humanity, not the reverse. Human love for God is real (it will be discussed in vv. 19-21), but it is responsive, not originary. God's love precedes, initiates, and defines what love is.

The word hilasmos ("propitiation, atoning sacrifice") appeared earlier in 2:2: "He Himself is the propitiation for our sins; and not for ours only, but also for the whole world." The repetition of hilasmos here, in the context of God's love, reveals something about the author's understanding of atonement. The hilasmos is not something offered by humanity to appease an angry God; it is something God provides out of his own love. God loved, and therefore God sent, and the purpose of the sending was atonement. The initiative belongs entirely to God. The same theological structure appears in the Fourth Gospel: "God so loved the world that He gave His only Son" (John 3:16). Love is the motive; the sending of the Son is the action; atonement for sins is the purpose.

The preposition peri ("concerning, about, for") with the genitive ton hamartion hemon ("our sins") indicates that the hilasmos addresses the problem of sin. The background in the Levitical Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) has already been discussed in connection with 2:2. Here the emphasis falls on the love that provides the atonement: the hilasmos is the expression of divine love, not the condition for it.

**"Beloved, if God so loved us, we also ought to love one another" (agapetoi, ei houtos ho theos egapesen hemas, kai hemeis opheilomen allelous agapan).** The third occurrence of agapetoi ("beloved") in this chapter. The logic is simple and forceful: God's love for the community creates an obligation (opheilomen, "we ought, we are obligated, we owe it") for the community to love one another. The verb opheilo ("to owe, to be obligated") carries the force of a debt -- those who have received God's love owe love to one another. This is not a new idea in the letter; it was stated in 3:16: "We know love by this, that He laid down His life for us; and we ought (opheilomen) to lay down our lives for the brothers and sisters." The same verb, the same obligation. The difference is that in 3:16 the model was Christ's self-sacrifice; here the model is God's sending of the Son. Both point to the same reality: love received generates love owed.

**"No one has ever seen God" (theon oudeis popote teatheatai).** This declaration echoes the Fourth Gospel almost verbatim: "No one has seen God at any time" (theon oudeis heoarken popote, John 1:18). The verb teatheatai (perfect middle/passive of theaomai, "to gaze at, to behold") is the same verb used in 1:1 for the eyewitnesses' sustained observation of the word of life: "which we have looked at" (ho etheasametha). The author who insisted on physical, visual encounter with the incarnate Word now states that God himself has never been visually observed by anyone. This is not a contradiction but a distinction: the Word made flesh was seen and touched; God in his essential being remains invisible. The incarnation did not render God visible in his fullness but made God's love visible through the Son.

In Israel's tradition, God's invisibility was axiomatic. Moses was told: "You cannot see My face, for mankind shall not see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20). Isaiah's vision in the temple was not a sight of God's essential being but of his glory and the train of his robe (Isaiah 6:1-5). The invisible God makes himself known through his acts, his words, and -- now -- through his Son. The statement "no one has ever seen God" sets up a question: if God is invisible, how is he known? The answer follows immediately.

**"If we love one another, God remains in us, and His love is perfected in us" (ean agapomen allelous, ho theos en hemin menei kai he agape autou en hemin teteleimene estin).** The invisible God is made present -- not to the eyes but in the community -- through love. When the community loves one another, God "remains" (menei) in them. The verb meno ("to remain, to abide") is the letter's characteristic word for the sustained presence of God in the community and the community's sustained connection to God. The mutual love of the community is the locus of God's dwelling, the place where the invisible God is present.

"His love is perfected in us" (he agape autou en hemin teteleimene estin) -- the perfect passive participle teteleimene ("having been perfected, having been brought to completion") from teleioo ("to complete, to bring to the goal") indicates that God's love reaches its intended destination and achieves its intended purpose when the community loves one another. God's love is not perfected in the act of sending the Son (though that is its supreme expression); it is perfected when that love, having been received by the community, produces love among the community's members. The love that originates in God, is expressed in the sending of the Son, and is received by the community reaches its telos -- its goal, its completion -- when it flows through the community in mutual love. God's love is not a static attribute; it is a dynamic force that moves from God through the Son into the community and then through the community toward one another. It is perfected -- brought to its full expression -- only when the entire circuit is complete.

This verse also connects to 2:5: "whoever keeps His word, in him the love of God has truly been perfected." There, love was perfected through obedience to God's word; here, it is perfected through love for one another. These are not two different perfections but two descriptions of the same reality: obedience to God's word includes the commandment to love one another (3:23), and love for one another is the concrete expression of obedience.

## Verses 13-16a: The Spirit, the Eyewitness Testimony, and Confession as the Basis of Mutual Indwelling

"By this we know that we remain in Him and He in us, because He has given to us of His Spirit. We have seen and testify that the Father has sent the Son to be the Savior of the world. Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God, God remains in him, and he in God. We have come to know and have believed the love which God has for us."

**"By this we know that we remain in Him and He in us, because He has given to us of His Spirit" (en touto ginoskomen hoti en auto menomen kai autos en hemin, hoti ek tou pneumatos autou dedoken hemin).** The formula "by this we know" (en touto ginoskomen) appears again, marking another diagnostic test. The mutual indwelling -- "we remain in Him and He in us" -- is the same reality described in 3:24: "The one who keeps His commandments remains in Him, and He in him." The evidence of this mutual indwelling is "because He has given to us of His Spirit" (hoti ek tou pneumatos autou dedoken hemin). The preposition ek ("of, from") with the genitive tou pneumatos autou ("His Spirit") is partitive: God has given "from" or "of" his Spirit. The phrasing suggests that the Spirit given to the community is a share in God's own Spirit -- not a separate substance but a participation in God's own being.

The verb dedoken ("has given," perfect tense of didomi) indicates a completed gift with continuing effect: God gave the Spirit, and the gift remains. This echoes the Fourth Gospel's promise: "I will ask the Father, and He will give you another Advocate, that He may be with you forever -- the Spirit of truth" (John 14:16-17). The Spirit is the permanent gift that sustains the mutual indwelling.

This verse forms a bracket with 3:24. In 3:24, the Spirit was the evidence of God's remaining in the community; the opening of chapter 4 then addressed the need to test spirits. Now, having established the test (vv. 1-6), the author returns to the Spirit as the evidence of mutual indwelling. The argument has come full circle: the Spirit is the assurance (3:24), but the Spirit must be tested (4:1-3), and the community has overcome the false spirits (4:4-6), so the genuine Spirit remains the reliable evidence of God's presence (4:13).

**"We have seen and testify that the Father has sent the Son to be the Savior of the world" (kai hemeis tetheametha kai martyroumen hoti ho pater apestalken ton huion sotera tou kosmou).** The "we" (hemeis) here is emphatic and returns to the eyewitness circle of 1:1-4. The verb tetheametha ("we have seen," perfect middle/passive of theaomai) is the same verb used in 1:1 ("which we have looked at") and in verse 12 ("no one has ever seen God"). The eyewitnesses cannot claim to have seen God, but they can and do testify to having seen the Son whom the Father sent. The present tense martyroumen ("we testify") indicates that the testimony is ongoing -- the eyewitness proclamation continues through this letter.

The title sotera tou kosmou ("Savior of the world") appears only here and in John 4:42, where the Samaritans declared after encountering Jesus: "we know that this One truly is the Savior of the world." The word soter ("savior, deliverer, rescuer") was used in the LXX for God as Israel's deliverer: "I, even I, am the LORD, and there is no savior (soter) besides Me" (Isaiah 43:11); "a righteous God and a Savior (soter)" (Isaiah 45:21). Judges uses the term for the deliverers God raised up to rescue Israel from oppression (Judges 3:9, 15). In the Greco-Roman world, soter was a title applied to gods (Zeus Soter), to kings, and to the Roman emperor. The declaration that the Father sent the Son as "Savior of the world" is a claim that the role of ultimate deliverer -- a role that belongs to God in Israel's scriptures -- is fulfilled in the Son, and that its scope encompasses the entire world, not Israel alone. This connects to 2:2 ("not for ours only but also for the whole world") and expresses the universal reach of God's redemptive love.

**"Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God, God remains in him, and he in God" (hos ean homologese hoti Iesous estin ho huios tou theou, ho theos en auto menei kai autos en to theo).** The verb homologeo ("to confess") returns from verse 2, where confessing "Jesus Christ come in the flesh" was the test of the Spirit of God. Now the content of the confession is stated differently: "Jesus is the Son of God." The two confessions are not contradictory but complementary -- they identify the same person from two angles. Verse 2 insists on the reality of the incarnation (come in the flesh); verse 15 insists on the identity of the incarnate one (the Son of God). Both are necessary. To confess the incarnation without confessing the divine sonship, or to confess the divine sonship without the incarnation, would be incomplete.

The result of the confession is mutual indwelling: "God remains in him, and he in God." The same mutual indwelling that was evidenced by the Spirit (v. 13) is now connected to the christological confession. The Spirit and the confession are not alternative paths to relationship with God; they are aspects of the same reality. The Spirit produces the confession (as 4:2 implied -- the Spirit of God is the one that confesses Jesus), and the confession is the verbal expression of the Spirit's work. Those who confess that Jesus is the Son of God do so because the Spirit of God is in them, and their confession confirms that God remains in them and they in God.

**"We have come to know and have believed the love which God has for us" (kai hemeis egnokamen kai pepisteukamen ten agapen hen echei ho theos en hemin).** Two perfect tenses -- egnokamen ("we have come to know and continue in that knowledge") and pepisteukamen ("we have believed and continue believing") -- describe the community's settled stance toward God's love. The order is notable: knowledge precedes belief, or more precisely, experiential acquaintance with God's love leads to and sustains trust in it. The community has known God's love -- encountered it, experienced it in the sending of the Son and in the mutual love of the fellowship -- and has put its trust in that love. The phrase "the love which God has for us" (ten agapen hen echei ho theos en hemin) or "in us" can be read as "the love God has among us" (i.e., the love God has displayed in our midst) or "the love God has toward us." Both readings converge on the same reality: God's love is known not in the abstract but in the community's concrete experience of it.

## Verses 16b-18: God Is Love, Mutual Indwelling, and Confidence in the Day of Judgment

"God is love, and the one who remains in love remains in God, and God remains in him. By this, love is perfected with us, so that we may have confidence in the day of judgment; because as He is, we also are in this world. There is no fear in love, but perfect love drives out fear, because fear involves punishment, and the one who fears is not perfected in love."

**"God is love" (ho theos agape estin).** The declaration is repeated from verse 8, now as the foundation for what follows. The repetition is not redundant; it anchors the subsequent argument. In verse 8, the declaration explained why the one who does not love does not know God (because God is love, and not loving means not knowing what God is). Here, the declaration grounds the mutual indwelling: remaining in love is remaining in God because God and love are identified.

**"And the one who remains in love remains in God, and God remains in him" (kai ho menon en te agape en to theo menei kai ho theos en auto menei).** The verb meno ("to remain, to abide") appears three times in this single clause, emphasizing the sustained, continuing nature of the relationship. The logic is: since God is love, the one who lives in love -- who makes love the sphere of their existence -- is thereby living in God, and God is living in them. Love is not a stepping-stone to relationship with God; it is the relationship itself. The one who abides in love does not need to seek some further spiritual experience to be in God; abiding in love is abiding in God, because God is love.

**"By this, love is perfected with us" (en touto teteleiotai he agape meth' hemon).** The perfect passive teteleiotai ("has been perfected, has been brought to its goal") echoes the same verb in verse 12 ("His love is perfected in us"). The phrase shifts slightly from "in us" (en hemin, v. 12) to "with us" (meth' hemon, v. 17). The preposition meta ("with") suggests accompaniment and partnership -- love has reached its completion alongside us, in our company. The "this" (en touto) points forward to the purpose clause that follows.

**"So that we may have confidence in the day of judgment" (hina parrhesian echomen en te hemera tes kriseos).** The purpose of love's perfection is stated: confidence (parrhesia) on the day of judgment. The word parrhesia ("confidence, boldness, freedom of speech") appeared in 2:28 ("abide in him, so that when he appears we may have confidence") and in 3:21 ("if our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God"). Here it is specifically connected to "the day of judgment" (te hemera tes kriseos). This phrase is not common in the Johannine literature but echoes the prophetic tradition of "the day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) -- the day when God acts decisively in judgment (Isaiah 2:12; Joel 2:1, 31; Amos 5:18-20; Zephaniah 1:14-18; Malachi 4:5). In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus speaks of judgment coming through himself: "The Father judges no one but has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22); "I have many things to speak and to judge concerning you" (John 8:26). The day of judgment is a future reckoning, and the perfection of love provides the community with confident expectation rather than dread.

**"Because as He is, we also are in this world" (hoti kathos ekeinos estin kai hemeis esmen en to kosmo touto).** The demonstrative pronoun ekeinos ("that one") refers to Jesus, as it does consistently throughout the letter (2:6; 3:3, 5, 7, 16). The clause states a present correspondence: "as He is, we also are." The verb estin ("is") is present tense -- it describes Jesus's current state, not his historical earthly life. The community's present existence in this world (en to kosmo touto) corresponds to Jesus's present existence. The nature of this correspondence is defined by the context: since the passage is about love, the correspondence is that the community embodies in this world the same love that characterizes Jesus. Just as Jesus is defined by love (he laid down his life, 3:16; he came in the flesh as the expression of God's love, 4:9-10), so the community is defined by love in this world. The confidence on the day of judgment derives from this correspondence: those who are like Jesus in love need not fear Jesus as judge.

**"There is no fear in love" (phobos ouk estin en te agape).** The statement is categorical. Fear (phobos) and love (agape) are incompatible occupants of the same space. The word phobos in the LXX can denote reverent awe toward God (Deuteronomy 6:13; Psalm 19:9 [18:10 LXX]: "The fear of the LORD is clean, enduring forever") or terrified dread before a threat (Genesis 3:10: "I heard the sound of You in the garden, and I was afraid"). In this context, the fear in view is specifically fear related to judgment (as the next clause makes explicit), not the reverential awe that characterizes the faithful worshiper. The author is not dismissing the "fear of the LORD" in its traditional Israelite sense but addressing the terror that arises from the prospect of condemnation.

**"But perfect love drives out fear" (all' he teleia agape exo ballei ton phobon).** The adjective teleia ("perfect, complete, having reached its goal") is the adjectival form of the noun telos ("end, goal, completion") that underlies the verb teleioo ("to perfect, to complete") used throughout this passage. Perfect love -- love that has been brought to completion -- actively expels fear. The verb ekballo ("to cast out, to drive out, to throw out") is forceful; it is the same verb used in the Gospels for the expulsion of demons (Matthew 8:16; Mark 1:34; Luke 11:20). Perfect love does not merely calm fear; it ejects fear. The two cannot coexist: where love reaches its completion, fear is driven out.

**"Because fear involves punishment" (hoti ho phobos kolasin echei).** The word kolasis ("punishment, torment, correction") appears in Matthew 25:46 ("these will go away into eternal punishment [kolasin aionion]"). In classical Greek, kolasis originally referred to corrective punishment (as distinct from timoria, retributive punishment), but by the first century the terms were often used interchangeably for any penalty. The author's point is that fear is oriented toward punishment -- it anticipates penalty, it envisions condemnation. The one who fears is living under the shadow of expected punishment, which means they have not yet experienced the love that removes that expectation.

**"And the one who fears is not perfected in love" (ho de phoboumenos ou teteleiotai en te agape).** The one who continues to fear (present participle phoboumenos) has not yet arrived at love's completion (ou teteleiotai, perfect passive, "has not been brought to perfection in love"). This is a diagnostic statement, not a condemnation. The author does not say the fearful person is beyond help but that their fear indicates that love's work in them is not yet complete. The remedy for fear is not the suppression of emotion but the perfection of love -- the deeper experience and practice of God's love that progressively displaces fear with confidence.

## Verses 19-21: The Source of Our Love and the Inseparability of Love for God and Love for the Brother

"We love, because He first loved us. If someone says, 'I love God,' and yet he hates his brother or sister, he is a liar; for the one who does not love his brother and sister whom he has seen, cannot love God, whom he has not seen. And this commandment we have from Him, that the one who loves God must also love his brother and sister."

**"We love, because He first loved us" (hemeis agapomen, hoti autos protos egapesen hemas).** Some manuscripts add "him" (auton) after "we love," yielding "we love him" (i.e., God); others add "God" (ton theon). The shorter reading -- simply "we love" (hemeis agapomen) without an object -- is probably original and is theologically richer: the community's love in general, in every direction, toward God and toward one another, is grounded in God's prior love. The verb agapomen may be indicative ("we love") or hortatory subjunctive ("let us love"); both readings fit the context, and the ambiguity may be intentional: the statement is simultaneously a description of what the community does and an exhortation to what it should do.

The word protos ("first") establishes temporal and causal priority: God loved before the community loved. God's love is the cause; the community's love is the effect. Human love is always responsive, never originary. This is the same principle stated in verse 10: "not that we loved God, but that He loved us." The entire movement of redemption begins in God's initiative and love. The community does not love God in order to earn his love; the community loves because God has already loved.

**"If someone says, 'I love God,' and yet he hates his brother or sister, he is a liar" (ean tis eipe hoti agapo ton theon kai ton adelphon autou mise, pseustes estin).** The "if someone says" (ean tis eipe) formula returns one final time, continuing the diagnostic pattern that has run throughout the letter (1:6, 8, 10; 2:4, 6, 9). Each instance presents a verbal claim and evaluates it against conduct. Here, the claim is the most pious one imaginable: "I love God." The behavior that contradicts it is hatred of one's brother or sister. The verdict is direct: "he is a liar" (pseustes estin). The word pseustes ("liar") has been used in 1:10 (making God a liar) and 2:4 (claiming to know God while disobeying) and 2:22 (the one who denies Jesus is the Christ). The liar is a recurring character in this letter -- the one whose words do not match reality.

The word mise ("hates," present subjunctive of miseo) indicates an ongoing disposition, not a momentary feeling. The "brother or sister" (ton adelphon autou) is a fellow member of the community, as in 2:9-11; 3:10, 14-17. The test is internal to the fellowship: do you love the people you live alongside in the community of faith?

**"For the one who does not love his brother and sister whom he has seen, cannot love God, whom he has not seen" (ho gar me agapon ton adelphon autou hon heoraken, ton theon hon ouch heoraken ou dynatai agapan).** The argument is from the lesser to the greater (qal wahomer in rabbinic terminology, a fortiori in logic). The brother is visible, tangible, present -- the one "whom he has seen" (hon heoraken, perfect tense of horao, "the one he has seen and continues to see"). God is invisible -- the one "whom he has not seen" (hon ouch heoraken). If a person cannot love the visible, present, accessible brother, how can that person possibly love the invisible, transcendent God? Love for the invisible God is demonstrated through love for the visible brother. The brother is not a substitute for God but the concrete, accessible locus where love for God takes actual form.

This verse connects directly to the declaration of verse 12: "No one has ever seen God." If God is invisible, then love for God cannot take the form of direct visual or physical interaction with God. Love for God must be expressed through some other channel. That channel, according to the author, is love for the brother. The invisible God is loved through the visible brother. This is not a reduction of love for God to mere humanitarianism; it is the insistence that love for God that does not produce love for the brother is a fraud.

The verb dynatai ("is able, can") indicates capability, not merely willingness. The author does not say the person "does not" love God but that the person "cannot" (ou dynatai) love God. The inability is logical and spiritual: the person who hates the visible brother is constitutionally incapable of loving the invisible God, because hatred of the brother reveals an absence of the love that comes from God (v. 7). If love is from God, and this person does not have love for the brother, then this person does not have God's love at all, and therefore cannot love God.

**"And this commandment we have from Him, that the one who loves God must also love his brother and sister" (kai tauten ten entolen echomen ap' autou, hina ho agapon ton theon agapa kai ton adelphon autou).** The chapter closes with a commandment that binds the two loves together as an inseparable unity. The phrase "from Him" (ap' autou) refers to God (or to Christ -- the ambiguity is characteristic of the author and may be deliberate). The commandment is that the person who loves God "must also" (kai) love the brother. The word kai ("also, and") makes the second love a necessary accompaniment of the first. The two loves are not options from which one may choose; they are a single commandment. Love for God that does not include love for the brother is not merely incomplete; it violates the commandment.

This commandment echoes Jesus's teaching in the Synoptic tradition, where he identified the two greatest commandments: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind" (Deuteronomy 6:5; Matthew 22:37) and "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18; Matthew 22:39). Jesus declared: "On these two commandments depend the whole Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:40). The author of 1 John has taken this same combination and expressed it in his own vocabulary: the one who loves God must also love the brother. The commandment in 3:23 already unified the two dimensions: "This is His commandment, that we believe in the name of His Son Jesus Christ, and love one another." Right belief about Jesus and love for the community are the two components of the single commandment.

The chapter ends without a formal closing -- the argument flows directly into 5:1, where the themes of belief, love, divine parentage, and commandment-keeping are drawn together: "Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the Father loves the child born of Him."

## Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. **The chapter bridges the Spirit's role as evidence of God's indwelling (3:24) with the need for practical discernment (4:1-6).** The mention of the Spirit at the end of chapter 3 immediately raises the question of how to distinguish the Spirit of God from false spirits. The author's answer is christological: the Spirit of God confesses Jesus Christ come in the flesh; the spirit of the antichrist does not. The test is not subjective experience but doctrinal content -- specifically, the reality of the incarnation.

2. **The false prophets are identified with the departed community members of 2:19.** They "have gone out into the world" (4:1), just as they "went out from us" (2:19). Their teaching denies that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh, which is the spirit of the antichrist that the community was warned about (2:18). Their departure from the community and their denial of the incarnation are related events: to deny the flesh-and-blood reality of Jesus is to sever oneself from the community that was founded on the eyewitness encounter with his physical presence (1:1-4).

3. **The community's victory over the false prophets is already accomplished (4:4) and is grounded in the superior power of God's Spirit within them.** The community need not fear the false teachers because "greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world." The victory is not achieved by superior argument or human strength but by the indwelling Spirit of God.

4. **Two diagnostic criteria operate in tandem: reception of the apostolic message (4:6) and the christological confession (4:2-3).** Those who know God listen to the apostolic community; those from the world listen to the false prophets. The spirit of truth and the spirit of error are distinguished by their relationship to the apostolic testimony and by their confession about Jesus. These are external, communal, verifiable tests, not private subjective experiences.

5. **"God is love" (4:8, 16) is the second great declaration about God's nature, parallel to "God is light" (1:5).** Light described God's moral character as it relates to holiness and truth. Love describes God's active character as it relates to redemption and relationship. Together, they present the full character of the God the community claims to know.

6. **The incarnation and the atonement are the supreme expression of God's love (4:9-10).** God demonstrated his love by sending his unique Son into the world so that the community might live through him, and by sending the Son as the hilasmos (atoning sacrifice) for sin. The initiative is entirely God's: "not that we loved God, but that He loved us." Atonement is the product of love, not the precondition for it.

7. **Love is perfected in the community through mutual love, not merely through reception of God's love (4:12).** God's love reaches its intended goal when the community loves one another. The circuit of love -- from God through the Son to the community and then among the community's members -- is complete only when the community practices mutual love. The invisible God is made present in the community through love.

8. **Perfected love produces confidence on the day of judgment (4:17) and drives out fear (4:18).** The community that loves as God loves -- that embodies in this world the love that characterizes Jesus -- need not dread the coming judgment. Fear of punishment is incompatible with perfected love. The one who still fears has not yet been brought to completion in love. This does not mean fear is punished but that it indicates love's work is not yet finished.

9. **Love for God and love for the brother are inseparable, and the claim to love God while hating the brother is a lie (4:20-21).** This is the chapter's final diagnostic test, continuing the "if someone says" pattern that runs through the entire letter. The argument proceeds from the lesser to the greater: if you cannot love the visible brother, you cannot love the invisible God. The commandment explicitly binds the two loves together as a single obligation. Love for God without love for the brother is a theological impossibility.

10. **The chapter weaves together the two themes that chapter 5 will unite: right confession about Jesus and love for the community.** Verses 1-6 address confession; verses 7-21 address love; but the two are not separate topics. The confession that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (vv. 2-3) is a confession about divine love (vv. 9-10), and the love that the community practices (vv. 7-12) is the lived expression of the confession. The one who truly confesses the incarnation -- that God loved the world enough to send his Son in real flesh -- will live out that love in real, tangible love for the brothers. The two themes are inseparable because they describe the same reality from two perspectives: what we believe about God's love and how we live in response to it.
