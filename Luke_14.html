Luke 14 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 13): Luke 13 opens by connecting to the urgency that closed chapter 12. Two recent catastrophes — the Galileans killed by Pilate during sacrifice and the eighteen crushed by the tower of Siloam — are not evidence that the victims were worse sinners, but occasions for a universal demand: "unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (13:3, 5). The parable of the barren fig tree adds a temporal boundary to this urgency — one more year of intensive cultivation, then the axe falls. The healing of the bent woman on the Sabbath (13:10-17) demonstrates what the kingdom's arrival looks like: a daughter of Abraham bound by Satan for eighteen years is released, while the synagogue official objects on procedural grounds. The mustard seed and leaven parables redefine expectations about the kingdom's nature — small, hidden, working from within. The narrow door teaching (13:22-30) shifts from the question of how many will be saved to the demand that the audience strive to enter. Those who assume their proximity to Jesus guarantees inclusion — "we ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets" — will be thrown out, while people from the four compass points take their places at the table with Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and the prophets. The chapter concludes with Jesus dismissing Herod's threat ("Go and tell that fox") and lamenting over Jerusalem: "How often I wanted to gather your children together, just as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you would not have it! Behold, your house is left to you desolate" (13:34-35). The entire chapter thus builds a sustained argument about the urgency of repentance, the surprising nature of the kingdom, and the reversal by which insiders are excluded and outsiders are welcomed.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 15): Luke 15 opens with a scene directly provoked by the kind of table fellowship Jesus has been practicing: "Now all the tax collectors and sinners were coming near Him to listen to Him. Both the Pharisees and the scribes began to grumble, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them'" (15:1-2). The grumbling triggers three parables of recovery: the shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to find the one that is lost, the woman who sweeps her house to find a lost coin, and the father who welcomes back the prodigal son with a feast while the older brother stands outside in resentment, refusing to enter. All three parables center on the joy in heaven over one sinner who repents (15:7, 10) and on the question of who will celebrate when the lost are found. The older brother's complaint — "I have been serving you for so many years and I have never neglected a command of yours; and yet you have never given me a young goat, so that I might celebrate with my friends; but when this son of yours came, who has devoured your wealth with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him" (15:29-30) — mirrors the resentment of the originally invited guests who refuse to come to the banquet in chapter 14. The father's response — "Son, you have always been with me, and all that is mine is yours. But we had to celebrate and rejoice, for this brother of yours was dead and has begun to live, and was lost and has been found" (15:31-32) — extends the logic of the great dinner parable: the invitation stands, but refusal to celebrate the inclusion of the lost is itself a form of self-exclusion.

How chapter 14 connects these: Luke 13 ended with the lament over Jerusalem and the declaration that "your house is left to you desolate," concluding a sustained argument about the reversal of insiders and outsiders, the narrow door, and the refusal of those who should have been first in line for the kingdom. Luke 15 will present the Pharisees and scribes grumbling about Jesus's table fellowship with sinners, triggering the parables of the lost. Chapter 14 is the bridge between these two movements. It translates the abstract reversal of chapter 13 — first become last, outsiders replace insiders — into a concrete social setting: a meal at the house of a leader of the Pharisees. Within this meal, Jesus observes the guests jockeying for positions of honor (the reversal of self-exaltation and humility), teaches the host to invite those who cannot repay (the poor, crippled, lame, and blind instead of friends and relatives), and responds to a guest's pious exclamation about eating bread in the kingdom with a parable in which those originally invited to a great dinner refuse to come and are replaced by exactly the marginalized people Jesus has just named. The chapter then shifts from the meal to the road, where large crowds following Jesus are confronted with the radical cost of discipleship: hating one's own family, carrying the cross, giving up all possessions. The salt saying at the chapter's end warns that those who begin but do not follow through are useless. Chapter 14 thus moves from the interior of the Pharisee's house (where the insiders' values are exposed and reversed) to the open road (where the outsiders who have been invited must reckon with what the invitation demands), creating the precise conditions under which chapter 15's conflict — "This man receives sinners and eats with them" — will erupt.

Verses 1-6: Healing a Man with Dropsy on the Sabbath

The chapter opens: "It happened that when He went into the house of one of the leaders of the Pharisees on the Sabbath to eat bread, they were watching Him closely. And there in front of Him was a certain man suffering from dropsy. And Jesus answered and spoke to the lawyers and Pharisees, saying, 'Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath, or not?' But they kept silent. And He took hold of him and healed him, and sent him away. And He said to them, 'Which one of you will have a son or an ox fall into a well, and will not immediately pull him out on a Sabbath day?' And they could make no reply to this."

The setting is deliberate and charged. Jesus enters "the house of one of the leaders of the Pharisees" (eis oikon tinos ton archonton ton Pharisaion) on the Sabbath "to eat bread" (phagein arton). The phrase "to eat bread" is a Hebraism for sharing a meal. The host is not merely a Pharisee but an archon, a ruler or leading member of the Pharisaic party. This is a meal among the religious elite. The notice that "they were watching Him closely" (kai autoi esan parateroumenoi auton) signals that the meal is also a test. The verb paratereo means "to watch carefully, to observe closely" — it is used in Luke 6:7 for the scribes and Pharisees watching Jesus in the synagogue to see whether he would heal on the Sabbath, and in 20:20 for the spies who watched Jesus to catch him in something he said. The watching is not neutral observation but scrutiny with intent.

"And there in front of Him was a certain man suffering from dropsy" (kai idou anthropos tis en hydrōpikos emprosthen autou). The phrase "there in front of him" (emprosthen autou) is pointed — the man is placed directly before Jesus, perhaps deliberately, perhaps providentially, but in either case the situation forces a response. The condition is "dropsy" (hydrōpikos), a medical term for the accumulation of fluid in the body's tissues, what modern medicine would call edema. The word appears only here in the New Testament. In first-century medical and popular understanding, dropsy was associated with excess and insatiability. Horace (Odes 2.2.13) uses the dropsical person as a metaphor for greed: just as the dropsical person drinks more water but only swells further, so the greedy person accumulates more wealth but is never satisfied. Rabbinic literature later associated dropsy with sexual immorality and excess. Whether Luke intends any symbolic resonance with the Pharisees' own "swelling" through religious accumulation cannot be demonstrated from the text, but the cultural associations would have been available to a Hellenistic audience.

Jesus takes the initiative: "And Jesus answered and spoke to the lawyers and Pharisees" (kai apokritheis ho Iesous eipen pros tous nomikous kai Pharisaious). The verb apokritheis ("answered") is notable because no one has spoken. Jesus is "answering" the unspoken question created by the situation — the question that everyone in the room is thinking but no one will voice. He makes it explicit: "Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath, or not?" (exestin to sabbato therapeusai e ou). The question is identical in form to the ones posed in 6:9 ("Is it lawful to do good or to do harm on the Sabbath, to save a life or to destroy it?") and answered implicitly in 13:14-16 through the qal wa-homer argument about untying animals. By now in Luke's narrative, this is the third Sabbath healing controversy (after the man with the withered hand in 6:6-11 and the bent woman in 13:10-17), and the pattern is established.

"But they kept silent" (hoi de hesychasan). The silence is telling. They cannot say "no" — the legal arguments Jesus has already made in chapter 13 have exposed the inconsistency. They cannot say "yes" — it would concede his authority and undermine the basis for their surveillance. So they say nothing. Their silence is not agreement but refusal to engage.

Jesus heals the man: "He took hold of him and healed him, and sent him away" (epilabomenos iasato auton kai apelysen). The sequence is compressed and decisive. The verb epilambanomai means "to take hold of, to grasp" — a physical action. He grasps the man, heals him (iaomai, the standard word for medical healing), and sends him away (apolyō, "to release, to send away" — the same verb used for the bent woman's release in 13:12). The brevity of the healing narrative contrasts with the length of the argument that follows. The act itself is simple and immediate; the controversy it provokes is what occupies the text.

The argument Jesus then offers is another qal wa-homer: "Which one of you will have a son or an ox fall into a well, and will not immediately pull him out on a Sabbath day?" (tinos hymōn huios e bous eis phrear peseitai, kai ouk eutheos anaspasei auton en hemera tou sabbatou). There is a textual variant here: some manuscripts read "son" (huios), others read "donkey" (onos). If "son" is the correct reading, the argument is even stronger: if you would rescue your own child from a well on the Sabbath, how much more should this man be healed. If "donkey" is correct, the argument parallels 13:15 exactly (untying an ox or donkey on the Sabbath). In either case, the logic is the same as in 13:15-16: if an animal (or child) in distress warrants Sabbath action, a human being certainly does.

"And they could make no reply to this" (kai ouk ischysan antapokrithēnai pros tauta). The verb antapokrinomai means "to reply in turn, to respond to an argument." They lack the capacity (ischyō, "to be strong enough, to be able" — the same word used in 13:24 for those who will "seek to enter and will not be able"). Their intellectual position has no answer to Jesus's argument, but their silence does not signal acceptance. It signals entrenched hostility that cannot be argued away.

This Sabbath healing, the third in Luke, follows the same pattern as the previous two but escalates the setting. The first (6:6-11) was in a synagogue among scribes and Pharisees. The second (13:10-17) was in a synagogue where a synagogue official objected. The third (14:1-6) is in the private home of a Pharisaic leader, at his own table, surrounded by lawyers and Pharisees. Jesus moves from public religious spaces into the inner sanctum of the religious establishment itself. Each time, the response is the same: silence or hostility in the face of an argument they cannot counter.

Verses 7-11: Teaching about Humility at the Table

"And He began speaking a parable to the invited guests when He noticed how they had been picking out the places of honor at the table, saying to them, 'When you are invited by someone to a wedding feast, do not take the place of honor, for someone more distinguished than you may have been invited by him, and he who invited you both will come and say to you, "Give your place to this man," and then in disgrace you proceed to occupy the last place. But when you are invited, go and recline at the last place, so that when the one who has invited you comes, he may say to you, "Friend, move up higher"; then you will have honor in the sight of all who are at the table with you. For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted.'"

Luke introduces this as "a parable" (parabolēn) spoken to "the invited guests" (tous keklemenous), prompted by Jesus's observation of their behavior: "He noticed how they had been picking out the places of honor" (epechōn pōs tas prōtoklisias exelegonto). The verb epechō means "to hold upon, to direct attention to, to observe carefully." Jesus is watching the watchers. They have been watching him closely (v. 1); now he watches them. And what he sees is a scramble for the prōtoklisia — the "first reclining place," the seat of highest honor at the table.

In Greco-Roman dining customs, the arrangement of guests at a meal was a display of social hierarchy. The triclinium — the three-sided dining arrangement — had specific positions of varying honor. The most honored guests reclined nearest the host. Plutarch (Quaestiones Convivales 1.2-3) discusses at length the proper assignment of places at dinner and the social complications that arise from getting it wrong. In Jewish practice, similar conventions held: the most important person reclined at the head, and disputes about seating could be acute. The Tosefta (Berakhot 5:5) records rules about reclining order at meals. The Pharisees' guests are doing what is socially expected — claiming the best positions for themselves.

Jesus's instruction appears, on the surface, to be prudential social advice: do not take the high seat because you might be demoted. Take the low seat so you might be promoted. Proverbs 25:6-7 offers virtually identical counsel: "Do not claim honor in the presence of the king, and do not stand in the place of great men; for it is better that it be said to you, 'Come up here,' than for you to be placed lower in the presence of the prince." But the concluding statement lifts the instruction beyond social strategy into a theological principle: "for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted" (hoti pas ho hypsōn heauton tapeinōthēsetai kai ho tapeinōn heauton hypsōthēsetai).

This exact formulation appeared in 13:30 as the reversal of first and last. It appears verbatim in 18:14 at the end of the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector. The passive voice — "will be humbled" (tapeinōthēsetai), "will be exalted" (hypsōthēsetai) — is the divine passive, a standard Jewish circumlocution for God's action: God will humble the self-exalting; God will exalt the self-humbling. The principle is not that one should strategically take the lower seat to achieve eventual promotion. The principle is that God reverses human hierarchies. Those who grasp at honor will lose it; those who accept lowliness will be raised.

This connects to the Magnificat in Luke 1:52: "He has brought down rulers from their thrones, and has exalted those who were humble." It connects to the beatitudes and woes in 6:20-26, where the poor, hungry, and weeping are blessed while the rich, full, and laughing receive woe. The pattern of reversal that has been building throughout Luke's Gospel is now performed in miniature at a dinner table. The Pharisees' guests scrambling for the best seats are enacting the self-exaltation that God will reverse. The teaching is addressed to them but carries implications far beyond table etiquette: the entire orientation of grasping versus receiving, self-promotion versus God-assigned honor, is at stake.

Verses 12-14: Teaching to the Host about Whom to Invite

"And He also went on to say to the one who had invited Him, 'When you give a luncheon or a dinner, do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, otherwise they may also invite you in return and that will be your repayment. But when you give a reception, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind, and you will be blessed, since they do not have the means to repay you; for you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.'"

Having addressed the guests, Jesus now turns to the host (to keklekoti auton, "the one who had invited him"). The shift is significant: the guests were told how to behave when invited; the host is told whom to invite. Jesus addresses both sides of the social transaction.

The instruction is stark. "Do not invite" (mē phonei) — the verb phoneō means "to call, to summon" — your friends (philous), brothers (adelphous), relatives (syngeneis), or rich neighbors (geitonas plousious). The reason given is economic: "otherwise they may also invite you in return and that will be your repayment" (mēpote kai autoi antikalesōsin se kai genētai antapodoma soi). The word antapodoma means "repayment, return, recompense." The point is not that having friends, brothers, or relatives at dinner is sinful. The point is that inviting those who can reciprocate turns hospitality into a transaction. The meal becomes an exchange of social capital rather than an act of generosity. When you invite those who can invite you back, you have already received your reward — the system is closed, self-referential, and ultimately self-serving.

Instead: "invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" (kalei ptōchous, anapeirous, chōlous, typhlous). This fourfold list will reappear in verse 21, when the master sends his slave to bring in exactly these categories of people from the streets and lanes. The list is not random. In Leviticus 21:17-23, the same categories of physical impairment disqualify a priest from approaching the altar: "No man of your offspring throughout their generations who has a defect shall approach to offer the food of his God. For no one who has a defect shall approach: a blind man, or a lame man, or he who has a disfigured face, or any deformed limb, or a man who has a broken foot or broken hand, or a hunchback or a dwarf, or one who has a defect in his eye or eczema or scabs or crushed testicles." The categories Jesus names are precisely those excluded from cultic participation in the Levitical system. At Qumran, the Community Rule (1QSa 2:5-7) similarly excluded the lame, blind, deaf, and those with physical blemishes from the messianic banquet. Jesus's instruction inverts both the Levitical exclusion and the Qumran exclusion: those barred from the priestly table and the sectarian feast are the ones who should be invited.

The promise: "you will be blessed" (makarios esē) — the same word that opens the beatitudes in 6:20. The host who invites the uninvitable receives a blessing. The reason: "they do not have the means to repay you" (ouk echousin antapodounai soi). The absence of reciprocity is the condition of blessing, not its obstacle. When there is no possibility of return, the gift is pure. And the recompense comes from God: "you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous" (antapodothēsetai gar soi en tē anastasei tōn dikaiōn). The divine passive again: God will repay you. The repayment is located at "the resurrection of the righteous" (tē anastasei tōn dikaiōn), a phrase that assumes the Pharisaic belief in bodily resurrection (which the Sadducees denied; cf. 20:27). Jesus is speaking in terms his Pharisaic host would affirm doctrinally — he believes in the resurrection of the righteous. The challenge is whether his practice matches his doctrine.

Verses 15-24: The Parable of the Great Dinner

"When one of those who were reclining at the table with Him heard this, he said to Him, 'Blessed is everyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!' And He said to him, 'A man was giving a big dinner, and he invited many; and at the hour of the dinner he sent his slave to say to those who had been invited, "Come; for everything is ready now." But they all alike began to make excuses. The first one said to him, "I have bought a piece of land and I need to go out and look at it; please consider me excused." Another one said, "I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I am going to try them out; please consider me excused." Another one said, "I have married a wife, and for that reason I cannot come." And the slave came back and reported this to his master. Then the head of the household became angry and said to his slave, "Go out at once into the streets and lanes of the city and bring in here the poor and crippled and blind and lame." And the slave said, "Master, what you commanded has been done, and still there is room." And the master said to the slave, "Go out into the highways and along the hedges, and compel them to come in, so that my house may be filled. For I tell you, none of those men who were invited shall taste of my dinner."'"

The parable is triggered by a guest's exclamation: "Blessed is everyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!" (makarios hostis phagetai arton en tē basileia tou theou). The language echoes Isaiah 25:6-8, where the LORD of hosts prepares a banquet for all peoples on "this mountain" — a lavish feast of aged wine and choice food, at which God will "swallow up death for all time" and "wipe tears away from all faces." The guest's statement is a pious commonplace: how wonderful it will be to feast in God's kingdom! The assumption is that those present at this Pharisee's table — the religiously observant, the socially prominent — will naturally be among those eating bread in the kingdom.

Jesus's parable challenges this assumption directly. The parable concerns "a man" (anthrōpos tis) — in the context of the discourse, this figure corresponds to God — who was "giving a big dinner" (epoiei deipnon mega) and "invited many" (ekalesen pollous). The double-invitation structure reflects actual first-century practice, well documented in both Jewish and Greco-Roman sources. A preliminary invitation was issued, and when the feast was prepared, a second summons went out: "Come; for everything is ready now" (erchesthe, hoti ēdē hetoima estin panta). The preliminary invitation had been accepted; these are people who said yes initially. The scandal is not that they were uninvited strangers but that they are reneging on a commitment already made.

The three excuses are revealing in their specificity.

The first: "I have bought a piece of land and I need to go out and look at it" (agron egorasa kai echō anankēn exelthōn idein auton). The man has already purchased the field — the transaction is complete. Going to "look at it" is not urgent. No one buys a field and then needs to inspect it before attending a dinner. The excuse is transparently inadequate; the land is more interesting than the dinner.

The second: "I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I am going to try them out" (zeugē boōn egorasa pente kai poreuomai dokimasai auta). Five yoke of oxen (ten animals) indicates a substantial landholder. Again, the purchase is already made. "Trying them out" (dokimasai, "to test, to examine") is not an emergency. The verb dokimazō implies careful evaluation — the man wants to assess his investment. His priorities are clear: the new acquisition takes precedence over the accepted invitation.

The third: "I have married a wife, and for that reason I cannot come" (gynaika egēma kai dia touto ou dynamai elthein). This excuse differs from the first two. The man does not ask to be excused; he declares flatly that he "cannot come." Deuteronomy 24:5 provides that "When a man takes a new wife, he shall not go out with the army, nor be charged with any duty; he shall be free at home one year and shall give happiness to his wife." This provision exempts the newly married man from military service and public obligation. But the guest applies a military exemption to a dinner invitation — a gross miscalibration. The Torah's provision for newlyweds was not meant to override every social and covenantal obligation. The excuse is technically grounded in scripture but misapplied.

All three excuses share a common structure: possessions and relationships have become more important than responding to the host's summons. Land, oxen, and marriage are not evil things — they are good things that have become ultimate things. They have displaced the invitation itself. The pattern recalls Jesus's teaching in 12:15-21 (the rich fool who accumulated possessions), 12:22-31 (anxiety about food and clothing), and 8:14 (the seed choked by "worries and riches and pleasures of this life"). The excuses represent the same orientation: life is organized around acquisitions rather than around response to God's call.

The master's reaction is anger: "the head of the household became angry" (orgistheis ho oikodespotēs). The word orgē in the prophetic tradition is consistently associated with God's response to covenant unfaithfulness (Isaiah 5:25; Jeremiah 4:8; Hosea 8:5). The anger is not petulant but just: these guests accepted an invitation and then, at the moment of fulfillment, preferred their own affairs to the host's generosity.

The replacement guests come in two waves. First, the slave is sent "into the streets and lanes of the city" (eis tas plateias kai rhymas tēs poleōs) to bring in "the poor and crippled and blind and lame" (tous ptōchous kai anapeirous kai typhlous kai chōlous). This is the exact list from verse 13, where Jesus told his host whom he should invite. The parable enacts what the teaching prescribed. The people whom the host's social peers would never invite — those who cannot reciprocate, those excluded by Levitical standards from cultic participation — are the first replacements.

But the slave reports: "what you commanded has been done, and still there is room" (ho epetaxas gegonen, kai eti topos estin). The feast is larger than the city's marginalized can fill. So the master sends the slave out again, this time beyond the city: "Go out into the highways and along the hedges" (exelthe eis tas hodous kai phragmous). The "highways" (hodous) are the main roads connecting cities; the "hedges" (phragmous) are the fence lines and boundary walls along rural properties. These are the places where travelers, vagrants, and those outside any settled community would be found. The command is to "compel them to come in" (anankason eiselthein) — the verb anankadzō means "to compel, to urge strongly." People on the highways and hedgerows would not assume they were welcome at a rich man's feast. They must be pressed to believe the invitation is genuine.

The purpose clause: "so that my house may be filled" (hina gemisthē mou ho oikos). The master's desire is a full house. The original guests' refusal does not cancel the feast; it opens it to those who would never have been included otherwise.

The parable's final statement is a verdict: "none of those men who were invited shall taste of my dinner" (oudeis tōn andrōn ekeinōn tōn keklēmenōn geusetai mou tou deipnou). The plural "you" (hymin) in "I tell you" (legō gar hymin) is ambiguous — it could be addressed to the characters within the parable or to the guests at the Pharisee's table. The effect is the same: those who were originally invited and refused are permanently excluded. Their refusal is treated as final.

The parable, read in its context, maps onto the argument that has been building since chapter 13. The originally invited guests are Israel's religious establishment — those who accepted the covenant, received the promise, attended the feasts, and kept the observances. The "big dinner" is the kingdom of God, the eschatological banquet that Isaiah prophesied. The second summons — "Come, for everything is ready now" — corresponds to the present moment of Jesus's ministry: the kingdom is at hand, the feast is prepared, the call to enter has been issued. The excuses represent the preoccupations that prevent response: property, possessions, personal arrangements. The replacement guests correspond to the reversal announced in 13:29-30: people from unexpected places taking their seats at the table while the original invitees are excluded.

The two waves of replacement also suggest a structure. The first wave — the poor, crippled, blind, and lame from the city's streets — may correspond to the marginalized within Israel: the tax collectors and sinners whom Jesus has been eating with and whom the Pharisees despise (5:30; 7:34; 15:1-2). The second wave — those from the highways and hedgerows outside the city — may correspond to the Gentiles, those outside the covenant community altogether, the people from "east and west and from north and south" who will recline at the table (13:29). The two waves thus mirror the progressive expansion of the invitation: first to the outcasts within Israel, then to the nations beyond.

Verses 25-27: The Cost of Following — Hating One's Own

"Now large crowds were going along with Him; and He turned and said to them, 'If anyone comes to Me, and does not hate his own father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, yes, and even his own life, he cannot be My disciple. Whoever does not carry his own cross and come after Me cannot be My disciple.'"

The scene shifts abruptly from the intimate dinner at the Pharisee's house to the open road. "Large crowds" (ochloi polloi) are "going along with" (syneporeuonto) Jesus. The verb synporeuomai means "to journey together with, to travel alongside." The crowds are following him on his way to Jerusalem. Jesus "turned" (strapheis) to address them — a deliberate pivot from forward movement to face those following. The turning suggests a challenge: you are walking with me, but do you know what that entails?

The conditions for discipleship are stated in three successive declarations, each ending with the same verdict: "cannot be My disciple" (ou dynatai einai mou mathētēs). This phrase appears three times in this passage (vv. 26, 27, 33), creating a threefold definition by exclusion: these are the things without which discipleship is impossible.

The first condition: "If anyone comes to Me, and does not hate his own father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, yes, and even his own life, he cannot be My disciple." The verb miseō ("to hate") is stark and has generated extensive discussion. In Semitic usage, "hate" in the context of preference does not necessarily mean emotional hostility but rather "to love less, to set aside in favor of." Genesis 29:31 says that Leah was "hated" (LXX: misouménē), but the context makes clear that the meaning is that she was less loved than Rachel (cf. Genesis 29:30: "he loved Rachel more than Leah"). Deuteronomy 21:15-17 uses the same language for a man with two wives, one "loved" and one "hated" — the law protects the firstborn rights of the "hated" wife's son. Malachi 1:2-3 says "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" — a statement about God's election of Israel, not about emotional hostility toward Esau as a person.

In this context, "hate" means to give absolute priority to following Jesus over every other loyalty, including the most fundamental human bonds: father, mother, wife, children, brothers, sisters. The list is comprehensive — it covers every kinship tie. The addition of "yes, and even his own life" (eti te kai tēn psuchēn heautou) extends the demand beyond social relationships to one's own existence. The word psuchē means "life, soul, self." Discipleship requires that even the preservation of one's own life must not take priority over following Jesus. This connects to 9:24: "For whoever wishes to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake, he is the one who will save it."

The second condition: "Whoever does not carry his own cross and come after Me cannot be My disciple." The image of carrying one's cross (bastazei ton stauron heautou) was not a metaphor for general difficulty. In the first-century Roman world, the cross was an instrument of execution reserved for slaves, rebels, and the worst criminals. To "carry one's cross" meant to walk toward one's own execution, as condemned prisoners carried the horizontal beam (patibulum) to the site of crucifixion. The crowds following Jesus toward Jerusalem would have seen crucifixions — they were public, common, and designed to humiliate. Jesus has already used this image in 9:23: "If anyone wishes to come after Me, he must deny himself, and take up his cross daily and follow Me." The addition of "daily" in 9:23 is absent here, but the meaning is the same: following Jesus means accepting the possibility of the same fate he is walking toward.

The parable of the great dinner has just established that those originally invited refused because of competing priorities — land, oxen, marriage. Now Jesus names those same categories as things that must be subordinated to discipleship: family (the third excuse involved a wife), possessions (the first two excuses involved property and livestock), and one's very life. The two passages are mirrors of each other: the dinner guests refused the invitation because these things were more important; the would-be disciples are told that these things must be less important.

Verses 28-33: Two Parables about Counting the Cost

"For which one of you, when he wants to build a tower, does not first sit down and calculate the cost to see if he has enough to complete it? Otherwise, when he has laid a foundation and is not able to finish, all who observe it begin to ridicule him, saying, "This man began to build and was not able to finish." Or what king, when he sets out to meet another king in battle, will not first sit down and consider whether he is strong enough with ten thousand men to encounter the one coming against him with twenty thousand? Or else, while the other is still far away, he sends a delegation and asks for terms of peace. So then, none of you can be My disciple who does not give up all his own possessions.'"

The two parables function as a pair, each illustrating the same point from a different angle: the necessity of reckoning fully with what discipleship demands before committing to it.

The first parable concerns a builder. The tower (pyrgos) could be a defensive tower, a watchtower for a vineyard (cf. Isaiah 5:2; Mark 12:1), or a multi-story structure. The point is not the type of building but the calculation required. The builder must "sit down and calculate the cost" (kathisas psēphizei tēn dapanēn) — the verb psēphizō means "to count with pebbles, to reckon, to calculate." The word dapanē means "expense, cost." Before breaking ground, the builder must determine whether he has "enough to complete it" (ei echei eis apartismon). The noun apartismos means "completion, finishing" — it appears only here in the New Testament. The builder who lays a foundation but cannot finish becomes an object of public ridicule: "This man began to build and was not able to finish" (houtos ho anthrōpos ērxato oikodomein kai ouk ischysen ektelesai). The verb ischyō appears again — the same word used for those who "will not be able" to enter the narrow door (13:24) and for the Pharisees who "could not" reply to Jesus's argument (14:6). The inability to finish what one started is a public humiliation.

The second parable concerns a king going to war. The numbers are starkly disproportionate: ten thousand against twenty thousand. The king with the smaller army must "sit down and consider" (kathisas bouleusetai) — the verb bouleuō means "to deliberate, to take counsel" — whether he is "strong enough" (dynatos estin) to engage the larger force. If not, the prudent course is to send a delegation (presbeian) and "ask for terms of peace" (erōta ta pros eirēnēn). The verb erōtaō means "to ask, to request" — the weaker king must negotiate, not fight a battle he cannot win.

The two parables are complementary but not identical. The builder must calculate whether he has sufficient resources to finish what he starts. The king must assess whether he has sufficient strength to prevail in a conflict he will face. Both involve sitting down and deliberating before acting. Both warn against beginning what one cannot complete. But the application to discipleship works differently than expected.

In the builder parable, the point seems to be: do not begin following Jesus unless you are willing to pay the full cost, lest you become a half-finished project, publicly shamed. In the king parable, the point seems similar but the dynamics are different: the king who cannot win must sue for peace — he must surrender. Applied to discipleship, the "king" may be the would-be disciple who must reckon with the overwhelming demands of following Jesus and, recognizing that he cannot prevail on his own terms, must surrender completely rather than fight a losing battle against the demands of the kingdom. The "terms of peace" are unconditional surrender — which is precisely what verse 33 demands.

The conclusion draws the application explicit: "So then, none of you can be My disciple who does not give up all his own possessions" (houtōs oun pas ex hymōn hos ouk apotassetai pasin tois heautou hyparchousin ou dynatai einai mou mathētēs). The verb apotassomai means "to take leave of, to renounce, to say farewell to." It is used in Acts 18:18 and 2 Corinthians 2:13 for taking leave of people, and in Mark 6:46 for dismissing a crowd. Applied to possessions (hyparchousin, literally "existing things, belongings"), it means a complete renunciation. This is the third and final "cannot be My disciple" statement: without hating family and life (v. 26), without carrying the cross (v. 27), and without renouncing all possessions (v. 33), discipleship is impossible.

The demand is total. It is also consistent with what has come before. In the parable of the great dinner, the originally invited guests refused because of land, oxen, and a wife. In the cost-of-discipleship teaching, the would-be follower must renounce precisely these categories: possessions (land and oxen) and family loyalty (wife). The parable showed the consequences of refusing the invitation because of these attachments; now Jesus states that accepting the invitation requires releasing them entirely.

Verses 34-35: Salt That Has Lost Its Taste

"Therefore, salt is good; but if even salt has become tasteless, with what will it be seasoned? It is useless either for the soil or for the manure pile; it is thrown out. He who has ears to hear, let him hear."

The salt saying concludes the chapter with a warning. The connective "therefore" (oun) links this saying to the preceding cost-of-discipleship teaching. Salt that has lost its taste (mōranthē — the verb mōrainō means "to become foolish, to become insipid" — the same root as mōros, "fool") is a disciple who has begun but not followed through, who has accepted the invitation but not reckoned with the cost.

In the ancient world, salt served multiple purposes: it preserved food, flavored food, and was used as a fertilizer and catalyst in composting (applied to manure piles to accelerate decomposition). Pure sodium chloride does not actually lose its saltiness, but the salt commonly available in Palestine — mined from deposits around the Dead Sea — was mixed with gypsum and other impurities. When the actual sodium chloride leached out through exposure to moisture, what remained looked like salt but had no salt properties. It was, as Jesus says, "useless either for the soil or for the manure pile" (oute eis gēn oute eis koprian eutheton estin). The adjective euthetos means "fit, suitable, useful." Tasteless salt is unfit for every purpose salt serves. It is not merely diminished; it is completely without function. The only option is to throw it out (exō ballousin auto — the verb ekballō, "to cast out," is the same word used in 13:28 for the insiders being "thrown out" of the kingdom).

The closing formula — "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (ho echōn ōta akouein akouetō) — is a standard marker in Jesus's teaching for sayings that demand more than surface comprehension (cf. 8:8, at the end of the parable of the sower). The saying is not self-explanatory; it requires the hearer to apply it personally. The question implied is: Are you salt or tasteless residue? Have you counted the cost and committed fully, or have you begun something you will not finish?

The salt saying gathers the chapter's argument into a final image. The originally invited dinner guests became useless when they refused the summons. The guests at the Pharisee's table, scrambling for honor, are operating on principles that God will reverse. The host who invites only those who can repay him has closed the circuit of generosity into a mere transaction. And now the large crowds following Jesus are warned that following without full commitment — without hating family and life, carrying the cross, and renouncing possessions — will leave them like tasteless salt: thrown out, useless, fit for nothing.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 14 is a unified argument that moves from the interior of a Pharisee's house to the open road, from the behavior of the religiously secure to the demands placed on would-be followers, from the question of who is honored at the table to who is fit for the kingdom.

The Sabbath healing (vv. 1-6) establishes the setting and the tension. In the home of a Pharisaic leader, under close surveillance, Jesus heals a man with dropsy and silences the legal objections of the lawyers and Pharisees with an argument they cannot answer. The healing, like the two Sabbath healings before it, demonstrates that the kingdom's arrival overrides the procedural interpretations that the religious establishment enforces.

The teaching on table places (vv. 7-11) observes the guests' scramble for honor and announces the divine reversal: every self-exalting person will be humbled, every self-humbling person will be exalted. The Pharisees' dinner becomes a parable of the kingdom's inverted values.

The teaching to the host (vv. 12-14) challenges the transactional logic of hospitality: stop inviting those who can repay and start inviting those who cannot. The fourfold list — poor, crippled, lame, blind — names exactly those excluded from cultic participation and Qumran's messianic banquet, placing them at the center of true generosity.

The parable of the great dinner (vv. 15-24) translates the pious assumption that "everyone at this table will feast in the kingdom" into a narrative that overturns it. Those originally invited refuse because of possessions and family obligations. Their places are filled, first by the marginalized within the city, then by those found on the highways and hedgerows beyond it. The master's verdict is final: none of the original invitees will taste the dinner. The reversal of insiders and outsiders that was announced in 13:28-30 is here dramatized in full.

The cost of discipleship (vv. 25-33) turns from the refused invitation to the accepted one and asks what acceptance actually requires. The three "cannot be My disciple" statements demand the subordination of family (the same attachment that kept the third dinner guest from attending), the acceptance of the cross (the path Jesus himself is walking toward Jerusalem), and the renunciation of possessions (the same acquisitions that occupied the first two dinner guests). The builder and king parables warn against beginning without counting the full cost.

The salt saying (vv. 34-35) closes the chapter with a warning about partial commitment. Salt that has lost its nature is thrown out. Disciples who have lost their distinctive commitment are similarly useless. The call to hear is a call to apply the entire chapter personally.

The chapter's movement is thus from exposure (the Pharisees' values are revealed at their own table) to invitation (the kingdom feast is offered and refused by the expected guests, then filled by the unexpected) to demand (those who accept must pay the total cost). The dinner parable is the hinge: it explains why the insiders are out and the outsiders are in, and it frames the cost-of-discipleship teaching as the necessary reckoning for those who accept the invitation that the insiders refused.
