1 John 2 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 John 1): The document opens without any of the standard features of a first-century letter — no sender's name, no recipients, no greeting. Instead, it begins with a declaration about what was "from the beginning" (ap' arches) — what was heard, seen, looked upon, and touched with hands concerning "the word of life" (tou logou tes zoes). The vocabulary and structure deliberately echo the opening of the Fourth Gospel: "In the beginning was the Word... in him was life" (John 1:1, 4). But the emphasis is different. The Gospel's prologue established the Word's pre-existence and incarnation; this document's opening establishes the author's firsthand, physical encounter with the incarnate Word: hearing, seeing, touching. The author writes as an eyewitness (or a representative of the eyewitness community), and his purpose is stated explicitly: "so that you also may have fellowship (koinonian) with us; and indeed our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ" (1:3). The word koinonia — shared participation, communal bond — defines the goal of the entire document. Everything the author writes aims to bring the readers into the same fellowship the author's community has with God.

The author then introduced the core declaration: "God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all" (1:5). This is presented as "the message we have heard from him and announce to you" — a summary of what the apostolic community received from Jesus himself. The metaphor of light and darkness, drawn from the same vocabulary as the Fourth Gospel (John 1:5, 3:19-21, 8:12, 12:35-36), becomes the framework for the ethical tests that follow. The author then presented three conditional claims, each introduced by "if we say" (ean eipomen), exposing the gap between verbal profession and actual conduct:

First: "If we say we have fellowship with him and walk in the darkness, we lie and do not practice the truth" (1:6). Walking in darkness while claiming fellowship is a contradiction the author calls a lie.

Second: "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us" (1:8). Claiming sinlessness is self-deception.

Third: "If we say we have not sinned, we make him a liar, and his word is not in us" (1:10). Denying that one has sinned accuses God of lying.

Between the second and third of these, the author offered the remedy: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1:9). And before the second, he established that "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1:7), but only for those who "walk in the light as he himself is in the light." The chapter thus established a framework in which genuine fellowship with God requires honesty about sin, walking in the light, and dependence on the cleansing provided by Jesus's blood. The chapter ended on a note of tension: sin is real, confession is necessary, cleansing is available — but what about ongoing sin in the life of the believer? That tension is precisely what chapter 2 opens by addressing.

What the author argues immediately after (1 John 3): Chapter 3 opens with an exclamation about identity: "See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God — and so we are!" (3:1). The language of divine parentage ("born of him") that appeared at the end of chapter 2 (2:29) is now developed into a full exploration of what it means to be "children of God" (tekna theou). The author contrasts the children of God with the children of the devil (3:10), using the same criteria he has been developing: obedience, love, and right conduct. "Everyone who practices sin also practices lawlessness, and sin is lawlessness" (3:4). "No one who abides in him keeps on sinning; no one who keeps on sinning has seen him or known him" (3:6). The tests of genuineness become sharper: "By this the children of God and the children of the devil are evident: everyone who does not practice righteousness is not of God, nor the one who does not love his brother" (3:10). The commandment from chapter 2 — "love one another" — is restated as "the message which you have heard from the beginning" (3:11). The example of Cain, who "was of the evil one and murdered his brother" (3:12), provides the negative illustration. The passage culminates in the declaration: "We know that we have passed from death to life because we love the brothers. Whoever does not love remains in death" (3:14).

How chapter 2 connects these: Chapter 1 established the reality of sin and the provision for its cleansing. Chapter 3 will develop the criteria that distinguish God's children from the devil's children. Chapter 2 is the bridge: it begins by addressing the tension left by chapter 1 (sin is real, but sin is not to be taken lightly — 2:1-2), then develops three tests by which genuine fellowship with God can be recognized (obedience in 2:3-6, love in 2:7-11, and right confession about Jesus in 2:18-27), pauses to reassure the community of their standing (2:12-14), warns against the world's competing claim on their allegiance (2:15-17), and addresses the crisis of community members who have departed (2:18-27). The chapter's final statement — "if you know that he is righteous, you know that everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him" (2:29) — hands the argument directly to chapter 3's exploration of what it means to be born of God. The entire chapter functions as the author's development of how the fellowship described in 1:3 is maintained, tested, and threatened.

Verses 1-2: The Remedy for Sin and the Scope of Atonement

"My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. And he himself is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the whole world."

"My little children" (teknia mou). The diminutive teknia ("dear children, little children") establishes the author's pastoral relationship to the recipients. The term appears seven times in this document (2:1, 2:12, 2:28, 3:7, 3:18, 4:4, 5:21) and is characteristic of the author's voice. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus uses the term once, in the farewell discourse: "Little children (teknia), I am with you a little while longer" (John 13:33). The author of this document adopts the posture of one who stands in continuity with Jesus's relationship to his disciples — an elder addressing a community he regards as his spiritual children.

"I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin" (hina me hamartete). The aorist subjunctive hamartete suggests the author has in view specific acts of sin, not a general condition of sinfulness. The purpose of everything the author writes — the entire document — is oriented toward the community's not sinning. This connects directly to chapter 1's framework: the author is not promoting a casual attitude toward sin. The confession of sin (1:9) and the cleansing by Jesus's blood (1:7) are not invitations to continue sinning but provisions that exist precisely because the goal is holiness.

"But if anyone does sin" (ean tis hamarte). The conditional acknowledges reality without endorsing it. The author has just stated that the goal is not sinning; he now addresses what happens when someone does sin. The "if" does not express uncertainty about whether sin will occur (chapter 1 has already established that claiming sinlessness is self-deception) but provides the remedy without making the remedy an excuse. The balance is deliberate: aim for sinlessness, but when sin occurs, there is provision.

"We have an advocate with the Father" (parakleton echomen pros ton patera). The word parakletos carries legal force. In Greek usage, a parakletos was one called alongside to help in a judicial proceeding — an advocate, a defender, someone who speaks on another's behalf before a judge or authority. The term appears in the Fourth Gospel four times (John 14:16, 14:26, 15:26, 16:7), where Jesus uses it for the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send after Jesus's departure. In John 14:16, Jesus says, "I will ask the Father, and he will give you another advocate (allon parakleton)" — the word "another" (allon, "another of the same kind") implies that Jesus himself was the first parakletos. This document makes that implicit identification explicit: Jesus Christ is the parakletos who advocates for believers before the Father. The spatial language — pros ton patera ("with the Father," or "toward the Father," using the same preposition that described the Word's relationship to God in John 1:1) — places Jesus in the Father's presence, oriented toward the Father, interceding.

The image is drawn from Israel's legal and cultic traditions. In Zechariah 3:1-5, the high priest Joshua stands before the angel of the LORD while the accuser (ho satanas in the LXX, "the adversary") stands at his right hand to accuse him. The LORD rebukes the accuser, removes Joshua's filthy garments, and clothes him in clean garments. The scene depicts an advocate-figure (the angel of the LORD) defending the accused against the accuser in the heavenly court. Job 16:19-21 expresses the longing for such a figure: "Even now, behold, my witness is in heaven, and my advocate (martys) is on high... that he would argue the case of a man with God." The author of 1 John identifies Jesus as this heavenly advocate, the one who stands before the Father and speaks on behalf of the one who has sinned.

"Jesus Christ the righteous" (Iesoun Christon dikaion). The adjective dikaion ("righteous") is not ornamental. The advocate's standing before the Father depends on his own character. A parakletos who was himself guilty could not effectively advocate for others. Jesus's righteousness — his conformity to the Father's character and will — is the basis of his advocacy. The term will recur at the chapter's end: "if you know that he is righteous" (2:29). The author uses "righteous" (dikaios) for both the Father (1:9: "he is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins") and the Son (2:1). The advocate shares the character of the judge.

"And he himself is the propitiation for our sins" (autos hilasmos estin peri ton hamartion hemon). The word hilasmos requires careful attention to its LXX background. The hilask- word group in the LXX is the primary vocabulary for atonement. The cognate noun hilasterion appears in Exodus 25:17-22 for the kapporet, the "mercy seat" — the gold cover of the ark of the covenant where the high priest sprinkled blood on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:14-15). The verb hilaskomai appears throughout the Pentateuch for the act of making atonement (Leviticus 4:20, 26, 31, 35; 5:6, 10, 13, 16, 18; 16:6, 11, 16, 17, 24, 30, 33, 34). Paul used hilasterion in Romans 3:25, identifying Christ as the mercy seat — the place where atonement occurs.

The noun hilasmos itself appears in the LXX in several significant places. Psalm 130:4 (129:4 LXX): "For with you there is forgiveness (hilasmos), that you may be feared." Leviticus 25:9: the day of atonement is called hemera tou hilasmou ("the day of propitiation/atonement"). Numbers 5:8 uses hilasmos for the ram of atonement. Ezekiel 44:27 refers to the sin offering in atonement terms. Daniel 9:9 (Theodotion): "To the Lord our God belong mercy (oiktirmoi) and forgivenesses (hilasmoi)."

The concept is rooted in the Day of Atonement ritual of Leviticus 16. On that day, the high priest entered the Holy of Holies with the blood of the sin offering and sprinkled it on and before the mercy seat (hilasterion). This act addressed the contamination of sin, restored the holiness of God's dwelling place, and renewed the covenant relationship between God and Israel. The blood was the means by which the barrier created by sin was removed, so that God could continue to dwell among his people without his holiness consuming them.

When the author says Jesus himself "is the hilasmos for our sins," he identifies Jesus not merely as one who provides atonement but as the atonement itself. Jesus does not simply offer a sacrifice; he is the sacrifice. The high priest on the Day of Atonement entered the holy place with blood that was not his own; Jesus functions as both the priest (the advocate before the Father) and the offering (the hilasmos). The author's statement collapses the roles of priest, sacrifice, and mercy seat into a single person.

"And not for ours only but also for the whole world" (kai ou peri ton hemeteron monon alla kai peri holou tou kosmou). The scope is universal. The pronoun "ours" (hemeteron) refers to the believing community — the "we" who have fellowship with the Father and the Son (1:3). The hilasmos provided by Jesus is not restricted to those who are already in the community but extends to "the whole world" (holou tou kosmou). The phrase echoes the Fourth Gospel's declaration: "God so loved the world" (John 3:16) and "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). The author uses "world" (kosmos) in this document in two senses, much as the Fourth Gospel does: the human world for which God provides (here and in 4:14, where the Father "sent the Son as Savior of the world"), and the system of values and desires that opposes God (2:15-17). Here the sense is the former: the atonement is sufficient for the entire human order, not limited to the believing community.

The theological implication is significant. If the hilasmos covers "the whole world," then the provision for sin is not limited by the number who avail themselves of it. The sufficiency of the atonement and the application of the atonement are distinguishable: the author says the provision extends to all, while the rest of the document will make clear that the benefits are experienced by those who walk in the light, confess their sins, keep the commandments, love the brothers, and confess that Jesus is the Christ.

Verses 3-6: The Test of Genuine Knowledge — Obedience

"And by this we know that we have come to know him, if we keep his commandments. The one who says 'I have come to know him' and does not keep his commandments is a liar, and the truth is not in this person. But whoever keeps his word, in this person the love of God has truly been perfected. By this we know that we are in him. The one who says he abides in him ought himself to walk in the same way that one walked."

"By this we know that we have come to know him" (en touto ginoskomen hoti egnokamen auton). The author introduces a formula — "by this we know" (en touto ginoskomen) — that will recur throughout the document as the marker of a test or criterion (2:5, 3:16, 3:19, 3:24, 4:2, 4:13, 5:2). The verb ginosko ("to know") appears in two tenses: the present ginoskomen ("we know," ongoing recognition) and the perfect egnokamen ("we have come to know him," a past action with continuing results). The question being addressed is how a person can have confidence that their claimed knowledge of God is genuine. The answer is not subjective feeling or mystical experience but observable conduct.

The pronoun "him" (auton). The immediate antecedent is "Jesus Christ the righteous" from verse 1. However, the author's usage throughout this document frequently moves between the Father and the Son without clear transition, reflecting the theological conviction that knowledge of the Son is knowledge of the Father (cf. John 14:7: "If you had known me, you would have known my Father also"). In verse 5, "the love of God" (he agape tou theou) shifts the referent toward the Father. The ambiguity may be deliberate: knowing God and knowing Jesus are not separable realities for this author.

"If we keep his commandments" (ean tas entolas autou teromen). The verb tereo ("to keep, to guard, to observe") is the same verb used extensively in the Fourth Gospel's farewell discourse: "If you love me, you will keep (teresete) my commandments" (John 14:15). "Whoever has my commandments and keeps (teron) them, he it is who loves me" (John 14:21). "If anyone loves me, he will keep (teresei) my word" (John 14:23). The "commandments" (entolai) here are not identified with the Mosaic Torah but with the teaching of Jesus — the word and instruction that the community received "from the beginning" (2:7). The test of genuine knowledge of God is not mystical illumination, emotional experience, or correct doctrinal formulation in the abstract but the observable practice of obedience to what Jesus commanded.

"The one who says 'I have come to know him' and does not keep his commandments is a liar" (ho legon hoti egnoka auton kai tas entolas autou me teron pseustes estin). This follows the pattern established in 1:6, 8, 10 — the "if we say" / "the one who says" formula that exposes the contradiction between profession and practice. The word pseustes ("liar") is strong. In the LXX, lying is associated with those who are estranged from God: Psalm 63:11 (62:12 LXX), "The mouths of liars will be stopped." Proverbs 19:22: "A poor man is better than a liar." The author is not describing a person who occasionally fails to keep the commandments (he has already provided for that in vv. 1-2) but a person who claims to know God while habitually disregarding God's instructions. The claim to knowledge is falsified by the pattern of disobedience.

"And the truth is not in this person" (kai en touto he aletheia ouk estin). This echoes 1:8: "the truth is not in us" (said of those who deny having sin). "Truth" (aletheia) in this document is not abstract correctness but the lived reality of God's character expressed in human conduct. The Fourth Gospel uses truth in the same way: "doing the truth" (John 3:21), "the truth will set you free" (John 8:32), "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Truth is something that dwells in a person and manifests in their actions.

"But whoever keeps his word, in this person the love of God has truly been perfected" (hos d' an tere autou ton logon, alethos en touto he agape tou theou teteleiostai). The shift from "commandments" (entolai) to "word" (logos) is not a change of subject but a synonymous variation. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus uses "commandments," "word," and "words" interchangeably for his teaching (John 14:15, 23, 24). "Keeping his word" and "keeping his commandments" describe the same reality.

The phrase "the love of God" (he agape tou theou) is ambiguous in Greek: it could mean the love that God has (subjective genitive), the love directed toward God (objective genitive), or the love that comes from God and characterizes God (genitive of origin/quality). Given the author's usage elsewhere — 4:9: "In this the love of God was made manifest among us, that God sent his only Son into the world"; 4:12: "if we love one another, God abides in us and his love is perfected in us" — the phrase likely encompasses both the love that originates from God and the love that is directed back toward God and toward others. The love of God is a reality that begins in God, enters the believer, and reaches its intended fullness (is "perfected") when the believer lives in obedience.

The verb teteleiostai ("has been perfected," perfect passive of teleioo) means "has been brought to completion, has reached its intended goal." The root telos ("end, goal, completion") indicates that the love of God has a trajectory: it aims at something, and that something is fully expressed in the person who keeps God's word. Love is not perfected in the person who merely feels love or who knows about God's love but in the person whose conduct is shaped by obedience to what God has said.

"By this we know that we are in him" (en touto ginoskomen hoti en auto esmen). The phrase "in him" (en auto) introduces the language of mutual indwelling that pervades this document and the Fourth Gospel. Being "in" God or Christ is the Johannine equivalent of Paul's "in Christ" (en Christo) — the sphere of relationship and participation that defines the believer's existence. The test for whether one is actually "in him" is the keeping of his word. Position is verified by practice.

"The one who says he abides in him ought himself to walk in the same way that one walked" (ho legon en auto menein opheilei, kathos ekeinos periepatesen, kai autos houtos peripatein). The verb menein ("to abide, to remain, to dwell") is one of the most characteristic words in the Johannine literature. It appears approximately twenty-four times in this letter and approximately forty times in the Fourth Gospel. In John 15:4-10, Jesus used it to describe the relationship between himself and his disciples: "Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it abides in the vine, neither can you unless you abide in me." The concept is not static residence but active, sustained, living connection.

The demonstrative pronoun ekeinos ("that one") refers to Jesus. The author uses ekeinos elsewhere to refer to Jesus at a distance of respect or emphasis (3:3, 5, 7, 16; 4:17). The claim to abide in Jesus creates an obligation (opheilei, "ought, is obligated") to walk (peripatein, "to conduct one's life") as Jesus walked. The standard for the believer's life is not an abstract ethical code but the actual pattern of Jesus's own life. The word "walk" (peripateo) is used throughout the LXX for the conduct of life (the Hebrew halak carries the same metaphorical sense) and has already appeared in 1:6-7 for walking in darkness or in the light. The author's point is that the one who claims to be connected to Jesus must live as Jesus lived — not in the sense of performing miracles or teaching in synagogues, but in the orientation of life toward the Father's will that characterized Jesus's entire ministry.

Verses 7-11: The Commandment — Old and New — and the Love Test

"Beloved, I am not writing a new commandment to you, but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning. The old commandment is the word which you have heard. Again, I am writing a new commandment to you, which is true in him and in you, because the darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining. The one who says he is in the light and hates his brother is in the darkness until now. The one who loves his brother abides in the light, and there is no cause for stumbling in him. But the one who hates his brother is in the darkness and walks in the darkness, and does not know where he is going, because the darkness has blinded his eyes."

"Beloved" (agapetoi). The author shifts from "my little children" (teknia mou) to "beloved" (agapetoi), a term of affection and solidarity. The word agapetos is from the same root as agape ("love") and agapao ("to love"), and its appearance here, as the author transitions to the love commandment, is fitting. The author addresses the community as those who are loved — by God and by the author.

"Not a new commandment... but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning" (ouk entolen kainen... all' entolen palaian hen eichete ap' arches). The phrase "from the beginning" (ap' arches) is used by this author in several distinct ways throughout the document: for what existed before creation (1:1, echoing John 1:1), for what was true from the start of the Christian community's life (2:7, 24; 3:11), and for what has been true from the beginning of human history (3:8: "the devil has been sinning from the beginning"). Here, "from the beginning" refers to the start of the community's reception of the message — what they heard when they first believed. The "old commandment" is "the word which you have heard" — the original teaching transmitted to them. This is not the Mosaic Torah but the instruction that came with the gospel proclamation. The author reassures the community: he is not introducing novelty; he is recalling what they already received.

"Again, I am writing a new commandment to you" (palin entolen kainen grapho hymin). The apparent contradiction — old, then new — is resolved by what follows. The commandment is old in the sense that the community has had it from the beginning. It is new in the sense that it is "true in him and in you" (ho estin alethes en auto kai en hymin) — its reality is being actualized in both Jesus and the community. The newness is not novelty of content but novelty of realization: the commandment is being fulfilled in a new way because "the darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining" (he skotia paragetai kai to phos to alethinon ede phainei).

The language echoes the Fourth Gospel directly. In John 13:34, Jesus said: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another." The commandment to love was not new in content — Leviticus 19:18 ("You shall love your neighbor as yourself") had been in the Torah for centuries. What was new was the standard ("just as I have loved you") and the eschatological context: Jesus's love, demonstrated in his laying down of his life, established a new pattern and empowered a new practice of love within the community. The author of 1 John reflects this: the commandment is old (it comes from the Torah, and from the community's founding teaching), but it is new because it is now being lived out in the new era inaugurated by Christ.

"The darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining." The present tenses are significant: the darkness "is passing away" (paragetai) and the light "is already shining" (ede phainei). The author describes an overlap of ages — the old era of darkness is in the process of departing, but the new era of light has already begun. This is not a completed transition but an ongoing one. The community lives in the overlap: the light is shining, but the darkness has not yet fully passed. This temporal framework explains why the author can simultaneously describe the community as being "in the light" and warn them against falling into the darkness. The transition is underway but not yet complete.

"The one who says he is in the light and hates his brother is in the darkness until now" (ho legon en to photi einai kai ton adelphon autou mison en te skotia estin heos arti). The "one who says" formula continues the pattern of testing profession against practice. The word "hates" (mison, present participle of miseo) indicates an ongoing disposition, not a momentary emotion. "His brother" (ton adelphon autou) refers to a fellow member of the community. The test is internal to the fellowship: do you love the people alongside whom you confess Christ? The phrase "until now" (heos arti) means that the person who hates has never actually entered the light — they have been in darkness the entire time, regardless of their verbal claim.

"The one who loves his brother abides in the light, and there is no cause for stumbling in him" (ho agapon ton adelphon autou en to photi menei, kai skandalon en auto ouk estin). The word skandalon ("stumbling block, cause for falling, trap") appears in the LXX for the obstacle that causes someone to fall (Leviticus 19:14: "You shall not put a stumbling block before the blind"; Psalm 119:165 [118:165 LXX]: "Great peace have those who love your Torah; nothing can make them stumble"). The meaning may be twofold: the person who loves has nothing in himself that causes others to stumble, and the person who loves has no cause for stumbling within himself — he can see where he is going because he is in the light. The loving person walks securely, while the hating person stumbles blindly.

"But the one who hates his brother is in the darkness and walks in the darkness, and does not know where he is going, because the darkness has blinded his eyes" (he skotia etyphlosen tous ophthalmous autou). The image of darkness blinding the eyes recalls Isaiah 6:10 (cited in John 12:40): "He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart." In the Fourth Gospel, blindness is both physical and spiritual, and the story of the man born blind (John 9) functions as an extended exploration of who can see and who is blind. Here, the darkness is not merely the absence of light but an active agent that blinds — it prevents the person from seeing the direction of their life. The verb typhlou ("to blind") in the aorist tense (etyphlosen) suggests that the blinding has already occurred: the person who hates is already blinded, already unable to see, already disoriented. Hatred has done this to them.

Verses 12-14: The Author's Reassurance of the Community

"I am writing to you, little children, because your sins have been forgiven you for his name's sake. I am writing to you, fathers, because you know him who is from the beginning. I am writing to you, young men, because you have overcome the evil one. I have written to you, children, because you know the Father. I have written to you, fathers, because you know him who is from the beginning. I have written to you, young men, because you are strong, and the word of God abides in you, and you have overcome the evil one."

After the sharp tests of verses 3-11 — which could leave the community wondering whether they measure up — the author pauses to reassure. The pattern is striking: six statements, arranged in two groups of three, addressing three categories within the community. The tense shifts between "I am writing" (grapho, present) and "I have written" (egrapsa, aorist), but the shift appears to be stylistic variation rather than a distinction between the present document and a prior letter. The author addresses the same three groups with the same essential content.

"Little children" (teknia) / "Children" (paidia). The first group is addressed with two different terms: teknia (v. 12) and paidia (v. 14). Both are diminutive forms indicating a parent-child relationship. The characteristic attributed to them is foundational: "your sins have been forgiven you for his name's sake" (v. 12) and "you know the Father" (v. 14). The "children" represent the entire community (as teknia does throughout the letter), and the attributes named — forgiveness and knowledge of the Father — are the baseline realities that apply to all believers.

The phrase "for his name's sake" (dia to onoma autou) connects forgiveness to the name of Jesus. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus's name carries his identity and authority: "whatever you ask in my name, I will do it" (John 14:13); "the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name" (John 14:26). In Acts 4:12: "there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved." Forgiveness comes through what Jesus's name represents — his person, his work, his identity as the righteous advocate and the hilasmos.

"Fathers" (pateres). The characteristic of the fathers is that they "know him who is from the beginning" (ton ap' arches). The phrase "from the beginning" (ap' arches) here most likely refers to the one who existed from the beginning — either Jesus (as the Word who was "in the beginning," John 1:1; 1 John 1:1) or God the Father. Given the document's opening — "That which was from the beginning (ap' arches)... concerning the word of life" (1:1) — the referent is probably Jesus as the pre-existent Word. The "fathers" are those with deep, mature knowledge of Christ's identity stretching back to the foundation of the community's life, possibly even the earliest generation of believers. Their defining mark is longstanding, settled knowledge.

"Young men" (neaniskoi). The characteristic of the young men is threefold: they have "overcome the evil one" (nenikekate ton poneron, v. 13), they are "strong" (ischyroi, v. 14), and "the word of God abides in you" (ho logos tou theou en hymin menei, v. 14). The language of victory (nikao, "to conquer, to overcome") will recur at 4:4 ("you have overcome them") and 5:4-5 ("this is the victory that has overcome the world — our faith"). The "evil one" (ho poneros) is identified elsewhere in the letter as the devil (3:8, 10), the one whose works Christ came to destroy (3:8). The young men's victory is not achieved by their own strength but because "the word of God abides in them." The word of God — the message they received, the truth about Jesus — dwelling in them is the source of their strength. This connects to the broader theme of "abiding" (menein): the word abides in them, and they have therefore overcome.

The passage serves a specific rhetorical function. The author has been issuing stern tests: if you don't keep the commandments, you don't know God; if you hate your brother, you're in the darkness. These tests could produce anxiety. The author now reassures the community: your sins are forgiven, you know the Father, you know the one from the beginning, you are strong, you have overcome. The tests are not meant to destroy confidence but to confirm it — the community that hears this letter is the community that passes the tests. The reassurance gives them ground to stand on as the author turns to sharper warnings.

Verses 15-17: The Warning Against Loving the World

"Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world — the desire of the flesh, the desire of the eyes, and the pride of life — is not from the Father but is from the world. And the world is passing away along with its desires, but the one who does the will of God abides forever."

"Do not love the world" (me agapate ton kosmon). The word kosmos here carries a different sense than in verse 2, where the author said Jesus is the hilasmos "for the whole world." In verse 2, "world" meant the entire human order for which God provided atonement. Here, "world" means the system of values, desires, and priorities that operates in opposition to God. The Fourth Gospel uses kosmos in the same dual sense: "God so loved the world" (John 3:16) and "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you" (John 15:18). The world as the object of God's redemptive love and the world as the system that opposes God are not two different worlds but two different aspects of the same reality — humanity as loved by God and humanity as organized against God.

The command "do not love" (me agapate) is a present imperative with the negative me, indicating "stop loving" or "do not continue to love." The author addresses a community that may be tempted to align its affections with the world's values. The prohibition is absolute: love of the world and love of the Father are mutually exclusive. "If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him" — the two loves cannot coexist. This echoes Jesus's teaching in the Synoptic tradition: "No one can serve two masters; either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other" (Matthew 6:24 / Luke 16:13).

"The desire of the flesh, the desire of the eyes, and the pride of life" (he epithymia tes sarkos kai he epithymia ton ophthalmon kai he alazoneia tou biou). The author identifies three categories that characterize "all that is in the world."

The word epithymia ("desire, longing, craving") in the LXX and in first-century Jewish usage carries the connotation of misplaced desire — desire that reaches for what is not appropriate or not given. The tenth commandment (Exodus 20:17, Deuteronomy 5:21) uses the cognate verb epithymeo in the LXX: "You shall not covet (ouk epithymeseis)." Paul identified this commandment as the one through which sin found its opportunity (Romans 7:7-8). The "desire of the flesh" (he epithymia tes sarkos) is desire that originates in the human condition apart from God's Spirit — the pull toward satisfying physical and material appetites without reference to God's will. The "desire of the eyes" (he epithymia ton ophthalmon) is desire generated by seeing — covetousness, the acquisitive gaze that wants what it sees. The eyes are the gateway for desire in Israel's tradition: Eve "saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes" (Genesis 3:6); Achan "saw among the spoil a beautiful cloak... and I coveted them and took them" (Joshua 7:21); David "saw from the roof a woman bathing" (2 Samuel 11:2). The eyes see, desire follows, and sin results.

The word alazoneia ("pride, arrogance, pretension, boastfulness") describes the posture of one who makes claims about life (bios) — resources, status, security — that exceed reality. In the LXX, the alazon is the boaster, the one who trusts in wealth or power rather than in God (Psalm 94:4 [93:4 LXX]; Proverbs 21:24; Wisdom of Solomon 5:8). The bios ("life" in the sense of livelihood, material existence, daily life) is distinguished from zoe ("life" in the deeper sense — the eternal life that God gives). The "pride of life" is the arrogance that comes from material security, the illusion of self-sufficiency.

"Is not from the Father but is from the world" (ouk estin ek tou patros all' ek tou kosmou estin). The preposition ek ("from, out of") indicates origin and source. The three desires do not originate in God; they originate in the world-system. The author draws a sharp line: things are either from the Father or from the world. There is no neutral territory.

"And the world is passing away along with its desires" (kai ho kosmos paragetai kai he epithymia autou). The verb paragetai ("is passing away") is the same verb used in verse 8 for the darkness: "the darkness is passing away." The world and its desires are part of the old order that is in the process of departing. The present tense indicates an ongoing process — the world has not yet fully passed, but its passing is underway.

"But the one who does the will of God abides forever" (ho de poion to thelema tou theou menei eis ton aiona). The contrast is between what is temporary (the world and its desires) and what is permanent (the one who does God's will). The verb menei ("abides, remains") is the same word used throughout the document for the believer's relationship with God. The one who does God's will shares in God's permanence. The phrase eis ton aiona ("into the age, forever") echoes the LXX's le'olam ("forever"): Psalm 125:1 (124:1 LXX), "Those who trust in the LORD are like Mount Zion, which cannot be moved but abides forever." The permanence of the one who does God's will is contrasted with the impermanence of the world — an argument for where to invest one's love and allegiance.

Verses 18-23: The Crisis of the Community — False Teachers and the Denial of Christ

"Children, it is the last hour; and just as you heard that antichrist is coming, even now many antichrists have appeared; from this we know that it is the last hour. They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have remained with us; but they went out, so that it would be shown that they all are not of us. But you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you all know. I have not written to you because you do not know the truth, but because you do know it, and because no lie is of the truth. Who is the liar but the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, the one who denies the Father and the Son. Everyone who denies the Son does not have the Father; the one who confesses the Son has the Father also."

"Children, it is the last hour" (paidia, eschate hora estin). The phrase "last hour" (eschate hora) appears only here in the entire New Testament. The author declares that the community is living in the final period of the present age. In the prophetic tradition, "the last days" (hai eschatai hemerai) designated the period of God's decisive intervention (Isaiah 2:2, Micah 4:1, Joel 2:28-32, cited by Peter in Acts 2:17). The author specifies "hour" (hora) rather than "days" — suggesting an intensified sense of imminence. In the Fourth Gospel, "hour" (hora) is the term for the decisive moment of Jesus's death and glorification (John 2:4, 7:30, 8:20, 12:23, 13:1, 17:1). The use of "hour" here connects the present crisis to the eschatological framework: the community is living in the period immediately before the consummation.

"Just as you heard that antichrist is coming, even now many antichrists have appeared" (kathoos ekousate hoti antichristos erchetai, kai nyn antichristoi polloi gegonasin). The term antichristos appears only in the Johannine letters (here, 2:22, 4:3, and 2 John 7) — nowhere else in the New Testament. The prefix anti- can mean "against" (in opposition to Christ) or "in place of" (a substitute or counterfeit Christ). The community had been taught to expect "antichrist" — the singular suggests a specific figure anticipated in eschatological expectation. But the author's point is that the expected singular has been realized in an unexpected plural: "many antichrists have appeared." The eschatological expectation has been fulfilled not in a single dramatic figure but in multiple persons who have arisen within the community itself. This realization is the evidence that "it is the last hour" — the proliferation of antichrists confirms the eschatological timeline.

"They went out from us, but they were not of us" (ex hemon exelthan all' ouk esan ex hemon). The preposition ek ("from, out of") appears four times in this verse, creating an emphatic pattern. These persons were physically part of the community ("from us," indicating origin within the group), but they were not genuinely part of it ("not of us," indicating true belonging). Their departure from the community revealed their true condition. The author does not describe an external attack on the community but an internal fracture — people who were within the fellowship have left.

"For if they had been of us, they would have remained with us" (ei gar ex hemon esan, memeneskeisan an meth' hemon). The contrary-to-fact conditional (ei + imperfect indicative in the protasis, imperfect + an in the apodosis) states that genuine belonging would have produced remaining. The verb memeneskeisan is a pluperfect of meno ("to remain, to abide") — the same verb that pervades the document. The logic is: remaining is the evidence of genuine belonging; departure is the evidence of its absence. This is not a speculation about hidden internal states but a reading of observable behavior as evidence. The author interprets the departure in light of the abiding/remaining theology: those who are truly "of" the community will remain; those who leave reveal that they were never genuinely "of" it.

"But they went out, so that it would be shown that they all are not of us" (all' hina phanerothesin hoti ouk eisin pantes ex hemon). The hina ("so that, in order that") clause attributes purpose to their departure: it happened so that their true nature would be made manifest (phanerothensin, "made visible, revealed"). The author reads the departure as a divinely permitted unveiling. What was hidden — their lack of genuine belonging — has now been exposed. The word pantes ("all") may mean "all of them" (every one of the departing group) or may modify the "us" — "not all are of us," meaning that not everyone who is physically present in the community necessarily belongs to it.

"But you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you all know" (kai hymeis chrisma echete apo tou hagiou, kai oidate pantes). The word chrisma ("anointing") derives from the same root as Christos ("Christ, Anointed One"). The community has received a "chrisma" from the "Christos" — an anointing from the Anointed One. The wordplay is deliberate and would be unmistakable to a Greek-speaking audience.

In Israel's tradition, anointing was the act by which priests, kings, and prophets were set apart for their office. The anointing of Aaron and his sons (Exodus 29:7, 30:30, Leviticus 8:12) consecrated them for priestly service. The anointing of Saul (1 Samuel 10:1), David (1 Samuel 16:13), and Solomon (1 Kings 1:39) designated them as God's chosen king. When the Spirit came upon the anointed one, the anointing and the Spirit were closely linked: "The Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon David from that day forward" (1 Samuel 16:13). Isaiah 61:1 links anointing and Spirit explicitly: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me, because the LORD has anointed me." Jesus applied this text to himself in Luke 4:18-21. Peter described Jesus's ministry in terms of anointing: "God anointed (echrisen) Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38).

The "anointing" the community has received is almost certainly the Holy Spirit. The author will say in verse 27 that "the anointing which you received from him abides in you, and you have no need for anyone to teach you." In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus promised that "the Spirit of truth" would "guide you into all the truth" (John 16:13) and "teach you all things" (John 14:26). The anointing that teaches and abides is the Spirit. The community does not need the departed teachers because they have the Spirit's anointing — the same anointing that rests on the Christ himself.

"The Holy One" (tou hagiou). In the Fourth Gospel, Peter confessed Jesus as "the Holy One of God" (ho hagios tou theou, John 6:69). In the LXX, "the Holy One" (ho hagios) is a title for God, particularly in Isaiah (Isaiah 1:4, 5:19, 10:20, 12:6, 29:19, 30:11-12, 37:23, 40:25, 41:14, 16, 20, 43:3, 14, 45:11, 47:4, 48:17, 49:7, 54:5, 55:5, 60:9, 14). The "Holy One of Israel" is one of Isaiah's characteristic names for God. Whether the author refers here to God the Father or to Jesus Christ (or whether the distinction matters in the author's theology, since "the one who confesses the Son has the Father also," v. 23) is ambiguous. The point is that the anointing comes from the divine source.

"And you all know" (kai oidate pantes). Some manuscripts read "you know all things" (oidate panta) instead of "you all know" (oidate pantes). Either reading emphasizes that the community already possesses the knowledge necessary to discern truth from falsehood. The author writes not to supply what they lack but to activate what they already have.

"Who is the liar but the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ?" (tis estin ho pseustes ei me ho arnoumenos hoti Iesous ouk estin ho Christos). The specific content of the antichrists' denial is now identified: they deny that Jesus is the Christ — that is, they deny the identification of the historical person Jesus with the expected Messiah (Christos). This may refer to a teaching that separated the divine Christ from the human Jesus — a view that some later traditions would develop, but which in its earliest form may have held that the divine "Christ-spirit" descended on Jesus at his baptism and departed before his crucifixion, so that the human Jesus was not truly the Christ who suffered and died. Such a teaching would undermine the confession that "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14) and that Jesus Christ came "in the flesh" (which 4:2 and 2 John 7 will make explicit). The author calls this denial "the lie" and the denier "the liar" — the definite articles (ho pseustes, "THE liar") suggest this is the supreme or paradigmatic lie, the falsehood that stands behind all others.

"This is the antichrist, the one who denies the Father and the Son" (houtos estin ho antichristos, ho arnoumenos ton patera kai ton huion). The denial of the Son is simultaneously a denial of the Father. In the Fourth Gospel, Jesus stated this principle repeatedly: "Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him" (John 5:23); "If you knew me, you would know my Father also" (John 8:19); "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). The Father and the Son are so bound together that rejecting one necessarily means rejecting the other. There is no access to the Father that bypasses the Son.

"Everyone who denies the Son does not have the Father; the one who confesses the Son has the Father also" (pas ho arnoumenos ton huion oude ton patera echei; ho homologon ton huion kai ton patera echei). The verb echei ("to have") describes a relationship of possession and access. To "have" the Father means to have a relationship with the Father, to have access to God, to be in fellowship with God. The logic is stated both negatively and positively: denial of the Son excludes relationship with the Father; confession of the Son includes relationship with the Father. The test is christological: right belief about Jesus's identity is not an optional doctrinal detail but the gateway to relationship with God.

Verses 24-27: The Safeguard — Let What You Heard from the Beginning Remain in You

"As for you, let that abide in you which you heard from the beginning. If what you heard from the beginning abides in you, you also will abide in the Son and in the Father. And this is the promise which he himself made to us: eternal life. These things I have written to you concerning those who are trying to deceive you. As for you, the anointing which you received from him abides in you, and you have no need for anyone to teach you; but as his anointing teaches you about all things, and is true and is not a lie, and just as it has taught you, you abide in him."

"Let that abide in you which you heard from the beginning" (hymeis ho ekousate ap' arches en hymin meneto). The author now turns from describing the false teachers to addressing the community directly: "as for you" (hymeis, emphatic). The remedy for false teaching is not a new revelation but the original message. The verb meneto (imperative of meno, "let it remain") urges the community to hold fast to the teaching they received at the start. The "from the beginning" (ap' arches) here, as in verse 7, refers to the beginning of their faith — the original apostolic proclamation. Stability is found not in novelty but in remaining anchored to what was transmitted from the beginning.

"If what you heard from the beginning abides in you, you also will abide in the Son and in the Father" (ean en hymin meine ho ap' arches ekousate, kai hymeis en to huio kai en to patri meneite). The mutual indwelling is described as conditional on the message remaining in the community. The logic is: the message abides in you, therefore you abide in the Son and the Father. The message is not merely information; it is the medium of relationship. When the community holds to the original teaching about Jesus, they remain in fellowship with the Father and the Son. When they abandon it (as the departing group did), they lose that fellowship.

"And this is the promise which he himself made to us: eternal life" (kai haute estin he epangelia hen autos epenggeilato hemin, ten zoen ten aionion). "Eternal life" (he zoe he aionios) is not presented here as a future reward only but as a present reality that comes with abiding. In the Fourth Gospel, eternal life is both present ("Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life," John 3:36; "He who hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life and does not come into judgment," John 5:24) and future ("I will raise him up on the last day," John 6:40). The author of 1 John will state later: "God gave us eternal life, and this life is in his Son. Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life" (5:11-12). Eternal life is not a substance deposited in the believer but a relational reality that exists in connection with the Son. Having the Son = having life. Denying the Son = not having the Father and therefore not having life. The departed teachers, by denying that Jesus is the Christ, have severed themselves from the Son and therefore from the life that is in the Son.

"These things I have written to you concerning those who are trying to deceive you" (tauta egrapsa hymin peri ton planontion hymas). The participle planontion ("those who are deceiving, leading astray") is a present participle, indicating that the deception is ongoing. The departed teachers have left the community but are apparently still attempting to influence those who remain. The author's purpose is protective: he writes to inoculate the community against the continuing influence of the false teachers.

"The anointing which you received from him abides in you" (to chrisma ho elabete ap' autou menei en hymin). The chrisma (anointing/Spirit) is not something the community must seek but something they already possess. The verb menei (present active indicative, "abides, remains") is not an imperative but an indicative — the author is stating a fact, not issuing a command. The anointing is already present and already active in the community.

"And you have no need for anyone to teach you" (kai ou chreian echete hina tis didaske hymas). This statement must be read in context. The author is not dismissing all teaching (he is, after all, teaching the community through this very document). He is addressing the specific situation: the community does not need the departed teachers. Their anointing — the Spirit — provides the capacity to discern truth from falsehood. The author is not promoting anti-intellectualism or the rejection of all human instruction but assuring the community that they are not dependent on those who have left. The departed teachers claimed to have superior knowledge, a higher teaching. The author counters: you already have the anointing; you already know what you need to know; those teachers have nothing you lack.

"But as his anointing teaches you about all things, and is true and is not a lie" (all' hos to autou chrisma didaskei hymas peri panton, kai alethes estin kai ouk estin pseudos). The anointing is characterized as true (alethes) and not a lie (ouk estin pseudos) — in direct contrast to the antichrists, who are liars (v. 22). The Spirit's teaching is reliable; the departed teachers' doctrine is false. The community has an internal teacher — the Spirit — whose instruction is trustworthy.

"And just as it has taught you, you abide in him" (kai kathos edidaxen hymas, menete en auto). The verb menete could be either indicative ("you abide") or imperative ("abide!"). If indicative, the author is describing what is already the case: because the anointing has taught you, you are abiding. If imperative, it is a command: remain in him, in accordance with what the anointing has taught you. The ambiguity may be intentional — the author is both describing their present state and urging them to continue in it. The verb meno ties the section together: the message abides in them (v. 24), the anointing abides in them (v. 27), and they abide in the Son and the Father (v. 24) and in him (v. 27). Everything converges on remaining, staying, not departing — in contrast to those who "went out" (v. 19).

Verses 28-29: Abiding, Confidence, and the Test of Righteousness

"And now, little children, abide in him, so that when he appears we may have confidence and not shrink away from him in shame at his coming. If you know that he is righteous, you know that everyone also who practices righteousness has been born of him."

"And now, little children, abide in him" (kai nyn, teknia, menete en auto). The menete here is clearly imperative: "abide!" The "and now" (kai nyn) marks a transition from the exposition about the antichrists to a closing exhortation. The address teknia ("little children") returns to the term from verse 1, creating a frame around the chapter. The command to abide — to remain in Christ, to not depart as the others did — is the practical application of everything the chapter has argued.

"So that when he appears" (hina ean phanerothe). The verb phaneroo ("to appear, to be made manifest") is used in this document for both Jesus's past appearing (1:2, 3:5, 3:8) and his future appearing (here and 3:2). The author anticipates a future, visible manifestation of Jesus — a return, a revealing. This connects to the "last hour" of verse 18: the community is living in the final period before Jesus's appearing.

"We may have confidence" (schomen parrhesian). The word parrhesia ("confidence, boldness, freedom of speech") originally referred to the right of a citizen to speak freely in the assembly. In the LXX and in first-century Jewish usage, it came to describe the boldness of those who stand before God with a clear conscience. The author will return to this concept in 3:21 ("if our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God") and 4:17 ("by this, love is perfected with us, so that we may have confidence in the day of judgment"). The confidence is not self-generated but the product of abiding — those who have remained in Christ will not be ashamed when he appears.

"And not shrink away from him in shame at his coming" (kai me aischynthomen ap' autou en te parousia autou). The verb aischyno ("to be ashamed, to shrink back in disgrace") describes the response of those who are exposed as not belonging. The word parousia ("coming, arrival, presence") in first-century usage referred to the official visit of a king or dignitary to a city. The citizens would prepare for the parousia, and those who had been faithful during the ruler's absence would be honored, while those who had been negligent or disloyal would be shamed. The author uses the word to describe Jesus's anticipated return, with the same dynamic: those who have abided will have confidence; those who have not will shrink back in shame.

"If you know that he is righteous, you know that everyone also who practices righteousness has been born of him" (ean eidete hoti dikaios estin, ginoskete hoti kai pas ho poion ten dikaiosynen ex autou gegennetai). The "he" (the one who is righteous) connects back to "Jesus Christ the righteous" in verse 1. The adjective dikaios, attributed to Jesus at the chapter's beginning, now becomes the basis for a test of genuineness at the chapter's end. The logic is: if you know the character of the parent (righteous), you can identify the children by their family resemblance (they practice righteousness). The phrase "has been born of him" (ex autou gegennetai) introduces the language of divine birth that will dominate chapter 3. The perfect tense gegennetai ("has been born and remains in that state") indicates a past event with continuing results — the new birth is not a one-time experience that fades but an ongoing reality that produces visible effects.

The verb poion (present participle of poieo, "to do, to practice") indicates habitual action, not a single act. The test is not whether a person has ever done something righteous but whether righteousness characterizes their conduct. This is the same distinction the author has been drawing throughout the chapter: the "one who says" versus the one who actually does. Those who practice righteousness reveal their parentage; those who do not, regardless of their claims, reveal that they have not been born of God.

This final verse hands the argument to chapter 3, which will explore at length what it means to be "born of God" (or "born of him"), what characterizes the children of God versus the children of the devil, and how the practice of righteousness and the practice of love serve as the visible evidence of divine parentage.

Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 2

The chapter moves through a coherent sequence:

1. The tension between sin and holiness is addressed (vv. 1-2). The community should aim for sinlessness, but when sin occurs, Jesus serves as advocate and atoning sacrifice — and this provision extends to all humanity.

2. The first test of genuineness is obedience (vv. 3-6). Knowing God is demonstrated by keeping his commandments and walking as Jesus walked. Verbal claims without corresponding conduct are lies.

3. The second test is love (vv. 7-11). The commandment to love is both old (received from the beginning) and new (being realized in the era of the true light). Hating a brother while claiming to be in the light is a contradiction; loving one's brother is the evidence of genuine illumination.

4. The community is reassured (vv. 12-14). Before the warnings escalate, the author affirms what the community already possesses: forgiveness, knowledge of God, strength, and victory over the evil one.

5. The competing claim of the world is rejected (vv. 15-17). The world's desires — flesh, eyes, pride — are passing away. The one who does God's will endures permanently.

6. The crisis of false teachers is addressed (vv. 18-23). Those who denied that Jesus is the Christ departed the community, revealing that they were never genuinely part of it. The community has the anointing (the Spirit) that teaches them and enables them to discern truth from falsehood.

7. The safeguard is to remain (vv. 24-27). Remaining in the original message ensures remaining in the Son and the Father. The anointing already present in the community is sufficient; the community does not depend on the departed teachers.

8. The chapter concludes with a forward-looking exhortation (vv. 28-29). Abide, so that at Jesus's appearing there will be confidence rather than shame. The practice of righteousness is the evidence of having been born of God — a theme that chapter 3 will develop at length.

Throughout, the author's method is consistent: verbal profession is tested against observable conduct. Claiming to know God, to be in the light, to abide in Christ — none of these claims is accepted at face value. Each is verified or falsified by the person's obedience, love, and confession about Jesus. The author is not constructing abstract theology but addressing a concrete community facing a specific crisis: people they trusted have left and are teaching things that contradict the original message. The author's response is to provide tests by which the community can distinguish the genuine from the counterfeit, and to assure them that what they already have — the original message, the anointing, the fellowship with the Father and the Son — is sufficient.
