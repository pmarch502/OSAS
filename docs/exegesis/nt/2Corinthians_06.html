2 Corinthians 6 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author argues immediately before (2 Corinthians 5): Chapter 5 continues the argument from chapter 4 about the mortal body and eschatological hope. Paul discusses the "earthly tent" being destroyed and the "building from God" that awaits (5:1), extending the clay-vessel imagery of 4:7 and the outer-person-wasting language of 4:16. He speaks of groaning, longing to be clothed with the heavenly dwelling (5:2-4), and identifies the Spirit as the arrabon ("guarantee, down payment") of this future (5:5, echoing 1:22). He states the principle that governs present conduct: "we walk by faith, not by sight" (5:7), and that whether in the body or away from it, "we make it our aim to please him" (5:9). The accountability statement arrives in 5:10: "we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive what is due for what he has done in the body, whether good or evil." This judgment-seat awareness drives what follows: "knowing the fear of the Lord, we persuade others" (5:11). Paul is not commending himself again but giving the Corinthians grounds to answer those who boast in outward appearances (5:12). "The love of Christ controls us" (5:14), grounded in the conclusion that "one has died for all, therefore all have died" (5:14b), and "those who live might no longer live for themselves but for him who for their sake died and was raised" (5:15). This leads to the "new creation" declaration: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (5:17). Paul then describes the "ministry of reconciliation" (5:18-19): "in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting to us the message of reconciliation." Paul is an "ambassador for Christ, God making his appeal through us" (5:20). The appeal is direct: "be reconciled to God" (5:20b). The chapter closes with 5:21: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

What the author argues immediately after (2 Corinthians 7:1-4): 7:1 continues directly from the catena of OT quotations in 6:14-18: "Since we have these promises, beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and spirit, bringing holiness to completion in the fear of God." The "promises" (epangelias) are the divine words just quoted — God dwelling among them, being their God, being father to them. The cleansing language connects to the "unclean thing" (akatharton) of 6:17. The "fear of God" echoes the "fear of the Lord" from 5:11. Then 7:2-4 resumes the personal appeal that 6:14-7:1 reframed: "Make room in your hearts for us" (7:2) echoes "widen your hearts also" (6:13). Paul reaffirms his integrity: "We have wronged no one, we have corrupted no one, we have taken advantage of no one" (7:2), paralleling the blameless-ministry language of 6:3. He expresses boldness and pride in them (7:4). From 7:5 onward, Paul returns to the travelogue interrupted in 2:13 — his arrival in Macedonia and the coming of Titus.

How chapter 6 connects the two: Chapter 6 continues without interruption from 5:20-21. The appeal to "be reconciled to God" does not end at the chapter boundary. 6:1 begins with synergountes de kai ("working together with him, then"), a participial phrase that directly continues the ambassador-and-appeal language of 5:20. The "we" who work together with God are the same ambassadors who make God's appeal. What they appeal is that the Corinthians not "receive the grace of God in vain" (6:1b) — which picks up the reconciliation and grace themes of 5:18-21. The chapter then develops in two movements. First (6:1-10): Paul grounds the appeal in the prophetic "now" of Isaiah 49:8, then demonstrates the authenticity of his ministry through an extensive catalogue of sufferings, virtues, and paradoxes. Second (6:11-18, continuing through 7:1): Paul makes a direct emotional appeal for the Corinthians to open their hearts, and this opens into a warning against being yoked with unbelievers, supported by a catena of OT texts about God's dwelling among his people, separation from impurity, and the covenant relationship. The entire chapter sits at the pivot point between Paul's theological exposition of the ministry of reconciliation (5:11-21) and the resumption of the personal narrative about Titus (7:5-16). It is the practical application of the reconciliation theology — addressed directly to the Corinthians and demanding a response.

Verse 1: The Appeal Not to Receive Grace in Vain

"Working together with him, then, we appeal to you not to receive the grace of God in vain" (6:1).

The participial phrase synergountes de kai ("working together, and also") connects directly to the preceding argument. The implied object of "working together" (synergountes) is God. In 5:20, Paul described himself as an "ambassador for Christ, God making his appeal through us." Paul is God's co-worker in the ministry of reconciliation. The verb synergeo ("to work together, to cooperate, to collaborate") matches 1 Corinthians 3:9: "For we are God's fellow workers (theou gar esmen synergoi)." Paul is a co-laborer with God, not an independent agent.

The verb parakaleo ("to appeal, to urge, to exhort, to comfort") has appeared throughout the letter's opening (1:4, 6; 2:7, 8) and will dominate chapter 7. Here it carries the force of urgent appeal: Paul urges, begs, exhorts the Corinthians.

The content of the appeal is negative: "not to receive the grace of God in vain" (me eis kenon ten charin tou theou dexasthai hymas). The word kenos ("empty, vain, without effect, for nothing") describes a result: receiving the grace of God in such a way that it produces nothing, remains empty, bears no fruit. The verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to accept") is the word for actively receiving something offered. Grace (charis) is not forced; it is offered and must be received. But it can be received eis kenon — "into emptiness," so that it comes to nothing.

This is a warning. Paul does not say "you have received grace in vain"; he urges them not to. The aorist infinitive dexasthai with the negative me functions as a preventive exhortation. The implication is that it is possible to receive the grace of God and have it come to nothing. The reconciliation described in 5:18-21 is real but requires ongoing response.

The word kenos is significant in Paul's usage within this correspondence. In 1 Corinthians 15:2, Paul warned that unless the Corinthians hold fast to the gospel he preached, they "believed in vain" (eike episteusate). In 1 Corinthians 15:10, Paul says "his grace toward me was not in vain" (he charis autou he eis eme ou kene egenethe) — and immediately explains why: "I worked harder than any of them, though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me." Grace that is "not in vain" is grace that produces labor, effort, faithfulness. Grace received "in vain" would be grace that leaves the recipient unchanged, that enters but does not work.

The warning assumes that it is genuinely possible for grace, once received, to be rendered empty — to come to nothing. The exhortation would be meaningless if this were impossible. Paul would not urge them not to do something that could not happen.

Verse 2: Isaiah 49:8 and the Prophetic "Now"

"For he says, 'In a favorable time I listened to you, and in a day of salvation I have helped you.' Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (6:2).

Paul quotes Isaiah 49:8 (LXX): kairo dekto epekousa sou kai en hemera soterias eboethesa soi ("In an acceptable time I listened to you, and in a day of salvation I helped you"). The connective gar ("for") indicates that this quotation supports the preceding appeal. The urgency of not receiving grace in vain is grounded in the prophetic identification of the present moment.

In the Isaiah context, the words are spoken by God to his Servant. Isaiah 49 is the second of the Servant passages — the Servant called from the womb (49:1), whose mouth was made like a sharp sword (49:2), who was told "You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3). But the Servant confesses apparent failure: "I have labored in vain (kenon); I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity (eis kenon kai mataion)" (49:4 LXX). The word kenon — "in vain" — appears in the Servant's own lament. Despite this apparent fruitlessness, God affirms the Servant and expands his mission: "It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob and to bring back the preserved of Israel; I will make you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth" (49:6). The Servant's labor that appeared kenos ("empty") is not kenos after all; it is the vehicle of worldwide salvation.

Isaiah 49:8, the verse Paul quotes, comes immediately after this expanded commission. God tells the Servant: "In a favorable time I listened to you, and in a day of salvation I helped you." The verse continues (though Paul does not quote this part): "I will keep you and give you as a covenant to the people, to establish the land, to apportion the desolate heritages, saying to the prisoners, 'Come out,' to those who are in darkness, 'Appear'" (49:8b-9). The Servant himself is given "as a covenant to the people" — the Servant becomes the instrument of the covenant relationship. The rest of 49:8-13 describes the restoration from exile: prisoners freed, the hungry fed, highways on mountains, comfort for the afflicted.

Paul applies this to the present moment with emphatic doubling: "Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (idou nyn kairos euprosdektos, idou nyn hemera soterias). The doubled idou nyn ("behold, now") presses the point. The kairos euprosdektos ("well-received time, favorable time, acceptable time") is not a future hope; it is the present reality. The "day of salvation" is today. Paul reads Isaiah 49:8 as being fulfilled in the present ministry of reconciliation. The Servant's commission to be "a light for the nations" and "a covenant to the people" is being enacted now, through the apostolic proclamation.

The verbal link between kenos in 6:1 and kenon in Isaiah 49:4 (LXX) may not be accidental. The Servant feared his labor was kenos; God responded by declaring the favorable time of salvation. Paul urges the Corinthians not to receive grace eis kenon and then immediately cites the passage in which God answered the Servant's fear of kenos labor. The implication: if God has declared that this is the day of salvation, the favorable time, then receiving that grace emptily is a squandering of the very moment the prophet announced.

Verses 3-4a: Blameless Ministry and Its Commendation

"We put no obstacle in anyone's way, so that no fault may be found with our ministry, but as servants of God we commend ourselves in every way" (6:3-4a).

The verb didomi ("to give, to place") with proskope ("stumbling block, obstacle, cause of offense") indicates that Paul deliberately avoids placing anything in anyone's path that could cause stumbling. The word proskope is related to proskopto ("to strike against, to stumble"), which Paul used in Romans 9:32-33, quoting Isaiah 8:14, about Israel stumbling over the stumbling stone. Here the concern is practical: Paul orders his conduct so that no one has grounds to criticize the ministry (diakonia) itself. The verb momethenai ("may be blamed, may be found fault with") is the aorist passive subjunctive of momaomai ("to blame, to censure, to find fault with"). The ministry — the same diakonia that has been Paul's subject since 3:6 — must be above reproach not because Paul's reputation matters for its own sake but because a blameworthy minister discredits the message.

The pivot: "but as servants of God we commend ourselves in every way" (all' en panti synistanontes heautous hos theou diakonoi). The verb synistemi ("to commend, to present, to demonstrate") has appeared at every stage of the letter's argument about credentials. In 3:1, Paul raised the question of letters of recommendation (systatikai epistolai). In 4:2, he claimed to commend himself "by the open statement of the truth." In 5:12, he insisted he was "not commending ourselves to you again." Each time, Paul wrestles with the tension between necessary self-defense and inappropriate self-promotion. Here he resolves it: the commendation is not by rhetoric or credentials but by the content of his life and suffering — what follows in the catalogue of verses 4b-10.

The phrase "servants of God" (theou diakonoi) links back to 3:6, where God "made us sufficient to be ministers (diakonous) of a new covenant." The role is the same: Paul is God's servant. His commendation consists in the character of that service, not in letters or eloquence.

Verses 4b-5: The Catalogue of Sufferings

"By great endurance, in afflictions, hardships, calamities, beatings, imprisonments, riots, labors, sleepless nights, hunger" (6:4b-5).

The catalogue opens with en hypomone polle ("in much endurance, in great steadfastness"). The word hypomone ("endurance, steadfastness, patient perseverance") appeared in 1:6, where Paul told the Corinthians that his comfort operates "in your patient endurance (hypomones) of the same sufferings that we suffer." Endurance is the umbrella under which all that follows is gathered. It is not passive resignation but active, sustained perseverance under pressure.

What follows is structured in three groups of three, forming a carefully ordered catalogue.

First triad — general terms for suffering:

  • thlipsesin ("afflictions, pressures") — the word that dominated 1:4-8 and appeared in 4:8 ("afflicted in every way"). It describes external pressure that squeezes and confines.
  • anankais ("hardships, necessities, constraints") — situations of forced suffering, things that cannot be avoided. In 1 Corinthians 7:26, Paul spoke of the "present distress" (enestotan anankken). The word carries the sense of unavoidable constraint.
  • stenochooriais ("calamities, dire straits, confinements") — the same word appeared in 4:8, where stenochoria was the extreme condition Paul was spared from ("afflicted but not crushed"). Here it appears as something Paul does endure. This does not contradict 4:8; the "not crushed" of 4:8 means that the affliction does not reach its final, destroying consequence, not that Paul is exempt from severe distress.

Second triad — specific forms of suffering:

  • plegais ("beatings, blows, stripes") — physical violence inflicted on Paul's body. Paul will catalogue his beatings later in 11:24-25: "Five times I received at the hands of the Jews the forty lashes less one. Three times I was beaten with rods."
  • phylakais ("imprisonments") — a recurring feature of Paul's apostolic career, attested in Acts at Philippi (Acts 16:23-24) and later in Caesarea and Rome.
  • akatastasiais ("riots, tumults, disorders") — public upheaval and mob action directed against Paul. Acts records such events in Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:50), Iconium (14:5), Lystra (14:19), Thessalonica (17:5), and Ephesus (19:23-41). The word carries the sense of chaotic instability.

Third triad — voluntary hardships:

  • kopois ("labors, toils") — exhausting physical work. Paul used it in 1 Corinthians 4:12: "we labor (kopiomen), working with our own hands." This is the tent-making labor by which Paul supported himself, refusing financial support from the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 9:12, 15-18; 2 Corinthians 11:7-9).
  • agrypniais ("sleepless nights, watchings") — nights without sleep, whether from work, travel, danger, or prayer. Paul will list this again in 11:27.
  • nesteiais ("hunger, fastings") — whether voluntary fasting or involuntary deprivation. In 11:27, Paul will again mention "hunger and thirst, often without food."

The progression from general to specific to voluntary is significant. Paul suffers not only what is inflicted on him but also what he voluntarily accepts for the sake of the ministry. The labors, sleepless nights, and hunger are costs he could reduce — by accepting financial support, by traveling less, by moderating his work pace — but chooses not to. These voluntary hardships are part of his credentials because they demonstrate that his ministry is not motivated by personal gain.

Verses 6-7a: The Catalogue of Virtues and Powers

"By purity, knowledge, patience, kindness, the Holy Spirit, genuine love, by truthful speech, and the power of God" (6:6-7a).

The catalogue shifts from what Paul endures to how he conducts himself. These are the marks of character that accompany the suffering.

  • hagnoteti ("purity, integrity, moral cleanness") — freedom from contamination, the opposite of the "disgraceful, underhanded ways" renounced in 4:2. The word connects to the "holiness and godly sincerity" (hagiosyne kai eilikrineia) claimed in 1:12.
  • gnosei ("knowledge") — not abstract intellectual achievement but the knowledge of God's purposes, the understanding that the Spirit produces. In 2:14, Paul spoke of God spreading "the fragrance of the knowledge of him" through the apostles. In 4:6, God shone in hearts "to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ."
  • makrothymia ("patience, long-suffering, forbearance") — the capacity to endure provocation without retaliation. This is a quality attributed to God himself in the LXX: Exodus 34:6 (LXX) describes the LORD as makrothymos ("slow to anger"). Paul imitates the divine patience.
  • chrestoteti ("kindness, goodness, generosity") — also a divine quality: Romans 2:4, "the riches of his kindness (chrestotetos)" that is meant to lead to repentance.
  • en pneumati hagio ("in the Holy Spirit") — the inclusion of the Holy Spirit in a list of virtues and character traits is striking. It signals that the preceding and following qualities are not merely human achievements but manifestations of the Spirit's presence. The Spirit was identified in 3:6 as the defining characteristic of the new covenant ministry; in 3:8, the new covenant was called "the ministry of the Spirit"; in 3:3, the Spirit of the living God wrote on hearts of flesh. Here the Spirit appears as the animating power behind Paul's entire character and conduct.
  • agape anypokritos ("genuine love, love without hypocrisy") — the adjective anypokritos ("unhypocritical, without pretense, genuine") appears in Romans 12:9: "Let love be genuine (anypokritos)." Paul's love is not performance. This responds to the charge of insincerity that has been a running concern since 1:12.
  • en logo aletheias ("in the word of truth, by truthful speech") — this echoes 4:2, "the open statement of the truth" (te phanerosei tes aletheias). Paul's speech corresponds to reality; he does not distort, manipulate, or falsify.
  • en dynamei theou ("in the power of God") — the divine power that was placed in clay vessels (4:7), the "surpassing power" (hyperbole tes dynameos) that belongs to God and not to Paul. Paul does not operate by his own force but by the energy of God working through him.

Verse 7b: Weapons of Righteousness

"With the weapons of righteousness for the right hand and for the left" (6:7b).

The military metaphor introduces hopla ("weapons, instruments, arms") — the same word Paul used in Romans 6:13, where he exhorted: "Do not present your members to sin as instruments (hopla) for unrighteousness, but present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life, and your members to God as instruments (hopla) for righteousness." In Romans 13:12: "Let us put on the armor (hopla) of light."

"For the right hand and for the left" (ton dexion kai aristeron) refers to offensive weapons (the right hand wielding sword or spear) and defensive weapons (the left hand bearing shield). The imagery is of a soldier fully armed on both sides — equipped for both attack and defense. But the weapons are "of righteousness" (dikaiosynes), not of worldly force. In 10:3-4, Paul will develop this: "For though we walk in the flesh, we are not waging war according to the flesh. For the weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh but have divine power to destroy strongholds." The same contrast operates here: Paul fights, but not with human weapons. His armament is the righteousness that belongs to the ministry described in 3:9 as "the ministry of righteousness" (he diakonia tes dikaiosynes).

Verses 8-10: The Catalogue of Paradoxes

"Through honor and dishonor, through slander and praise. We are treated as impostors, and yet are true; as unknown, and yet well known; as dying, and behold, we live; as punished, and yet not killed; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, yet possessing everything" (6:8-10).

This is the climactic section of the catalogue, and it returns to the paradox-of-power-in-weakness logic that has governed the letter since 4:7. Each pair names an apparent negative and its hidden positive.

"Through honor and dishonor, through slander and praise" (dia doxes kai atimias, dia dysphemias kai euphemias) — the paired contrasts describe the oscillation of Paul's public reception. The word doxa here means "honor, reputation" (not "glory" in the theological sense used in chapter 3), paired with atimia ("dishonor, disgrace"). The word dysphemia ("slander, evil report") is paired with euphemia ("praise, good report"). Paul encounters both extremes: some honor him, others dishonor him; some speak well, others slander. The ministry is not measured by consistent public acclaim but by faithfulness through both.

Then seven paradoxes, each introduced by hos ("as, like, in the manner of"):

First: "As impostors, and yet true" (hos planoi kai aletheis) — the word planos ("deceiver, impostor, one who leads astray") is the charge leveled against Paul by his opponents. The same word was applied to Jesus in Matthew 27:63: "that impostor (ho planos) said while he was still alive, 'After three days I will rise.'" Paul is called a deceiver; he is in reality aletheis ("true, genuine"). The charge of deception has been a running theme: Paul denied practicing cunning or tampering with God's word (4:2); he insisted on sincerity (1:12); he claimed love "without hypocrisy" (6:6). The opponents say planos; the reality is otherwise.

Second: "As unknown, and yet well known" (hos agnoumenoi kai epiginoskomenoi) — Paul lacks the public credentials, the letters of recommendation (3:1), the visible prestige that the rival teachers display. In worldly terms, he is "unknown" — without social standing, without the recognition that comes from external validation. Yet he is "well known" (epiginoskomenoi, "fully known, recognized, acknowledged") — known to God (5:11, "what we are is known to God"), known to the Corinthians' conscience (5:11), known by the fruit of his ministry.

Third: "As dying, and behold, we live" (hos apothnneskontes kai idou zomen) — this echoes 4:10-11 directly: "always carrying in the body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be manifested." The paradox of death and life is the core of Paul's apostolic existence. The exclamation idou ("behold!") adds surprise and emphasis: against all expectation, Paul is alive.

Fourth: "As punished, and yet not killed" (hos paideuomenoi kai me thanatoumenoi) — the word paideuo ("to discipline, to train, to chastise, to educate through correction") is the LXX word for God's disciplinary action toward those he loves. Proverbs 3:11-12 (LXX): "My son, do not despise the LORD's discipline (paideias kyriou) or be weary of his reproof, for the LORD reproves him whom he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights." And Psalm 118:18 (LXX 117:18) is particularly close to Paul's language: "The LORD has disciplined me severely (paideuon epaideusen me ho kyrios), but he has not given me over to death (to de thanato ou paredoken me)." Paul's formulation — paideuomenoi kai me thanatoumenoi — echoes the Psalm almost verbatim: disciplined, but not given over to death. The suffering is understood as God's fatherly discipline, not as divine abandonment or punishment for wrongdoing. The Psalm's explicit statement that severe discipline is not the same as being given over to death is precisely Paul's point. The connection to 2 Samuel 7:14 is also present: God's promise regarding David's son includes "When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him (paideuso auton) with the rod of men, with the stripes of the sons of men" — the same disciplinary language within a covenant of faithful love. Paul will quote from this very Davidic covenant passage in verse 18.

Fifth: "As sorrowful, yet always rejoicing" (hos lypoumenoi aei de chairontes) — sorrow and joy coexist simultaneously. The word lypeo ("to grieve, to cause sorrow") will dominate chapter 7 (7:8, 9, 10, 11), where Paul discusses the grief his previous letter caused the Corinthians. Here the sorrow is Paul's own — real grief from real suffering — but accompanied by constant joy. The "always" (aei) is the same word used in 4:11 ("always being given over to death"). Just as death is always at work, joy is always present.

Sixth: "As poor, yet making many rich" (hos ptochoi pollous de ploutizontes) — Paul lacks material resources; he works with his hands (1 Corinthians 4:12); he refused financial support from the Corinthians (11:7-9). Yet he makes many rich — not materially but in the wealth that comes through the gospel. This anticipates 8:9, where Paul will say of Christ: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich." Paul participates in the same pattern as Christ: poverty that enriches others.

Seventh: "As having nothing, yet possessing everything" (hos meden echontes kai panta katechontes) — the final paradox is the most sweeping. Paul has nothing (meden echontes) in worldly terms. Yet he possesses everything (panta katechontes). The verb katecho ("to hold fast, to possess, to retain") is stronger than simple echo ("to have"); it implies firm grip, secure possession. What Paul possesses is not specified, but the context supplies it: the treasure in clay vessels (4:7), the knowledge of God's glory in the face of Christ (4:6), the Spirit as guarantee (5:5), the ministry of reconciliation (5:18), the promises of God. These constitute "everything" in a way that material possessions cannot.

The cumulative effect of the seven paradoxes is to present Paul's ministry as a living enactment of the cross-pattern described in chapters 4-5. Apparent weakness conceals real power; apparent poverty conceals genuine wealth; apparent death is the means of life. The catalogue is not mere rhetoric; it is evidence. Paul commends his ministry not by listing credentials but by displaying the shape of his life, and that shape is the shape of the crucified and risen Messiah. The rival teachers who boast in outward appearances (5:12) commend themselves by impressiveness; Paul commends himself by the pattern of Jesus' death and resurrection enacted in his own body.

Verses 11-13: The Direct Emotional Appeal

"We have spoken freely to you, Corinthians; our heart is wide open. You are not restricted by us, but you are restricted in your own affections. In return (I speak as to children) widen your hearts also" (6:11-13).

Paul shifts from catalogue to direct, emotional appeal. He addresses the Corinthians by their city name — Korinthioi — one of the rare instances in Paul's letters where he does this (cf. Galatians 3:1, "O foolish Galatians"; Philippians 4:15, "You yourselves know, Philippians"). The direct address signals intensity and urgency.

"Our mouth is open to you" (to stoma hemon aneogen pros hymas) — the verb anoigo ("to open") in the perfect tense indicates a completed action with ongoing result: Paul's mouth has been opened and remains open. He has been holding nothing back. The parresia ("boldness, freedom of speech") claimed in 3:12 is enacted: Paul speaks freely, openly, without concealment. The entire catalogue of 6:4-10 is evidence of that openness — he has hidden nothing of his suffering, his conduct, or his paradoxical condition.

"Our heart is wide open" (he kardia hemon peplatyntai) — the verb platynoo ("to widen, to broaden, to enlarge") in the perfect passive describes a heart that has been opened wide and remains so. In the LXX, the word appears in Psalm 119:32 (LXX 118:32): "I will run in the way of your commandments when you enlarge my heart (platyneis ten kardian mou)." The widened heart in the Psalm is a heart opened by God to receive and follow his instructions joyfully. Paul's heart is wide open toward the Corinthians — there is no restriction, no holding back, no narrowing of affection on his side.

"You are not restricted by us, but you are restricted in your own affections" (ou stenochooreisthe en hemin, stenochooreisthe de en tois splanchnois hymon) — the verb stenochooreo ("to restrict, to confine, to cramp, to make narrow") is the verbal form of stenochoria, the "dire straits" that appeared in 6:4 and 4:8. Paul uses the same root in two different registers: in 6:4, stenochoria described physical calamity; here it describes relational constriction. The word splanchna ("bowels, inward parts, visceral affections") is the Greek for the seat of deep emotion. Paul's point: the restriction is not on his side. He has opened his heart completely. If the relationship is cramped, the constriction is in the Corinthians' own affections. They are the ones holding back.

"In return — I speak as to children — widen your hearts also" (ten de auten antimisthian, hos teknois lego, platynthete kai hymeis) — the word antimisthia ("repayment, recompense, fair exchange") asks for reciprocity: Paul has opened his heart; the Corinthians should respond in kind. The phrase "as to children" (hos teknois) identifies the relationship: Paul is their spiritual father (cf. 1 Corinthians 4:14-15: "I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel"). A father speaks to children with directness and affection; children should respond with trust and openness. The imperative platynthete ("be widened, be enlarged") is a passive form: let your hearts be opened. This is not merely a command but an invitation to allow the process to happen — to stop restricting their affections toward Paul and toward the God whose ambassador Paul is.

Verses 14-16a: The Warning Against Incompatible Yoking

"Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers. For what partnership has righteousness with lawlessness? Or what fellowship has light with darkness? What accord has Christ with Belial? Or what portion does a believer share with an unbeliever? What agreement has the temple of God with idols?" (6:14-16a).

The connection of this section to the preceding appeal is often questioned, but within the flow of the argument it is intelligible. Paul has asked the Corinthians to open their hearts to him (6:11-13). A reason their hearts may be restricted is that they are entangled with parties, practices, or allegiances that are incompatible with the apostolic relationship and with the God Paul represents. The call to separate from incompatible partnerships is the necessary condition for the heart-widening Paul has requested. They cannot fully open themselves to Paul and to God while remaining yoked to what belongs to the opposing realm.

"Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers" (me ginesthe heterozygountes apistois) — the verb heterozygeo ("to yoke together with a different kind, to be unequally yoked") appears only here in the NT. Its background is the Torah's prohibition against mismatched yoking. Leviticus 19:19 (LXX): "You shall not let your cattle breed with a different kind (kktene sou ou katabibaseis heterozygoo)." Deuteronomy 22:10: "You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together." The principle in the Torah is that two incompatible animals should not be yoked together for a common task because their differing natures make the partnership unworkable. Paul applies this agricultural principle to the community's relationships with apistois ("unbelievers, faithless ones"). The word apistos appeared in 4:4, where "the god of this age has blinded the minds of the unbelievers (ton apiston)." The contrast is between those who have received the light of the gospel (4:6) and those who remain in the blinded condition (4:4).

Paul then supports the prohibition with five rhetorical questions, each deploying a different pair of incompatible categories. Each question expects the answer "none."

First: "What partnership has righteousness with lawlessness?" (tis gar metoches dikaiosyne kai anomia) — the word metoches ("partnership, sharing, participation") asks what common ground exists between dikaiosyne ("righteousness") and anomia ("lawlessness, violation of law"). These are antithetical. The "ministry of righteousness" (he diakonia tes dikaiosynes) was contrasted with the "ministry of condemnation" in 3:9; righteousness and lawlessness cannot share the same yoke any more than those two ministries could coexist.

Second: "What fellowship has light with darkness?" (tis de koinonia photi pros skotos) — the word koinonia ("fellowship, communion, sharing, partnership") is Paul's word for the communal bond that defines the believing community (1 Corinthians 1:9, "the fellowship of his Son"; 1 Corinthians 10:16, "the cup of blessing... is it not a participation/koinonia in the blood of Christ?"). Light and darkness are the categories Paul used in 4:6: God who said "Light out of darkness" shone in hearts. The believing community belongs to the light; the unbelieving world remains in the darkness from which God's creative act separated them.

Third: "What accord has Christ with Belial?" (tis de symphonesis Christou pros Beliar) — the word symphonesis ("agreement, harmony, accord") comes from symphoneo ("to sound together, to agree"). The name Beliar (Belial) is a Hebrew term (beliyyaal) meaning "worthlessness" or "wickedness," used throughout the Hebrew scriptures for extreme depravity: Deuteronomy 13:13 (LXX), "worthless men (huioi paranomoi)" who draw a city into idolatry; Judges 19:22, "worthless fellows" who commit the outrage at Gibeah; 1 Samuel 2:12, the sons of Eli who "knew not the LORD." In Second Temple Judaism (Jubilees, Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, Dead Sea Scrolls), Belial became a personal name for the chief adversary of God — the prince of darkness who rules the present age. The Dead Sea Scrolls (War Scroll, Community Rule) consistently pit the "sons of light" against the "sons of darkness/Belial." Paul's usage draws on this Second Temple tradition: Christ and Belial are the heads of two opposing realms, and membership in one is incompatible with membership in the other. This parallels "the god of this age" in 4:4, who blinds the minds of unbelievers.

Fourth: "What portion does a believer share with an unbeliever?" (tis de meris pisto meta apistou) — the word meris ("portion, share, lot") echoes the LXX inheritance language. In Deuteronomy 10:9, the Levites have no meris ("portion") among their brothers because "the LORD is their inheritance." In Psalm 16:5 (LXX 15:5), "The LORD is the portion (meris) of my inheritance." The pistos ("believer, faithful one") and the apistos ("unbeliever, faithless one") do not share the same portion; their inheritances are different, their lots are incompatible.

Fifth: "What agreement has the temple of God with idols?" (tis de synkatathesis nao theou meta eidolon) — the word synkatathesis ("agreement, consent, joint approval") asks whether the temple of God can coexist with idols. The question echoes Israel's central struggle throughout its history: the persistent temptation to place idols in or near YHWH's temple. Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28-29). Manasseh's abominations in the temple (2 Kings 21:4-7). Josiah's cleansing of the temple of all idolatrous objects (2 Kings 23:4-7). Ezekiel's vision of idol worship within the temple itself (Ezekiel 8:3-16). Every instance provoked divine judgment. The answer to each of these five rhetorical questions is the same: none. These pairs are irreconcilable. One must choose.

Verses 16b-18: The Catena of Covenant Promises and Commands

"For we are the temple of the living God; as God said, 'I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. Therefore go out from their midst, and be separate from them, says the Lord, and touch no unclean thing; then I will welcome you, and I will be a father to you, and you shall be sons and daughters to me, says the Lord Almighty'" (6:16b-18).

This is a composite quotation — a catena — weaving together multiple OT texts into a unified prophetic word. Paul introduces it with a foundational identification: "we are the temple of the living God" (hemeis gar naos theou esmen zontos). The phrase "the living God" (theos zon) appeared in 3:3, where the Spirit of "the living God" wrote on hearts of flesh. The title distinguishes the true God from the dead idols just mentioned. And the community itself is the temple — not the Jerusalem building but the gathered people. Paul made this identification in 1 Corinthians 3:16-17: "Do you not know that you are God's temple (naos theou) and that God's Spirit dwells in you? If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple." The identification is the same here, but the consequence drawn is different: because they are God's temple, idols have no place among them, and the community must maintain the holiness appropriate to God's dwelling place.

The catena draws from several texts, each carrying its own context into the argument:

First strand: "I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people."

This combines Leviticus 26:11-12 with Ezekiel 37:27.

Leviticus 26:11-12 (LXX): "I will set my tabernacle (skenen) among you, and my soul shall not abhor you. And I will walk among (emperipateso en) you, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people." In Leviticus 26, this promise is part of the covenant blessings that are explicitly conditional: "If you walk in my statutes and observe my commandments and do them" (26:3). The blessings include agricultural abundance (26:4-5), peace in the land (26:6), military victory (26:7-8), and the covenant's confirmation (26:9). The climax of the blessings is God's dwelling among his people (26:11-12). But Leviticus 26 also contains the covenant curses (26:14-39), which describe what happens if Israel does not obey — terror, disease, defeat, exile, and the scattering among the nations. The conditional structure is essential: the blessing of God's presence is real but is tied to Israel's covenant faithfulness. The passage concludes: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, that you should not be their slaves. And I have broken the bars of your yoke and made you walk erect" (26:13) — the yoke language connects directly to Paul's prohibition against being "unequally yoked" in 6:14. God broke the yoke of Egypt; the Corinthians should not take on a new yoke with unbelievers.

Ezekiel 37:27: "My dwelling place shall be with them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people." This is part of the restoration vision — the dry bones raised to life, the reunited kingdom, the everlasting covenant of peace (37:26). Unlike Leviticus 26, Ezekiel 37 presents the divine dwelling as part of God's unconditional restoration of Israel after judgment and exile. But even in Ezekiel, the promise includes: "They shall walk in my rules and be careful to obey my statutes" (37:24). The divine presence and obedient life are inseparable.

Paul is arguing that the believing community has become the location of God's dwelling. The tabernacle/temple presence, which was localized in a building in Jerusalem, is now realized in the community of believers. This is why the question of verse 16a ("What agreement has the temple of God with idols?") is so urgent: if they are God's dwelling place, then introducing incompatible elements — idolatrous practices, partnerships with unbelievers that compromise faithfulness — is the equivalent of placing idols in the temple, the very thing that provoked God's wrath throughout Israel's history. Ezekiel 8-11, in which God's glory departed from the temple because of the abominations committed within it, stands as the negative precedent. If the community is the temple and the community tolerates what is incompatible with God's presence, the precedent is not encouraging.

Second strand: "Therefore go out from their midst, and be separate from them, says the Lord, and touch no unclean thing."

This draws primarily from Isaiah 52:11 (LXX): "Depart, depart, go out from there (exelthate exelthate exelthate ekeithen); touch no unclean thing (akathartou me haptesthe); go out from the midst of her (exelthate ek mesou autes); be separate (aphoristhete), you who bear the vessels of the LORD." In Isaiah 52, the command is addressed to the exiles leaving Babylon. The "unclean thing" is the contamination of Babylon's idolatrous culture. The command to "go out" is the command of the second exodus — the departure from Babylonian captivity, parallel to the departure from Egypt. Isaiah 52:11 is addressed specifically to "you who bear the vessels of the LORD" — the priests and Levites responsible for the sacred temple vessels. The holiness demanded is priestly holiness, the holiness required of those who handle sacred things.

Paul applies this to the Corinthians: they must come out from the contamination of the surrounding pagan culture, just as Israel came out from Babylon. As the temple of the living God (6:16b), they are the ones who "bear the vessels of the LORD" — they themselves are the sacred vessels. The clay vessels of 4:7 are now, in a different register, the temple vessels of Isaiah 52:11. The holiness required is not optional; it is demanded by the nature of what they carry and what they are.

"Then I will welcome you" (kago eisdexomai hymas) — the verb eisdechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to take in") describes God's reception of his people, conditional upon their separation from uncleanness. The logic is not arbitrary exclusion but the logic of holiness: God's presence and impurity cannot coexist, as the entire tabernacle/temple system demonstrated. The separation comes first; the welcome follows.

Third strand: "And I will be a father to you, and you shall be sons and daughters to me, says the Lord Almighty."

This draws primarily from 2 Samuel 7:14 (LXX), God's promise to David regarding his descendant: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (ego esomai auto eis patera, kai autos estai moi eis huion). The Davidic covenant promise was addressed to the royal son — Solomon in the immediate context, but understood in the prophetic tradition as ultimately pointing to the messianic king. Paul makes two significant interpretive moves.

First, he expands the promise from the singular "son" (2 Samuel 7:14) to the plural "sons and daughters" (huious kai thygateras). The entire community, not just a single royal heir, is included in the Davidic covenant promise. This expansion may draw also from Isaiah 43:6 (LXX): "Bring my sons from afar and my daughters from the ends of the earth." The addition of "daughters" alongside "sons" makes the inclusion explicit: the covenant family is not restricted by gender.

Second, the placement of this promise at the climax of the catena connects the Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7) to the tabernacle/dwelling covenant (Leviticus 26), the restoration covenant (Ezekiel 37), and the exodus-from-Babylon command (Isaiah 52). Paul weaves these disparate covenantal strands into a single fabric: the God who promised to dwell among his people, who commanded separation from contamination, and who pledged a father-son relationship with David's heir — this God now extends all of these promises to the believing community. They are the temple where God dwells, the people who must separate from uncleanness, and the sons and daughters of the Lord Almighty.

The title "Lord Almighty" (kyrios pantokrator) is the LXX rendering of YHWH tsebaot ("the LORD of hosts"), used throughout the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi). The title emphasizes God's sovereign power over all things and lends divine authority to the composite quotation. It is the title of the God who commands armies, who governs nations, who cannot be resisted — and this God claims these people as his own children.

It is worth noting that 2 Samuel 7:14 continues, in its original context, with a discipline clause: "When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him (paideuso auton) with the rod of men, with the stripes of the sons of men, but my steadfast love will not depart from him" (7:14b-15a). The verb paideuo ("to discipline") is the same verb Paul used in 6:9: "as punished/disciplined (paideuomenoi), and yet not killed." The fatherly discipline described in 2 Samuel 7:14 — painful but not destructive, corrective but not rejecting — is what Paul experiences in 6:9. The God who promises to be father (6:18) is the same God who disciplines as a father (6:9, Psalm 118:18).

7:1 as the Conclusion of the Argument

"Since we have these promises, beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and spirit, bringing holiness to completion in the fear of God" (7:1).

Although this verse falls in chapter 7, it is the conclusion of the argument begun in 6:14. The "promises" (epangelias) are the divine words just quoted: God dwelling among them, being their God, welcoming them, being their father, making them sons and daughters. These promises carry obligations.

"Let us cleanse ourselves" (katharisomen heautous) — the verb katharizo ("to cleanse, to purify, to make clean") is the word used for ritual and moral purification throughout the LXX. Leviticus uses it extensively for the cleansing of persons, garments, and houses from impurity. Psalm 51:2 (LXX 50:4): "Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me (katharison me) from my sin." Isaiah 52:11, just quoted in 6:17, commanded the bearers of the LORD's vessels to "purify yourselves" (katharisthete). The cleansing Paul calls for is the practical application of the separation commanded in 6:17.

"From every defilement of body and spirit" (apo pantos molysmou sarkos kai pneumatos) — the word molysmos ("defilement, contamination, stain") appears only here in Paul's letters. It describes pollution, staining, contamination. The defilement covers the whole person: sarx ("flesh, body") and pneuma ("spirit"). The unclean thing of 6:17 produces defilement; the cleansing of 7:1 removes it.

"Bringing holiness to completion in the fear of God" (epitelountes hagiosynen en phobo theou) — the verb epiteleo ("to complete, to bring to fulfillment, to finish, to perfect") indicates that holiness (hagiosyne) is a process that must be brought to its full completion. It is not finished at the moment of conversion or at the reception of the Spirit; it must be actively perfected, completed, brought to its intended end. The word hagiosyne ("holiness, sanctification") appeared in 1:12, where Paul described his conduct as governed by "holiness and godly sincerity" (hagiosyne kai eilikrineia tou theou). The holiness that characterizes Paul's ministry is also required of the community.

The phrase "in the fear of God" (en phobo theou) echoes 5:11: "knowing the fear of the Lord (ton phobon tou kyriou), we persuade others." That verse followed the judgment-seat statement of 5:10. The "fear" is not terror but reverent awareness that God sees, evaluates, and holds accountable. The judgment seat of Christ (5:10) stands behind the exhortation. Holiness is pursued not merely because it is desirable but because God will assess what has been done in the body.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

2 Corinthians 6 is not a self-contained unit but a continuation and application of the argument that began in 5:11. Paul has described the ministry of reconciliation (5:18-21) and now presses its implications on the Corinthians in two movements.

First movement (6:1-10): The urgency of the present moment and the evidence of authentic ministry. Paul appeals to the Corinthians not to receive God's grace emptily — not to let the reconciliation remain merely formal (6:1). The appeal is grounded in Isaiah 49:8: the present moment is the prophetically announced day of salvation, the favorable time God promised through the Servant passages (6:2). This makes the appeal urgent. Paul then demonstrates the authenticity of the ministry that delivers this grace through an extensive three-part catalogue: sufferings endured (6:4b-5), virtues displayed (6:6-7a), and paradoxes embodied (6:8-10). The catalogue functions as Paul's credentials — not letters of recommendation or rhetorical skill but the shape of a life that enacts the death-and-resurrection pattern of Christ described in 4:10-12. The paradoxes of 6:8-10 (dying yet living, poor yet enriching, having nothing yet possessing everything) are the practical expression of the clay-vessel theology of 4:7 and the reconciliation theology of 5:14-21.

Second movement (6:11-7:1): The personal appeal and the demand for separation. Paul turns from defense to plea. He has opened his heart completely to the Corinthians; now he asks them to reciprocate (6:11-13). But reciprocation requires more than emotional warmth; it requires a fundamental reorientation of commitments. The Corinthians cannot open their hearts fully to Paul — and to the God Paul represents — while remaining yoked to partners, practices, and allegiances that are incompatible with the God who dwells among them (6:14-16a). The incompatibility is grounded in five antithetical pairs — righteousness/lawlessness, light/darkness, Christ/Belial, believer/unbeliever, temple/idols — each expressing the absolute either/or of covenant loyalty. The catena of OT quotations (6:16b-18) provides the scriptural basis: God has promised to dwell among his people, to be their God, to be father to them — but these promises are inseparable from the command to come out, be separate, and touch nothing unclean. The promises and the commands are a single package. The covenant relationship that makes them God's sons and daughters also requires them to maintain the holiness appropriate to God's temple. 7:1 draws the conclusion: possessing these promises, they must cleanse themselves and bring holiness to completion.

Several threads connect this chapter to the rest of the letter and to 1 Corinthians:

The "grace in vain" warning (6:1) and the conditionality of ongoing faithfulness. The warning that grace can be received emptily connects to 1 Corinthians 15:2 (believing "in vain" unless one holds fast), to 2 Corinthians 5:10 (the judgment seat of Christ before which each gives account), and to the holiness exhortation of 6:14-7:1. Throughout this letter, Paul presents salvation as real, God-initiated, and grace-powered — but also as requiring ongoing faithfulness, active moral response, and the deliberate avoidance of incompatible allegiances. The grace is genuinely given; it can also be genuinely wasted. Paul's language does not support a reading in which the warning is merely hypothetical or pedagogical. He urges them not to receive grace in vain because that is a real possibility, and the consequence — receiving God's grace and having it come to nothing — is a real danger.

The Isaiah 49 quotation (6:2) and the Servant framework. By quoting Isaiah 49:8, Paul places his ministry within the framework of the Isaianic Servant. The verbal link between kenos in 6:1 and kenon in Isaiah 49:4 ties the two passages together. The Servant who feared his labor was kenos but was commissioned for a greater purpose — to be "a light for the nations" and "a covenant to the people" — prefigures Paul's own experience: suffering that appears fruitless but serves God's larger purpose of reconciling the world (5:19). The "day of salvation" is not a future eschatological event in Paul's usage here but the present period of the gospel's proclamation, a period that demands response.

The catalogue of paradoxes (6:8-10) and the clay-vessel theology (4:7-12). The paradoxes of 6:8-10 are the concrete catalogue of what 4:7-12 described theologically. The treasure in clay vessels (4:7) manifests as dying yet living, poor yet enriching, having nothing yet possessing everything (6:9-10). The death of Jesus carried in the body (4:10) manifests as beatings, imprisonments, riots (6:5). The life of Jesus displayed in mortal flesh (4:11) manifests as the Spirit, genuine love, truthful speech, and the power of God (6:6-7). Chapter 6 does not introduce new theology; it provides the concrete evidence for what chapter 4 argued in principle.

The temple identification (6:16) and 1 Corinthians 3:16-17. Paul identified the Corinthian community as God's temple in 1 Corinthians 3:16-17, with the warning that "if anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple." In 1 Corinthians 6:19, the individual believer's body was called "a temple of the Holy Spirit." Here in 2 Corinthians 6:16, the community is again called the temple of the living God, and the consequence is the same: the temple must be kept free of contamination. Idols and God's temple cannot coexist. The Corinthians' situation — living in a city saturated with pagan worship, social life intertwined with temple meals (1 Corinthians 8-10), business partnerships entangled with idolatrous practices — made this not abstract but daily and practical. The idol-meat controversy of 1 Corinthians 8-10 is the specific background against which this general principle operates.

The Belial reference (6:15) and the god of this age (4:4). The naming of Belial as Christ's opposite draws on the same two-realm framework as "the god of this age" in 4:4. Both expressions divide reality into two opposing domains: one ruled by Christ, the other by the adversary. Believers belong to Christ's domain; yoking themselves to what belongs to Belial's domain is a fundamental confusion of identity and allegiance. The five rhetorical questions in 6:14-16a spell out the impossibility of dual citizenship in these two realms.

The "sons and daughters" promise (6:18) and the new creation (5:17). The composite quotation's expansion of the Davidic covenant promise to include the whole community as God's sons and daughters corresponds to the "new creation" language of 5:17. If anyone is in Christ — new creation. The old has passed away; the new has come. Part of what it means to be the "new creation" is to be constituted as God's family — sons and daughters of the Lord Almighty. But this family identity carries obligations: cleansing, holiness, separation from what is incompatible. The new creation is not merely a status conferred but a life to be lived, and 7:1 makes explicit that this life requires active pursuit of holiness "to completion."

The conditional structure of the Leviticus 26 promise. The original context of "I will walk among you and be your God and you shall be my people" (Leviticus 26:11-12) is explicitly conditional: "If you walk in my statutes and observe my commandments and do them" (26:3). The blessings of divine presence follow obedience; the curses of divine withdrawal follow disobedience (26:14-39). Paul quotes the promise without the conditional introduction, but the separation commands of 6:17 function as the new-covenant equivalent of the conditional: the promise of divine presence is real, but it is accompanied by the demand for holiness. The parallel is not exact — Paul does not quote the Leviticus curses — but the structure is the same: God's dwelling among his people and the community's covenant faithfulness are bound together. Furthermore, Leviticus 26:13 speaks of God breaking the bars of the yoke of Egyptian slavery and making Israel "walk erect." Paul's command not to take on a new yoke (heterozygountes, 6:14) echoes this: the God who freed them from one yoke does not intend them to submit to another.

Pronoun tracking through the chapter. "We" in 6:1-10 refers primarily to Paul and his co-workers — the apostolic team that delivers the message of reconciliation. The catalogues describe Paul's experience as an apostle. In 6:11-13, the pronouns shift to direct address: "you, Corinthians" (hymeis) are the ones whose hearts are restricted; "our" (hemon) hearts are the ones wide open. In 6:14-18, the second person plural imperatives are addressed to the Corinthian community: "do not be yoked," "go out," "be separate," "touch no unclean thing." But in 7:1, Paul includes himself: "let us cleanse ourselves" (katharisomen heautous) — the first person plural includes Paul alongside the Corinthians. The holiness imperative is not something Paul imposes on others from above; it is something he shares with them. The apostle and the community stand together under the same obligations, the same promises, and the same accountability before the judgment seat of Christ.

The coherence of 6:14-7:1 within the letter's argument. The abrupt shift from the emotional appeal (6:11-13) to the separation warning (6:14-18) has led some to treat 6:14-7:1 as an interpolation — perhaps a fragment of the "previous letter" mentioned in 1 Corinthians 5:9, which dealt with not associating with sexually immoral people. However, within the letter's argument, the section is intelligible in its present position. Paul has been defending his ministry since chapter 3. He has demonstrated its glory (chapter 3), its cost (chapter 4), its theological ground in reconciliation (chapter 5), and its evidence in his own suffering and conduct (6:1-10). He has opened his heart and asked the Corinthians to open theirs (6:11-13). The separation warning answers why their hearts may be closed: they are divided in their loyalties, entangled with the very system that opposes the gospel. Until they disentangle, their hearts cannot fully open to Paul or to the God he represents. The resumption of the appeal in 7:2 ("Make room in your hearts for us") is not the return to an interrupted thought but the completion of the same thought: separate from what is incompatible (6:14-7:1), and then you will be able to make room for us (7:2). The logic runs: open hearts (6:11-13) require clean hearts (6:14-7:1), which produce receptive hearts (7:2-4).
