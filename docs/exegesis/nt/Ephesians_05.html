Ephesians 5 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Ephesians 4): Chapter 4 opened with "I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, urge you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (4:1). The word oun ("therefore") drew the ethical consequence from everything established in chapters 1-3 — the election, the mystery of gentile inclusion, the prayer for comprehension. The first practical implication was unity: humility, gentleness, patience, bearing with one another in love, "eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (4:3). Seven unities were enumerated — one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all (4:4-6) — each rooted in the theological exposition of chapters 1-3. Christ's gifts to the community (apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers, 4:11) were given "for the equipping of the saints for the work of ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ" (4:12), until the community reaches "the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ" (4:13).

At 4:17, the author turned from the community's internal structure to its ethical character: "Now this I say and testify in the Lord, that you must no longer walk as the gentiles do, in the futility of their minds" (4:17). The verb peripateo ("to walk") reappears — the same verb from 2:2 (formerly "you walked" in trespasses) and 2:10 ("walk" in good works) and 4:1 ("walk worthy"). The gentiles' former way of life was characterized by darkened understanding, alienation from the life of God, ignorance, hardness of heart, callousness, sensuality, greediness, and every kind of impurity (4:17-19). The audience, by contrast, "learned Christ" and were "taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus" (4:20-21). They were instructed to "put off the old self" (ton palaion anthropon, 4:22) — the former manner of life corrupted by deceitful desires — and to "put on the new self" (ton kainon anthropon, 4:24), "created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness." The word kainon ("new") and the verb ktisthenta ("created") echo 2:15, where Christ created "one new humanity" (hena kainon anthropon). The "new self" the individual puts on is participation in the "one new humanity" Christ created.

From 4:25 through 4:32, the author issued a series of specific ethical commands that flow from putting on the new self: put away falsehood and speak truth (4:25, quoting Zechariah 8:16); be angry but do not sin, and do not let the sun go down on your anger, giving no opportunity to the devil (4:26-27); the thief must no longer steal but must labor to share with those in need (4:28); let no corrupt talk come out of your mouths, but only what builds up and gives grace to those who hear (4:29); do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, with whom you were sealed for the day of redemption (4:30); put away all bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, slander, and malice (4:31); and be kind, tenderhearted, forgiving one another "as God in Christ forgave you" (4:32).

The final clause of chapter 4 — "as God in Christ forgave you" (kathos kai ho theos en Christo echarisato hymin) — is the immediate launching point for chapter 5. The divine act of forgiveness becomes the model for the community's conduct, and the word kathos ("just as, even as") establishes the pattern of imitation that 5:1-2 will make explicit.

What the author argues immediately after (Ephesians 6:1-9): The household code that begins at 5:22 with the instruction to wives continues into 6:1-4 with instructions to children and parents, and 6:5-9 with instructions to slaves and masters. The pattern of reciprocal obligation persists: children obey parents "in the Lord" (6:1) because this is right, and the command "Honor your father and mother" (Exodus 20:12/Deuteronomy 5:16) is cited as "the first commandment with a promise" (6:2). Fathers are told not to provoke their children to anger but to bring them up "in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (6:4). Slaves are to obey their earthly masters "with fear and trembling, with a sincere heart, as to Christ" (6:5), and masters are to treat their slaves justly, "knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him" (6:9). The "as to Christ" and "as to the Lord" language pervades all three household relationships (wife-husband, child-parent, slave-master), making Christ the reference point for every relationship within the community.

At 6:10, the letter shifts to its final section: the call to "be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his might" (6:10), the armor of God (6:11-17), and the call to prayer (6:18-20). The household code thus constitutes the practical center of the letter's ethical section, and chapter 5 contains both the conclusion of the general ethical exhortation (5:1-21) and the beginning of the household code (5:22-33).

How chapter 5 connects these: Ephesians 4:17-32 drew the contrast between the old way of life (the "old self" characterized by the gentile manner) and the new (the "new self" created in righteousness and holiness), and issued specific commands about speech, anger, theft, and forgiveness. Chapter 5 continues this ethical instruction without interruption. The oun ("therefore") of 5:1 draws the inference directly from 4:32: because God forgave you in Christ, therefore imitate God. The "walk" language continues: walk in love (5:2), walk as children of light (5:8), walk carefully (5:15). The contrast between the community's present identity and the practices of "the sons of disobedience" (5:6) — the same phrase used in 2:2 for those under the ruler of the air — intensifies the warnings. The chapter then transitions from general ethical instruction to the household code (5:22-33), where the author uses the husband-wife relationship as the vehicle for a sustained reflection on Christ's relationship to the church. The Christ-church analogy in 5:25-27 reaches back to the very beginning of the letter: the purpose of Christ's self-giving is that the church might be "holy and blameless" (hagia kai amomos, 5:27) — the exact words used in 1:4 for the purpose of election. The chapter thus connects the ethical exhortation of chapter 4 to the household code of chapter 6, while simultaneously linking the practical conduct of daily life back to the letter's foundational theological claims about election, holiness, and the mystery of Christ's union with his people.

Verses 1-2: The Pattern of Imitation — Walking in Love

"Therefore be imitators of God, as beloved children" (5:1).

The word oun ("therefore") connects this verse directly to 4:32: "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you. Therefore be imitators of God." The logic is: God forgave you; therefore imitate God. The pattern of divine action becomes the template for human conduct. The imperative ginesthe ("become, be") is present tense, indicating an ongoing process — not a one-time act but a continuing manner of life.

The word mimetai ("imitators") comes from mimeomai ("to imitate, to follow the example of"). Paul used the word in 1 Corinthians 4:16 ("I urge you, be imitators of me"), 1 Corinthians 11:1 ("Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ"), and 1 Thessalonians 1:6 ("you became imitators of us and of the Lord"). In those contexts, imitation was of Paul, or of Paul as he imitates Christ. Here the object of imitation is God himself (tou theou). The standard is raised to its highest possible level: the community is not merely to follow an apostle's example but to imitate God's own character and conduct.

The qualifying phrase hos tekna agapeta ("as beloved children") supplies the relational ground. The word tekna ("children") identifies the community's relationship to God as filial — they are his children, and children imitate their parents. The adjective agapeta ("beloved, dearly loved") echoes the language of 1:6, where grace was bestowed "in the Beloved" (en to egapemeno) — Christ as the Beloved Son. The community, as God's beloved children, shares in the love that characterizes the Beloved Son. In the LXX, Israel is called God's child: "Israel is my firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22); "When Israel was a child, I loved him" (Hosea 11:1). The filial relationship that grounds the call to imitation is rooted in Israel's own experience as God's son, now extended to the community of Jews and gentiles together through adoption (1:5).

"And walk in love, as Christ loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (5:2).

The verb peripateite ("walk") continues the letter's dominant ethical metaphor. In 2:2, the recipients formerly "walked" according to the course of this world. In 2:10, they were created for good works "that we should walk in them." In 4:1, they were urged to "walk worthy of the calling." In 4:17, they were told to "no longer walk as the gentiles." Now: peripateite en agape — "walk in love." Each use of peripateo in the letter defines a different sphere of walking; the trajectory moves from the old walk (in sin) through the transitional walk (worthy of the calling, no longer as gentiles) to the defining quality of the new walk: love.

The standard for this love-walk is stated: kathos kai ho Christos egapesen hemas — "as Christ also loved us." The word kathos ("just as, even as") establishes a correspondence — the community's love is to be modeled on Christ's love. The verb egapesen (aorist active of agapao) is past tense, pointing to a definite act: Christ loved. The content of that love is immediately specified: kai paredoken heauton hyper hemon — "and gave himself up on our behalf." The verb paradidomi ("to hand over, to deliver up, to give over") is the standard term for Christ's being delivered to death: in Romans 4:25, Christ "was delivered up (paredothe) for our trespasses"; in Romans 8:32, God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up (paredoken) for us all"; in Galatians 2:20, the Son of God "loved me and gave himself (paradontos heauton) for me." The reflexive heauton ("himself") emphasizes that Christ was both the subject and the object — he was not merely delivered by another but gave himself.

The prepositional phrase hyper hemon ("on behalf of us, for our sake") shifts the pronoun to "us" — first person plural, inclusive of the author and the audience, the entire community. Christ's self-giving was for the community as a whole.

The apposition prosphoran kai thysian to theo eis osmen euodias ("an offering and sacrifice to God for a fragrant aroma") places Christ's death in sacrificial categories. The word prosphora ("offering, that which is brought to the altar") is the general term for a presentation to God. The word thysia ("sacrifice, victim offered") specifies a blood sacrifice. Together they describe Christ's death as a voluntary presentation of himself as a sacrificial victim. The phrase eis osmen euodias ("for a fragrant aroma" or "as a sweet-smelling fragrance") is drawn directly from the LXX's sacrificial vocabulary. In Genesis 8:21, after Noah's sacrifice, "the LORD smelled the pleasing aroma" (LXX: osphranethe kyrios osmen euodias). In Exodus 29:18, the burnt offering is described as "a pleasing aroma (osmen euodias), a food offering to the LORD." In Leviticus 1:9, 13, 17, the same formula recurs: the burnt offering is "a food offering with a pleasing aroma (osmen euodias) to the LORD." The phrase appears dozens of times in Leviticus and Numbers for sacrifices that are acceptable and pleasing to God. The author identifies Christ's self-offering with the sacrificial system of the Torah: what the animal sacrifices pointed toward, Christ fulfilled. His death was the offering that God received as genuinely and completely pleasing — the ultimate "fragrant aroma" that the Levitical sacrifices anticipated.

The verse thus establishes the ethical foundation for everything that follows in the chapter: the community is to walk in love, and the definition of love is Christ's self-giving sacrifice. Love is not defined as sentiment or emotion but as costly, voluntary self-giving on behalf of others.

Verses 3-7: The Warning — What Has No Place in the Community

"But sexual immorality and all impurity or covetousness must not even be named among you, as is proper among saints" (5:3).

The conjunction de ("but") marks a contrast. The community's walk in love (v. 2) is now distinguished from behaviors that are incompatible with it. Three categories are named:

First: porneia ("sexual immorality, fornication, illicit sexual conduct"). In the LXX, porneia covers a wide range of sexual violations: prostitution (Genesis 38:24), idolatrous unfaithfulness as metaphor (Hosea 1:2, 2:4; Ezekiel 16:15-22), and general sexual immorality. In the apostolic decree of Acts 15:20, 29, porneia was one of the requirements placed on gentile believers. In 1 Corinthians 5-6, Paul addressed porneia in the community at length, treating it as fundamentally incompatible with membership in the body of Christ: "Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? Shall I then take the members of Christ and make them members of a prostitute?" (1 Corinthians 6:15). For gentile converts coming from Greco-Roman culture, where sexual mores were considerably more permissive than Jewish standards, the prohibition of porneia was a significant boundary marker of the new identity.

Second: pasa akatharsia ("all impurity, every kind of uncleanness"). The word akatharsia ("impurity, uncleanness, moral defilement") appeared in 4:19, where the former gentile walk was described as living "in every kind of impurity" (en pase akatharsia). The word pasa ("all, every") emphasizes totality: not some impurity but all of it.

Third: pleonexia ("covetousness, greediness, insatiable desire for more"). The word comes from pleon ("more") + echo ("to have") — the desire to have more than one's share, to grasp beyond what is given. In Colossians 3:5, Paul identified pleonexia as idolatry: "covetousness, which is idolatry." The equation of greed with idolatry has roots in the Hebrew prophets' denunciation of those who serve wealth rather than God (cf. Isaiah 56:11; Jeremiah 6:13, 8:10; Ezekiel 33:31; Micah 2:2).

The phrase mede onomazesthoo en hymin ("must not even be named among you") uses the present passive imperative of onomazo ("to name, to mention, to speak of"). The force is not merely prohibition of the acts but prohibition of their presence in the community's conversation and culture — these things should be so absent that they are not even spoken of as possibilities. The standard is kathos prepei hagiois ("as is fitting for saints, as is proper among holy ones"). The word prepei ("it is fitting, it is proper, it is appropriate") appeals to the community's identity: because you are hagioi ("saints, holy ones, set-apart ones" — the same term from 1:1, 1:15, 1:18, 2:19, 3:8, 3:18), certain behaviors are simply inappropriate. The argument is from identity: you are holy, therefore these things have no place among you.

"Let there be no filthiness nor foolish talk nor crude joking, which are out of place, but instead let there be thanksgiving" (5:4).

Three additional behaviors are named, now concerning speech: aischrotees ("filthiness, obscenity, shameful speech"), morologia ("foolish talk, silly speech") — a compound of moros ("foolish, dull") and logos ("word, speech") — and eutrapelia ("crude joking, coarse jesting, ribaldry"). The word eutrapelia is interesting; in classical Greek (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.8), it described the virtue of wit, the ability to turn a conversation cleverly. But by the first century it had acquired a pejorative sense: humor that turns vulgar, wit that serves indecency rather than wisdom. The relativizing phrase ha ouk aneken ("which are not fitting, which are out of place") uses the imperfect of aneko ("to be proper, to come up to a standard") — these things do not rise to the standard of what is appropriate for the community.

The alternative: alla mallon eucharistia ("but rather thanksgiving"). The word eucharistia ("thanksgiving, gratitude, giving of thanks") replaces the corrupt speech. Where filthy talk, foolish speech, and crude humor occupied the mouth, thanksgiving is to take their place. The contrast is between speech that degrades and speech that ascends — thanksgiving directs the mouth toward God rather than toward the gutter. This is not a ban on humor but a redirection of speech: the community's talk should be characterized by gratitude, not by obscenity.

"For you may be sure of this, that every sexually immoral or impure person, or one who is covetous (that is, an idolater), has no inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God" (5:5).

The phrase touto gar iste ginoosikontes ("for this you know, knowing") combines the present indicative iste ("you know") with the present participle ginoskontes ("knowing, recognizing"), creating emphasis: you know this, and you should recognize it clearly. The knowledge is not new information but a restatement of something the audience already understands.

Three types of persons are named, corresponding to the three vices of verse 3. First: pas pornos ("every sexually immoral person") — the substantive adjective from porneia. Second: akathartos ("an impure person") — the adjective corresponding to akatharsia. Third: pleonektes ("a covetous person, a greedy person") — the substantive from pleonexia. The third is given an appositive definition: ho estin eidololatres ("who is an idolater"). The word eidololatres ("idolater") is a compound: eidolon ("image, idol") + latres ("worshipper, servant"). The equation of pleonexia with eidolatria is explicit and deliberate: the greedy person is an idolater because the object of their insatiable desire has displaced God as the object of their devotion. The greedy person worships the thing desired rather than the God who gives.

The verdict: ouk echei kleronmian en te basileia tou Christou kai theou ("has no inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God"). The word kleronomia ("inheritance") has been a central term in the letter. In 1:11, the "we" group received an inheritance (eklerothemen). In 1:14, the Spirit is the guarantee of "our inheritance" (tes kleronomias hemon). In 1:18, Paul prayed that the recipients would know "the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints." The inheritance is what God has promised to his people — the full realization of the blessings declared in the berakah of 1:3-14. The statement that the pornos, akathartos, and pleonektes "has no inheritance" in the kingdom is a disinheritance warning: those who practice these things are excluded from what God has promised.

The phrase en te basileia tou Christou kai theou ("in the kingdom of Christ and God") combines the kingdom of Christ and the kingdom of God as a single reality. The word basileia ("kingdom, reign, royal rule") in the LXX describes God's sovereign rule: "The LORD has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingdom rules over all" (Psalm 103:19 [LXX 102:19]). In the Gospels, the "kingdom of God" (or "kingdom of heaven" in Matthew) is the central announcement of Jesus' preaching (Mark 1:15). That the kingdom is here attributed to both Christ and God reflects the letter's sustained argument that God's purposes are administered through Christ (1:10, 1:20-22). The single article (te) governing both "Christ" and "God" may indicate that the phrase treats the kingdom as one: the reign of Christ is the reign of God.

The warning is stated in the present tense: ouk echei ("does not have," present active of echo). The person characterized by these behaviors does not presently possess the inheritance. This is not merely a future exclusion but a present dispossession — the person engaged in these practices is, right now, outside the inheritance.

A first-century hearer familiar with Israel's scriptures would hear echoes of the covenant's conditionality. In the LXX, the inheritance of the land was contingent on covenant faithfulness: "if you walk in my statutes and observe my commandments and do them... but if you will not listen to me and will not do all these commandments... I will set my face against you" (Leviticus 26:3, 14-15, 17). The prophets repeatedly warned that the inheritance could be forfeited through idolatry and immorality (Jeremiah 7:3-15; Ezekiel 33:24-29). The author of Ephesians applies the same logic to the new community: the inheritance declared in 1:11-14 can be forfeited by those whose conduct is characterized by the three named vices.

"Let no one deceive you with empty words, for because of these things the wrath of God comes upon the sons of disobedience" (5:6).

The imperative medeis hymas apatatoo ("let no one deceive you") uses the present active imperative of apatao ("to deceive, to cheat, to mislead"). The word apatao appeared in 4:22 for the "deceitful desires" (tas epithymias tes apates) that corrupt the old self. The deception the author warns against is likely the claim that the behaviors listed in verses 3-5 are permissible — perhaps that grace covers them, or that they are merely physical acts without spiritual consequence, or that the prohibition of porneia does not apply to those who are "in Christ." The phrase kenois logois ("with empty words") describes the content of the deception: the words have no substance, no truth, no weight. They are hollow — persuasive in form but void of reality.

The reason the deception must be resisted: dia tauta gar erchetai he orge tou theou epi tous huious tes apeitheias ("for because of these things the wrath of God comes upon the sons of disobedience"). The phrase dia tauta ("because of these things") refers to the porneia, akatharsia, and pleonexia of verses 3-5. These are not harmless behaviors; they provoke the orge tou theou ("the wrath of God"). The word orge ("wrath, anger, settled indignation") is the same word from 2:3, where both Jews and gentiles were "children of wrath" (tekna orges) by nature. The wrath of God is his judicial response to human rebellion — not caprice but righteous reaction to covenant violation (cf. Romans 1:18, "the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men").

The objects of this wrath are tous huious tes apeitheias ("the sons of disobedience"). This exact phrase appeared in 2:2: "the spirit that is now at work in the sons of disobedience" (en tois huiois tes apeitheias). In chapter 2, the "sons of disobedience" were the category from which the recipients were rescued — they formerly walked among them, under the governance of the ruler of the air. The reappearance of this exact phrase creates a direct connection between the current warning and the community's past: you were once among the sons of disobedience (2:2); God's wrath comes upon the sons of disobedience (5:6); therefore do not go back to what characterized them.

"Therefore do not become partners with them" (5:7).

The imperative me oun ginesthe symmetochoi auton ("therefore do not become co-sharers with them") draws the conclusion (oun, "therefore"). The word symmetochoi ("co-sharers, co-participants, partners") is one of the syn- ("together with") compounds that have structured the entire letter. In 3:6, the gentiles are described as symmetocha tes epangelias ("co-sharers of the promise") — this was the very content of the mystery. The word is now used negatively: do not become co-sharers with the sons of disobedience. The ironic parallel is pointed — the gentiles have become co-sharers of the promise (3:6), and they must not now become co-sharers with those who stand under wrath. The verb ginesthe ("become") is present middle/passive imperative with the negative me: do not continue becoming, do not let yourselves become. The implication is that partnership with the sons of disobedience is a possibility that must be actively resisted. The warning assumes that the community could, in fact, become partners in these practices.

The pronoun auton ("with them") distinguishes the community from "them" — the sons of disobedience. The community is not currently among them (as they once were, 2:2), but the command not to become partners assumes proximity and temptation. The community lives in the world alongside those whose conduct provokes God's wrath, and the danger is assimilation.

Verses 8-14: The Identity Argument — Darkness, Light, and Exposure

"For at one time you were darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light" (5:8).

The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the ground of the preceding warning. The statement is remarkable for its directness: the author does not say "you were in darkness" but "you were darkness" (ete gar pote skotos). The recipients were not merely surrounded by darkness or affected by it; they were, in their very being, darkness. The word skotos ("darkness") in the LXX describes the realm opposed to God's purposes: "darkness was upon the face of the deep" (Genesis 1:2); "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:2 [LXX 9:1]); "I form light and create darkness" (Isaiah 45:7). The identification of the recipients with darkness itself — not merely with a dark environment — matches the radicality of 2:1, where they were not merely sick or struggling but dead.

The contrast is equally radical: nyn de phos en kyrio ("but now you are light in the Lord"). The temporal marker pote... nyn ("once... but now") is the same structure used in 2:2-5 (you once walked... but God) and 2:13 ("you who were once far off... but now have been brought near"). The transformation is total: what was darkness is now light. The location of this new identity is en kyrio ("in the Lord") — the light they are exists within the sphere of the Lord's presence and authority. Outside the Lord, they are still darkness; in the Lord, they are light. The phrase echoes Isaiah 60:1-3 (LXX): "Arise, shine, for your light has come, and the glory of the LORD has risen upon you. For behold, darkness shall cover the earth, and thick darkness the peoples; but the LORD will arise upon you, and his glory will be seen upon you. And nations shall come to your light." In Isaiah, the light is God's own glory shining upon Israel, and the nations come to that light. In Ephesians, the community — including the gentiles who have been brought near — has become that light.

The imperative hos tekna photos peripateite ("walk as children of light") returns to the letter's central ethical metaphor. The community's walk is to be consistent with their identity: if you are light, walk as light's children. The Semitic idiom "children of light" (tekna photos) designates people who belong to light, who are characterized by light, whose origin and nature is light. The phrase appears in the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QS 1:9-10, 3:13-4:26, the "Rule of the Community"), where the "sons of light" are distinguished from the "sons of darkness" in a cosmic dualism. In 1 Thessalonians 5:5, Paul used the same language: "you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness." The dualistic language is not philosophical abstraction but ethical description: there are two ways to live, two domains, two identities, and the community has been transferred from one to the other.

"(For the fruit of light is found in all that is good and right and true)" (5:9).

The parenthetical statement ho gar karpos tou photos ("for the fruit of light") identifies what light produces when it governs a life. The word karpos ("fruit, produce, result") is the standard metaphor for the natural product of a living thing: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16, 20); "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace" (Galatians 5:22). The fruit of light consists in three qualities:

First: agathosyne ("goodness, generosity, moral excellence"). The word describes active goodness — not merely the absence of evil but the positive quality of doing good. In the LXX, the noun is rare, but the adjective agathos ("good") pervades the description of what God is and what God's people are to embody.

Second: dikaiosyne ("righteousness, justice, right conduct"). In the LXX, dikaiosyne translates the Hebrew tsedaqah — the quality of being in right relationship with God and with others, of acting justly and faithfully within the covenant. The word appeared in 4:24, where the "new self" was described as "created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (en dikaiosyne kai hosioteti tes aletheias).

Third: aletheia ("truth, truthfulness, reality"). The word appeared in 4:21 ("as the truth is in Jesus"), 4:24 ("the new self... in true righteousness"), and 4:25 ("speaking the truth with his neighbor"). Truth in this letter is not abstract propositional accuracy but the reality of what God has done and who the community is — it is the opposite of the "deceitful desires" (4:22) and the "empty words" (5:6) that characterize the old life and the deceptive teachings.

These three qualities — goodness, righteousness, truth — are the natural product of a life lived as light. They are the evidence that the identity transformation from darkness to light is real.

"And try to discern what is pleasing to the Lord" (5:10).

The participle dokimazontes ("testing, approving, discerning") comes from dokimazo ("to test, to examine, to prove, to approve after testing"). In Romans 12:2, Paul urged: "be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing (dokimazein) you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect." The same verb appears here with the same meaning: the community is to test and discern what is "pleasing" (euareston, "well-pleasing, acceptable") to the Lord. The word euarestos in Romans 12:1 described the living sacrifice as "holy and acceptable (euareston) to God." The adjective implies that God has preferences, that some conduct pleases him and some does not, and that the community is responsible for discerning the difference. The "Lord" (to kyrio) is the standard against which conduct is measured — not cultural norms, not personal preference, not philosophical consensus, but the Lord's pleasure.

"Take no part in the unfruitful works of darkness, but instead expose them" (5:11).

The imperative me synkoinoneite tois ergois tois akarpois tou skotous ("do not participate in the unfruitful works of darkness") uses another syn- compound: synkoinoneo ("to share in, to participate in, to have fellowship with"). The community is not to share in the works of darkness. The works are described as akarpois ("unfruitful, barren, producing nothing") — a direct contrast with the "fruit" of light in verse 9. Darkness has works but no fruit; light has fruit. The works of darkness produce nothing of value; they are sterile, barren, without lasting result. The genitive tou skotous ("of darkness") identifies the source: these works belong to the domain of darkness, the domain from which the community has been transferred.

The alternative: mallon de kai elenchete ("but rather even expose them, but rather even reprove them"). The verb elenchoo ("to expose, to reprove, to convict, to bring to light") has a dual sense in the LXX and in first-century usage. It can mean "to rebuke, to correct" a person (Leviticus 19:17, "You shall surely rebuke [elenchon elenxeis] your neighbor"; Proverbs 3:12, "the LORD reproves [elenchei] the one he loves") and "to expose, to bring to light" a deed (John 3:20, "everyone who does wicked things hates the light and does not come to the light, lest his works should be exposed [elenchthai]"). Both senses are operative here: the community is to both confront wrongdoing and bring hidden evil into the open.

"For it is shameful even to speak of the things that they do in secret" (5:12).

The phrase ta gar kryphee ginomena hyp' auton aischron estin kai legein ("for the things done by them in secret are shameful even to speak of") intensifies the warning. The word kryphee ("in secret, in hiding, covertly") locates the deeds in concealment — they are things done in the dark, away from observation. The word aischron ("shameful, base, disgraceful") describes their character: they are beneath the community's speech. The kai legein ("even to speak of") indicates that the things themselves are so degrading that merely naming them pollutes the conversation. This connects back to verse 3, where the named vices were "not even to be named among you."

The pronoun hyp' auton ("by them") distinguishes the community from those who do these things. The "them" are the sons of disobedience (v. 6), those outside the community whose conduct provokes God's wrath. The community is not to participate in their works (v. 11) and should find their deeds too shameful even to discuss in detail.

"But when anything is exposed by the light, it becomes visible" (5:13).

The statement ta de panta elenchomena hypo tou photos phaneroutai ("but all things being exposed by the light are made manifest") shifts from the ethical imperative to a principle about how light operates. The verb elenchoo ("to expose") from verse 11 reappears, now in a general statement: whatever is exposed by light becomes visible. The word phaneroutai (present passive of phaneroo, "to make visible, to make manifest, to reveal") describes the effect of light's exposure: what was hidden becomes visible, what was secret becomes known. This is the nature of light — it reveals.

"For anything that becomes visible is light" (5:14a).

The statement pan gar to phanaroumenon phos estin ("for everything that is made manifest is light") is compressed and has been read in several ways. The most natural reading within the argument is that when something is brought from darkness into the light — when it is exposed, revealed, made manifest — it takes on the character of light. The act of exposure itself transforms: what was hidden in darkness, once brought into the light, becomes light. The implication for the community is that their task of exposing the works of darkness is not merely punitive but redemptive — exposure can transform darkness into light.

"Therefore it says, 'Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you'" (5:14b).

The introductory formula dio legei ("therefore it says") introduces a quotation. The subject of legei is unstated — the formula typically introduces scripture, but the quotation that follows does not exactly match any known Old Testament passage. It appears to be an early Christian hymn or prophetic oracle, possibly a baptismal hymn, that draws on multiple scriptural threads:

The call egeire ho katheudoon ("awake, O sleeper") echoes Isaiah 51:17 (LXX): "Awake, awake, stand up, O Jerusalem" (exegeirou exegeirou anasta Ierousalem); and Isaiah 52:1: "Awake, awake, put on your strength, O Zion" (exegeirou exegeirou Sion). The prophet calls sleeping, captive Jerusalem to rouse herself because her deliverance has come.

The phrase kai anasta ek ton nekron ("and arise from the dead") echoes Isaiah 26:19 (LXX): "The dead shall rise, and those in the tombs shall be raised" (anastesontai hoi nekroi kai egerthesontai hoi en tois mnemiois). It also connects directly to the letter's own language: in 2:1, the recipients were "dead in trespasses"; in 2:5, God "made us alive together with Christ." The rising from the dead is both the spiritual transformation already described in chapter 2 and a continuing call to those who may be spiritually drowsy within the community.

The promise kai epiphausei soi ho Christos ("and Christ will shine upon you") echoes Isaiah 60:1-2 (LXX): "Arise, shine, for your light has come, and the glory of the LORD has risen upon you" (photidzou photidzou Ierousalem hekei gar sou to phos kai he doxa kyriou epi se anateltaken). In Isaiah, the glory of the LORD rises upon Zion like the sun. In the hymn, Christ replaces the LORD's glory as the source of illumination. The verb epiphausko ("to shine upon, to give light to, to dawn upon") is from the same root as phos ("light") and connects to the light/darkness contrast of the entire section. Christ is the light that shines upon those who awake from the sleep of death.

Whether this is a baptismal hymn — sung to the newly baptized as they rose from the water — cannot be confirmed from the text alone, but the imagery fits: the baptized person "awakes" from their former death, "rises" from the water, and Christ's light shines upon them. The context within the letter's argument is clear: the community that was once darkness and is now light (v. 8) exists because Christ has shone upon them. Their identity as light is derivative — it comes from the one who illumines. The call to "awake" may also function as a warning to those within the community who are drifting back toward the behaviors described in verses 3-5: those who are spiritually drowsy need to rouse themselves and return to the light.

Verses 15-21: Walking Carefully — Wisdom, the Spirit, and Communal Life

"Look carefully then how you walk, not as unwise but as wise" (5:15).

The imperative blepete oun akriboos poos peripateite ("see then carefully how you walk") combines the verb blepo ("to see, to watch, to take care") with the adverb akriboos ("carefully, precisely, exactly, with strictness"). In Acts 18:25-26, Apollos taught "accurately" (akriboos) about Jesus, and Priscilla and Aquila explained the way of God "more accurately" (akribesteron) to him. The adverb conveys precision, diligence, and attention to detail. The "walk" verb peripateo recurs for the fifth time in the chapter (vv. 2, 8, 15) and the sixth in the ethical section (beginning at 4:1). The contrast is between walking hos asophoi ("as unwise, as foolish") and hos sophoi ("as wise"). The word sophos ("wise") in the LXX and in Jewish wisdom tradition designates the person who understands God's ways and orders their life accordingly: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10; Psalm 111:10 [LXX 110:10]). Wisdom is not intelligence or cleverness but the skill of living rightly before God.

"Making the best use of the time, because the days are evil" (5:16).

The participle exagorazomenoi ton kairon ("buying up the time, redeeming the time, making the most of the opportunity") uses the verb exagorazo ("to buy out, to buy up, to redeem, to ransom from the market"). In Galatians 3:13, the same verb described Christ's redemptive work: "Christ redeemed us (exegorasen) from the curse of the law." In Galatians 4:5, Christ came "to redeem (exagorase) those under the law." Here the object is not persons but kairos ("time, season, opportune moment"). The word kairos (as distinct from chronos, "duration, linear time") designates the critical, decisive moment — the window of opportunity that must be seized. The participle is middle voice: the community is to actively secure the kairos for itself, to claim the time rather than letting it slip away.

The reason: hoti hai hemerai ponerai eisin ("because the days are evil"). The word ponerai ("evil, wicked, morally bad") describes the character of the present age. The "days" are the present period of history, which the letter has already characterized as governed by "the ruler of the authority of the air" (2:2) and populated by spiritual forces of wickedness (6:12). The evil of the days does not make the community passive; it makes them urgent. Because the time is evil, wisdom demands that every moment be used well.

"Therefore do not be foolish, but understand what the will of the Lord is" (5:17).

The imperative me ginesthe aphrones ("do not become senseless, do not be foolish") uses the adjective aphron ("senseless, foolish, lacking understanding") — a word that in the LXX describes the person who disregards God: "The fool (aphron) says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1 [LXX 13:1]). The positive alternative: alla syniete ti to thelema tou kyriou ("but understand what the will of the Lord is"). The verb syniemi ("to understand, to comprehend, to perceive") in the LXX describes the insight that comes from attending to God's instruction: "Give me understanding, that I may keep your law" (Psalm 119:34 [LXX 118:34]). The thelema tou kyriou ("the will of the Lord") echoes 1:5 ("the purpose of his will"), 1:9 ("the mystery of his will"), and 1:11 ("the counsel of his will"). The will of God has been a structural theme throughout the letter — it is the eternal purpose that has driven the entire narrative of election, redemption, and unification. Now the community is to understand that will in its practical implications for daily conduct. The movement from cosmic purpose (1:9-10) to daily discernment (5:17) is complete: the mystery of God's will, which is the unification of all things in Christ, must now be lived out in the community's moment-by-moment choices.

"And do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery, but be filled with the Spirit" (5:18).

The prohibition me methyskesthe oino ("do not get drunk with wine") uses the present passive imperative of methysko ("to make drunk, to intoxicate"). The present tense suggests an ongoing prohibition: do not continue getting drunk, do not make this a habit. The phrase en ho estin asootia ("in which is debauchery") explains the problem. The word asootia ("debauchery, dissipation, reckless wastefulness") comes from a-sootos ("unsaving, wasteful, profligate") and describes the squandering of life in excess. The warning against drunkenness has roots in the wisdom tradition: Proverbs 20:1, "Wine is a mocker, strong drink a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise"; Proverbs 23:29-35 describes the sorrows of the drunkard at length. In the prophetic tradition, drunkenness symbolized spiritual stupor and abandonment of God's purposes: Isaiah 5:11-12, "Woe to those who rise early in the morning, that they may run after strong drink"; Isaiah 28:1-8, the drunkards of Ephraim who stumble in vision and reel in judgment.

The positive alternative is alla pleerousthe en pneumati ("but be filled with the Spirit"). The verb pleeroo ("to fill, to make full") is present passive imperative: keep being filled, continue to be filled. The passive voice indicates that the Spirit does the filling — the community receives it. The prepositional phrase en pneumati ("with the Spirit" or "in the Spirit") identifies the filling agent. The contrast between wine and the Spirit may echo the Pentecost narrative, where the Spirit-filled community was accused of being drunk (Acts 2:13-15), and Peter responded: "these people are not drunk, as you suppose." The point is not merely that the Spirit replaces wine as a source of joy but that the Spirit produces the fullness that wine falsely promises. Wine fills the body with dissipation; the Spirit fills the person with God's presence and power. The phrase connects to 3:19, where the prayer was that the community "may be filled to all the fullness of God" (pleerootheete eis pan to pleeroma tou theou). The filling with the Spirit is the means by which the prayer of 3:19 is answered.

"Addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody to the Lord with your heart" (5:19).

The participle lalountes heautois ("speaking to one another") introduces a series of participles (vv. 19-21) that describe what Spirit-filled communal life looks like. The first expression is corporate worship: psalmois kai hymnois kai oodais pneumatikais ("in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs"). The word psalmos ("psalm") in the LXX titles the canonical psalms of David (the Psalter is Psalmoi Daveid). The word hymnos ("hymn, song of praise") designates songs directed to God in praise. The word oode ("song, ode") is a broader term for any composed song. The adjective pneumatikais ("spiritual") modifies the songs, indicating they are inspired by or expressive of the Spirit's work — they arise from the Spirit's filling described in verse 18.

The phrase adontes kai psallontes te kardia hymoon to kyrio ("singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord") specifies the manner: the singing arises from the kardia ("heart") — the center of the person, the seat of will and intention (as defined throughout the letter, 1:18, 3:17). The music is not merely vocal performance but heart-directed worship, addressed to the Lord.

"Giving thanks always and for everything to God the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:20).

The participle eucharistountes ("giving thanks") continues the description of Spirit-filled life. The eucharistia ("thanksgiving") of verse 4 — named as the replacement for corrupt speech — now reappears as a comprehensive posture: pantote ("always, at all times") and hyper pantoon ("for everything, for all things"). The scope is total: thanksgiving in every circumstance and for everything. The thanksgiving is directed to theo kai patri ("to God the Father" or "to God, even the Father") en onomati tou kyriou heemon Iesou Christou ("in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ"). The formula "in the name of" indicates that the thanksgiving is offered through the mediation and authority of Christ — the same access described in 2:18 ("through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father").

"Submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ" (5:21).

The participle hypotassomenoi alleelois ("submitting to one another, being subject to one another") introduces the concept that will govern the household code of 5:22-6:9. The verb hypotasso ("to place under, to subject, to submit, to arrange under") in the middle/passive means "to submit oneself, to place oneself under." The reciprocal pronoun alleelois ("to one another") makes the submission mutual — not one-directional but characteristic of every member toward every other. The phrase en phobo Christou ("in the fear of Christ" or "out of reverence for Christ") identifies the motivation: the submission is not to human authority for its own sake but is an expression of reverence for Christ. The word phobos ("fear, reverence, awe") in the LXX describes the posture proper to those who stand before God: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 1:7). Here it is "fear of Christ" — Christ occupies the position that the LORD occupies in the wisdom tradition.

This verse functions as a hinge between the general ethical instruction (5:1-20) and the specific household relationships (5:22-6:9). The mutual submission en phobo Christou is the overarching principle; the household code specifies how that mutual submission operates in particular relationships.

Verses 22-24: Wives and Husbands — The First Instruction

"Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord" (5:22).

The word hai gynaikes ("wives") addresses women within the community. The verb hypotassesthe ("submit yourselves") is not present in the oldest manuscripts of verse 22; it is supplied from verse 21, where the participle hypotassomenoi ("submitting to one another") governs. Some manuscripts read the full imperative, but the textual evidence suggests the original text read simply: hai gynaikes tois idiois andrasin hos to kyrio — "wives, to your own husbands, as to the Lord." The submission of verse 21 extends into verse 22 without a new verb, indicating that the wives' submission to husbands is one specific application of the general mutual submission of verse 21. It is not a separate, unrelated command but a particularization of the broader principle.

The phrase tois idiois andrasin ("to your own husbands") uses idiois ("own, one's own") to specify the relationship: this is about the marriage bond, not about women's submission to men in general. The word aner ("man, husband") here carries the specific sense of "husband."

The simile hos to kyrio ("as to the Lord") establishes the standard and motivation. The submission is rendered as though rendered to the Lord — not because the husband is the Lord, but because the Lord is the one ultimately served in the relationship. The same "as to the Lord" formula will appear in 6:5 for slaves obeying masters, making clear that the pattern is relational submission oriented toward Christ, not the divinization of any human authority.

"For the husband is the head of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior" (5:23).

The word kephale ("head") has appeared in the letter at 1:22, where God gave Christ "as head (kephalen) over all things to the church," and 4:15, where the community is to "grow up in every way into him who is the head (kephalen), into Christ." In the LXX, kephale can mean physical head, leader/ruler (Judges 11:11; 2 Samuel 22:44; Isaiah 7:8-9, "the head of Damascus is Rezin"), or source/origin. The author draws an analogy: the husband is head of the wife as (hos) Christ is head of the church.

The appositional phrase autos soter tou somatos ("he himself being the savior of the body") is significant. The word soter ("savior, deliverer, rescuer") in the LXX describes God's saving activity: "I am the LORD your God, the Holy One of Israel, your Savior" (Isaiah 43:3, LXX: ego kyrios ho theos sou ho hagios Israel ho soozoon se). In the Pastoral Epistles, both God and Christ are called soter (1 Timothy 1:1, 2:3; Titus 1:3-4, 2:10-13, 3:4-6). Here Christ is soter tou somatos — savior of the body. The "body" (soma) is the church (1:23, "the church, which is his body"). Christ's headship over the church is not merely structural but salvific — he is the one who saves the body that he heads.

The clause complicates the analogy: the husband is head of the wife as Christ is head of the church — but only Christ is "savior of the body." The husband has a headship analogous to Christ's, but the husband does not save. The author's analogy is not a point-for-point correspondence but an asymmetric comparison that maintains Christ's unique role.

"Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands" (5:24).

The phrase alla hos he ekklesia hypotassetai to Christo ("but as the church submits to Christ") presents the church's submission to Christ as the model. The verb hypotassetai is present middle/passive: the church is presently, actively submitting to Christ. This is not a statement about a future state but about the present reality. The church's submission to Christ is the paradigm from which the wife's submission to her husband derives. The phrase en panti ("in everything") extends the scope: the submission is comprehensive, not selective.

Verses 25-27: The Husband's Love — Christ's Self-Giving as Model

"Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (5:25).

The address hoi andres ("husbands") shifts the instruction to the male partner. The imperative agapate tas gynaikas heautoon ("love your wives") uses the verb agapao — the same verb from 5:2 ("as Christ loved us") and the same love that has been the defining quality of the new walk. The standard is stated again: kathos kai ho Christos egapesen ten ekklesian kai heauton paredoken hyper autes ("as Christ also loved the church and gave himself up for her"). The language is virtually identical to 5:2: Christ "loved" (egapesen, aorist) and "gave himself up" (paredoken heauton). In 5:2, the self-giving was described as a sacrifice to God; here it is directed specifically toward the church. The husband's love is to follow this same pattern: self-giving, costly, voluntary.

The reflexive paredoken heauton ("gave himself up") reappears: Christ handed himself over. The prepositional phrase hyper autes ("for her, on her behalf") identifies the church as the beneficiary of Christ's self-sacrifice. The feminine pronoun autes refers to ekklesia, which is grammatically feminine. The husband is to love his wife with the same kind of self-sacrificial love with which Christ loved the church.

"That he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word" (5:26).

The purpose clause hina auten hagiase ("in order that he might sanctify her") specifies Christ's purpose in giving himself up. The verb hagiazo ("to sanctify, to make holy, to set apart, to consecrate") is from the same root as hagios ("holy") — the word used for the community throughout the letter ("the saints," hoi hagioi, 1:1, etc.) and for the purpose of election in 1:4 ("that we should be holy," hagious). Christ's self-giving was purposive: it aimed to make the church holy. Sanctification is not a byproduct of Christ's death but its stated purpose.

The means of sanctification is specified: katharisas to loutro tou hydatos en reemati ("having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word" or "by the bath of water in connection with a word/utterance"). The verb katharizo ("to cleanse, to purify, to make clean") in the LXX describes the purification rituals of the temple cult (Leviticus 14:7-8; Numbers 19:12, 19) and the prophetic promise of eschatological cleansing: Ezekiel 36:25, "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean. From all your uncleanness and from all your idols I will cleanse you." The phrase to loutro tou hydatos ("the washing of water, the bath of water") refers to water-washing, most naturally understood as baptism — the initiatory rite of the community. The word loutron ("bath, washing") appears in Titus 3:5 for "the washing (loutrou) of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit." The phrase en reemati ("with a word, in connection with a spoken word") likely refers to the confession or declaration spoken at baptism, or to the gospel word that accompanies the baptismal act.

The image is drawn from ancient marriage customs. In Jewish and Greco-Roman practice, the bride underwent a bridal bath before the wedding — a ritual washing that prepared her for presentation to the bridegroom. The author depicts Christ as the bridegroom who cleanses his bride (the church) through the baptismal washing, preparing her for presentation. The marital metaphor for God's relationship with his people has deep roots in the LXX: Hosea 1-3 (God as husband, Israel as wife); Isaiah 54:5-6 ("your Maker is your husband, the LORD of hosts is his name"); Jeremiah 2:2 ("I remember the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride"); Ezekiel 16:8-14 (God washing, clothing, and adorning Jerusalem as his bride).

"So that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish" (5:27).

The second purpose clause hina parastese autos heauto ten ekklesian endoxon ("in order that he might present to himself the church in glory/splendor") extends the bridal imagery. The verb paristemi ("to present, to set before, to place alongside") in the LXX and in Paul's usage describes the act of presenting something for inspection or approval: in Romans 12:1, believers are urged to "present your bodies as a living sacrifice"; in 2 Corinthians 11:2, Paul says "I betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a pure virgin to Christ." The word endoxon ("glorious, splendid, honored, magnificent") describes the condition of the church at her presentation: she is radiant, glorious, magnificent — not from her own inherent beauty but from the cleansing work of her bridegroom.

The negative descriptions me echousan spilon e rhytida e ti ton toiouton ("not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing") specify the purity in concrete terms. The word spilos ("spot, stain, blemish") describes a flaw or mark that defiles. The word rhytis ("wrinkle") describes the sign of aging or decay. Together they describe a bride who is flawless — without defect, without decay, without any disfiguring mark. The imagery connects to the LXX's sacrificial language: offerings to God were to be "without blemish" (amomos, Leviticus 1:3, 3:1; Numbers 6:14) — no animal with a defect could be presented at the altar.

The climactic purpose clause: hina e hagia kai amomos ("that she might be holy and without blemish"). The two adjectives hagia ("holy") and amomos ("blameless, without blemish") are the exact words from 1:4: "He chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him" (einai hemas hagious kai amomous katenopion autou). The verbal connection is unmistakable and deliberate. The purpose of election stated in 1:4 — holiness and blamelessness — is here identified as the purpose of Christ's self-giving love for the church. What God purposed before the foundation of the world (1:4), Christ accomplishes through his sacrificial death, cleansing, and presentation (5:25-27). The election was always aimed at this: a people sanctified, purified, and presented without defect to God. And the means of achieving this goal is Christ's love, not the community's self-effort. The holiness comes from Christ's action, not from the community's performance — he sanctifies, he cleanses, he presents. The church is the recipient of a work done for her and upon her.

This connection also carries implications for how the community understands its present life. If the purpose of election is holiness and blamelessness (1:4), and if Christ gave himself to achieve precisely that purpose (5:25-27), then the ethical commands of chapters 4-5 are not arbitrary impositions but the outworking of God's eternal purpose. The call to put away porneia, akatharsia, and pleonexia (5:3) is not merely moral advice but participation in the very purpose for which God chose a people before the world began.

Verses 28-30: The Husband's Love as Self-Care

"In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself" (5:28).

The phrase houtos opheilousin kai hoi andres agapan tas heauton gynaikas hos ta heauton somata ("thus husbands also ought to love their own wives as their own bodies") shifts the analogy from Christ-and-church to the practical level of the marital relationship. The word opheiloo ("to owe, to be obligated, to ought") indicates obligation, not option: husbands are indebted to love. The comparison hos ta heauton somata ("as their own bodies") introduces a self-referential argument: love your wife as you love your own body. The logic is that the wife is not an external party to whom kindness is extended as a favor, but is so closely united to the husband that to love her is to love himself. The author then states the principle directly: ho agapon ten heautou gynaika heauton agapa — "the one who loves his own wife loves himself."

"For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church" (5:29).

The statement oudeis gar pote ten heautou sarka emisesen ("for no one ever hated his own flesh") appeals to the self-evident nature of self-care. The word sarx ("flesh") here is used in its ordinary sense — the physical body, the embodied self. The two verbs that describe self-care are ektrephei ("nourishes, feeds, brings up, rears") and thalpei ("cherishes, warms, cares for tenderly"). The word ektrepho in the LXX describes the rearing of children (Ephesians 6:4, "bring them up [ektrephete] in the discipline and instruction of the Lord"). The word thalpo ("to warm, to cherish") appears in 1 Thessalonians 2:7, where Paul described his care for the Thessalonians: "we were gentle among you, like a nursing mother cherishing (thalpee) her own children." Both verbs describe intimate, sustaining care — the kind of attention one gives to one's own body.

The standard reappears: kathos kai ho Christos ten ekklesian ("just as Christ also [does] the church"). Christ nourishes and cherishes the church as a person nourishes and cherishes their own body. The comparison grounds the husband's obligation in Christ's active, sustaining care for the community. Christ's relationship to the church is not merely a past act of sacrifice (5:25) but an ongoing relationship of nurture and tender care (5:29).

"Because we are members of his body" (5:30).

The statement hoti melee esmen tou soomatos autou ("because we are members of his body") supplies the reason for Christ's nurturing care. The word melee ("members, limbs, parts") identifies the community as parts of Christ's body — the same body-language established in 1:23 ("the church, which is his body") and developed in 4:12-16 (the building up of the body of Christ, every member contributing). The pronoun shifts to "we" (esmen, first person plural) — the author includes himself in the community that is Christ's body. Christ nourishes and cherishes the church because the church is his own body; a head does not neglect its body. The logic of the analogy requires that the husband likewise not neglect his wife, who is "his own body" in the marital union.

Verses 31-33: The Mystery — Genesis 2:24 and Christ and the Church

"Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh" (5:31).

The quotation anti toutou kataleipsei anthropos ton patera kai ten metera kai proskolleethesetai pros ten gynaika autou kai esontai hoi duo eis sarka mian is drawn from Genesis 2:24 (LXX). The introductory phrase anti toutou ("for this reason, on account of this") translates the Hebrew 'al-ken ("therefore"). The quotation describes the foundational pattern of marriage as established in creation: the man leaves his parents, is joined to his wife, and the two become one flesh. The verb proskollaomai ("to join to, to cleave to, to be glued to") in the LXX describes the closest possible adhesion: Deuteronomy 10:20, "You shall fear the LORD your God. You shall serve him and hold fast (proskollethese) to him"; Deuteronomy 11:22, "to love the LORD your God, to walk in all his ways, and to hold fast (proskollasthai) to him." The verb describes the covenant bond — whether between Israel and God or between husband and wife. The phrase eis sarka mian ("into one flesh") describes the complete union of two persons into a single, embodied reality.

In its Genesis context, the text explains why marriage exists: because the woman was taken from the man (Genesis 2:21-23), the man leaves his origin and clings to his wife, and the two return to the unity from which the woman was taken. The "one flesh" is not merely sexual union but total union of life, identity, and purpose.

"This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (5:32).

The statement to mysterion touto mega estin ("this mystery is great/profound") applies the letter's key term mysterion to the Genesis quotation. The word mysterion has appeared at critical junctures throughout the letter: in 1:9, God "made known to us the mystery of his will"; in 3:3-6, the mystery is the inclusion of gentiles as co-heirs, co-members of the body, and co-sharers of the promise; in 3:9, Paul was commissioned to "bring to light for all what is the stewardship of the mystery." In every previous use, the mystery was the hidden plan of God now revealed — specifically, the unification of Jews and gentiles in one body in Christ.

Now the author declares that the Genesis passage about the one-flesh union of husband and wife is itself a mysterion — a hidden reality whose full meaning has now been disclosed. The phrase ego de lego eis Christon kai eis ten ekklesian ("but I am speaking with reference to Christ and the church") specifies the referent. The preposition eis ("toward, with reference to, in relation to") directs the interpretation: the deep meaning of Genesis 2:24 points toward Christ and the church. The "leaving" of the man, his "cleaving" to his wife, and the "one flesh" union all have a deeper referent in the union between Christ and his people.

The connection to the letter's larger argument is rich. The mystery throughout Ephesians has been the creation of one new humanity from two — Jews and gentiles united in one body in Christ (2:15-16). The Genesis text speaks of two becoming one flesh. The parallel is structural: as man and woman become one flesh in marriage, so Christ and the church (composed of Jew and gentile) become one body. The "one flesh" of Genesis 2:24 and the "one body" of Ephesians 2:16 are analogous realities, both expressing the divine purpose of unification.

The author is not allegorizing away the literal meaning of Genesis 2:24 for marriage. The word plēn ("nevertheless, however, but") at the beginning of verse 33 will return the instruction to the literal husband-wife relationship. Rather, the author is claiming that Genesis 2:24 has a depth of meaning — a mysterion — that goes beyond (and includes) its application to human marriage. The institution of marriage itself, as established in creation, was always a sign pointing toward the ultimate union: Christ and his people. The relationship between man and woman in marriage is a created pattern that images and anticipates the relationship between the Messiah and his community.

This is consistent with the prophetic tradition in which marriage was used as a metaphor for God's covenant with Israel. In Hosea 1-3, the marriage of Hosea and Gomer embodied God's relationship with unfaithful Israel. In Isaiah 54:5, God is Israel's husband. In Jeremiah 2:2, 31:32, the covenant is described as a marriage. In Ezekiel 16, Jerusalem is God's bride whom he washed, clothed, and adorned. The author of Ephesians stands in this tradition but makes a specific christological claim: the mystery hidden in Genesis 2:24 is not merely about God-and-Israel but about Christ-and-the-church. The bridegroom is now identified as Christ, and the bride is the ekklesia — the assembly of Jews and gentiles united in one body.

"However, let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband" (5:33).

The word pleen ("nevertheless, however, but in any case") returns from the theological depth of the mysterion to the practical instruction. The statement functions as a summary of the entire household-code section for husbands and wives. The husband's obligation: hina kai hymeis hoi kath' hena hekastos ten heautou gynaika houtos agapato hos heauton ("let each one of you individually love his own wife as himself"). The phrase hoi kath' hena hekastos ("each one individually") particularizes: the instruction is not merely a corporate ideal but applies to each specific husband in his specific marriage.

The wife's obligation: he de gyne hina phobetai ton andra ("and let the wife respect her husband" or "and let the wife fear/reverence her husband"). The verb phobeomai ("to fear, to respect, to revere") is the same root as phobos in verse 21 ("in the fear of Christ"). The wife's reverence for her husband is not servile terror but the same quality of respectful deference that characterizes the community's relationship to Christ. The word echoes the LXX's "fear of the LORD" — a reverence that arises from recognition of the other's position and role, not from coercion.

Summary of the Argument

Ephesians 5 continues and intensifies the ethical instruction that began at 4:1, while simultaneously reaching back to the letter's foundational theological claims and forward into the household code of 6:1-9. The chapter's argument moves through three interconnected sections:

First (5:1-7): The standard and the warning. The community is to imitate God and walk in love, following the pattern of Christ's self-sacrificial offering (vv. 1-2). Specific behaviors — sexual immorality, impurity, greed, obscene speech — are incompatible with the community's identity as saints (vv. 3-4). The warning is severe: those who practice these things have no inheritance in the kingdom (v. 5). God's wrath comes upon the sons of disobedience (v. 6), and the community must not become co-sharers with them (v. 7). The language of inheritance (kleronomia) connects directly to 1:11 and 1:14, where the inheritance was established as the community's possession. The language of "sons of disobedience" connects directly to 2:2, where that phrase described those under the ruler of the air's dominion. The warning is that the community can forfeit its inheritance by returning to the conduct that characterized its former life.

Second (5:8-21): The identity and its expression. The community was darkness but is now light in the Lord (v. 8). The fruit of light is goodness, righteousness, and truth (v. 9). The community is to expose the works of darkness (v. 11) and walk with the precision of the wise (vv. 15-16). Rather than drunkenness, they are to be filled with the Spirit (v. 18), expressing their Spirit-filled life in communal worship, thanksgiving, and mutual submission (vv. 19-21). The identity argument — you are light, therefore walk as light — parallels the identity argument of 2:1-10 (you were dead, now you are alive, therefore walk in good works). The filling with the Spirit connects to 3:19 (filled to all the fullness of God) and to 1:13-14 (sealed with the promised Spirit).

Third (5:22-33): The household code and the mystery. The mutual submission of verse 21 is particularized in the wife-husband relationship (vv. 22-33). The husband's love for his wife is modeled on Christ's love for the church (v. 25), which aimed at sanctifying and presenting the church as holy and blameless (vv. 26-27). The phrase "holy and blameless" (hagia kai amomos) in verse 27 is the exact language of 1:4, creating a direct link between the purpose of election and the purpose of Christ's sacrifice. The Genesis 2:24 quotation is identified as a mysterion referring to Christ and the church (v. 32), connecting the marital union to the letter's central theme of unification — the mystery hidden for ages and now revealed (3:3-9). The "one flesh" of Genesis parallels the "one body" and "one new humanity" of the letter's ecclesiology.

Observations on Pronoun Usage

The pronoun pattern in Ephesians 5 is largely consistent with the established pattern of the letter, with some notable features:

The second person "you" (hymeis, hymon, hymas) addresses the audience directly throughout the ethical instructions: "not even be named among you" (5:3), "let no one deceive you" (5:6), "you were darkness" (5:8), "you are light" (5:8), "look carefully how you walk" (5:15), "do not get drunk" (5:18). This "you" continues to address the predominantly gentile audience (as established in 2:11, 3:1).

The first person plural "we" (hemeis) appears in two key locations. In 5:2, the pronoun shifts to "us" (hemas): "Christ loved us and gave himself up for us" — inclusive of the author and the entire community. In 5:30, "we are members of his body" (melee esmen tou somatos autou) — again inclusive. The "we" in both cases encompasses the whole believing community, Jewish and gentile together.

The third person "them" (autoi/auton) in verses 6-7 and 11-12 refers to the "sons of disobedience" — those outside the community whose conduct the audience must avoid.

In the household code (5:22-33), the pronouns shift to address specific groups within the community: "wives" and "husbands" are addressed directly, while the Christ-church analogy uses "he/himself" (autos/heauton) for Christ and "her" (aute/autes) for the church. The "we" of verse 30 momentarily breaks the analogy to include the author and audience together as members of Christ's body.

Old Testament and LXX Resonances

The following Old Testament texts are active in the background of Ephesians 5:

  • Leviticus 1:9, 13, 17; Numbers 28-29 (the "fragrant offering" language): the phrase osmen euodias in 5:2 draws directly on the LXX's sacrificial terminology for offerings pleasing to God.
  • Genesis 8:21 (the LORD smelled the pleasing aroma after Noah's sacrifice): the same phrase osmen euodias establishes the pattern of sacrifice that pleases God.
  • Isaiah 9:2 [LXX 9:1] ("the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light"): the darkness-to-light transformation of 5:8 echoes this Isaianic promise.
  • Isaiah 60:1-3 ("Arise, shine, for your light has come"): the quoted hymn in 5:14 draws on this passage, with Christ replacing the LORD's glory as the source of light.
  • Isaiah 26:19 ("The dead shall rise"): the hymn's call to "arise from the dead" echoes this eschatological promise.
  • Isaiah 51:17, 52:1 ("Awake, awake, O Jerusalem/O Zion"): the call to the sleeper in 5:14 echoes the prophetic summons to sleeping/captive Zion.
  • Proverbs 20:1; 23:29-35 (warnings against drunkenness): the prohibition of wine-drunkenness in 5:18 draws on the wisdom tradition's sustained critique of intoxication.
  • Ezekiel 36:25 ("I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean"): the cleansing by "the washing of water" in 5:26 echoes this prophetic promise of eschatological purification.
  • Hosea 1-3; Isaiah 54:5-6; Jeremiah 2:2; Ezekiel 16:8-14 (God as husband, Israel as bride): the entire husband-wife/Christ-church analogy of 5:22-33 stands in the tradition of the marriage metaphor for the covenant.
  • Genesis 2:24 ("a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh"): directly quoted in 5:31 and identified as a mysterion referring to Christ and the church.
  • Leviticus 1:3; Numbers 6:14 (sacrificial animals "without blemish," amomos): the description of the church as "without spot or wrinkle... holy and without blemish" in 5:27 draws on the sacrificial vocabulary for unblemished offerings.
  • Deuteronomy 10:20; 11:22 (cleave to the LORD): the verb proskollaomai in the Genesis 2:24 quotation carries covenantal connotations from Deuteronomy's use of the same vocabulary for Israel's devotion to God.
