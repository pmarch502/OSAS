Acts 3 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 2): The promised Holy Spirit arrived at Pentecost with the sound of a violent wind and visible tongues of fire (2:1-4). The gathered community spoke in other languages, and the multinational crowd in Jerusalem heard and understood. Peter stood and delivered the first public proclamation, interpreting the event through Joel 2:28-32 and two psalms of David (Psalm 16, Psalm 110). The argument was sequential: David spoke of one whose flesh would not see decay and who would sit at God's right hand; David himself died and his tomb is still here; therefore David spoke prophetically about Jesus, whom God raised from the dead and exalted to his right hand; from that position Jesus poured out the Spirit that the crowd now sees and hears (2:29-33). The conclusion was announced: "God has made Him both Lord and Christ — this Jesus whom you crucified" (2:36). The crowd was cut to the heart and asked what to do. Peter answered: "Repent, and each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is for you and your children and for all who are far off, as many as the Lord our God will call to Himself" (2:38-39). Three thousand were added that day. The chapter closed with a summary of the community's life: they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer; many signs and wonders were taking place through the apostles; they shared possessions according to need; they continued daily with one mind in the temple, breaking bread from house to house, eating with gladness and sincerity of heart, praising God, and having favor with all the people; and the Lord was adding to their number day by day those who were being saved (2:42-47).

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 4): The priests, the captain of the temple, and the Sadducees come upon Peter and John while they are still speaking, "being greatly disturbed because they were teaching the people and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead" (4:1-2). Peter and John are arrested and held overnight. The next day they are brought before the rulers, elders, and scribes — the same council before which Jesus stood. They are asked: "By what power, or in what name, have you done this?" (4:7). Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, delivers a third speech identifying Jesus as the stone rejected by the builders that has become the cornerstone (Psalm 118:22), and declares: "There is salvation in no one else; for there is no other name under heaven that has been given among men by which we must be saved" (4:12). The council observes their confidence, recognizes them as uneducated men who had been with Jesus, and sees the healed man standing with them — they have nothing to say in reply (4:13-14). They threaten Peter and John and release them. The community responds with corporate prayer, citing Psalm 2, and the place is shaken; they are all filled with the Holy Spirit and speak the word of God with boldness (4:31). The arrest, trial, and release of Peter and John in Acts 4 are the direct consequence of the healing and speech narrated in Acts 3.

How chapter 3 connects these: Acts 2 ended with the summary statement that "many wonders and signs were taking place through the apostles" (2:43) and that the community continued "day by day... in the temple" (2:46). Acts 3 opens with the apostles going to the temple and performing a sign — the healing of the lame man. The chapter thus provides the first specific example of the "signs and wonders" that the summary in 2:43 had reported in general terms. Peter's speech in chapter 3 is the second major public address in Acts, and it advances the argument from where the Pentecost speech left it. At Pentecost, Peter argued that Jesus is Lord and Christ and called for repentance. In chapter 3, Peter identifies Jesus as God's Servant (the figure of Isaiah), as the Holy and Righteous One, as the Author of Life, and as the Prophet like Moses. The christological portrait deepens. The call to repentance also deepens: at Pentecost, Peter offered forgiveness of sins and the gift of the Spirit; here he adds the promise that repentance will bring "times of refreshing from the presence of the Lord" and the eventual sending of the appointed Christ. The speech in chapter 3 also introduces two new elements: the mitigating factor of the audience's ignorance (3:17) and the warning from Deuteronomy 18 that anyone who does not listen to the Prophet like Moses will be "utterly destroyed from among the people" (3:23). The tone has shifted from pure invitation to invitation with warning. And this speech, unlike the Pentecost speech which ended with mass conversion, ends with arrest (4:1-3). The opposition that will drive the rest of Acts 1-7 begins here.

Verses 1-3: Peter and John Go Up to the Temple

"Now Peter and John were going up to the temple at the ninth hour, the hour of prayer" (3:1).

The two named apostles are Peter and John. They have appeared together before in Luke-Acts: they were sent together to prepare the Passover (Luke 22:8), and they ran together to the empty tomb (though the author names only Peter in Luke 24:12, John's Gospel identifies both; the author of Acts consistently pairs them as the leading apostolic duo through Acts 8:14). Peter and John function as a pair of witnesses throughout the opening chapters of Acts, consistent with the principle that testimony is established by two witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15).

"Going up to the temple" (anebainen eis to hieron) — the imperfect tense indicates habitual action. They were on their way up, as they customarily did. This connects directly to Acts 2:46: "Day by day continuing with one mind in the temple." The apostles have not broken with the temple. They are observant Jews who continue to participate in the regular rhythms of temple worship. The temple remains, for this community, the house of prayer that Isaiah 56:7 described and that Jesus cited when he drove out the merchants (Luke 19:46). There is no rupture yet between the followers of Jesus and the temple institution.

"The ninth hour, the hour of prayer" (ten horan tes proseuches ten ennaten) — the ninth hour is approximately 3:00 in the afternoon. The daily temple service included the morning (tamid) sacrifice at the third hour (9:00 a.m.) and the evening sacrifice at the ninth hour (3:00 p.m.). Both occasions were accompanied by communal prayer, with incense offered on the golden altar while the people prayed in the courts outside (cf. Luke 1:10, where the people are praying outside while Zechariah offers incense). The ninth hour is the same hour at which Jesus died on the cross in Luke's account (Luke 23:44-46: darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour, and at the ninth hour Jesus cried out and breathed his last). The author does not comment on this connection, but a first-century reader of both volumes would notice it: the hour of Jesus's death has become the hour at which his power, working through his apostles, restores a man to wholeness.

"And a man who had been lame from his mother's womb was being carried along, whom they used to set down every day at the gate of the temple which is called Beautiful, in order to beg alms of those who were entering the temple" (3:2).

The man's condition is described with careful precision. He has been lame "from his mother's womb" (ek koilias metros autou) — his disability is congenital, lifelong, and therefore not a recent or temporary condition. He has never walked. This detail is emphasized to establish the magnitude of what is about to happen: this is not the alleviation of a recent injury but the reversal of a condition that has defined this man's entire existence. The phrase "from his mother's womb" appears in the LXX for conditions and callings that begin at birth (Judges 16:17, of Samson; Isaiah 49:1, of the Servant; Psalm 22:10 [LXX 21:11]).

"Was being carried along" (ebastazeto) — the imperfect passive indicates this was an ongoing practice. The man could not transport himself to the temple; he depended on others to carry him there daily. His access to the temple was entirely mediated by the help of others.

"The gate of the temple which is called Beautiful" (ten thyran tou hierou ten legomenen Horaian) — the identification of this gate has been debated, but the most commonly proposed candidate is the Nicanor Gate, which stood between the Court of the Gentiles and the Court of the Women. Josephus describes a gate of Corinthian bronze at this location that "greatly excelled those that were only covered over with silver and gold" (Jewish War 5.5.3). Whether or not this is the exact gate, the author's point is locational: the man is positioned at the threshold of the temple, at the entrance through which worshippers pass. He is near the sacred space but functionally excluded from full participation. He cannot walk into the courts where the worship takes place. He sits at the boundary.

"In order to beg alms" (tou aitein eleemosynen) — the man's purpose at the gate is economic survival. Almsgiving (eleemosyne) was a central obligation in Jewish piety. Tobit 4:7-11 instructs: "Give alms from your possessions... for almsgiving delivers from death and keeps you from going into the Darkness." Sirach 3:30: "As water extinguishes a blazing fire, so almsgiving atones for sin." The temple gate was a natural location for begging precisely because those entering to worship would be disposed toward acts of piety. The man's position exploits the intersection of religious devotion and charitable obligation.

"When he saw Peter and John about to go into the temple, he began asking to receive alms" (3:3).

The man initiates the encounter, but he initiates it on his own terms — he asks for money. He does not know who Peter and John are. He sees two men approaching the temple gate and does what he does with every passerby: he asks for alms. His request is shaped by his experience and his expectations. He expects charity. He will receive something entirely different.

Verses 4-8: The Healing

"But Peter, along with John, fixed his gaze on him and said, 'Look at us!'" (3:4).

The verb atenisas ("having fixed his gaze") is the same word used in Acts 1:10 for the disciples gazing intently into the sky at the ascension. It connotes intense, deliberate, focused attention. Peter does not walk past. He stops and looks directly at the man. The command "Look at us" (blepson eis hemas) demands reciprocal attention. The man has been asking for alms from anyone passing by — a generalized request. Peter insists on a personal encounter. The man must look at Peter and John specifically, not simply extend a hand toward the crowd.

"And he began to give them his attention, expecting to receive something from them" (3:5).

The man complies. He looks at them. The verb epeichen ("gave attention, fixed his mind on") indicates focused expectation. He looks at them because he expects to receive something — money. The word prosdokon ("expecting") defines what is in the man's mind: a coin. His horizon of expectation is entirely within the frame of his daily experience. He has no category for what is about to happen.

"But Peter said, 'I do not possess silver and gold, but what I do have I give to you: In the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene — walk!'" (3:6).

Peter first states what he does not have. "Silver and gold I do not possess" (argyrion kai chrysion ouch hyparchei moi) — the apostle is materially poor. The community described in Acts 2:44-45 sold property and distributed proceeds according to need; the apostles themselves carry no personal wealth. What Peter lacks in money he possesses in something else: the authority to act in the name of Jesus.

"What I do have I give to you" (ho de echo touto soi didomi) — the contrast is stark. The man asked for money; Peter has none. But Peter has something to give, and what he has is infinitely greater than what the man requested. The pattern recurs throughout scripture: the petitioner asks for one thing and receives something far beyond the original request.

"In the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene — walk!" (en to onomati Iesou Christou tou Nazoraiou [egeire kai] peripatei). The phrase "in the name of" (en to onomati) is not a magical formula; it denotes the authority, power, and person of the one named. To act "in the name of" someone is to act as their authorized representative, exercising their authority. The name invoked is full and specific: "Jesus" (his personal name), "Christ" (Christos, the anointed one, the Messiah), "the Nazarene" (tou Nazoraiou, identifying his place of origin). The specificity matters — this is not a generic invocation of divine power but an exercise of authority rooted in a particular historical person. The command "walk" (peripatei) is a present imperative: begin walking and keep walking. Some manuscripts include "rise and" (egeire kai) before "walk," echoing the language of Jesus's own healing commands (Luke 5:23: "Rise and walk"; Luke 7:14: "Young man, I say to you, arise").

"And seizing him by the right hand, he raised him up; and immediately his feet and his ankles were strengthened" (3:7).

Peter does not merely speak; he acts. He seizes (piasas) the man by the right hand and raises him (egeiren). The verb egeiro is the same word used for resurrection throughout Luke-Acts. Its use here for lifting a lame man to his feet carries resonance: what God did for Jesus in raising him from the dead, Jesus's power now does for this man in raising him from lameness. The right hand is the hand of strength, of blessing, of covenant (Genesis 48:14, 17-18; Psalm 16:11; Psalm 110:1).

"Immediately" (parachrema) — this word appears frequently in Luke-Acts for the instantaneous character of divine action (Luke 1:64, 4:39, 5:25, 8:44, 8:55, 13:13, 18:43). The healing is not gradual. It is complete and instant.

"His feet and his ankles were strengthened" (estereothesan hai baseis autou kai ta sphydra) — the author uses precise anatomical language. The word baseis ("feet" or "soles") and sphydra ("ankle bones, ankle joints") describe the specific body parts affected. The verb estereothesan ("were strengthened, were made firm") is from stereoo, which means to make solid, to establish firmly. The same root appears in LXX Isaiah 35:3: "Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees." Isaiah 35 is a prophecy of restoration: "Then the lame will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:6). The author's word choice deliberately echoes the Isaianic vision of eschatological healing.

"With a leap he stood upright and began to walk; and he entered the temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God" (3:8).

The progression is vivid: he leaps (exallomenos — a strong compound verb, "leaping out, springing up"), he stands upright (este, "he stood"), he begins to walk (periepatei, imperfect tense — continuous walking). Then he enters the temple. This detail is theologically significant. The man who sat at the gate of the temple, at the threshold of sacred space, unable to enter on his own, now walks into the temple. The boundary has been crossed. He is no longer excluded. He enters with the apostles (syn autois, "with them"), walking and leaping and praising God.

The phrase "walking and leaping and praising God" (peripatton kai hallomenos kai ainon ton theon) echoes Isaiah 35:6: "Then the lame will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will shout for joy." The verb hallomai ("to leap, to spring") is used in the LXX of Isaiah 35:6 (haleita hos elaphos ho cholos, "the lame will leap like a deer"). The healing of this congenital lame man at the temple gate is presented as a fulfillment of Isaiah's eschatological vision. The age of restoration that Isaiah described — when the blind see, the deaf hear, the lame leap, and the mute sing — is breaking into the present through the name of Jesus.

The man's response is to praise God (ainon ton theon). He does not praise Peter and John. His instinct is worship. The praise directs attention to the true source of the healing, and this will be the central point of Peter's speech that follows.

Verses 9-10: The Crowd's Reaction

"And all the people saw him walking and praising God; and they were taking note of him as being the one who used to sit at the Beautiful Gate of the temple to beg alms, and they were filled with wonder and amazement at what had happened to him" (3:9-10).

"All the people" (pas ho laos) — the word laos ("people") in Luke-Acts frequently refers specifically to the Jewish people, the people of God, as distinct from ochlos ("crowd"). The laos at the temple see the man walking and praising God. Their reaction is recognition followed by astonishment.

"They were taking note of him" (epeginoskon auton) — the verb epiginosko means "to recognize, to know fully, to identify." They recognize him. This is the man they have seen at the gate every day. The recognition establishes the reality of the miracle: this is not a stranger claiming to have been healed but a known figure whose condition was publicly visible for years.

"They were filled with wonder and amazement" (eplestheson thambous kai ekstaseos). Two words are used for the crowd's response. Thambos ("wonder, astonishment") connotes a stunned reaction to the unexpected. Ekstasis ("amazement, bewilderment") literally means "standing outside oneself" — being so overwhelmed that normal categories of understanding are disrupted. The double expression conveys the intensity of the crowd's response. What they have witnessed does not fit within their existing framework. They need an explanation. Peter will provide one.

Verses 11-16: Peter's Speech — The Healing Explained

"While he was clinging to Peter and John, all the people ran together to them at the so-called portico of Solomon, full of amazement" (3:11).

The healed man is physically clinging to (kratountos, "holding fast to, seizing") Peter and John. The gesture is natural — the man is grateful and physically attached to those who healed him. It also creates a visual tableau: when the crowd gathers, they see the formerly lame man holding onto the apostles. The evidence of the miracle is standing, walking, and clinging right in front of them.

"The portico of Solomon" (te stoa te Solomontos) — this is a colonnade on the eastern side of the temple's outer court. Josephus describes it as a structure attributed to Solomon's original construction (Antiquities 20.9.7). It was a public gathering space within the temple complex, a place where teaching occurred. Jesus taught there (John 10:23), and the early community gathered there regularly (Acts 5:12). It is a public space within sacred ground — the ideal location for Peter's explanation.

"But when Peter saw this, he replied to the people, 'Men of Israel, why are you amazed at this, or why do you gaze at us, as if by our own power or piety we had made him walk?'" (3:12).

Peter's opening move is to redirect attention. The crowd is looking at Peter and John as if they are the source of the miracle. Peter immediately corrects this. The question "why do you gaze at us?" (ti hemin atenizete) uses the same word (atenizo) that described Peter's gaze at the lame man in verse 4. Now the crowd is gazing at Peter and John with the same intensity, and Peter deflects it.

"As if by our own power or piety" (hos idia dynamei e eusebeia) — the two possible explanations Peter dismisses are personal power (dynamis — inherent supernatural ability) and personal piety (eusebeia — godliness, religious devotion that might earn divine favor). Peter and John have neither magical power nor special religious merit that produced this healing. The source lies elsewhere.

"The God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the God of our fathers, has glorified His servant Jesus" (3:13a).

Peter identifies the God who acted. He does not begin with an abstract deity but with the God who is known by his covenant relationships: "the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob" (ho theos Abraam kai [ho theos] Isaak kai [ho theos] Iakob). This is the self-identification of God at the burning bush in Exodus 3:6, 15: "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." By invoking this title, Peter places everything that follows within the stream of Israel's covenant history. What God is doing through Jesus is not something new or foreign; it is the continuation of what the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob has always been doing with and for his people.

"The God of our fathers" (ho theos ton pateron hemon) extends the identification through the whole ancestral line. The word "our" (hemon) is inclusive: Peter, John, and the audience share the same fathers. They are all children of this covenant. This is not outsiders bringing a foreign god; this is Israel's own God acting in Israel's own story.

"Has glorified His servant Jesus" (edoxasen ton paida autou Iesoun). The verb doxazo ("to glorify") and the noun pais ("servant" or "child") are loaded with scriptural resonance. The word pais can mean either "child/son" or "servant." In the LXX, it is the word used for the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah's Servant passages. Isaiah 52:13 (LXX): "Behold, my servant (ho pais mou) shall understand, and he shall be exalted (hypsothsetai) and glorified (doxasthesetai) exceedingly." The exact phrase — God glorifying his servant — comes from Isaiah 52:13, the opening of the fourth Servant passage that continues through Isaiah 53. Peter is identifying Jesus as the figure described in Isaiah's Servant texts. The word "glorified" (edoxasen) in the aorist tense points to a completed act — God has already glorified Jesus, through the resurrection and exaltation. What the crowd is witnessing in this healing is the outworking of that glorification.

The identification of Jesus as God's pais appears four times in these early chapters of Acts (3:13, 26; 4:27, 30), always in the context of prayer or proclamation to Jewish audiences. The early community's primary christological category in these opening chapters is not "Son of God" in the later developed sense but "Servant of God" in the Isaianic sense — the one through whom God accomplishes his purposes for Israel and the nations.

"The one whom you delivered and disowned in the presence of Pilate, when he had decided to release Him" (3:13b).

Peter now turns from God's action to the audience's action. The pronouns shift sharply. "You" (hymeis) delivered and disowned Jesus. The verb paredokate ("you delivered, you handed over") is from paradidomi, a word used throughout the passion narrative for the "handing over" of Jesus — by Judas (Luke 22:4, 6, 21, 22, 48), by the Sanhedrin to Pilate (Luke 23:1), and by Pilate to their will (Luke 23:25). The verb ernesthes ("you disowned, you denied") is from arneomai, meaning to deny, refuse, disown. It is the same word used for Peter's own denial of Jesus (Luke 22:57: "he denied it" — ernesato). Peter, who denied Jesus three times, now charges the crowd with denying Jesus before Pilate. The irony is sharp but unstated.

"In the presence of Pilate, when he had decided to release Him" (kata prosopon Pilatou, krinantos ekeinou apolyein) — Peter emphasizes that Pilate himself had judged (krinantos, "having decided, having given judgment") in favor of release. This aligns with Luke's triple emphasis that Pilate found no guilt in Jesus (Luke 23:4, 14, 22). The crowd's disowning of Jesus was done against the judgment of the Roman governor himself. The charge is intensified: even the pagan authority recognized Jesus's innocence, but "you" disowned him.

"But you disowned the Holy and Righteous One and asked for a murderer to be granted to you" (3:14).

The contrast is devastating. The titles "the Holy and Righteous One" (ton hagion kai dikaion) are not decorative; they are precise scriptural designations. "The Holy One" (ho hagios) echoes the messianic title in Psalm 16:10 (LXX 15:10): "You will not allow your Holy One (ton hosion sou) to see decay" — the very psalm Peter cited at Pentecost to prove the resurrection (Acts 2:27). Isaiah 49:7 describes the Servant as "the Holy One of Israel." "The Righteous One" (ho dikaios) appears in Isaiah 53:11: "the Righteous One, My Servant, will justify the many." The combination of both titles identifies Jesus with the highest categories of Israel's prophetic hope.

Against these titles stands what the crowd asked for: "a murderer" (andra phonea). The word phoneus means "killer, murderer." This refers to Barabbas, who is described in Luke 23:19 as one "thrown into prison for an insurrection made in the city, and for murder" (phonon). The audience chose a man who took life over the one who gives life. The irony is not accidental; it is structural.

"But put to death the Author of life, the one whom God raised from the dead, a fact to which we are witnesses" (3:15).

The title "the Author of life" (ton archegon tes zoes) introduces a new christological designation. The word archegos can mean "author, originator, founder, pioneer, prince, leader." It denotes the one who begins or leads the way, the source or cause. In the LXX, it is used for tribal leaders and military chiefs (Numbers 13:2-3, Judges 11:6). The author of Hebrews uses it for Jesus as "the author of salvation" (Hebrews 2:10) and "the author and perfecter of faith" (Hebrews 12:2). Here it is combined with "life" (zoe): Jesus is the originator of life, the source from which life flows, the leader into life. The paradox is stated with brutal clarity: "you put to death the Author of life." The source of life was killed. The one from whom life comes was subjected to death.

"The one whom God raised from the dead" (hon ho theos egeiren ek nekron) — but death could not hold the Author of life. God raised him. The verb egeiren ("raised") is aorist, denoting a completed historical event. The resurrection is the reversal of the killing: what the audience did (put to death), God undid (raised from the dead). The two actions stand in direct opposition, and the divine action prevails.

"A fact to which we are witnesses" (hou hemeis martyres esmen) — "we" (hemeis) is emphatic. Peter and John, and the broader apostolic community, are eyewitnesses (martyres) of the resurrection. The apostolic qualification defined in Acts 1:22 — to be "a witness of his resurrection" — is being exercised. The testimony is firsthand and personal.

"And on the basis of faith in His name, it is the name of Jesus which has strengthened this man whom you see and know; and the faith which comes through Him has given him this perfect health in the presence of you all" (3:16).

This verse is syntactically complex, but its logic is clear. The healing of the lame man is attributed to two things: the name of Jesus and faith in that name.

"On the basis of faith in His name" (epi te pistei tou onomatos autou) — the phrase "faith in his name" (pistis tou onomatos) denotes trust directed toward the person designated by the name. The "name" is not a magical incantation; it represents the authority and person of Jesus. Faith in the name means confidence in the living, glorified Jesus whose power is accessible.

"The name of Jesus which has strengthened this man" (to onoma autou touton hon theoreite kai oidate esterosen) — the verb esterosen ("strengthened, made firm") is the same root (stereoo) used in verse 7 for the strengthening of the man's feet and ankles. The repetition connects the theological explanation to the physical event.

"Whom you see and know" (hon theoreite kai oidate) — Peter appeals to the audience's own senses and prior knowledge. They can see the man standing. They know who he is. The evidence is in front of them.

"The faith which comes through Him" (he pistis he di autou) — the faith that produced the healing comes "through Him" (di autou), that is, through Jesus. Faith is not self-generated; it is mediated through the one in whom it trusts. The source of the faith and the source of the healing are the same: Jesus.

"Has given him this perfect health" (edoken auto ten holoklerian tauten) — the word holokleria means "wholeness, completeness, perfect soundness." It is used only here in the New Testament. The root components are holos ("whole") and kleros ("lot, portion, part") — literally, having all one's parts, complete integrity. The man is not partially improved; he is completely whole. Every part that was broken is restored.

"In the presence of you all" (apenanti panton hymon) — the healing is public, witnessed, undeniable. Peter is building his case from evidence that the entire crowd can verify.

Verses 17-18: The Concession of Ignorance and the Fulfillment of Prophecy

"And now, brethren, I know that you acted in ignorance, just as your rulers did also" (3:17).

The address shifts from accusation to something approaching mitigation. Peter has just charged the crowd with delivering, denying, and killing the Author of life. Now he grants a concession: "you acted in ignorance" (kata agnoian epraxate). The word agnoia ("ignorance") does not mean the action was not sinful; it means the action was done without full understanding of what was being done. This echoes Jesus's own words from the cross in Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing" (ou gar oidasin ti poiousin). Peter affirms the same assessment: they did not know. The ignorance extends to "your rulers" (hoi archontes hymon) as well — the Sanhedrin, the chief priests, the leaders who engineered the trial. They, too, acted without full comprehension of who they were condemning.

The term "brethren" (adelphoi) is significant. Despite the grave accusation just leveled, Peter addresses the crowd as brothers. They are fellow Israelites, fellow heirs of the covenant. The accusation has not severed the familial bond. The call to repentance that follows depends on this: they are still within the family, still addressable, still reachable.

The category of acting in ignorance has roots in the Torah's distinction between intentional and unintentional sin. Numbers 15:27-29 provides atonement for the person who sins "unintentionally" (LXX: akousioos). Leviticus 4 outlines the sin offering for sins committed "unintentionally" (LXX: akon). The unintentional sinner is still guilty — a sin offering is still required — but the sin is treated differently from deliberate, high-handed transgression (Numbers 15:30-31). Peter is placing the crowd's action in the category of unintentional sin, for which atonement is available.

"But the things which God announced beforehand by the mouth of all the prophets, that His Christ would suffer, He has thus fulfilled" (3:18).

Peter now places the events within the frame of prophetic necessity. What happened to Jesus was not an accident but the fulfillment of what God "announced beforehand" (prokatengellen) through "all the prophets" (dia stomatos panton ton propheton). The claim is sweeping: the suffering of God's Christ is the common testimony of the entire prophetic tradition.

"That His Christ would suffer" (pathein ton Christon autou) — the word pathein ("to suffer") is the same word used in Acts 1:3 ("after His suffering") and in Luke 24:26 ("Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things?"). The risen Jesus's own hermeneutic from Luke 24:25-27 — that Moses and all the prophets testified to the Christ's suffering — is now being proclaimed publicly by Peter. The private instruction that Jesus gave to the disciples on the Emmaus road and in the upper room is becoming public proclamation.

"He has thus fulfilled" (eplerosen houtos) — the word eplerosen ("fulfilled") is the same word used by Peter in Acts 1:16 ("the scripture had to be fulfilled") and throughout Luke's Gospel for the completion of prophetic expectation. The suffering of Jesus is the content of the fulfillment; the agency of fulfillment belongs to God. The crowd acted in ignorance; God acted through their actions to accomplish what he had announced. Human culpability and divine sovereignty are held together without either being dissolved.

Verses 19-21: The Call to Repentance and the Promise of Restoration

"Therefore repent and return, so that your sins may be wiped away, in order that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord" (3:19).

"Therefore" (oun) — the logical connector draws the conclusion from everything Peter has said. Because you acted in ignorance, because God fulfilled what he prophesied, because you still stand within the covenant relationship, therefore there is an appropriate response. That response is twofold.

"Repent" (metanoesate) — the aorist imperative calls for a decisive act: change your mind, change your direction. This is the same verb Peter used at Pentecost (Acts 2:38: "Repent"). Repentance is the consistent requirement in both speeches.

"And return" (kai epistrepsate) — the verb epistrepho means "to turn, to turn back, to return." In the LXX, this is the standard translation of the Hebrew shuv, the prophetic call to return to God that pervades the prophetic literature. Jeremiah 3:12: "Return, faithless Israel." Hosea 14:1: "Return, O Israel, to the LORD your God." Malachi 3:7: "Return to Me, and I will return to you." Joel 2:12-13: "Return to Me with all your heart." The pairing of "repent and return" covers the full movement: the inward change of mind (metanoeo) and the outward turn of direction back toward God (epistrepho). Luke's Gospel used this pair programmatically: Jesus was sent to "turn" (epistrepsai) the hearts of the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous (Luke 1:17, describing John the Baptist's mission quoting Malachi 4:6).

"So that your sins may be wiped away" (eis to exaleiphthenai hymon tas hamartias) — the verb exaleipho means "to wipe away, to wipe out, to blot out, to erase." In the LXX, it is used for God's obliteration of sin. Psalm 51:1 (LXX 50:3): "According to the greatness of your mercy, blot out (exaleipson) my transgression." Psalm 51:9 (LXX 50:11): "Blot out (exaleipson) all my iniquities." Isaiah 43:25: "I, even I, am the one who wipes out (exaleiphon) your transgressions for My own sake." The imagery is of writing on a surface being wiped clean — the record of sin erased completely. At Pentecost, Peter promised "the forgiveness of your sins" (aphesin ton hamartion, Acts 2:38); here the metaphor shifts from release (aphesis) to erasure (exaleipsis). The sins are not merely pardoned; they are obliterated.

"In order that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord" (hopos an elthosin kairoi anapsyxeos apo prosopou tou kyriou) — this phrase introduces a promise that goes beyond individual forgiveness. The word kairoi ("times, seasons, appointed moments") is the same word Jesus used in Acts 1:7 for the "times" the Father has fixed by his own authority. The word anapsyxis ("refreshing, relief, respite") appears only here in the New Testament. Its root is anapsycho, meaning "to cool, to refresh, to revive." In the LXX, the related verb appears in Exodus 23:12 for the rest given to servants and animals on the Sabbath, and in 2 Samuel 16:14 for David being refreshed after exhaustion. The noun conveys relief after affliction, coolness after heat, revival after exhaustion.

"From the presence of the Lord" (apo prosopou tou kyriou) — literally "from the face of the Lord." The phrase echoes the language of God's presence in the temple (Psalm 16:11: "In your presence [para prosopou sou] is fullness of joy") and in the prophetic visions of restoration. The "times of refreshing" are not merely private spiritual comfort but a corporate, historical reality — seasons of divine favor and renewal that come from God's own presence. The connection to the prophetic hope of Israel's restoration is unmistakable. The "refreshing" is what the prophets described: the age when God's presence would dwell among his people in fullness.

"And that He may send Jesus, the Christ appointed for you" (3:20).

The construction kai aposteilei ton prokecheirismenon hymin Christon Iesoun is remarkable. The verb apostello ("to send") is in the aorist subjunctive, indicating purpose: God's aim, contingent upon the repentance called for in verse 19, is to send the Christ. The word prokecheirismenon ("appointed, designated, chosen beforehand") is from procheirizomai, meaning "to appoint in advance, to designate." Jesus is described as the Christ who has been appointed "for you" (hymin) — for this audience, for Israel.

The logic of the sentence is consequential: repent and return --> your sins will be wiped away --> times of refreshing will come --> God will send the appointed Christ. The repentance of Israel is linked to a chain of consequences that culminates in the sending of the Messiah. This language draws on the prophetic pattern in which Israel's repentance precedes God's restorative action. Deuteronomy 30:1-3 establishes this pattern: "when you return to the LORD your God and obey him... then the LORD your God will restore your fortunes and have compassion on you." Hosea 6:1-3: "Come, let us return to the LORD... He will come to us like the rain."

"Whom heaven must receive until the period of restoration of all things about which God spoke by the mouth of His holy prophets from ancient time" (3:21).

"Whom heaven must receive" (hon dei ouranon men dexasthai) — the word dei ("must, it is necessary") signals divine necessity, the same word that governs the entire narrative of God's plan in Luke-Acts. Heaven must receive Jesus. The ascension is not an interruption but a necessity. Jesus is in heaven because it is where he must be during this period.

"Until" (achri) — this is a temporal boundary. Heaven receives Jesus until a specified point. The receiving is temporary, not permanent. There is an endpoint.

"The period of restoration of all things" (chronon apokatastaseos panton) — this is one of the most significant phrases in the speech. The word apokatastasis means "restoration, reconstitution, reestablishment." It is formed from the same root (apokathistemi) that the disciples used in their question in Acts 1:6: "Are you restoring (apokathistaneis) the kingdom to Israel?" Peter's speech now answers the question that Jesus redirected in Acts 1:7. Jesus told the disciples it was not for them to know the "times" (chronous); Peter now speaks of the "period" (chronon) of the restoration. The restoration the disciples hoped for is real and coming. It is not dismissed but located within the prophetic framework: it happens at the appointed time, and heaven must hold Jesus until that time arrives.

The word panton ("of all things") extends the scope beyond Israel's national restoration to a cosmic renewal. The "restoration of all things" encompasses what the prophets described: the renewal of creation, the reversal of the curse, the fulfillment of every promise God made through his prophets. Isaiah 65:17: "I create new heavens and a new earth." Isaiah 11:6-9: the peaceable kingdom. Ezekiel 37: the valley of dry bones brought to life. Amos 9:11-15: the rebuilding of David's booth and the restoration of the land. The "restoration of all things" is the sum total of the prophetic hope.

"About which God spoke by the mouth of His holy prophets from ancient time" (hon elalesen ho theos dia stomatos ton hagion ap aionos autou propheton) — the restoration is not a new idea. It is what God has been speaking about through his prophets "from ancient time" (ap aionos, literally "from the age," meaning from the earliest times). The claim is that the entire prophetic tradition, from its beginning, has been pointing toward this restoration.

Verses 22-23: The Prophet Like Moses

"Moses said, 'The Lord God will raise up for you a prophet like me from your brethren; to Him you shall give heed to everything He says to you. And it will be that every soul that does not heed that prophet shall be utterly destroyed from among the people'" (3:22-23).

Peter now cites a specific prophetic text: Deuteronomy 18:15-19. The citation is not exact but follows the LXX closely, with some adaptation. Moses is quoted as speaking about a future figure — "a prophet like me" (propheten ... hos eme) — whom God will "raise up" (anastesei) from among the people of Israel ("from your brethren," ek ton adelphon hymon).

The verb anastesei ("will raise up") is from anistemi, which means both "to raise up" (in the sense of bringing onto the scene, appointing) and "to raise from the dead" (the same word used for resurrection). The double meaning is almost certainly intentional. God "raised up" Jesus in both senses: he raised him up as the Prophet like Moses during his earthly ministry, and he raised him from the dead after his crucifixion. The same verb serves both meanings, and Peter's audience, having just heard about the resurrection (v. 15), would hear the resonance.

"A prophet like me" (propheten hos eme) — the comparison to Moses is the highest possible prophetic designation in Israel's framework. Moses was unique among the prophets: "there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10). To say that God will raise up a prophet "like Moses" is to say that this figure will have the same unmediated access to God, the same authority to deliver divine commands, the same role as mediator of a covenant. The Samaritan community expected this prophet (the Taheb); many Jews expected a prophetic figure at the end of the age (cf. the question posed to John the Baptist in John 1:21: "Are you the Prophet?"). Peter identifies Jesus as this figure.

The comparison is also structural. Moses delivered Israel from bondage in Egypt; Jesus delivers from the bondage of sin. Moses mediated the first covenant at Sinai; Jesus mediates a new covenant (Luke 22:20). Moses led the people through the wilderness to the promised land; Jesus leads through death to life. Moses instituted the Passover; Jesus was the Passover (Luke 22:15-16, 1 Corinthians 5:7). The typological correspondence between Moses and Jesus runs throughout Luke-Acts and reaches its explicit statement here.

"To Him you shall give heed to everything He says to you" (auton akousesthe kata panta hosa an lalese pros hymas) — the command to listen is absolute: "everything" (kata panta) he says. The verb akousesthe ("you shall hear, you shall listen to, you shall obey") echoes the divine command at the transfiguration: "This is My Son, My Chosen One; listen to Him!" (Luke 9:35, akouete autou). The voice from the cloud at the transfiguration and the Mosaic prophecy in Deuteronomy 18 issue the same command: listen to this one.

"And it will be that every soul that does not heed that prophet shall be utterly destroyed from among the people" (3:23). This is adapted from Deuteronomy 18:19 ("I Myself will require it of him") combined with language from Leviticus 23:29, which prescribes that anyone who does not practice self-denial on the Day of Atonement "shall be cut off from his people" (exolethreuthsetai ek tou laou autes). The verb exolethreuthsetai ("shall be utterly destroyed") is the standard LXX term for being "cut off" from the covenant community. Peter is applying the Mosaic penalty for disobedience to the prophetic figure: those who refuse to listen to the Prophet like Moses will be severed from the people of God.

The warning is severe and specific. It is addressed to a Jewish audience at the temple. The implication is direct: Jesus is the Prophet like Moses; to refuse to listen to him is to face the penalty that Moses himself prescribed — exclusion from the people. This is not a threat directed at outsiders; it is a warning directed at insiders. Those who are currently within the covenant community will be cut off from it if they do not heed the Prophet that Moses foretold.

Verses 24-26: The Prophets, the Covenant, and the Mission to Israel First

"And likewise, all the prophets who have spoken, from Samuel and his successors onward, also announced these days" (3:24).

Peter extends the prophetic testimony beyond Moses. "All the prophets" (pantes ... hoi prophetai) — the claim is comprehensive. Samuel is named as the beginning point because Samuel is traditionally regarded as the first of the prophetic succession after Moses. He anointed both Saul and David, and he inaugurated the prophetic tradition that continued through the monarchy. "From Samuel and his successors onward" (apo Samouel kai ton kathexes) covers the entire prophetic corpus — the former prophets (Joshua through Kings), the latter prophets (Isaiah through Malachi), and all who spoke God's word to Israel. The claim is that the events now unfolding — the suffering and glorification of God's Servant, the call to repentance, the promise of restoration — are what all these prophets were announcing.

"These days" (tas hemeras tautas) — the present moment. Peter is claiming that the audience is living in the days that the entire prophetic tradition pointed toward. The time of fulfillment is not future; it is now. "These days" are the days of the Messiah's coming, the outpouring of the Spirit, the call to repentance, and the opening of the way to restoration.

"It is you who are the sons of the prophets and of the covenant which God made with your fathers, saying to Abraham, 'And in your seed all the families of the earth shall be blessed'" (3:25).

"It is you" (hymeis este) — the pronoun is emphatic. Peter is telling his audience who they are, not merely addressing them. The identification has two parts.

First, "sons of the prophets" (huioi ton propheton). In Semitic usage, "sons of" denotes belonging to, participation in, inheritance of. To be "sons of the prophets" means to be heirs of the prophetic tradition, the ones to whom the prophets' words were addressed, the inheritors of the prophetic legacy. The prophets spoke for your benefit. Their message belongs to you.

Second, "sons of the covenant which God made with your fathers" (huioi tes diathekes hes ho theos dietheto pros tous pateras hymon). They are not merely descended from the patriarchs biologically; they are participants in the covenant relationship that God established with those patriarchs. The covenant is specified by its content: the promise to Abraham.

"Saying to Abraham, 'And in your seed all the families of the earth shall be blessed'" (legon pros Abraam, Kai en to spermati sou [en]eulogethesentai pasai hai patriai tes ges). The citation combines Genesis 22:18 ("in your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed") and Genesis 12:3 ("in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed"). The word patriai ("families, clans") follows Genesis 12:3 (LXX: phylai), while "in your seed" (en to spermati sou) follows Genesis 22:18. The Abrahamic promise is the foundation: God's covenant with Abraham was never limited to Abraham's physical descendants alone; it was always aimed at "all the families of the earth." The blessing that flows through Abraham's seed is universal in scope.

Peter's citation of the Abrahamic covenant establishes two things simultaneously. First, the audience is within the covenant — they are heirs of the promise, sons of the covenant. Second, the covenant always had a horizon beyond Israel — the blessing was intended for all the families of the earth. Both truths will matter as the narrative of Acts unfolds: the gospel goes to Israel first (v. 26), and through Israel it reaches the nations (the trajectory of Acts 1:8).

"For you first, God raised up His Servant and sent Him to bless you by turning every one of you from your wicked ways" (3:26).

"For you first" (hymin proton) — the adverb proton ("first") is critical. It implies a sequence: first to you, then to others. The gospel mission begins with Israel. This is consistent with Luke 24:47 ("beginning from Jerusalem"), Acts 1:8 (Jerusalem first, then Judea and Samaria, then the end of the earth), and the Abrahamic promise just cited (the blessing comes through Abraham's seed to all families). Israel's priority is not exclusive; it is sequential. They are first, not only.

"God raised up His Servant" (ho theos anastesas ton paida autou) — the verb anastesas is again the ambiguous anistemi: "raised up" can mean both "brought onto the scene" (appointed, commissioned) and "raised from the dead." Both meanings are active. God brought Jesus onto the scene as his Servant during the ministry, and God raised Jesus from the dead after the crucifixion. The word pais ("servant") returns for the second time in the speech (first in v. 13), reinforcing the Isaianic Servant identification.

"Sent Him to bless you" (apesteilen auton eulogounta hymas) — the verb apostello ("sent") is the root of "apostle." God sent Jesus to Israel. The purpose of the sending is stated: "to bless you" (eulogounta hymas). The blessing is the content of the Abrahamic covenant: "in your seed all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (v. 25). Jesus, as Abraham's seed, is the agent of that blessing. And the blessing begins with Israel.

"By turning every one of you from your wicked ways" (en to apostrephein hekaston apo ton ponerion hymon) — the content of the blessing is specified, and it is not what most would expect. The blessing is not material prosperity, military victory, or political independence. The blessing is repentance — being turned away from wickedness. The verb apostrephein ("to turn away") is the complement of epistrephein ("to turn toward") in verse 19. God turns them away from wickedness so that they may turn toward him. Repentance is itself the blessing. The ability to turn from sin is the gift that God sends through his Servant.

The phrase "every one of you" (hekaston) is singular and individual. The corporate address ("you," plural, the people of Israel) gives way to an individual application: every single person. The blessing of turning from wickedness applies person by person, not merely to the nation as an undifferentiated mass.

"From your wicked ways" (apo ton ponerion hymon) — the word ponerion ("wicked things, evil deeds") is the plural of poneria. These are the specific sins that require the repentance Peter has been calling for. In the immediate context, the chief "wicked way" is the rejection and killing of the Servant — but the call is broader than a single act. The entire orientation of life away from God's purposes is what needs to be reversed.

Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. The chapter provides the first specific example of the "signs and wonders" summarized in Acts 2:43. The general statement that "many wonders and signs were taking place through the apostles" is now illustrated with a concrete case. The author shows what apostolic power looks like in practice: a congenital condition healed instantaneously, in public, at the temple, in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene.

2. The healing of the lame man is presented as fulfillment of Isaiah 35. The specific vocabulary — the strengthening of feet and ankles, the leaping, the entering of sacred space — echoes Isaiah's vision of eschatological restoration. The healing is not merely a compassionate act for one man; it is a sign that the age of restoration has begun. What Isaiah described is happening.

3. Peter's speech advances the christological argument beyond the Pentecost sermon. At Pentecost, Peter established that Jesus is Lord and Christ. Here, Peter adds three further identifications: Jesus is God's Servant (the figure of Isaiah 52-53), the Holy and Righteous One, the Author of Life, and the Prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18). Each title locates Jesus within a specific strand of Israel's prophetic tradition, and together they build a comprehensive portrait. Jesus is not merely exalted; he is the culmination of everything Israel's scriptures anticipated.

4. The speech introduces the category of ignorance as a mitigating factor. The audience is guilty of rejecting and killing the Author of life. But they acted in ignorance, as did their rulers. This categorization matters because it places their sin within the Torah's framework for unintentional transgression, for which atonement is available (Leviticus 4, Numbers 15:27-29). The door of repentance remains open precisely because the sin was committed without full knowledge.

5. The call to repentance includes a promise of national and cosmic scope. Repentance will produce not only individual forgiveness ("your sins wiped away") but "times of refreshing from the presence of the Lord" and ultimately "the restoration of all things." Peter connects individual repentance to the cosmic prophetic hope. The restoration that the disciples asked about in Acts 1:6 is affirmed here as real and coming, linked to the prophetic testimony "from ancient time." Heaven must hold Jesus until that restoration arrives. The audience's repentance is presented as part of the pathway toward that consummation.

6. The citation of Deuteronomy 18 introduces a warning alongside the invitation. The Prophet like Moses must be heeded; those who refuse will be "utterly destroyed from among the people." This is not a threat of metaphysical punishment but a warning of covenant excision. Those who do not listen to the Prophet will be cut off from the people of God. For a Jewish audience at the temple, this is the most serious possible consequence: loss of membership in the covenant community. The text Peter cites (Leviticus 23:29 language combined with Deuteronomy 18:19) uses the standard formula for being "cut off" from Israel. The implication is that hearing and heeding Jesus is now the criterion for remaining within the covenant people.

7. The Abrahamic covenant is cited to establish both Israel's priority and the universal horizon of the gospel. Peter reminds his audience that they are "sons of the covenant" that God made with Abraham, and that this covenant promised blessing to "all the families of the earth." Israel's election is not denied but reaffirmed. At the same time, the purpose of that election is specified: through Abraham's seed (Jesus), blessing is to reach all peoples. The phrase "for you first" (hymin proton) implies "then to others." This lays the theological groundwork for the Gentile mission that will unfold in the second half of Acts.

8. The content of the blessing is defined as moral transformation, not material prosperity. God sent his Servant "to bless you by turning every one of you from your wicked ways." The Abrahamic blessing, as Peter presents it here, is not wealth, military power, or political sovereignty. It is the power to repent — the ability to turn from wickedness. This redefines the nature of the messianic blessing in terms that echo the prophetic tradition: Ezekiel 36:26-27 ("I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you... I will cause you to walk in My statutes"); Jeremiah 31:33 ("I will put My law within them and on their heart I will write it"). The blessing is interior transformation that produces changed behavior.

9. The chapter's placement within Acts 1-4 serves the author's structural purpose. Acts 1 prepared the community. Acts 2 brought the Spirit and the first proclamation. Acts 3 shows the first sign and the deepened proclamation. Acts 4 brings the first opposition. The progression is deliberate: preparation, power, proclamation, persecution. Each chapter advances the narrative one step further along the trajectory that will carry the story from Jerusalem to the end of the earth. The healing of the lame man and Peter's speech are the catalyst that draws the attention of the temple authorities, setting in motion the conflict between the apostolic community and the religious leadership that will dominate Acts 3-7.

10. Peter's speech answers the question left open from Acts 1:6. The disciples asked whether Jesus was now restoring the kingdom to Israel. Jesus declined to answer directly. Peter's speech in Acts 3 provides the framework: the restoration of all things is real and prophesied, but heaven must receive Jesus "until" that time. In the interim, the task is repentance and the receiving of the blessing that God's Servant brings. The restoration is not denied, delayed indefinitely, or spiritualized away; it is located in its proper sequence. Repentance comes first. Then refreshing. Then the sending of the Christ. Then the restoration of all things. The order matters, and the audience's response matters to the unfolding of the sequence.
