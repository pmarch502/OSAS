Colossians 4 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Colossians 3): Chapter 3 opened by drawing the practical consequence of the theological argument completed in chapter 2. The pivotal statement was: ei oun synegerthete to Christo, ta ano zeteite — "if then you were raised with Christ, seek the things above" (3:1). The verb synegerthete ("you were raised together with") picked up directly from 2:12-13, where the Colossians were described as having been buried and raised with Christ through faith. The oun ("therefore") made the connection explicit: because the theological reality of 2:6-15 is true — because you are complete in Christ, because you died with him to the stoicheia, because the powers have been defeated — now live accordingly.

The chapter unfolded in two broad movements. The first (3:1-17) established the pattern of the new life. The Colossians were told to "seek the things above" (3:1) and to "set your minds on things above, not on things on the earth" (3:2), grounded in the declaration that they died and their "life is hidden with Christ in God" (3:3). When Christ who is "your life" is revealed, they too will be revealed with him in glory (3:4). On this basis, they were commanded to "put to death" (nekrosate) the earthly members — porneia ("sexual immorality"), akatharsia ("impurity"), pathos ("passion"), epithymia kake ("evil desire"), and pleonexia ("greed"), which is idolatry (3:5). They were told to "put away" (apothesthe) orge ("anger"), thymos ("rage"), kakia ("malice"), blasphemia ("slander"), and aischrologia ("obscene talk") (3:8). They must not lie to one another, having "put off the old self" (ton palaion anthropon) with its practices and having "put on the new self" (ton neon), which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator (3:9-10). In this renewed humanity, the old identity markers dissolve: "there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all and in all" (3:11). The Colossians were then instructed to "put on" (endysasthe) compassion, kindness, humility (tapeinophrosyne — the same word used pejoratively of the philosophy's practitioners in 2:18, but now given its genuine, Christ-shaped content), gentleness, patience, bearing with one another and forgiving one another (3:12-13). Above all these, they were to put on love (agape), "which is the bond of perfection" (syndesmos tes teleiotetos, 3:14). The peace of Christ was to rule (brabeueto — the same root as katabrabeueto in 2:18, where the philosophy's proponents tried to "disqualify" them; now Christ's peace, not a self-appointed judge, acts as umpire) in their hearts, in the one body to which they were called (3:15). The word of Christ was to dwell richly among them (3:16), and everything they did was to be done in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him (3:17).

The second movement (3:18-4:1) applied these principles to specific relationships within the household. Wives were addressed first: "be subject to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord" (3:18). Husbands: "love your wives and do not be harsh toward them" (3:19). Children: "obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord" (3:20). Fathers: "do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged" (3:21). Slaves (douloi): "obey in everything those who are your masters according to the flesh" (3:22), not with eye-service as people-pleasers, but with sincerity of heart, fearing the Lord (3:22). Whatever they do, they are to work heartily (ek psyches, "from the soul"), as for the Lord and not for men (3:23), knowing that from the Lord they will receive the inheritance (ten antapodosin tes kleronomias) as a reward; they serve the Lord Christ (3:24). The one who does wrong will be repaid for his wrongdoing, and there is no partiality (prosopolempsia, 3:25). This household code, running from 3:18 through 4:1, is a single literary unit. The instruction to masters in 4:1 is its final element, not the beginning of a new section.

What the author argues in Colossians 4: Chapter 4 completes the letter in three movements. First, the final instruction of the household code addresses masters (4:1). Second, a series of practical exhortations addresses the community's prayer life and its conduct toward those outside the community (4:2-6). Third, an extended section of personal greetings, commendations, and instructions closes the letter (4:7-18). These closing materials are not mere formalities; they embody the letter's themes in concrete relationships and carry the argument's implications into the network of real persons who constitute the Messiah's body in the Lycus Valley.

How chapter 4 connects to what precedes: The household code of 3:18-4:1 is the most specific application of the principle stated in 3:17: "whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him." The instruction to masters (4:1) completes the final pair in the code (slaves/masters, 3:22-4:1), just as the instructions to wives/husbands (3:18-19) and children/fathers (3:20-21) completed earlier pairs. The exhortations of 4:2-6 then move from the internal ordering of the household to the community's posture toward the outside world — prayer, speech, and engagement with "those outside" (hoi exo, 4:5). The personal greetings of 4:7-18 operationalize the letter's theology: the people named are the ligaments and joints (2:19) through whom the body is nourished and knit together. The final chapter does not abandon the letter's argument; it embodies it in specific instructions, specific relationships, and specific persons.

Verse 1: The Final Instruction of the Household Code — Masters

"Masters, grant to your slaves justice and fairness, knowing that you too have a Master in heaven" (4:1).

The vocative hoi kyrioi ("masters, lords") addresses slave owners in the community. The word kyrios ("lord, master, owner") is the same word used throughout the letter for Christ: "Christ Jesus the Lord" (ton Christon Iesoun ton kyrion, 2:6), "the Lord Christ" (to kyrio Christo, 3:24). The verbal overlap is not accidental; it is the hinge on which the instruction turns. The earthly kyrios must recognize the heavenly kyrios. The word creates an implicit demotion: the slave owner who is called kyrios by his household is himself a doulos in relation to the true kyrios.

The command is: to dikaion kai ten isoteta tois doulois parechesthe — "provide to your slaves what is just and what is equal" or "grant to your slaves justice and fairness." The word dikaion ("justice, what is right, what is due") denotes what is owed — what a person has a right to receive. In the LXX, dikaios and its cognates describe the character of God's dealings (Deuteronomy 32:4, "a God of faithfulness and without injustice, just and upright") and the standard required of Israel's rulers and judges (Deuteronomy 16:18-20, "you shall judge the people with righteous judgment"). The word isoteta ("equality, fairness, equity") is striking. The noun isotes means "equality" in its basic sense — the condition of being equal, of being on the same level. In 2 Corinthians 8:13-14, Paul used isotes to describe financial equity among communities. Here the word does not necessarily mean that masters must treat slaves as social equals in every respect — the household code does not abolish the social institution — but it demands a fairness that recognizes the slave's dignity and rights before God. The pairing of dikaion and isoteta creates a standard that reaches beyond the minimum legal obligations of Roman slave law, which gave the dominus near-absolute power over the servus. Roman law treated slaves as property (res); the instruction here treats them as persons to whom justice and equity are owed.

The ground of the command is stated: eidotes hoti kai hymeis echete kyrion en ourano — "knowing that you also have a Master in heaven." The participle eidotes ("knowing") appeals to what the masters already understand from the gospel. The pronoun kai hymeis ("you also, you too") is emphatic: the same "also" that applies to slaves applies to masters. Just as the slave serves the Lord Christ (3:24), the master serves the Lord in heaven. The phrase en ourano ("in heaven") locates this Master where 3:1 told the community to seek the things above: "where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God." The heavenly Master is Christ, the one in whom "all the fullness of deity dwells bodily" (2:9), the one who is "head of every rule and authority" (2:10). The earthly master's authority is relativized by the heavenly Master's authority: you are a kyrios, but you have a kyrios. Your power over your slaves is not absolute; it is exercised under the oversight of one whose judgment is without prosopolempsia — "partiality" (3:25). The instruction transforms the master-slave relationship without abolishing it: it subjects the earthly kyrios to the authority of the heavenly one and requires that his treatment of slaves correspond to the justice and equity that the heavenly Master exercises.

The placement of this instruction at the head of chapter 4 (rather than at the end of chapter 3) is an artifact of later chapter division, not a marker of thematic shift. The household code runs continuously from 3:18 through 4:1. The instruction to masters is the last word of the code, completing the slaves-masters pair that began at 3:22. Its position as the code's final instruction gives it rhetorical weight: the last word of the household section addresses those with the most social power and subjects that power to the heavenly kyrios.

Verses 2-4: Prayer — Persistence, Alertness, and the Open Door

"Devote yourselves to prayer, keeping alert in it with thanksgiving" (4:2).

The imperative proskarterite ("devote yourselves, persist, continue steadfastly") is a strong word. The verb proskartereo means "to be devoted to, to persist in, to adhere firmly to, to attend constantly to." In Acts 1:14, the disciples proskarterountes ("were devoting themselves") to prayer. In Acts 2:42, the Jerusalem community proskarterountes ("devoted themselves") to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers. In Romans 12:12, believers are te proseuchē proskarterountes — "devoted to prayer." The word connotes not occasional prayer but habitual, determined persistence in prayer. The dative te proseuchē ("to prayer") identifies the activity to which they are to be devoted.

Two participles qualify the manner of this devotion. First: gregorountes en aute — "keeping alert in it" or "being watchful in it." The verb gregoreo ("to watch, to stay awake, to be vigilant") carries associations with eschatological watchfulness. In the Olivet discourse, Jesus commanded: gregoreite, "watch" (Mark 13:35, 37; Matthew 24:42; 25:13; Luke 21:36). The word describes the posture of someone who stays awake through the night, alert for the master's return or the enemy's approach. In Colossians' argument, where the community faces the danger of being captivated by a rival philosophy (2:8) and shifted from the gospel's hope (1:23), the call to watchfulness in prayer has concrete application: they are to be spiritually alert, not drowsy or distracted, aware of the threats to their fidelity.

Second: en eucharistia — "with thanksgiving." The word eucharistia ("thanksgiving, gratitude") has appeared repeatedly in the letter as a marker of the community's proper stance. In 1:12, Paul gave thanks to the Father who qualified them for the inheritance. In 2:7, they were to be "abounding in thanksgiving." In 3:15, they were called to "be thankful" (eucharistoi ginesthe). In 3:16-17, thanksgiving accompanied their singing, their worship, and everything done in the Lord's name. The recurrence is not repetitive but structural: thanksgiving is the constant accompaniment of a community that grasps the sufficiency of what God has done in Christ. Those who understand that they are complete in him (2:10) and that the mystery — Christ among them — contains all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge (2:3) respond with gratitude, not with anxious striving after supplementary spiritual achievements.

"Praying at the same time for us as well, that God will open up to us a door for the word, so that we may speak forth the mystery of Christ, for which I have also been imprisoned" (4:3).

The participle proseuchomenoi ("praying") adds a specific request to the general command. The phrase hama kai peri hemon ("at the same time also for us") asks the community to include Paul and his circle in their prayers. The first-person plural hemon ("us") encompasses Paul and Timothy (named as co-senders in 1:1) and likely the wider circle of co-workers who will be named in 4:7-14.

The content of the requested prayer is that God would "open a door for the word" (hina ho theos anoixe hemin thyran tou logou). The metaphor of the "open door" (thyra, "door, gate, entrance") appears in Paul's other letters for opportunities for gospel proclamation: 1 Corinthians 16:9, "a great and effective door has opened to me" (thyra moi aneogen megale kai energes); 2 Corinthians 2:12, "a door was opened for me in the Lord" (thyras moi aneogmenes en kyrio). In Acts 14:27, God "opened a door of faith to the Gentiles." The "door" is not a literal entrance but a metaphor for an opportunity, an opening, an occasion for the word to advance. The prayer is not for personal release from imprisonment (though that might be implied) but for the advancement of the message. The "word" (logos) is identified by the purpose clause: lalesai to mysterion tou Christou — "to speak the mystery of Christ." The word mysterion ("mystery") returns from its central role in the letter's argument. In 1:26-27, the mystery was described as that which was "hidden from ages and generations but has now been manifested to his saints," identified as "Christ in you, the hope of glory." In 2:2, Paul's struggle was directed toward the community's attainment of "the knowledge of God's mystery, which is Christ." The mystery is the letter's term for the core content of the gospel: the Messiah present among the Gentile nations. Paul's prayer request is that opportunity would arise for this specific message to be spoken.

The phrase di' ho kai dedemai — "for which I have also been imprisoned" or "on account of which I am also bound" — identifies Paul's circumstance. The verb deo ("to bind, to tie, to imprison") in the perfect passive dedemai ("I have been bound and remain bound") describes his current state: he is in chains. The relative pronoun ho ("which") with the preposition di' ("on account of") specifies that his imprisonment is the direct result of his proclamation of the mystery of Christ. His captivity is not incidental to his message; it is caused by it. The detail grounds the letter's authority in concrete suffering. Paul does not write from a position of comfort but from confinement — a reality that lends weight to his claim that the gospel is worth the cost and that the rival philosophy's comfortable accommodations with the cosmic powers offer a false security.

"That I may make it clear in the way I ought to speak" (4:4).

The hina clause states the second purpose of the requested prayer: hina phaneroso auto — "that I may make it manifest." The verb phaneroo ("to make manifest, to reveal, to make clear, to make visible") appeared in 1:26 for the manifestation of the mystery ("has now been manifested to his saints") and in 3:4 for the future revelation of Christ and the community with him ("when Christ who is your life is manifested"). The same verb now describes Paul's task: to make the mystery visible, clear, and accessible to his hearers. The phrase hos dei me lalesai — "as I ought to speak" or "as it is necessary for me to speak" — adds the element of obligation. The verb dei ("it is necessary, one must, one ought") indicates that Paul's manner of speaking the mystery is not a matter of preference but of necessity. There is a way the mystery must be spoken — clearly, faithfully, without distortion — and Paul asks for prayer that he will speak it in exactly that way. The request implicitly acknowledges that faithful proclamation is not automatic even for an apostle; it requires divine provision and the community's intercession.

Verses 5-6: Conduct Toward Those Outside

"Conduct yourselves with wisdom toward outsiders, making the most of the time" (4:5).

The imperative peripateite ("walk, conduct yourselves") returns from its programmatic use earlier in the letter: in 2:6, the community was commanded to "walk in him" (en auto peripateite); in 3:7, they formerly "walked" in the vices they have now put off. The manner of walking here is specified: en sophia — "in wisdom." The word sophia ("wisdom") is charged in this letter's context. In 2:3, "all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" were declared to reside in Christ. In 2:23, the ascetic regulations of the rival philosophy were exposed as having only an "appearance of wisdom" (logon sophias) but no real substance. The wisdom in which the Colossians are to walk is not the philosophy's counterfeit wisdom but the genuine article — the wisdom stored in the Messiah, the wisdom that comes from knowing God's mystery (2:2-3). Their conduct is to be governed by this wisdom, not by the human traditions and cosmic regulations of the rival teaching.

The objects of this wise conduct are designated: pros tous exo — "toward those outside." The phrase hoi exo ("those outside, outsiders") is a standard early Christian designation for those who are not part of the community of believers. Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 5:12-13: "what have I to do with judging those outside (tous exo)? . . . God judges those outside." In 1 Thessalonians 4:12, the community is told to "walk properly toward those outside" (pros tous exo). Mark 4:11 uses the phrase for those not among Jesus's inner circle: "to those outside (tois exo) everything is in parables." The designation creates a boundary between the community and the wider world without establishing a hostile posture toward it. The community is to engage "those outside" — not to withdraw from them, not to fear them, and not to antagonize them, but to walk toward them in wisdom.

The participle exagorazomenoi ton kairon — "making the most of the time" or "buying up the opportunity" or "redeeming the time" — adds urgency. The verb exagorazo ("to buy out, to buy up, to redeem, to acquire by purchase") is a commercial metaphor: purchasing something from the marketplace. In Galatians 3:13 and 4:5, the same verb described Christ "redeeming" (exagorasas) those under the law. Here the object is ton kairon — "the time" or "the opportune moment." The word kairos differs from chronos: chronos is time in its duration and sequence; kairos is time in its quality and opportunity — the right moment, the decisive occasion, the window of possibility. The community is to buy up every kairos — to seize every opportune moment for wise engagement with outsiders. The commercial metaphor suggests a marketplace where valuable opportunities are available but will not last forever. The community must be alert (cf. the gregorountes of 4:2) to recognize and acquire these moments.

"Let your speech always be with grace, as though seasoned with salt, so that you will know how you should respond to each person" (4:6).

The command turns to speech specifically: ho logos hymon pantote en chariti — "your word always in grace" or "let your speech always be with grace." The word logos ("word, speech, discourse") here refers to the community's everyday communication, especially in encounters with outsiders. The qualifier en chariti ("in grace") specifies the quality of their speech: it is to be characterized by charis — "grace, favor, attractiveness, generosity of spirit." The word charis, which in the letter's opening blessing denoted the unmerited favor of God (1:2, "grace to you and peace"), here describes the quality of human speech that reflects divine character. Grace-filled speech is not harsh, not combative, not defensive, not manipulative; it is generous, winsome, and attuned to the hearer.

The metaphor halati ertymenos — "seasoned with salt" — draws on the function of salt in the ancient world. Salt preserved food, enhanced flavor, and in some contexts symbolized the binding quality of covenant (Numbers 18:19, "a covenant of salt"; 2 Chronicles 13:5, "a covenant of salt"). In the LXX, salt appears in Leviticus 2:13 in connection with offerings: "with all your offerings you shall offer salt" — the salt of the covenant was not to be absent from any grain offering. Salt made food palatable and prevented corruption. Speech "seasoned with salt" is speech that has flavor rather than blandness, that preserves truth rather than allowing it to decay, and that maintains the covenant quality of faithfulness in communication. The metaphor also connects to Jesus's description of his followers as "the salt of the earth" (Matthew 5:13; Mark 9:50; Luke 14:34), though the specific application here is to conversation rather than to the community's broader social function.

The purpose: eidenai pos dei hymas heni hekasto apokrinesthai — "so that you may know how you ought to answer each person." The verb apokrinesthai ("to answer, to respond, to reply") implies dialogue: the community is engaged in conversations with outsiders who ask questions, raise objections, or express curiosity. The phrase heni hekasto ("to each one, to each individual") emphasizes that the responses are not scripted or formulaic but adapted to each person. Different people require different answers. The wisdom of 4:5 and the grace of 4:6a are directed toward the concrete, case-by-case engagement with individual outsiders. This instruction resonates with 1 Peter 3:15: "always being prepared to make a defense to everyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you." The community is expected to be in dialogue with the surrounding culture, and that dialogue is to be conducted with wisdom, grace, and salt — not with the rigid regulations or esoteric claims of the philosophy, but with speech shaped by the gospel.

Verses 7-9: The Letter Carriers — Tychicus and Onesimus

"As to all my affairs, Tychicus, our beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow bond-servant in the Lord, will make them known to you" (4:7).

The phrase ta kat' eme panta — "all the things concerning me" or "all my affairs" — signals a shift from general exhortation to personal communication. Paul will not detail his circumstances in writing; that task falls to the letter carrier. The man entrusted with this task is named: Tychikos ("Tychicus"). Tychicus appears elsewhere in the Pauline corpus: in Acts 20:4 as a companion from the province of Asia who accompanied Paul on his journey to Jerusalem; in Ephesians 6:21-22 in language nearly identical to what appears here; in 2 Timothy 4:12, where Paul says he has sent Tychicus to Ephesus; and in Titus 3:12. He is a member of Paul's inner circle, a trusted associate from the province in which Colossae is located.

Three descriptors establish Tychicus's credentials, and these descriptors echo the language used for Epaphras in 1:7. Epaphras was called "our beloved fellow bond-servant" (ho agapetos syndoulos hemon) and "a faithful minister of Christ" (pistos hyper hemon diakonos tou Christou). Tychicus is called:

First, ho agapetos adelphos — "the beloved brother." The word agapetos ("beloved, dear") is the same word used of Epaphras (1:7) and of the Son himself (1:13, "the Son of his love"). The word adelphos ("brother") places Tychicus within the family of the community.

Second, pistos diakonos — "faithful minister" or "trustworthy servant." The word pistos ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") validates his reliability — the same word used for Epaphras (1:7). The word diakonos ("servant, minister, agent") identifies his function: he serves in the spread and communication of the gospel. He is the same kind of figure as Epaphras — a faithful minister through whom the gospel and its attendant communications are transmitted.

Third, syndoulos en kyrio — "fellow bond-servant in the Lord." The word syndoulos ("fellow slave, fellow servant") is the same word applied to Epaphras in 1:7 (syndoulos hemon). The syn- prefix ("together with") binds Tychicus to Paul in shared service: they are both douloi ("slaves, servants") of the same kyrios. The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") locates this shared service within the sphere of the Lord's authority.

The parallel between Tychicus's description and Epaphras's description is deliberate. Epaphras was the one who originally brought the gospel to Colossae (1:7); Tychicus is now bringing the letter that defends and reinforces that gospel. Both are validated with the same vocabulary: beloved, faithful, fellow servant. The repetition assures the Colossians that the letter they are receiving comes through the same kind of trustworthy channel as the gospel they originally received.

"For I have sent him to you for this very purpose, so that you may know about our circumstances and that he may encourage your hearts" (4:8).

The verb epempsa ("I sent," aorist of pempo, "to send") describes the commissioning of Tychicus. The purpose is twofold: first, hina gnote ta peri hemon — "so that you may know the things concerning us." The community needs information about Paul's situation — his imprisonment, his welfare, the progress of his work. This information will be conveyed orally by Tychicus, not in the letter itself. The letter handles theology and exhortation; the messenger handles personal news.

Second, hina parakalese tas kardias hymon — "so that he may encourage your hearts." The verb parakaleo ("to encourage, to comfort, to exhort") echoes 2:2, where Paul's struggle was directed toward the goal that "their hearts may be encouraged" (paraklethoosin hai kardiai auton). The word kardia ("heart") denotes the center of the person's will and understanding. Tychicus's mission is not merely informational but pastoral: he is sent to strengthen the community from the inside, to bolster their resolve, to comfort them in the face of whatever pressures — from the rival philosophy or from the general difficulties of their situation — they are experiencing.

"And with him Onesimus, our faithful and beloved brother, who is one of your number. They will inform you of everything that has taken place here" (4:9).

Onesimus is the second person sent. His name, Onesimos ("useful, profitable"), is common among slaves in the Roman world. If this is the same Onesimus who is the subject of Paul's letter to Philemon — and the convergence of names, circumstances, and associated persons (Philemon, Archippus, and the Colossian setting all appear in both letters) strongly suggests that he is — then significant background attends his mention here. In Philemon, Onesimus is a slave of Philemon who has come to Paul (possibly as a runaway, possibly sent, possibly having sought Paul as an advocate in a dispute with his master), has become a believer through Paul's ministry in prison, and is being sent back to Philemon with a letter urging Philemon to receive him "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16). The letter to Philemon requests his reception hos adelpohon agapeton — "as a beloved brother" — and here in Colossians 4:9, Onesimus is described as ton piston kai agapeton adelphon — "the faithful and beloved brother." The vocabulary is identical. What Paul requested of Philemon is enacted in the letter to the community: Onesimus is presented publicly, before the whole Colossian assembly, as a faithful and beloved brother.

The phrase hos estin ex hymon — "who is one of you" or "who is from among you" — identifies Onesimus as a member of the Colossian community. The preposition ex ("from, out of") indicates origin: Onesimus belongs to their community by local origin. He is not an outsider being imposed upon them; he is one of their own, now returning.

The final statement: panta hymin gnorisousin ta hode — "they will make known to you all things here" — assigns to both Tychicus and Onesimus the task of conveying full information about Paul's circumstances and the state of affairs at the place of his imprisonment. The plural verb gnorisousin ("they will make known") includes both men as authorized informants. Together they carry the letter and the oral report that accompanies it.

Verses 10-14: Greetings from Co-Workers — The Network of the Messiah's Body

The greetings section (4:10-14) names six individuals who are with Paul and who send their greetings to the Colossian community. The section is not mere social convention; it maps the relational network through which the body of the Messiah (1:18, 24; 2:19) functions. Each person named is a "joint" or "ligament" (cf. 2:19, "nourished and knit together through its joints and ligaments") connecting parts of the body to the Head and to one another.

"Aristarchus, my fellow prisoner, sends you his greetings; and also Barnabas's cousin Mark (about whom you received instructions; if he comes to you, welcome him)" (4:10).

Aristarchus is described as ho synaichmalotos mou — "my fellow prisoner" or "my fellow captive." The word synaichmalotos (syn + aichmalotos, "captive taken by the spear, prisoner of war") is a military term: an aichmalotos is a prisoner taken in warfare. The syn- prefix ("together with") identifies Aristarchus as sharing Paul's captivity. In Acts 19:29, Aristarchus is a Macedonian from Thessalonica who was seized during the riot in Ephesus. In Acts 20:4, he accompanies Paul on the journey to Jerusalem. In Acts 27:2, he travels with Paul to Rome. The description "fellow prisoner" either means that Aristarchus is literally imprisoned alongside Paul or that he has voluntarily shared Paul's conditions of confinement. In either case, Aristarchus embodies the letter's vision of shared suffering for the gospel (cf. 1:24, "I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake").

Mark is identified as ho anepsios Barnaba — "the cousin of Barnabas." The word anepsios means "cousin" (not "nephew," as some older translations render it). Barnabas is well known from Acts: a Levite from Cyprus (Acts 4:36), an early leader of the Jerusalem community who introduced Paul to the apostles (Acts 9:27), and Paul's companion on the first missionary journey (Acts 13-14). The sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over Mark (Acts 15:36-40), which resulted in their separation, lies in the background. Mark had departed from them in Pamphylia and "had not gone with them to the work" (Acts 15:38). Whatever that rupture meant, it has been resolved by the time of this letter. Mark is now with Paul, sending greetings to Colossae. The parenthetical note — peri hou elabete entolas, ean elthe pros hymas dexasthe auton — "about whom you received instructions; if he comes to you, welcome him" — indicates that the Colossians had previously received directions concerning Mark. The content of those prior instructions is not specified, but the command dexasthe auton ("receive him, welcome him") suggests that there may have been reason for the community to be uncertain about Mark's standing — perhaps awareness of his earlier departure from Paul's mission — and Paul addresses this by explicitly endorsing his welcome.

"And also Jesus who is called Justus. These are the only men of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God, and they have proved to be an encouragement to me" (4:11).

A third person is named: Iesous ho legomenos Ioustos — "Jesus who is called Justus." The name Iesous (Jesus, the Greek form of the Hebrew Yeshua/Joshua) was common among first-century Jews. The Latin cognomen Justus ("righteous, just") functions as a distinguishing surname, a practice common among Hellenistic Jews who bore both a Semitic and a Greco-Roman name (cf. "John who was also called Mark," Acts 12:12, 25; "Simeon who was called Niger," Acts 13:1). This Jesus Justus is otherwise unknown in the New Testament.

The editorial comment that follows — houtoi monoi ek peritomes — "these alone of the circumcision" — identifies Aristarchus, Mark, and Jesus Justus as the only Jewish believers (hoi ek peritomes, "those of the circumcision," i.e., Jews) among Paul's current fellow workers (synergoi) for the kingdom of God (eis ten basileian tou theou). The phrase ek peritomes ("of the circumcision") is Paul's standard way of identifying Jewish believers (cf. Galatians 2:12; Romans 4:12; Titus 1:10). The phrase eis ten basileian tou theou ("for the kingdom of God") identifies the goal toward which they work. The basileia tou theou ("kingdom of God") connects to 1:13, where the Father "transferred us to the kingdom of his beloved Son." The co-workers labor for the advancement of the kingdom into which the Colossians have been transferred.

The phrase hoitines egenethesan moi paregoria — "who have been a comfort to me" or "who have proved to be an encouragement to me" — describes the personal effect of these three Jewish co-workers. The word paregoria ("comfort, encouragement, consolation") appears only here in the New Testament. In medical Greek, paregoria referred to soothing or alleviating treatment. Paul, in prison, has found comfort in these three Jewish fellow workers. The note carries implicit pathos: the "only ones" of the circumcision who work with him and encourage him suggests that other Jewish believers have not been similarly supportive. The letter does not elaborate, but the loneliness of the note is palpable.

"Epaphras, who is one of your number, a bond-servant of Christ Jesus, sends you his greetings, always laboring earnestly for you in his prayers, that you may stand perfect and fully assured in all the will of God" (4:12).

Epaphras was introduced in 1:7 as the one who brought the gospel to Colossae — "our beloved fellow servant, a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf." His reappearance in the closing greetings creates an inclusio: the letter begins with Epaphras's validation (1:7-8) and closes with his continuing presence and intercession (4:12-13). The phrase ho ex hymon — "who is one of you" or "who is from you" — identifies Epaphras as a member of the Colossian community, just as Onesimus was identified as ex hymon in 4:9. He is not an outsider; he belongs to the Colossian church.

His designation here is doulos Christou Iesou — "a slave of Christ Jesus." The word doulos ("slave, bond-servant") is a stronger designation than the diakonos ("servant, minister") of 1:7. A diakonos performs a service; a doulos is owned. Epaphras belongs to Christ Jesus as a slave belongs to a master. The designation echoes the household code, where douloi were told to serve "the Lord Christ" (3:24, to kyrio Christo douleuete). Epaphras practices what the household code preaches: he is Christ's slave, and his service is directed toward the welfare of the Colossian community.

His activity is described: pantote agonizomenos hyper hymon en tais proseuchais — "always striving for you in his prayers." The verb agonizomai ("to contend, to struggle, to strive") is the same word Paul used for his own labor in 1:29 (agonizomenos, "striving according to his power") and for his struggle in 2:1 (helikon agona echo, "how great a struggle I have"). The agon metaphor applies to Epaphras as it applied to Paul: prayer is not passive or effortless but a contest, a form of spiritual labor. Epaphras wages the same kind of struggle for the Colossians that Paul does, but his battlefield is prayer.

The content of Epaphras's prayer is stated: hina stathete teleioi kai peplerophoremenoi en panti thelemati tou theou — "that you may stand perfect and fully assured in all the will of God." The verb histemi ("to stand, to take a stand, to remain standing") in the aorist passive stathete describes being established in a firm position. The word teleioi ("perfect, complete, mature") is the same word Paul used in 1:28, where his goal was to "present every person complete (teleion) in Christ." Epaphras's prayer and Paul's mission share the same objective: the community's completeness in Christ. The word peplerophoremenoi ("fully assured, completely convinced," perfect passive participle of plerophoreo, "to fill completely, to be fully convinced") describes a settled, complete conviction. The community is to be not tentatively committed but fully persuaded. The sphere of this standing, maturity, and assurance is en panti thelemati tou theou — "in all the will of God." The phrase echoes the prayer of 1:9, where Paul prayed that they would be "filled with the knowledge of his will" (ten epignosin tou thelematos autou). Epaphras prays for the same thing Paul prayed for: the community's full alignment with God's will.

The convergence of language is not accidental. Epaphras's prayer mirrors Paul's prayer because they serve the same gospel and struggle for the same community. The repetition assures the Colossians that the concern expressed in Paul's letter is also embodied in the ongoing intercession of the man who founded their community and who remains dedicated to their welfare.

"For I testify for him that he has a deep concern for you and for those who are in Laodicea and Hierapolis" (4:13).

Paul adds his personal testimony: martyro gar auto hoti echei polun ponon hyper hymon — "for I bear witness for him that he has much toil for you." The verb martyreo ("to witness, to testify, to bear testimony") is the language of legal attestation: Paul formally testifies to Epaphras's character and commitment. The word ponon ("toil, labor, pain, hardship") describes the effort Epaphras expends — not casual concern but exhausting labor. The scope of his concern extends beyond Colossae: kai ton en Laodikeia kai ton en Hierapolei — "and for those in Laodicea and those in Hierapolis." The three cities of the Lycus Valley — Colossae, Laodicea, and Hierapolis — form a connected network, and Epaphras labors for all three communities. This geographic note confirms what was implied in 2:1 (where Paul's struggle was for the Colossians "and for those at Laodicea and for all who have not seen my face in the flesh"): the letter addresses a regional situation. The rival philosophy and the gospel's defense against it are not confined to a single congregation but concern the entire Lycus Valley network.

"Luke, the beloved physician, sends you his greetings, and also Demas" (4:14).

Two more co-workers are named. Loukas ho iatros ho agapetos — "Luke the physician, the beloved" — identifies Luke by his profession (iatros, "physician, healer, doctor") and by his personal quality (agapetos, "beloved"). If this Luke is the author of the Third Gospel and Acts, as early tradition identifies him, then the companion of Paul's journeys (the "we" sections of Acts: 16:10-17, 20:5-15, 21:1-18, 27:1-28:16) is present with him in prison. His identification as iatros is notable: his profession is named, which is unusual in the Pauline greetings. This may be because his medical skills were relevant to Paul's imprisonment or health, or simply because it was how the Colossians would identify him.

Demas is named without any descriptor: kai Demas — "and Demas." The brevity is striking in comparison to the warm descriptions of other co-workers. In Philemon 24, Demas appears among Paul's "fellow workers" (synergoi). In 2 Timothy 4:10, a later letter reports: "Demas, having loved this present world, has deserted me and gone to Thessalonica." Whether that later departure casts any shadow back onto this bare mention is uncertain, but the absence of any commending epithet — no "beloved," no "faithful," no "fellow servant" — is conspicuous when every other named person in the greeting list receives one or more such descriptors.

The six named co-workers in 4:10-14 divide into two groups. The first three — Aristarchus, Mark, and Jesus Justus — are identified as "those of the circumcision," Jewish believers. The second three — Epaphras, Luke, and Demas — are by implication Gentile believers (or at least not "of the circumcision"). The mixed composition of Paul's immediate circle embodies the reality described in 3:11: "there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised . . . but Christ is all and in all." The new humanity proclaimed in the letter is visible in the letter's own greetings section. Jewish and Gentile co-workers labor side by side, bound together not by ethnic identity or circumcision status but by shared service to the Lord Christ.

Verses 15-17: Instructions for the Lycus Valley Network and Archippus

"Greet the brethren who are in Laodicea and also Nympha and the church that is in her house" (4:15).

The imperative aspasasthe ("greet") asks the Colossians to convey greetings to a wider circle. The first recipients are tous en Laodikeia adelphous — "the brothers in Laodicea." The Laodicean community is not merely a distant audience; it is a sibling community linked to Colossae by geography, by shared gospel transmission through Epaphras (4:13), and by the letter-sharing arrangement that follows in 4:16.

Nympha is singled out from the Laodicean community: kai Nymphan kai ten kat' oikon autes ekklesian — "and Nympha and the church in her house." The name Nymphan is either the accusative of Nympha (a woman's name) or Nymphas (a man's name); the pronoun autes ("her") in significant manuscripts identifies the person as female. The phrase kat' oikon ("according to the house, in the house") identifies the meeting place: the community gathers in Nympha's home. In the first century, Christian assemblies met in private houses rather than in purpose-built religious structures (cf. Romans 16:5, "the church in their house" [Prisca and Aquila]; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Philemon 2, "the church in your house"). The house-church was the basic unit of the early community's organizational life. That Nympha is the host of the Laodicean assembly (or a house-church within it) indicates that she held a position of sufficient social standing and resources to provide a meeting space, and that her name was significant enough to warrant specific mention in an apostolic letter.

The word ekklesia ("assembly, church, congregation") here designates a local gathering — one specific house-church in Laodicea — rather than the universal body of Christ (as in 1:18, 24). The letter uses ekklesia in both senses: the cosmic body with Christ as head (1:18) and the concrete local gathering in a specific house (4:15).

"When this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and you, for your part, read my letter that is coming from Laodicea" (4:16).

The instructions about letter circulation are specific and practical. First: hotan anagnosthe par' hymin he epistole — "when the letter has been read among you" — the verb anaginosko ("to read, to read aloud") refers to the public reading of the letter in the assembly. Letters in the ancient world, especially letters to communities, were read aloud; most members of the community could not read privately. The letter's theology and exhortations were heard, not silently scanned. This means the argument of the letter — the sufficiency of Christ, the exposure of the philosophy, the household instructions, the personal greetings — was delivered as oral performance in the gathered assembly.

The instruction continues: poiesate hina kai en te Laodikeon ekklesia anagnosthe — "make sure that it is also read in the church of the Laodiceans." The Colossian letter is not the exclusive property of the Colossian community; it is to circulate to the neighboring community at Laodicea. This directive confirms that the letter's concerns are regional, not merely local. The rival philosophy, the dangers Paul identifies, and the theological resources he deploys apply to the Laodicean situation as well. The communities are interconnected, and the apostolic instruction is shared.

The second part of the instruction is more puzzling: kai ten ek Laodikeia hina kai hymeis anagnoste — "and that you also read the one from Laodicea." The phrase ten ek Laodikeia ("the one from Laodicea") refers to a letter that is either (a) currently at Laodicea and will be sent on to Colossae, or (b) written to Laodicea and is now to be shared with Colossae. The letter is not further identified. What this letter was — whether it was a separate Pauline letter now lost, or the letter we know as Ephesians (which some early traditions associate with a Laodicean destination, given the absence of "in Ephesus" in some manuscripts of Ephesians 1:1), or another document entirely — cannot be determined from the text of Colossians alone. What can be determined is the practice: Paul's letters circulated among communities in a shared network. The letter was not a private communication but a regional instrument, intended to instruct multiple assemblies facing related challenges.

The circulation instruction embodies the body imagery of 2:19: the body is nourished and knit together through its joints and ligaments. The letters themselves function as ligaments — connecting the Head's instruction to various parts of the body, binding community to community, ensuring that the whole body receives the same nourishment.

"Say to Archippus, 'Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, and see that you fulfill it'" (4:17).

The final specific instruction addresses an individual: Archippo. Archippus appears in Philemon 2 as "our fellow soldier" (to systratiote hemon), named alongside Philemon and Apphia as a leader in the house-church to which Paul's letter to Philemon is addressed. His mention in both letters, along with the overlap of Onesimus and the Colossian setting, connects the two letters' situations.

The message to Archippus is: blepe ten diakonian hen parelabes en kyrio, hina auten plerois — "watch the ministry which you received in the Lord, so that you may fulfill it." The verb blepe ("watch, look, take heed, pay attention") is an imperative — a direct command, the same verb used in 2:8 (blepete, "see to it that no one takes you captive"). The echo is probably intentional: in 2:8, the community was told to "watch out" against the philosophy's captivity; here, Archippus is told to "watch" his ministry. Both uses of blepo call for vigilance.

The word diakonia ("ministry, service, commission") names the task Archippus holds. The verb parelabes ("you received," aorist of paralambano) is the same technical term used in 2:6 for the community's reception of Christ: "as you received (parelabete) Christ Jesus the Lord." The parallel is instructive: the community received Christ; Archippus received a ministry. Both receptions carry obligation. The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") locates the reception within the Lord's sphere: the ministry was received from and within the Lord's authority. It was not a self-appointed task but a divinely assigned commission.

The hina clause states the purpose: hina auten plerois — "so that you may fulfill it." The verb pleroo ("to fill, to fulfill, to complete, to bring to completion") is the same word that runs throughout the letter's theological argument. In 1:9, Paul prayed that the Colossians would be "filled" (plerothe) with the knowledge of God's will. In 1:25, Paul's stewardship was directed toward "fully carrying out" (plerosai) the word of God. In 2:10, the Colossians were "filled" (pepleromenoi) in Christ. In 4:12, Epaphras prays that they would stand "fully assured" (peplerophoremenoi, from the related plerophoreo). Now Archippus is told to pleroo his ministry — to bring it to its full completion, to carry it through to its intended end. The word pleroo creates a verbal thread linking God's purposes (the fullness dwelling in Christ), the community's condition (filled in him), and the individual's responsibility (fulfill your ministry). The fullness of God in Christ does not eliminate human responsibility; it grounds it. Because the community is filled in Christ, Archippus has a ministry to fulfill.

The public nature of this instruction — delivered through a letter to the whole assembly, not in a private note to Archippus — suggests that the community bears corporate responsibility for Archippus's faithfulness. The community is told to relay the message to him; they are thereby enlisted as witnesses and encouragers of his completion of the task. If this Archippus is the same "fellow soldier" named in Philemon 2, and if the ministry he must fulfill has any connection to the situation of Onesimus (who is being sent back to that very community, 4:9), then the public instruction may carry additional weight: the community is watching whether Archippus will carry out what has been asked of him.

Verse 18: Paul's Personal Closing

"I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. Remember my imprisonment. Grace be with you" (4:18).

The closing shifts to the first person singular: ho aspasmos te eme cheiri Paulou — "the greeting by my own hand, Paul's." The phrase te eme cheiri ("by my own hand") indicates that Paul has taken the pen from the amanuensis (the secretary who wrote the body of the letter at Paul's dictation) and is now writing personally. This practice appears in other Pauline letters: Galatians 6:11 ("See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand"), 1 Corinthians 16:21 ("the greeting of Paul with my own hand"), 2 Thessalonians 3:17 ("the greeting of Paul with my own hand, which is the distinguishing mark in every letter of mine; this is the way I write"). The autograph serves two functions: it authenticates the letter as genuinely from Paul (the amanuensis's handwriting might be unknown to the recipients, but Paul's personal hand would be recognized or at least serve as a marker of personal involvement), and it creates a moment of personal intimacy — the apostle's own hand touching the papyrus that will be read in the assembly.

The second sentence is brief and poignant: mnemoneute mou ton desmon — "remember my bonds" or "remember my imprisonment." The imperative mnemoneute ("remember") is a call to active, ongoing remembrance — not merely to know that Paul is in prison but to hold his situation in mind, to pray for him (as requested in 4:3), and to draw from his example the conviction that the gospel is worth suffering for. The word desmon ("bonds, chains, imprisonment") is the genitive plural of desmos, the physical chains that bind a prisoner. The word is concrete: Paul is in chains. The request for remembrance is also implicitly an argument: the man who writes this letter about the sufficiency of Christ and the emptiness of the rival philosophy writes from prison, having paid the cost of his convictions. The rival philosophy's proponents, whatever they offer, do not write from chains. Paul's bonds authenticate his message in a way that comfortable religious speculation cannot.

The final words: he charis meth' hymon — "grace be with you." The benediction is spare — shorter than the closing greetings of most Pauline letters (cf. Romans 16:20, "the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you"; 1 Corinthians 16:23; 2 Corinthians 13:14; Galatians 6:18; Philippians 4:23; 1 Thessalonians 5:28). The single word charis ("grace") encapsulates the entire letter's message: everything the Colossians have and need comes to them as unmerited gift from God through the Messiah. The grace that opened the letter (1:2, "grace to you and peace from God our Father") closes it. The letter's argument began and ends in grace — the undeserved, unsupplementable gift of God in Christ that renders every addition superfluous.

The Argument of the Chapter as a Whole

1. The chapter completes the household code by addressing masters (4:1), grounding their treatment of slaves in the reality that they themselves serve a heavenly Master. The instruction does not abolish the social institution of slavery but subjects the earthly kyrios to the heavenly kyrios. The same word, kyrios, names both the slave owner and the Lord Christ, creating an implicit theological tension: the human master's authority is real but derivative, exercised under a higher authority that demands justice and equity. The instruction connects to the letter's central claim: because Christ is "head of every rule and authority" (2:10), every human authority structure — including the household — operates under his headship. The master who withholds justice from his slaves acts as though there were no heavenly Master — as though he were the ultimate authority. The instruction corrects this by placing the earthly kyrios under the heavenly one.

2. The prayer instructions (4:2-4) embody the letter's theology in practice. The call to persistent, watchful, thankful prayer is the positive counterpart to the negative warnings of chapter 2. Instead of submitting to ascetic regulations that have "an appearance of wisdom" but accomplish nothing (2:23), the community is to devote itself to prayer — the direct address to the God who has qualified them for an inheritance (1:12), rescued them from darkness (1:13), and filled them in Christ (2:10). The request for an "open door" for the mystery of Christ locates the community's prayer within the larger mission of the gospel's advancement. The letter does not envision a community turned inward, focused on its own spiritual attainment; it envisions a community praying for the gospel's spread to others.

3. The instructions about conduct toward outsiders (4:5-6) complete the ethical vision of the letter. The household code governed internal relationships; these verses govern external ones. The community's engagement with "those outside" is to be characterized by wisdom (the genuine wisdom stored in Christ, not the philosophy's counterfeit), opportunism (seizing every kairos for witness), grace (speech that reflects the unmerited generosity of God), and individuality (responding to each person according to their specific situation). The community is not a fortress against the world but a body walking in the world with wisdom, grace, and salt.

4. The personal greetings (4:7-14) are not appendices to the argument but embodiments of it. The people named are the ligaments and joints through whom the body is nourished and held together (2:19). Tychicus and Onesimus carry the letter; Aristarchus shares Paul's imprisonment; Mark has been restored to fellowship; Jesus Justus provides encouragement; Epaphras prays with the same intensity Paul does; Luke and Demas are present. The mixture of Jewish and Gentile co-workers instantiates 3:11. The descriptions echo the letter's key vocabulary: agapetos ("beloved"), pistos ("faithful"), syndoulos ("fellow servant"), diakonos ("minister"), doulos ("slave"), synaichmalotos ("fellow prisoner"), agonizomenos ("striving"). The same words that described God's actions and the community's identity describe these individual persons. The theology is not abstract; it is enfleshed in specific people doing specific work.

5. The instructions about letter circulation (4:15-16) reveal the corporate and regional nature of the letter's concerns. The letter was not written for Colossae alone; it was designed to be read by the Laodicean community as well, and a corresponding letter from Laodicea was to be shared with Colossae. The rival philosophy is a regional threat requiring a regional response. The body of the Messiah in the Lycus Valley is interconnected, and the apostolic instruction circulates through the network.

6. The instruction to Archippus (4:17) applies the letter's theology to a single individual's commission. The verb blepe ("take heed, watch") echoes the communal warning of 2:8. The verb pleroo ("fulfill") ties Archippus's responsibility to the letter's central concept of fullness: the fullness dwells in Christ (1:19, 2:9), the community is filled in him (2:10), and Archippus must bring his ministry to fullness. The public nature of the instruction enlists the community in holding Archippus accountable to his calling.

7. Paul's autograph closing (4:18) grounds the letter's theological claims in personal cost. The man who proclaims the sufficiency of Christ writes from chains. The brevity of the closing — "remember my bonds; grace be with you" — strips away all rhetorical elaboration and leaves two realities: suffering and grace. The imprisonment authenticates the message; the grace summarizes it. What the Colossians need is not the philosophy's ascetic regulations, not angelic mediators, not visionary experiences, not calendrical observances — but the grace of the God who filled the Messiah with all his fullness and, in the Messiah, filled them.

8. The chapter's contribution to the letter's central concern — the ongoing faithfulness of the community — is primarily structural rather than directly argumentative. Chapter 4 does not repeat the theological arguments of chapters 1-2 or the ethical commands of chapter 3. Instead, it builds the infrastructure of support that will sustain the community's faithfulness after the letter has been read. Tychicus will encourage their hearts (4:8). Epaphras prays for their completeness (4:12). The letter will be shared with Laodicea so the communities can support each other (4:16). Archippus is charged with fulfilling his ministry (4:17). The community is told to pray, to walk wisely, to speak with grace (4:2-6). The conditional of 1:23 — "if indeed you continue in the faith" — is not left as a bare demand; chapter 4 provides the relational and practical resources that make continuation possible. The community does not continue in isolation; it continues within a network of persons, prayers, letters, and shared accountability. The body of the Messiah is not an abstraction but a functioning organism, held together by the joints and ligaments that chapter 4 names by name.

9. The pronoun pattern of the chapter follows the letter's overall trajectory toward concrete specificity. The chapter opens with a second-person address to masters within the community ("you," 4:1). It moves to a communal "you" for the prayer and conduct instructions (4:2-6). It shifts to Paul's first-person description of his co-workers and his own circumstances (4:7-14). It returns to second-person commands directed at the community's specific actions (4:15-17). It closes with Paul's singular "I" hand and the communal "you" of the benediction (4:18). The movement tracks from general category (masters) through communal instruction (pray, walk wisely) through named individuals (Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Jesus Justus, Epaphras, Luke, Demas) to specific assignments (greet Laodicea, share letters, charge Archippus) to personal presence (my hand, my chains). The letter ends not in abstraction but in the most concrete realities available: a hand, chains, and grace.
