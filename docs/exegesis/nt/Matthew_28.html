Matthew 28

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 27): Chapter 27 narrated the entire sequence from Jesus's delivery to Pilate through his death and burial. The chapter accumulated testimonies to Jesus's innocence from every quarter — Judas declared the blood "innocent" (27:4), Pilate's wife called Jesus "that righteous man" (27:19), Pilate himself could identify no crime (27:23) — while simultaneously embedding the crucifixion in Israel's scriptural framework: the thirty pieces of silver fulfilled Zechariah 11, Jesus's silence before Pilate fulfilled Isaiah 53:7, the division of garments and the mockery fulfilled Psalm 22, the darkness at noon fulfilled Amos 8:9, and the cry of dereliction quoted Psalm 22:1. The death was attended by signs of cosmic and covenantal significance: the temple curtain was torn from top to bottom (27:51), the earth shook, rocks split, tombs opened, and saints were raised (27:51-53). The centurion and his soldiers confessed, "Truly this was the Son of God" (27:54). The body was buried by Joseph of Arimathea in a new rock-hewn tomb, with Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" watching where it was placed (27:61). The chapter closed with the chief priests and Pharisees securing the tomb with a Roman guard and an official seal (27:62-66), establishing every possible safeguard against tampering. The author's narrative strategy was to verify the death beyond dispute — witnessed by enemies, allies, and bystanders, sealed and guarded by the authorities who feared the very outcome the chapter is preparing to report.

What comes after: There is no chapter after Matthew 28. This is the final chapter of the document. What follows is the life of the community that the risen Jesus commissions. The document that began with "the book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, son of David, son of Abraham" (1:1) ends with the risen Jesus declaring universal authority and sending his disciples to "all nations" (28:19). The trajectory of the document — from Abraham's particular covenant to the inclusion of the nations — reaches its terminus. The reader is left not with an ending but with a commission: the story does not close, it opens outward.

How chapter 28 connects them: Chapter 27 established the death as real, public, and secured. Chapter 28 reports that the secured tomb is empty and the dead man is alive. The connection is not merely sequential but argumentative: the more thoroughly the death was verified and the tomb was sealed, the more significant the empty tomb becomes. The guards posted at 27:65-66 reappear at 28:4 and 28:11-15, creating a continuous narrative thread. The women who witnessed the crucifixion (27:55-56) and the burial (27:61) are the same women who arrive at the tomb (28:1), establishing an unbroken chain of testimony from death through burial to the discovery of the empty tomb. The one who was mocked as "King of the Jews" throughout chapter 27 — by the soldiers (27:29), by the inscription on the cross (27:37), by the passersby and chief priests (27:42) — now declares that "all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18). The kingship that was parodied is real. The declaration before the Sanhedrin — "from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (26:64) — is vindicated: the one the Sanhedrin condemned now possesses the dominion Daniel described (Daniel 7:14). The shepherd who was struck (26:31, citing Zechariah 13:7) and whose sheep scattered (26:56) now regathers his flock in Galilee, exactly as he promised (26:32).

The Women at the Tomb and the Angel's Announcement (vv. 1-8)

"Now after the Sabbath (opse de sabbaton), toward the dawn of the first day of the week (te epiphoskouse eis mian sabbaton), Mary Magdalene and the other Mary went to see the tomb (elthen Mariam he Magdalene kai he alle Maria theoresai ton taphon)."

The temporal marker places the scene precisely: the Sabbath has ended, and the first day of the new week is dawning. The author has tracked time carefully through the passion: "the first day of Unleavened Bread" (26:17), "when it was evening" (26:20), "this very night" (26:34), "when morning came" (27:1), "the day of Preparation" (27:62), "the next day, after the day of Preparation" (27:62). Now it is the day after the Sabbath. Two full nights and the intervening Sabbath day have passed since the burial.

The two women — Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" — are the same pair identified at 27:61 as sitting opposite the tomb and watching the burial. They are also among the group identified at 27:55-56 as present at the crucifixion. Their continuity across three scenes — cross, burial, tomb — establishes them as the primary witnesses whose testimony links the dead Jesus to the empty tomb. The verb theoreo (to see, to observe, to look at carefully) indicates purposeful observation, not a casual passing by. They come to "see the tomb" (theoresai ton taphon) — the text does not state that they came to anoint the body (as in Mark 16:1), only to see the place where Jesus was buried.

"And behold, there was a great earthquake (seismos megas egeneto), for an angel of the Lord descended from heaven (angelos gar kyriou katabas ex ouranou) and came and rolled back the stone (proselthon apekulisen ton lithon) and sat on it (kai ekatheto epano autou)."

The word seismos (earthquake, shaking) appeared at 27:54, where the earthquake at the moment of Jesus's death prompted the centurion's confession. The repetition of seismos connects the two events: the earth that shook when Jesus died shakes again at his vindication. The adjective megas (great) intensifies the event. In the Hebrew scriptures, earthquakes accompany theophanies — divine appearances that shake the natural order. At Sinai, "the whole mountain trembled greatly" (Exodus 19:18). In Elijah's experience at Horeb, "a great and strong wind tore the mountains and broke in pieces the rocks before the LORD, and after the wind an earthquake" (1 Kings 19:11). The earthquake signals that God is acting.

The angel (angelos kyriou — "angel of the Lord") descends from heaven. The phrase "angel of the Lord" (angelos kyriou) has appeared at critical moments in the document: to Joseph in a dream, announcing Jesus's conception and naming (1:20), warning him to flee to Egypt (2:13), and instructing his return (2:19-20). The angel of the Lord bracketed the beginning of Jesus's life; now the same figure appears at his emergence from death. The angel does not open the tomb to let Jesus out — the risen Jesus is not depicted as confined by the stone. The angel rolls the stone away to reveal that the tomb is already empty, and sits upon it. The rolling stone (apekulisen ton lithon), described as "great" (megan) at 27:60 when Joseph placed it, is moved by a single angelic action. The angel sits on the stone — a posture of authority and completion. What the chief priests sealed and guarded, the angel opens and occupies.

"His appearance was like lightning (he eidea autou hos astrape), and his clothing white as snow (kai to endyma autou leukon hos chion)."

The description uses language drawn from Israel's theophanic and apocalyptic tradition. Daniel 10:6 describes the angelic figure whose "face was like the appearance of lightning" (kai to prosopon autou hosei horasis astrapas in the LXX). Daniel 7:9 describes the Ancient of Days whose "clothing was white as snow" (to endyma autou hosei chion leukon). The angel at the tomb carries the visual markers of the heavenly court described in Daniel. The word astrape (lightning) appeared in Jesus's own teaching at 24:27: "For as the lightning (astrape) comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man." Lightning in the author's framework signals the breaking in of divine reality.

"And for fear of him the guards trembled and became like dead men (apo de tou phobou autou eseisthesan hoi terountes kai egenothesan hos nekroi)."

The irony is sharp and deliberate. The guards (hoi terountes — the ones keeping watch, from tereo, to guard, to keep) who were posted to prevent any disturbance at the tomb are themselves undone by the disturbance. The verb seio (to shake, to tremble) is the same root as seismos (earthquake) — the guards are shaken as the earth is shaken. They "became like dead men" (egenothesan hos nekroi). The men stationed to guard a dead body become as dead themselves, while the dead man they were guarding is alive. The living become as dead; the dead one lives. The reversal is complete. The Roman military guard — the strongest human force available — is rendered powerless by the angelic presence. The stone, the seal, and the soldiers, which represented the full capacity of human authority to keep the tomb closed, are all overcome without resistance.

"But the angel said to the women (apokritheis de ho angelos eipen tais gynaixin), 'Do not be afraid (me phobeisthe hymeis), for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified (ton estauromenon). He is not here, for he has risen, as he said (egerththe gar kathos eipen). Come, see the place where he lay (deute idete ton topon hopou ekeito). Then go quickly and tell his disciples (tacheian poreutheisai eipate tois mathetais autou) that he has risen from the dead (hoti egerthe apo ton nekron), and behold, he is going before you to Galilee (idou proagei hymas eis ten Galilaian); there you will see him (ekei auton opsesthe). See, I have told you (idou eipon hymin).'"

The angel's address distinguishes the women from the guards: "Do not be afraid — you" (me phobeisthe hymeis). The pronoun hymeis (you, emphatic) separates them from the guards who have every reason to fear. The women came seeking Jesus; the guards were stationed to prevent anyone from finding him. The angel addresses the seekers, not the sentinels.

The identification "Jesus who was crucified" (ton estauromenon) uses the perfect passive participle — the one who was crucified and remains defined by that crucifixion. The risen one is still the crucified one. The identity is continuous: resurrection does not erase the cross but vindicates the one who hung on it.

The central declaration is "he has risen, as he said" (egerthe gar kathos eipen). The verb egeiro (to raise, to rise) is the same verb used in Jesus's predictions of his resurrection: "on the third day he will be raised" (16:21, egerthenai; 17:23, egerthesetai; 20:19, egerthesetai). The phrase "as he said" (kathos eipen) is the angel's attestation that the resurrection fulfills Jesus's own words. The document has consistently presented Jesus's predictions as coming true: the betrayal by Judas (26:21-25, fulfilled at 26:47-50), the scattering of the disciples (26:31, fulfilled at 26:56), Peter's threefold denial (26:34, fulfilled at 26:69-75), the delivery to the Gentiles (20:19, fulfilled at 27:2). The resurrection is the final prediction, and the angel's testimony is that it too has been fulfilled exactly "as he said."

The invitation "come, see the place where he lay" (deute idete ton topon hopou ekeito) turns the women into firsthand witnesses of the empty tomb. They are not told merely to believe the angel's report; they are invited to verify it with their own eyes. The tomb is open, the body is absent, and the witnesses can confirm it.

The commission to the women is threefold: go quickly (tacheian poreutheisai), tell the disciples (eipate tois mathetais), and announce the message — he has risen from the dead and is going before them to Galilee. The verb proago (to go before, to lead the way) appeared at 26:32, where Jesus told the disciples: "After I am raised up, I will go before you (proaxo hymas) to Galilee." The angel's words repeat Jesus's own promise verbatim. The scattered flock will be regathered; the shepherd who was struck is going ahead to the appointed meeting place. Galilee is where the ministry began (4:12-17), where the first disciples were called (4:18-22), and where the core of Jesus's teaching and healing took place. The risen Jesus does not summon the disciples to Jerusalem, the center of religious authority that condemned him, but to Galilee, the place of their calling.

"So they departed quickly from the tomb with fear and great joy (meta phobou kai charas megales), and ran to tell his disciples (edramon apangeilai tois mathetais autou)."

The combination of "fear and great joy" (phobou kai charas megales) is the characteristic response to theophany — the encounter with divine action produces both trembling and exhilaration. The two emotions are not contradictory but complementary: the women have witnessed something that exceeds the boundaries of ordinary experience, and the appropriate response includes both awe and elation. They run (edramon — from trecho, to run) to carry the message. The women who "followed" (akoloutheo) and "ministered" (diakoneo) during Jesus's ministry (27:55) now become the first messengers of the resurrection. The ones who remained at the cross when the male disciples fled (26:56) are the ones entrusted with the foundational announcement.

Jesus Meets the Women (vv. 9-10)

"And behold, Jesus met them (ho Iesous hypantesen autais) and said, 'Greetings! (chairete)' And they came up and took hold of his feet (ekratesan autou tous podas) and worshiped him (kai prosekynasan auto)."

The risen Jesus appears to the women while they are on their way to deliver the angel's message. The greeting chairete (rejoice, greetings) is the ordinary Greek salutation, but in this context it carries its literal force: "Rejoice!" The word chairo is the root of chara (joy), which the women already carry (v. 8). Jesus affirms and deepens their joy by his presence.

The women "took hold of his feet" (ekratesan autou tous podas). The verb krateo (to seize, to grasp, to hold) indicates physical contact — the risen Jesus has a body that can be touched and grasped. The feet are the part of the body associated with prostration and worship in the Hebrew scriptures (cf. 2 Kings 4:27, the Shunammite woman grasping Elisha's feet). The act of holding his feet combines recognition, reverence, and the tactile confirmation that this is not a vision or an apparition but a bodily presence.

The women worship him (prosekynasan auto). The verb proskyneo (to bow down, to worship, to do obeisance) has been a significant action throughout the document. The magi worshiped the child (2:11). The disciples worshiped Jesus after he walked on the sea (14:33). The Canaanite woman worshiped him (15:25). The mother of James and John worshiped him (20:20). At 4:10, Jesus declared, "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve" (quoting Deuteronomy 6:13). The proskyneo offered to Jesus throughout the document — by magi, disciples, and supplicants — reaches its fullest expression here: the women worship the risen one without rebuke, without correction, without any indication that the worship is misdirected. The author presents the worship of the risen Jesus as the proper response.

"Then Jesus said to them, 'Do not be afraid (me phobeisthe); go and tell my brothers (hypagete apangeilate tois adelphois mou) to go to Galilee (hina apelthosin eis ten Galilaian), and there they will see me (kakei me opsontai).'"

Jesus repeats the angel's commission with one significant alteration: the angel said "tell his disciples" (tois mathetais autou, v. 7); Jesus says "tell my brothers" (tois adelphois mou). The shift from "disciples" to "brothers" is remarkable. The disciples who abandoned him (26:56), whose leader denied him three times (26:69-75), are now called "brothers" (adelphoi). The word adelphos in the document has two primary uses: biological brothers (4:18, 21; 12:46-47; 13:55) and members of the community of faith (5:22-24, 47; 7:3-5; 12:49-50; 18:15, 21, 35; 23:8; 25:40). At 12:49-50, Jesus defined his "brothers" as "whoever does the will of my Father in heaven." By calling the scattered, failed disciples "my brothers," the risen Jesus restores the relationship that their desertion had fractured. The desertion was real, but it is not the final word. The shepherd who was struck regathers the scattered sheep and names them brothers.

The destination is Galilee, repeated for the third time (26:32, 28:7, 28:10). The insistence on Galilee as the meeting place is not incidental. Galilee was the place of origins and calling; the return there after the passion constitutes a new beginning from the place of first beginnings.

The Guards' Report and the Bribery (vv. 11-15)

"While they were going (poreuomenon de auton), behold, some of the guard went into the city (tines tes koustodias elthontes eis ten polin) and told the chief priests all that had taken place (apangeilan tois archiereusin hapanta ta genomena)."

The narrative now follows two tracks simultaneously: the women are going to the disciples with the message of resurrection, and the guards are going to the chief priests with the same report. Both groups witnessed the same events — the earthquake, the angel, the open tomb. Both report what happened. The word apangeilan (reported, announced) is the same verb used for the women's mission to the disciples (apangeilai, v. 8). The parallel is deliberate: the testimony goes to both the believing community and to the hostile authorities. The facts are the same; the response will differ.

"And when they had assembled with the elders and taken counsel (symboulion labontes), they gave a sufficient sum of money to the soldiers (argyria hikana edokan tois stratiotais), saying, 'Tell people, "His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were sleeping" (hoi mathetai autou nyktos elthontes eklepsan auton hemon koimomenon).'"

The phrase "assembled with the elders and taken counsel" (symboulion labontes) echoes 27:1, where the same groups "took counsel" (symboulion elabon) to put Jesus to death. The same phrase marks the same pattern: the religious leadership responds to a crisis by conspiring. Having failed to keep Jesus in the tomb, they now attempt to control the narrative about why it is empty.

The word argyria (silver, money) recalls the "thirty pieces of silver" (triakonta argyria) paid to Judas at 26:15. Silver was the currency of betrayal in this narrative; now silver is the currency of suppression. Judas was paid silver to deliver Jesus to death; the guards are paid silver to deny that he has risen from it. The adjective hikana (sufficient, considerable) indicates that the bribe was large enough to ensure compliance.

The fabricated story — "his disciples came by night and stole him away while we were sleeping" — is internally contradictory, a point the author expects the reader to notice. If the guards were sleeping, they could not know what happened to the body. If they were awake, they could not claim the disciples overpowered a Roman military guard. The story is designed not for logical coherence but for public circulation. The author has already established at 27:62-66 that the chief priests themselves requested the guard precisely because they feared the disciples might steal the body. Having established the guard against that possibility, the chief priests now invent the very scenario they sought to prevent, as the cover story for what actually occurred.

"'And if this comes to the governor's ears (ean akousthe touto epi tou hegemonos), we will satisfy him (hemeis peisomen auton) and keep you out of trouble (hymas amerimnous poiesomen).'"

Sleeping on guard duty was a capital offense in the Roman military (Polybius, Histories 6.37). The chief priests promise to protect the soldiers from the consequences of the story they are being told to tell. The verb peitho (to persuade, to satisfy) was used at 27:20 for the chief priests "persuading" (epeisan) the crowd to ask for Barabbas. The same verb marks the same tactic: the chief priests use persuasion — here combined with money — to control the outcome. The word amerimnous (free from care, without anxiety) appeared at 6:25, 31, 34, in Jesus's teaching about anxiety: "Do not be anxious" (me merimnate). The chief priests promise freedom from anxiety — but the freedom they offer is the freedom of complicity in a lie, not the freedom from fear that the angel and the risen Jesus offered to the women (28:5, 10).

"So they took the money and did as they were directed (edidachthesan). And this story has been spread among the Jews to this day (diephemsthe ho logos houtos para Ioudaiois mechri tes semeron hemeras)."

The verb didasko (to teach, to instruct) appears in the passive: the soldiers "were instructed" (edidachthesan). The irony is that the word for what the chief priests do to the soldiers is the same word used for what Jesus does with his disciples — both "teach." But the content is opposite: Jesus teaches truth; the chief priests teach a fabrication.

The editorial note "to this day" (mechri tes semeron hemeras) is the author's address to the first-century reader. This phrase appeared at 27:8 regarding the "Field of Blood" and signals a present-tense reality known to the author's audience. The author acknowledges that the counter-narrative — the claim that the disciples stole the body — is actively circulating at the time of writing. The entire passage (27:62-66 through 28:11-15) functions as the author's response to this specific objection: the tomb was guarded and sealed; the guards witnessed the events; the guards were bribed to lie; the lie is still being told. The author does not merely assert the resurrection; he addresses the most prominent alternative explanation and argues that it was fabricated by the same authorities who engineered the crucifixion.

The phrase "among the Jews" (para Ioudaiois) reflects the perspective of a community that distinguishes itself from the broader Jewish population that has not accepted the resurrection claim. The phrase does not denote an ethnic category from outside but an intra-communal distinction: those who follow Jesus and those who follow the teaching of the chief priests have parted ways on this foundational question.

The Risen Jesus on the Mountain in Galilee (vv. 16-20)

"Now the eleven disciples went to Galilee (hoi de hendeka mathetai eporeuthsan eis ten Galilaian), to the mountain to which Jesus had directed them (eis to oros hou etaxato autois ho Iesous)."

The number "eleven" (hendeka) rather than "twelve" (dodeka) silently registers Judas's absence. The twelve were constituted at 10:1-4; now they are eleven. The gap in the number is the permanent scar of the betrayal. The text does not explain what happened to Judas — the reader already knows from 27:5 that he hanged himself. The eleven are what remains of the community that Jesus called and formed.

They go to Galilee, as instructed three times (26:32, 28:7, 28:10), and to a specific mountain (to oros — the mountain) "to which Jesus had directed them" (hou etaxato autois). The verb tasso (to appoint, to direct, to assign) indicates a prior arrangement — Jesus specified this location. The document does not identify which mountain, but mountains have served as sites of revelation and commission throughout the text. The Sermon on the Mount was delivered from a mountain (5:1). Jesus went up on a mountain to pray (14:23). The transfiguration occurred on "a high mountain" (17:1). The eschatological discourse was given on the Mount of Olives (24:3). In the Hebrew scriptures, mountains are the places where God meets his people and delivers his commands: Sinai (Exodus 19-20), Horeb (1 Kings 19), Zion (Psalm 2:6, Isaiah 2:2-3). The mountain in Galilee continues this pattern: the risen Jesus meets his reconstituted community on a mountain and delivers his final commission, as Moses received the Torah on Sinai and delivered it to Israel.

"And when they saw him they worshiped him (kai idontes auton proseknyesan), but some doubted (hoi de edistasan)."

The response is divided: worship and doubt exist simultaneously. The verb proskyneo (to worship) continues the pattern from verse 9, where the women worshiped the risen Jesus. Now the eleven also worship. But the author adds the striking note: hoi de edistasan — "but some doubted." The verb distazo (to doubt, to hesitate, to waver) appeared only once before in the document, at 14:31, when Peter began to sink while walking on the water and Jesus said, "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (eis ti edistasas). The word describes not outright disbelief but divided conviction — the wavering between trust and uncertainty, the inability to commit fully to what the senses report and the mind struggles to accept.

The author's inclusion of the doubt is remarkable. This is the final scene of the document, the culminating moment, and the author does not present it as a scene of unanimous, triumphant faith. Some worship; some waver. The honesty of the detail argues against fabrication — a writer inventing this scene for propaganda purposes would not include doubt among the witnesses at the climactic moment. The detail also connects to the document's consistent portrayal of the disciples as people of "little faith" (oligopistoi, 6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8; 17:20). Even the resurrection does not instantly resolve the disciples' struggle to trust. The community that will receive the commission is not a community of perfect faith but a community of worshipers who still wrestle with uncertainty.

"And Jesus came and said to them (kai proselthon ho Iesous elalesen autois legon), 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me (edothe moi pasa exousia en ourano kai epi ges).'"

Jesus approaches them (proselthon — he comes to them, not they to him) and speaks. The first word of the commission is a declaration of authority. The verb didomi (to give) is in the aorist passive: "has been given" (edothe). The passive voice indicates that the authority comes from God — it is the Father who gives, as at 11:27: "All things have been delivered to me by my Father (panta moi paredothe hypo tou patros mou)." The scope is total: "all authority" (pasa exousia), in "heaven and on earth" (en ourano kai epi ges). No domain is excluded. The word exousia (authority, power, right to act) has been contested and demonstrated throughout the document: authority to forgive sins (9:6), authority over unclean spirits (10:1), the question of by what authority Jesus acts (21:23-27), and the authority of the Son of Man enthroned at God's right hand (26:64).

This declaration directly fulfills Daniel 7:13-14. In Daniel's vision, "one like a son of man" comes to the Ancient of Days and receives dominion: "And to him was given (edothe auto in the LXX) dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed." The verbal and conceptual parallels are exact: authority is "given" (edothe) to the Son of Man, it extends over all domains, and it encompasses "all peoples, nations, and languages." At 26:64, Jesus declared to the Sanhedrin that they would see "the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven." The Sanhedrin's response was to condemn him to death. Now, on the mountain in Galilee, the condemned one announces that the Daniel 7 investiture has taken place. The authority the Sanhedrin rejected has been conferred by the one whose authority the Sanhedrin cannot touch.

"'Go therefore and make disciples of all nations (poreuthentes oun matheteusate panta ta ethne), baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit (baptizontes autous eis to onoma tou patros kai tou huiou kai tou hagiou pneumatos), teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you (didaskontes autous terein panta hosa eneteilamen hymin).'"

The word oun (therefore) is crucial: because all authority has been given to Jesus, therefore the disciples are sent. The commission flows from the authority. The imperative is matheteusate (make disciples) — from matheteuo, the same verb used to describe Joseph of Arimathea as one who "had been made a disciple" (ematheuthe, 27:57). The verb appeared at 13:52 for the "scribe trained (matheutheisas) for the kingdom of heaven." To "make disciples" is to do what Jesus did when he called the fishermen at 4:18-22, when he called Matthew at 9:9, and when he formed the twelve at 10:1-4 — to draw people into the relationship of learning, following, and obedience that defines a mathetetes (learner, disciple).

The scope is "all nations" (panta ta ethne). The phrase panta ta ethne has appeared at decisive moments in the document: at 24:9 ("you will be hated by all nations"), at 24:14 ("this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world as a testimony to all nations"), and at 25:32 ("before him will be gathered all the nations"). The trajectory has been building since the opening genealogy, which included Gentile women (Rahab, Ruth) in Jesus's lineage (1:5), through the magi from the east (2:1-12), through the centurion whose faith surpassed all in Israel (8:10), through Jesus's declaration that "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven" (8:11), through the Canaanite woman's great faith (15:28), and through the centurion's confession at the cross (27:54). The boundary of Israel's covenant is now explicitly opened to include every nation. The particularism of 10:5-6 ("Go nowhere among the Gentiles... but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel") and 15:24 ("I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel") was the appointed scope for the earthly ministry; the commission of the risen Jesus extends the mission to every people.

The formula "baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" brings together three identifications that have appeared throughout the document. The Father has been identified as the source of Jesus's authority (11:27), the one whose will Jesus submits to (6:10, 26:39, 42), and the one who has revealed Jesus's identity (3:17, 16:17, 17:5). The Son has been identified as the one through whom the Father is known (11:27), confessed by Peter (16:16) and the centurion (27:54), and declared by God himself (3:17, 17:5). The Holy Spirit has been present at Jesus's conception (1:18, 20), his baptism (3:16), his temptation (4:1), and his teaching about blasphemy against the Spirit (12:31-32). The formula does not explain the relationship among the three; it names the reality into which the baptized person is brought. The word onoma (name) in Jewish usage represents the person's identity and character — to be baptized "into the name" (eis to onoma) is to be brought into the reality the name represents.

The instruction "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (didaskontes autous terein panta hosa eneteilamen hymin) points back to the content of Jesus's teaching throughout the document. The verb entellomai (to command, to charge) is the word used for authoritative instruction. The word panta (all) — "all that I have commanded" — encompasses the entire body of Jesus's teaching: the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5-7), the mission discourse (chapter 10), the parables of the kingdom (chapter 13), the community discourse (chapter 18), and the eschatological discourse (chapters 23-25). The five great teaching blocks of the document are the content of what the disciples are to teach the nations. The verb tereo (to keep, to observe, to guard) indicates not merely hearing but doing — the practical obedience that has been the standard of genuine discipleship since 7:24-27, where the wise person is the one who "hears these words of mine and does them." The commission is not merely to spread information but to form communities of obedience.

"'And behold, I am with you always (kai idou ego meth' hymon eimi), to the end of the age (heos tes synteleias tou aionos).'"

The final words of the document form an inclusio — a literary bracket — with the first chapter. At 1:23, the author cited Isaiah 7:14: "Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Emmanuel (Emmanouel), which means, God with us (meth' hemon ho theos)." The document that began with the declaration that the child would be called "God with us" ends with the risen Jesus declaring "I am with you" (ego meth' hymon eimi). The promise of divine presence that Isaiah announced and Matthew applied to Jesus at his birth is now spoken by Jesus himself at his commissioning of the community. The document's christological claim is stated in its opening and closing frames: Jesus is the presence of God with his people.

The phrase "I am" (ego eimi) carries resonance from the Hebrew scriptures. At Exodus 3:14, God identified himself to Moses as "I AM" (ego eimi ho on in the LXX). The phrase does not necessarily function here as a formal divine self-identification in every context, but within the author's framework — where Jesus has just declared universal authority (v. 18), is worshiped without correction (v. 17), and is named "God with us" (1:23) — the resonance is unavoidable. The one who speaks is the one through whom God's presence is realized.

The temporal scope is "to the end of the age" (heos tes synteleias tou aionos). The phrase synteleia tou aionos (consummation of the age, completion of the age) appeared four times in the document, all in Jesus's teaching: at 13:39, 40, 49 (the harvest at the end of the age in the parables of the weeds and the net), and at 24:3 (the disciples' question, "what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?"). The risen Jesus's promise of presence extends to the very boundary the disciples asked about. The period between the resurrection and the consummation of the age — the time in which the community exists, makes disciples, baptizes, and teaches — is not a period of Jesus's absence but of his continuing presence. The document does not end with Jesus ascending or departing. It ends with Jesus present, speaking, commissioning, and promising to remain. The final word is not absence but presence, not ending but continuation.

The Chapter's Function as the Conclusion of the Document

1. It vindicates every prediction Jesus made. The document has recorded Jesus's predictions of his death and resurrection at 16:21, 17:22-23, and 20:17-19. Each prediction specified that the Son of Man would be killed and "on the third day be raised." The angel's declaration "he has risen, as he said" (v. 6) confirms the fulfillment. The pattern extends beyond the resurrection itself: Jesus predicted the scattering of the disciples (26:31), and it occurred (26:56); he predicted Peter's denial (26:34), and it occurred (26:69-75); he predicted his meeting them in Galilee (26:32), and it occurs (28:16). The cumulative effect is to establish Jesus as one whose words are completely reliable. The promises that remain unfulfilled at the end of the document — the coming of the Son of Man in glory (24:30), the gathering of the elect (24:31), the final judgment (25:31-46) — carry the same authority as the predictions that have already been fulfilled. What he said about the resurrection came true; what he said about the consummation will come true.

2. It completes the Daniel 7 arc that runs through the document. The title "Son of Man" has been Jesus's primary self-designation throughout the document, appearing over thirty times. At 26:64, Jesus declared before the Sanhedrin that they would see the Son of Man "seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven," combining Daniel 7:13 with Psalm 110:1. In Daniel's vision, the Son of Man receives "dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him" (Daniel 7:14). The declaration at 28:18 — "all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" — is the announcement that Daniel's investiture has occurred. The commission to "all nations" (panta ta ethne, 28:19) corresponds to Daniel's "all peoples, nations, and languages." The Son of Man who was condemned by the Sanhedrin now possesses the universal dominion Daniel described. The authority that was contested throughout the document — questioned by the Pharisees (12:24), challenged by the chief priests (21:23), rejected by the Sanhedrin (26:65-66) — is now definitively conferred.

3. It completes the Emmanuel inclusio. The document opens with the name "Emmanuel, which means God with us" (1:23) and closes with the declaration "I am with you always" (28:20). The entire document is framed by the promise of divine presence realized in Jesus. The birth narrative identified Jesus as the one through whom God dwells with his people; the resurrection narrative confirms that this presence does not end with death. The inclusio shapes the reading of everything between: the healings, the teachings, the conflicts, the passion, and the resurrection are all expressions of "God with us." The promise extends beyond the lifetime of the eleven who stand on the mountain — "to the end of the age" — encompassing every generation of the community that the commission will create.

4. It reverses the pattern of failure that dominated chapters 26-27. The disciples abandoned Jesus (26:56). Peter denied him (26:69-75). Judas betrayed him and destroyed himself (27:3-5). The community constituted by Jesus's ministry disintegrated under the pressure of the passion. Chapter 28 reverses this trajectory. The women who remained faithful become the first witnesses (28:1-8). The risen Jesus calls the failed disciples "my brothers" (28:10). The eleven who scattered are regathered on the mountain in Galilee (28:16). The community that was shattered is reconstituted, not on the basis of their performance during the crisis but on the basis of the risen Jesus's initiative in meeting them, restoring them, and commissioning them. The failure was real; the restoration is also real. The document does not erase the failure or pretend it did not happen — it includes the doubt of the eleven even on the mountain (28:17) — but it presents the risen Jesus as the one who works through and beyond human failure to accomplish his purpose.

5. It opens the boundary of Israel's covenant to include the nations. The movement from the particular to the universal has been building throughout the document. Abraham's covenant included the promise that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). The genealogy included Gentile women (1:3-6). The magi came from the east (2:1-12). Jesus commended Gentile faith as surpassing Israel's (8:10, 15:28). The parable of the tenants warned that "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (21:43). The centurion and his soldiers confessed Jesus as Son of God at the cross (27:54). Now the risen Jesus commissions the making of disciples among "all nations." The restriction of the earthly ministry to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:6, 15:24) is superseded by the risen Jesus's universal authority and universal commission. The covenant is not abandoned but expanded: what began with Abraham now extends to every people through the teaching and obedience that the commission prescribes.

6. It establishes the basis for the community's ongoing existence. The document does not end with a departure or an absence. It ends with a presence and a commission. The risen Jesus is "with" his community (meth' hymon) for the duration of the age. The community exists under his authority (v. 18), carries out his mission (v. 19), practices his instructions (v. 20a), and lives within his promised presence (v. 20b). The five great discourses of the document — ethical instruction (chapters 5-7), mission (chapter 10), kingdom parables (chapter 13), community life (chapter 18), and eschatological readiness (chapters 23-25) — are the content of the "all that I have commanded you" (v. 20a) that the community is to teach the nations. The document is not a historical record that ends when the events end; it is a charter for a community that continues to exist, make disciples, baptize, teach, and live in the presence of the risen Jesus until the consummation of the age.

7. It presents the resurrection as the hinge on which everything turns. Without the resurrection, the document's narrative would end in the defeat of its central figure — condemned, mocked, crucified, buried, and sealed in a guarded tomb. The predictions would be proved false, the teaching would lack authority, the disciples' abandonment would be the final word, and the promise of God's presence would be contradicted by God's apparent absence. The resurrection reverses every element: the predictions are confirmed, the authority is established, the failed disciples are restored, and the presence of God continues beyond death. The author does not present the resurrection as an isolated miracle but as the event that validates everything that preceded it and authorizes everything that will follow. The entire document has been written from the standpoint of the resurrection — every irony in the passion narrative (the soldiers unknowingly crowning a real king, the inscription proclaiming the truth, the mockers quoting Psalm 22 against its own conclusion) depends on the reader knowing that the crucified one was raised. Chapter 28 is not an appendix to the story of Jesus's death; it is the culminating event without which the story has no resolution.
