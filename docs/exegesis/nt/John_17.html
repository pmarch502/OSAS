John 17 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (John 16): Chapter 16 completed the farewell discourse's second major movement, which began at 15:18 with the announcement of the world's hatred. The chapter opened by specifying the purpose of the persecution warnings: "These things I have spoken to you so that you may be kept from stumbling" (16:1). The persecution would take two forms — synagogue expulsion (aposunagogous, 16:2) and religiously motivated killing (16:2) — both rooted in ignorance of the Father and of Jesus (16:3). Jesus then reframed his departure as advantageous: "It is to your advantage that I go away; for if I do not go away, the Helper will not come to you" (16:7). The Spirit's work was described in three dimensions: convicting the world concerning sin, righteousness, and judgment (16:8-11); guiding the disciples into all truth (16:12-15); and glorifying Jesus by taking what belongs to him and disclosing it (16:14-15). The "little while" saying (16:16) provoked the disciples' confusion, which Jesus addressed through the prophetic image of a woman in labor whose anguish gives way to joy when the child is born (16:21) — drawing on Isaiah 26:16-21, Isaiah 66:7-14, and Micah 4:9-10. The sorrow of the disciples at Jesus's death would become joy at his return, and this joy "no one will take away" (16:22). The new post-departure reality was defined by direct access to the Father through prayer "in my name" (16:23-24, 26) and by the Father's personal love (philei) for the disciples (16:27). The disciples declared their belief (16:29-30), but Jesus immediately predicted their scattering: "an hour is coming, and has already come, for you to be scattered, each to his own home, and to leave me alone" (16:32), echoing Zechariah 13:7. The discourse culminated in the declaration "I have overcome the world" (16:33, nenikeka, perfect tense — accomplished victory), grounding the disciples' peace and courage not in their own capacity but in Jesus's completed conquest.

What the author argues immediately after (John 18): Chapter 18 opens with an abrupt shift from speech to action: "When Jesus had spoken these things, he went forth with his disciples over the brook Kidron, where there was a garden, into which he and his disciples entered" (18:1). The prayer of chapter 17 is the last word before the events begin. Judas, "who was betraying him" (18:2), arrives with "the Roman cohort and officers from the chief priests and the Pharisees" carrying "lanterns and torches and weapons" (18:3) — the darkness of 13:30 ("it was night") now equipped with artificial light that cannot replace the true light. Jesus goes out to meet them and identifies himself with the absolute ego eimi ("I am," 18:5, 6, 8), and they draw back and fall to the ground (18:6) — confirming the sovereign authority he claimed in the prayer ("you gave him authority over all flesh," 17:2). Jesus then commands: "If you seek me, let these go their way" (18:8), and the author adds: "to fulfill the word which he spoke, 'Of those whom you have given me I lost not one'" (18:9) — a direct quotation of the prayer's claim in 17:12 ("not one of them perished"). The prayer's intercession is immediately enacted: the shepherd protects the sheep at the moment of his own arrest. Peter's sword-stroke (18:10) is rebuked (18:11), Jesus is bound and led to Annas and Caiaphas (18:12-14), and Peter's threefold denial follows (18:17, 25-27) — fulfilling 13:38 and demonstrating the very scattering Jesus predicted in 16:32. The trial before Pilate (18:28-19:16) raises the question of kingship ("Are you the King of the Jews?" 18:33), which the prayer has already answered: Jesus's kingdom is defined by truth (18:37), and his authority is over "all flesh" (17:2), not merely the political structures Pilate represents.

How chapter 17 connects the surrounding argument: Chapter 17 is not a new subject but the culmination of the entire farewell discourse that began in 13:1. The shift is in audience, not in content: Jesus turns from speaking to the disciples to speaking to the Father. The author marks this transition explicitly: "These things Jesus spoke; and lifting up his eyes to heaven, he said, 'Father...'" (17:1). The gesture — lifting up the eyes to heaven — is the posture of prayer (Psalm 123:1, LXX 122:1: "To you I lift up my eyes, O you who are enthroned in the heavens"; cf. Mark 6:41, Luke 18:13). Everything Jesus has said to the disciples in chapters 14-16 now becomes the substance of his prayer to the Father. The promises made to the disciples are now requested of the Father; the declarations about the world are now reported to the Father; the concerns expressed to the community are now laid before the one who can act on them.

The chapter's content maps directly onto the farewell discourse's themes. The mutual glorification of Father and Son, announced in 13:31-32 ("Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him") and referenced throughout (14:13; 16:14), now becomes the prayer's opening petition (17:1, 4-5). The authority over "all flesh" to give eternal life (17:2) is the realization of what the document has been claiming since the prologue: "In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (1:4). Eternal life defined as knowing the Father and Jesus Christ (17:3) restates the entire document's framework of knowledge, revelation, and relationship. The keeping of the disciples "in your name" (17:11) is the Father's answer to the scattering Jesus has just predicted (16:32). The sanctification "in the truth" (17:17) implements the Spirit's role of guiding into all truth (16:13). The unity of future believers (17:20-23) extends the "one flock, one shepherd" of 10:16. The desire that the disciples "be with me where I am" (17:24) fulfills the promise of 14:3 ("I will come again and receive you to myself, that where I am you may be also"). The prayer does not introduce material absent from the discourse; it recasts the discourse's teaching as petition, entrusting to the Father what Jesus has told the disciples he would do.

The prayer also serves as the bridge between word and action. The discourse (chapters 14-16) was Jesus's instruction; the prayer (chapter 17) is Jesus's intercession; the Passion narrative (chapters 18-19) is the enactment of what was instructed and prayed for. The three movements form a single sequence: teach the community, pray for the community, and then lay down life for the community. The prayer is positioned at the threshold: the last words before the garden, the last speech before the arrest, the last conversation before the cross. Its placement makes it the interpretive lens through which the Passion events are to be read. When the soldiers arrive and Jesus says "let these go their way" (18:8), the reader hears the prayer: "I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one" (17:15). When Jesus stands before Pilate and speaks of his kingdom (18:36-37), the reader hears the prayer: "You gave him authority over all flesh" (17:2) and "your word is truth" (17:17). The prayer interprets the Passion before it occurs.

Verses 1-5: The Son's Request for Glorification

"These things Jesus spoke; and lifting up his eyes to heaven, he said, 'Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son, that the Son may glorify you, even as you gave him authority over all flesh, that to all whom you have given him, he may give eternal life. This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent. I glorified you on the earth, having accomplished the work which you have given me to do. Now, Father, glorify me together with yourself, with the glory which I had with you before the world was.'"

"These things Jesus spoke; and lifting up his eyes to heaven, he said, 'Father'" (tauta elalesen Iesous, kai eparas tous ophthalmous autou eis ton ouranon eipen, Pater). The phrase "these things Jesus spoke" (tauta elalesen Iesous) marks the conclusion of the discourse directed at the disciples and the transition to prayer directed at the Father. The phrase tauta ("these things") refers to the entirety of chapters 14-16 — the teaching about the departure, the Spirit, the vine, the world's hatred, the sorrow-to-joy transformation, and the overcoming of the world. Everything Jesus has spoken to the disciples now serves as the background for what he says to the Father.

The gesture of lifting up the eyes to heaven (eparas tous ophthalmous eis ton ouranon) is the conventional posture of prayer in Israel's practice. Psalm 123:1 (LXX 122:1): "To you I lift up my eyes, O you who are enthroned in the heavens." Psalm 121:1 (LXX 120:1): "I will lift up my eyes to the mountains; from where does my help come?" The phrase occurs in narrative contexts of prayer elsewhere: in 11:41, at the tomb of Lazarus, "Jesus raised his eyes" (eren tous ophthalmous) and said "Father, I thank you that you have heard me." The same gesture at Lazarus's tomb precedes the greatest sign in the document; now the same gesture precedes the greatest prayer. The parallels invite the reader to hear this prayer with the same weight as the Lazarus episode: both are moments where Jesus addresses the Father directly in the hearing of others, and both concern the giving of life.

The address "Father" (Pater) is the dominant address throughout the prayer (17:1, 5, 11, 21, 24, 25). The word pater has been the most important relational term in the document, appearing over one hundred times. In this prayer, "Father" is used without qualification in verses 1, 5, and 21; as "Holy Father" (Pater hagie) in verse 11; as "righteous Father" (Pater dikaie) in verse 25. Each variation carries weight in its context, as the analysis below will show. The use of "Father" without elaboration in the opening address establishes the prayer's fundamental character: this is the Son speaking to the Father, in the intimacy of the relationship the document has described since the prologue ("the only begotten God who is in the bosom of the Father," 1:18).

"The hour has come" (eleluthen he hora). The perfect tense (eleluthen, from erchomai, "to come") indicates a completed arrival: the hour is here, fully present. The "hour" (hora) has been the document's central temporal concept, marking the appointed time for Jesus's death-as-glorification. In 2:4, at Cana: "My hour has not yet come." In 7:30 and 8:20: "No one seized him, because his hour had not yet come." In 12:23: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." In 12:27: "Now my soul has become troubled; and what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour?' But for this purpose I came to this hour." In 13:1: "Jesus knowing that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father." Now, in 17:1, the hour is stated as fully arrived in the context of prayer — Jesus addresses the Father at the very moment the hour is present. The prayer is spoken within the hour, not before it. Jesus does not pray to be prepared for the hour; he prays from within the hour itself.

"Glorify your Son, that the Son may glorify you" (doxason sou ton huion, hina ho huios doxase se). The verb doxazo ("to glorify") appeared in 13:31-32 when Judas departed: "Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him; if God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and will glorify him immediately." The mutual glorification announced there is now requested in prayer. The structure is reciprocal: the Father glorifies the Son so that the Son may glorify the Father. The glorification is not one-directional; it is a cycle of honor that passes between Father and Son and returns.

The word doxa ("glory") in the LXX translates the Hebrew kavod, which denotes weight, significance, and the visible manifestation of God's presence. The kavod of God filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34-35), appeared on Mount Sinai (Exodus 24:16-17), and was seen by Isaiah in the temple (Isaiah 6:3, "the whole earth is full of his glory"). In this document, Jesus's glory was seen at the first sign: "This beginning of his signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee, and manifested his glory" (2:11). The author identified Isaiah's temple vision as a vision of Jesus's glory (12:41). Now Jesus asks the Father to glorify him — to manifest through the events about to unfold (arrest, trial, crucifixion, resurrection) the same kavod that filled the tabernacle and the temple. The cross is not the interruption of glory but its fullest expression, consistent with the "lifting up" that is simultaneously crucifixion and exaltation (3:14; 8:28; 12:32-33).

The request "glorify your Son" uses the third person ("the Son") rather than the first person ("me"), which creates a formal, almost declaratory quality. Jesus speaks of himself in the Son-Father relational frame that has governed the document. The purpose clause — "that the Son may glorify you" — reveals that the Son's glorification is not an end in itself but serves the Father's glory. The Son receives glory in order to give it back. This is consistent with 14:13: "Whatever you ask in my name, that will I do, that the Father may be glorified in the Son."

"Even as you gave him authority over all flesh, that to all whom you have given him, he may give eternal life" (kathos edokas auto exousian pases sarkos, hina pan ho dedokas auto dose autois zoen aionion). The conjunction kathos ("even as, just as") links the glorification request to the authority already given. The authority (exousia, "authority, power, right") over "all flesh" (pases sarkos) echoes Daniel 7:14 (LXX), where the one like a son of man receives "authority (exousia) and all the nations of the earth by race (genei) and all glory, serving him." The phrase "all flesh" (pasa sarx) is a Hebraism for all humanity (Genesis 6:12, "all flesh had corrupted their way"; Isaiah 40:5, "all flesh shall see the salvation of God"; Joel 2:28 [LXX 3:1], "I will pour out my spirit on all flesh"). The authority is universal in scope — not limited to Israel but extending to all humanity.

The purpose of this authority is stated: "that to all whom you have given him, he may give eternal life" (hina pan ho dedokas auto dose autois zoen aionion). The word "all" (pan, neuter singular, referring to the collective whole) that the Father has "given" (dedokas, perfect tense — a completed gift with ongoing effect) to the Son receives eternal life from the Son. The language of "giving" runs throughout the document as the description of how people come to belong to Jesus: "All that the Father gives me will come to me" (6:37); "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (6:44); "No one can come to me unless it has been granted him from the Father" (6:65); "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me; and I give eternal life to them" (10:27-28). The Father gives people to the Son; the Son gives eternal life to them. The chain of giving mirrors the chain of love and authority that the farewell discourse has described.

"This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent" (haute de estin he aionios zoe, hina ginoskosin se ton monon alethinon theon kai hon apesteilas Iesoun Christon). This is the only definition of eternal life in the document. The word zoe ("life") has been central since the prologue ("In him was life," 1:4) and has appeared in connection with believing throughout (3:15, 16, 36; 5:24; 6:40, 47, 54; 10:28; 20:31). But here, eternal life is not defined as a quantity of existence or a destination; it is defined as knowledge — ongoing, relational knowledge. The verb ginoskosin (present subjunctive of ginosko, "to know") is in the present tense, indicating continuous, ongoing knowing. Eternal life is not a future possession but a present activity: knowing the Father and knowing Jesus.

The word ginosko in the LXX carries the sense of intimate, experiential knowledge — not merely intellectual awareness but relational engagement. In Genesis 4:1, Adam "knew" (egno) Eve — the most intimate form of knowledge. In Jeremiah 31:34 (LXX 38:34), the new covenant promise is: "They shall all know me (gnosontai me), from the least of them to the greatest." In Hosea 6:6: "I desire the knowledge of God (epignosin theou) rather than burnt offerings." The knowledge that constitutes eternal life is not the accumulation of information about God but the relational engagement with God that the prophets described and the new covenant promised.

The phrase "the only true God" (ton monon alethinon theon) echoes the Shema: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). The adjective alethinos ("true, genuine, real") is the same word used throughout the document for the ultimate reality as opposed to the preliminary version: "the true light" (1:9), "the true bread" (6:32), "the true vine" (15:1). The Father is the only God who is true — real, genuine, ultimate. The addition "and Jesus Christ whom you have sent" (kai hon apesteilas Iesoun Christon) places knowledge of Jesus alongside knowledge of the Father as constitutive of eternal life. The conjunction kai ("and") does not subordinate Jesus to the Father but coordinates them: eternal life requires knowing both. This is consistent with 14:7 ("If you had known me, you would have known my Father also"), 14:9 ("Whoever has seen me has seen the Father"), and 1:18 ("No one has seen God at any time; the only begotten God who is in the bosom of the Father, he has explained him"). The knowledge of the Father and the knowledge of the Son are not two separate knowledges but a single knowing — the Father is known through the Son, and the Son is the Father's self-disclosure.

The title "Jesus Christ" (Iesoun Christon) is unusual in this document. The author typically uses "Jesus" alone in the narrative and places the title "Christ" (Christos, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Mashiach, "Anointed One") in the mouths of characters or in confessional statements (1:41, "We have found the Messiah [which is translated Christ]"; 4:25, 29; 7:26, 27, 31, 41, 42; 9:22; 10:24; 11:27; 20:31). The combination "Jesus Christ" occurs only here and in 1:17 ("grace and truth came through Jesus Christ"). The full title in this prayer has a formal, declarative quality — it names the one through whom the Father is known, using both the human name and the messianic title.

"I glorified you on the earth, having accomplished the work which you have given me to do" (ego se edoxasa epi tes ges, to ergon teleiosas ho dedokas moi hina poieso). The aorist tense edoxasa ("I glorified") and the participle teleiosas ("having completed, having accomplished, having brought to perfection") speak of the work as already finished, even though the cross has not yet occurred. The word teleioo ("to complete, to bring to its goal, to perfect, to finish") is related to telos ("end, goal, completion"), the same root as eis telos in 13:1 ("he loved them to the end/to completion"). The work is treated as accomplished because the prayer is spoken within the hour; the glorification through death is so certain that it can be spoken of in the past tense.

The "work" (to ergon, singular) encompasses the entirety of Jesus's mission — the signs, the teaching, the revelation of the Father, the gathering of the disciples, and now the death that completes it all. In 4:34, Jesus said: "My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish (teleioo) his work (ergon)." In 5:36: "The works (erga) which the Father has given me to accomplish (teleioo) — the very works that I do — testify about me, that the Father has sent me." The plural "works" (erga) of 5:36 are now gathered into a single "work" (ergon) in 17:4 — the entire mission treated as one unified task, now brought to completion. The verb teleioo will appear again at the cross itself: "It is finished" (tetelestai, 19:30), where the perfect tense of the same verb declares the completion of the work.

"Now, Father, glorify me together with yourself, with the glory which I had with you before the world was" (kai nyn doxason me sy, Pater, para seauto te doxe he eichon pro tou ton kosmon einai para soi). The request is for the restoration of a glory that existed before the creation. The phrase "before the world was" (pro tou ton kosmon einai) echoes the prologue: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (1:1). The glory Jesus asks to receive is the glory he shared with the Father "before the world was" — the pre-creational glory of the Word who was "with God" (pros ton theon, 1:1). The preposition para ("with, alongside, in the presence of") in both "together with yourself" (para seauto) and "with you" (para soi) describes intimate proximity — the same preposition used in 1:1 (pros ton theon, toward God / in the presence of God) for the Word's relationship to God before creation.

The request is not for something new but for something restored. The incarnation — the Word becoming flesh (1:14) — involved a setting aside of this pre-creational glory (the verbal echo of 13:4, where Jesus "laid aside" his garments, and 10:17-18, where the shepherd "lays down" his life). Now Jesus asks the Father to restore what was set aside. The cross, resurrection, and return to the Father constitute the resumption of the glory that was temporarily veiled by the flesh. The prayer reveals the full arc of the document's narrative: the Word was with God in glory (1:1), the Word became flesh and dwelt among humanity (1:14), and now the Word asks to return to the glory he had before the creation of the world.

Verses 6-8: The Disciples as Those Given by the Father

"I have manifested your name to the men whom you gave me out of the world. They were yours, and you gave them to me, and they have kept your word. Now they have come to know that everything you have given me is from you; for the words which you gave me I have given to them; and they received them and truly understood that I came forth from you, and they believed that you sent me."

"I have manifested your name to the men whom you gave me out of the world" (ephanerosa sou to onoma tois anthropois hous edokas moi ek tou kosmou). The verb phaneroo ("to make visible, manifest, reveal, display") appeared in 1:31 (John the Baptist came "so that he might be manifested to Israel"), 2:11 ("manifested his glory"), 7:4 ("manifest yourself to the world"), 9:3 ("that the works of God might be displayed in him"), and 14:21-22 (Jesus will "manifest myself to him" / "manifest yourself to us and not to the world?"). The word describes making something visible that was previously hidden or inaccessible. Jesus has made the Father's "name" (onoma) visible.

The "name" (onoma) in Israel's scriptures is not a label but the self-disclosure of God's character and identity. In Exodus 3:13-15, Moses asks God for his name, and God reveals: "I AM WHO I AM" (ego eimi ho on, LXX). In Exodus 34:5-7, the LORD descends and proclaims his name: "The LORD, the LORD God, compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in lovingkindness and truth." The name is what God reveals about himself — his nature, his character, his way of relating to his people. When Jesus says "I have manifested your name," he claims to have made God's essential character visible and knowable to the disciples. The entire public ministry — the signs, the "I am" declarations, the discourses, the foot-washing, the teaching about mutual love — has been the manifestation of the Father's name.

The recipients are described as "the men whom you gave me out of the world" (tois anthropois hous edokas moi ek tou kosmou). The Father gave them; they came from the world; they now belong to Jesus. The preposition ek ("out of") indicates extraction, as in 15:19: "I chose you out of the world" (ek tou kosmou). The disciples were once part of the world-system that opposes God; they have been taken out of it and given to the Son.

"They were yours, and you gave them to me, and they have kept your word" (soi esan kai emoi autous edokas, kai ton logon sou teterekasin). The sequence of ownership is stated: the disciples belonged to the Father first ("they were yours"), and the Father gave them to Jesus ("you gave them to me"). The prior belonging explains how they came to Jesus — they were already the Father's before the Son received them. This is consistent with 6:44: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him." The disciples' coming to Jesus was not random self-selection but the Father's act of drawing those who belonged to him.

The verb teterekasin (perfect tense of tereo, "to keep, guard, observe") indicates a completed action with ongoing results: "they have kept and continue to keep your word." The "word" (logos) they have kept is the Father's word mediated through Jesus (14:24: "the word which you hear is not mine but the Father's who sent me"). Keeping the word is the condition of belonging that has been stated repeatedly: 8:51 ("If anyone keeps my word, he will never see death"), 14:23 ("If anyone loves me, he will keep my word"), 15:10 ("If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love"). Jesus testifies to the Father that the disciples have met this condition.

"Now they have come to know that everything you have given me is from you" (nyn egnosan hoti panta hosa dedokas moi para sou eisin). The aorist egnosan ("they came to know, they recognized") indicates a recognition that has occurred. The disciples have arrived at the understanding that everything Jesus possesses — his words, his works, his authority, his mission — originates from the Father. This knowledge is what qualifies them as those who genuinely belong: they understand the Father-Son relationship that defines Jesus's identity.

"For the words which you gave me I have given to them; and they received them and truly understood that I came forth from you, and they believed that you sent me" (hoti ta rhemata ha edokas moi dedoka autois, kai autoi elabon kai egnosan alethos hoti para sou exelthon, kai episteusan hoti sy me apesteilas). The chain of transmission is traced: the Father gave words (rhemata, the specific sayings and utterances) to Jesus; Jesus gave those words to the disciples; the disciples received (elabon) them. The verbs describe three responses: they received (elabon), they truly understood (egnosan alethos, "they knew with certainty"), and they believed (episteusan). The three responses are sequential: reception of the words leads to understanding of Jesus's origin ("I came forth from you") and belief in his mission ("you sent me").

The word alethos ("truly, genuinely, with certainty") qualifies the knowledge: the understanding is not superficial or tentative but real. This may seem in tension with the disciples' repeated misunderstandings throughout the farewell discourse (14:5, Thomas's question about the way; 14:8, Philip's request to see the Father; 16:17-18, the confusion about "a little while") and Jesus's own question "Do you now believe?" (16:31), immediately followed by the prediction of their scattering. But the prayer addresses the quality of the disciples' orientation, not the completeness of their comprehension. They have genuinely received the words, genuinely recognized Jesus's origin from the Father, and genuinely believed in his sending. Their understanding is real even if it remains partial and will be severely tested. Jesus does not pray to the Father about perfect disciples; he prays about disciples whose faith, though imperfect, is authentic.

Verses 9-13: Intercession for the Disciples' Keeping

"I ask on their behalf; I do not ask on behalf of the world, but of those whom you have given me; for they are yours; and all things that are mine are yours, and yours are mine; and I have been glorified in them. I am no longer in the world; and yet they themselves are in the world, and I come to you. Holy Father, keep them in your name, the name which you have given me, that they may be one even as we are. While I was with them, I was keeping them in your name which you have given me; and I guarded them and not one of them perished but the son of perdition, so that the Scripture would be fulfilled. But now I come to you; and these things I speak in the world so that they may have my joy made full in themselves."

"I ask on their behalf; I do not ask on behalf of the world, but of those whom you have given me; for they are yours" (ego peri auton eroto; ou peri tou kosmou eroto alla peri hon dedokas moi, hoti soi eisin). The verb eroto ("I ask, I request") is distinct from aiteo ("to ask for something, to petition"). In this document, eroto tends to describe relational requests — asking between persons of mutual dignity (as in 14:16: "I will ask [eroteso] the Father, and he will give you another Helper"). The prayer is an intercession — Jesus stands between the disciples and the Father, asking on their behalf.

The restriction — "I do not ask on behalf of the world" (ou peri tou kosmou eroto) — does not mean Jesus has no concern for the world. The entire document has affirmed God's love for the world (3:16, "God so loved the world that he gave his only begotten Son") and Jesus's mission to the world (3:17, "God did not send the Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him"). The restriction means that this particular prayer, at this particular moment, is focused on those the Father has given — the community of disciples. The world will be addressed later in the prayer, indirectly, through the unity and mission of the disciples (17:21, 23: "that the world may believe / that the world may know"). The saving of the world is accomplished not by praying for the world directly but by equipping the community whose witness will reach the world.

"And all things that are mine are yours, and yours are mine" (kai ta ema panta sa estin kai ta sa ema). The mutual ownership between Father and Son is stated without qualification. Everything belonging to the Son belongs to the Father, and everything belonging to the Father belongs to the Son. This echoes 16:15: "All things that the Father has are mine." The mutuality extends what the document has been building since the prologue: the Word who was with God and was God (1:1) shares all things with the Father. The disciples, who belong to the Father ("they are yours") and have been given to the Son, are part of this shared possession. They belong to both.

"And I have been glorified in them" (kai dedoxasmai en autois). The perfect tense dedoxasmai ("I have been glorified and continue to be glorified") indicates an ongoing state. Jesus has been glorified in the disciples — their reception of his words, their recognition of his origin, their belief in his sending constitute a glorification of Jesus. The disciples' faith, however imperfect, is a manifestation of the glory the Father gave the Son. The fruitful branches glorify the vine; the disciples' belief glorifies the one they believe in (cf. 15:8: "My Father is glorified by this, that you bear much fruit").

"I am no longer in the world; and yet they themselves are in the world, and I come to you" (kai ouketi eimi en to kosmo, kai autoi en to kosmo eisin, kago pros se erchomai). The phrase "I am no longer in the world" (ouketi eimi en to kosmo) speaks from the perspective of the completed departure — Jesus regards his presence in the world as already concluded, even though the arrest has not yet occurred. The prayer's temporal standpoint is the threshold: Jesus is already, in effect, departing. The "and yet" (kai) introduces the problem the prayer addresses: Jesus is leaving, but the disciples remain in the world. The world that hated Jesus (15:18) will hate them (15:19). The world that killed the shepherd will persecute the sheep (16:2). The world whose ruler has been judged (16:11) will still press upon the community. The prayer arises from this asymmetry: Jesus goes to the Father; the disciples stay in the hostile world.

"Holy Father, keep them in your name, the name which you have given me, that they may be one even as we are" (Pater hagie, tereson autous en to onomati sou ho dedokas moi, hina osin hen kathos hemeis). The address "Holy Father" (Pater hagie) is unique in the document. The adjective hagios ("holy, set apart, consecrated") is the LXX's primary term for God's distinct otherness: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3, LXX: hagios hagios hagios kyrios sabaoth). In Leviticus 11:44-45, 19:2, 20:7, 26, God's holiness is the ground of Israel's call to holiness: "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy." The address "Holy Father" invokes the Father's sanctifying character — the character that sets apart, that consecrates — precisely because the prayer is about to ask for the disciples' sanctification (17:17, "sanctify them in the truth").

The petition "keep them in your name" (tereson autous en to onomati sou) uses the imperative of tereo ("to keep, guard, protect, watch over"). The verb tereo has appeared throughout the farewell discourse for the disciples' keeping of Jesus's commandments (14:15, 21; 15:10, 20). Now the same verb is used in the opposite direction: not the disciples keeping Jesus's word, but the Father keeping the disciples. The keeping is "in your name" (en to onomati sou) — that is, within the sphere of the Father's revealed character and identity. The name is described as "the name which you have given me" (ho dedokas moi) — the Father's own name has been entrusted to Jesus, and the keeping of the disciples is in this shared name. The name is the sphere of divine reality in which the community is held secure.

The purpose of the keeping is unity: "that they may be one even as we are" (hina osin hen kathos hemeis). The oneness requested is modeled on the Father-Son unity. In 10:30, Jesus declared: "I and the Father are one" (ego kai ho pater hen esmen). The word hen ("one," neuter) describes not identity of person but unity of purpose, will, and action. The Father and Son are one in the sense that they act together, will together, and share all things (17:10). The disciples are to be one in the same way — united in purpose, love, and mission, reflecting the unity of the Father and Son. The unity is not organizational uniformity but relational oneness modeled on the divine relationship.

"While I was with them, I was keeping them in your name which you have given me; and I guarded them and not one of them perished but the son of perdition, so that the Scripture would be fulfilled" (hote emen met' auton ego etoroun autous en to onomati sou ho dedokas moi, kai ephylaxa, kai oudeis ex auton apoleto ei me ho huios tes apoleias, hina he graphe plerothe). Two verbs describe Jesus's protective activity: eteroun (imperfect of tereo, "I was keeping" — ongoing, continuous keeping throughout his time with them) and ephylaxa (aorist of phylasso, "I guarded" — a summary of the total protective act). The verb phylasso in the LXX is used for guarding treasure, keeping watch over a flock, and protecting against enemies (Psalm 121:7 [LXX 120:7]: "The LORD will guard you from all evil; he will guard your life"). Jesus has been functioning as both the keeper and the guard of the disciples throughout his ministry.

The result: "not one of them perished" (oudeis ex auton apoleto). The verb apollymi ("to perish, destroy, be lost") is the document's primary word for ultimate loss. In 3:16: "whoever believes in him shall not perish (apolotai) but have eternal life." In 6:39: "this is the will of him who sent me, that of all that he has given me I lose (apoleso) nothing." In 10:28: "I give eternal life to them, and they will never perish (apolontai)." In 11:50: "one man die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish (apoletai)." The claim is that throughout Jesus's earthly ministry, he kept all those the Father gave him, and none was lost.

The exception: "but the son of perdition" (ei me ho huios tes apoleias). The phrase ho huios tes apoleias ("the son of destruction, the son of perdition") uses a Hebrew idiom in which "son of" indicates a person characterized by or destined for the quality named. "Son of perdition" means one whose defining characteristic is destruction/perishing — one who belongs to apoleia ("destruction"). The word apoleia is cognate with apollymi — the same root. The "son of perdition" is the one whose nature is perishing, who embodies the very loss from which the others were kept. The reference is clearly to Judas, who departed into the night (13:30) and whose betrayal was described as the entry of Satan into him (13:27).

The phrase "so that the Scripture would be fulfilled" (hina he graphe plerothe) indicates that Judas's perishing belongs within the pattern of scriptural fulfillment. The specific Scripture is not quoted here, but the document has already applied Psalm 41:9 (LXX 40:10) to Judas in 13:18: "He who eats my bread has lifted up his heel against me." Additional scriptural backgrounds may include Psalm 109 (LXX 108), a psalm of betrayal by a companion, which verse 8 states: "Let another take his office" (applied to Judas in Acts 1:20). The point is that Judas's perishing was not an accident or a failure of Jesus's keeping; it was the fulfillment of a scriptural pattern. The betrayer's loss does not contradict the keeper's faithfulness; it fulfills a divine purpose attested in Israel's writings.

The grammatical structure of "not one of them perished but the son of perdition" (oudeis ex auton apoleto ei me ho huios tes apoleias) requires careful attention. The phrase ei me ("except, but") can function as a true exception ("none perished except this one, who did perish from among them") or as an adversative that marks a contrast without including the exception in the original group ("none of them perished, but the son of perdition [who was not truly of them]"). The document's own language supports the second reading. In 6:70-71, Jesus said: "Did I myself not choose you, the twelve, and yet one of you is a devil?" The "and yet" (kai) marks Judas as an anomaly within the group. In 13:10-11, "you are clean, but not all of you" — Judas was present but not clean. In 1 John 2:19 (from the same authorial tradition): "They went out from us, but they were not really of us; for if they had been of us, they would have remained with us." The son of perdition was among the disciples but was never truly "of" them in the sense that the prayer addresses. His perishing is not a loss from the group Jesus was keeping but the manifestation of a character that was never truly aligned with the Father's gift.

The significance for the prayer is direct: Jesus has kept all whom the Father gave him. Now that Jesus is departing, he asks the Father to take over the keeping. The transfer of protective responsibility is the heart of the petition.

"But now I come to you; and these things I speak in the world so that they may have my joy made full in themselves" (nyn de pros se erchomai, kai tauta lalo en to kosmo hina echosin ten charan ten emen pepleromenon en heautois). The phrase "I come to you" (pros se erchomai) restates the departure from 13:1, 16:28. The prayer is spoken "in the world" (en to kosmo) — while Jesus is still physically present, within the hearing of the disciples. The purpose of speaking the prayer in the world, in their hearing, is "so that they may have my joy made full in themselves" (hina echosin ten charan ten emen pepleromenon en heautois). The joy is "my joy" (ten charan ten emen) — Jesus's own joy, not a generic happiness. In 15:11: "These things I have spoken to you so that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be made full." In 16:22: "Your heart will rejoice, and no one will take your joy away from you." The joy that Jesus possesses is the joy of the one who abides in the Father's love (15:10) and who has accomplished the Father's work (17:4). This joy is to be transferred to the disciples and brought to fullness (pepleromenon, perfect passive participle — "filled to completion") within them. The disciples' overhearing of Jesus's prayer to the Father — hearing him commend them to the Father's care, hearing him testify to their faithfulness, hearing him ask for their protection — is itself a source of joy. The prayer is pastoral even in its heavenly address.

Verses 14-19: The Disciples in the World but Not of It

"I have given them your word; and the world has hated them, because they are not of the world, even as I am not of the world. I do not ask you to take them out of the world, but to keep them from the evil one. They are not of the world, even as I am not of the world. Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth. As you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world. For their sakes I sanctify myself, that they themselves also may be sanctified in truth."

"I have given them your word; and the world has hated them, because they are not of the world, even as I am not of the world" (ego dedoka autois ton logon sou, kai ho kosmos emisesen autous, hoti ouk eisin ek tou kosmou kathos ego ouk eimi ek tou kosmou). The word (logos, singular) given is the Father's word — the comprehensive message, the total revelation, not merely individual sayings (rhemata, verse 8) but the unified message. The giving of the word has produced a consequence: the world's hatred (emisesen, aorist of miseo). The cause of the hatred is stated: the disciples "are not of the world" (ouk eisin ek tou kosmou). The preposition ek ("from, out of") indicates origin and belonging. The disciples do not originate from the world-system, they do not belong to it, they are not characterized by it. The comparison "even as I am not of the world" (kathos ego ouk eimi ek tou kosmou) places the disciples' alien status on the same footing as Jesus's own: just as Jesus does not belong to the world, so the disciples do not belong to it. Their foreignness to the world is derivative of his foreignness.

This restates the teaching of 15:18-19: "If the world hates you, you know that it has hated me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, because of this the world hates you." The prayer reports to the Father what the discourse told the disciples: the world hates them because they have been extracted from the world and given the word that the world rejects.

"I do not ask you to take them out of the world, but to keep them from the evil one" (ouk eroto hina ares autous ek tou kosmou, all' hina tereses autous ek tou ponerou). The petition is not for removal but for protection. The verb ares (aorist subjunctive of airo, "to take away, remove, lift up") is the same verb used in 15:2 for the vinedresser's removal of the fruitless branch (pan klema en emoi me pheron karpon airei auto) and in 1:29 for the Lamb taking away the sin of the world. Jesus does not ask the Father to extract the disciples from the world. They are needed in the world — they have a mission to the world (17:18, 20-21). The request is for preservation within the world, not escape from it.

The phrase ek tou ponerou ("from the evil one") can be translated either "from evil" (the neuter to poneron, abstract evil) or "from the evil one" (the masculine ho poneros, a personal agent of evil). The document's consistent personification of the opposition supports the latter reading. The "ruler of this world" (ho archon tou kosmou, 12:31; 14:30; 16:11) is a personal agent. The devil (ho diabolos) put the intention of betrayal into Judas's heart (13:2). Satan (ho Satanas) entered Judas (13:27). The "evil one" from whom Jesus asks protection is the personal adversary who rules the world-system and has already demonstrated his capacity to destroy from within the community (Judas). The prayer asks the Father to keep the disciples from the same fate — to prevent the evil one from destroying them as he destroyed Judas.

"They are not of the world, even as I am not of the world" (ouk eisin ek tou kosmou kathos ego ouk eimi ek tou kosmou). The repetition of verse 14's statement (verbatim) is deliberate. It functions as the hinge between the negative petition (do not take them out) and the positive petition (sanctify them). Because they are not of the world, they need not be removed from it — they are already fundamentally distinct from it. And because they are not of the world, they are candidates for sanctification — for being set apart for holy purpose within the world.

"Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth" (hagiason autous en te aletheia; ho logos ho sos aletheia estin). The verb hagiaso ("to sanctify, to make holy, to set apart, to consecrate") is from the same root as hagios ("holy"), the adjective used in the address "Holy Father" (Pater hagie, verse 11). In the LXX, hagiaso describes the consecration of persons and objects for God's service. Exodus 28:41: Aaron and his sons are "sanctified" (hagiaseis) for priestly service. Exodus 29:44: "I will sanctify the tent of meeting and the altar; I will also sanctify Aaron and his sons to minister as priests to me." Leviticus 20:7-8: "Consecrate yourselves therefore, and be holy, for I am the LORD your God. Keep my statutes and practice them; I am the LORD who sanctifies you." Jeremiah 1:5: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated (hegiasa) you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations." In each case, sanctification is not merely moral purification but vocational consecration — being set apart for a specific purpose in God's service.

The sphere of sanctification is "in the truth" (en te aletheia). Truth (aletheia) in this document is not an abstraction but a person and his word: Jesus is "the truth" (14:6), and the Father's word is truth (17:17b). The Spirit is "the Spirit of truth" (14:17; 15:26; 16:13) who will "guide into all the truth" (16:13). The sanctification is accomplished through the word — the Father's word mediated through Jesus and activated by the Spirit. The disciples are consecrated by being immersed in the truth that Jesus has revealed.

The declaration "your word is truth" (ho logos ho sos aletheia estin) identifies the Father's word as truth itself — not merely truthful (as an attribute) but truth (as an identity). The word is what is real, what is reliable, what corresponds to the way things actually are in God's economy. The disciples are sanctified by this word — set apart from the world's lies and consecrated for service within the world.

"As you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world" (kathos eme apesteilas eis ton kosmon, kago apesteilas autous eis ton kosmon). The conjunction kathos ("just as, in the same way as") establishes correspondence between the Father's sending of Jesus and Jesus's sending of the disciples. The verb apostello ("to send with a commission, to dispatch as an authorized agent") is the technical term for missional sending throughout the document (3:17, 34; 5:36, 38; 6:29, 57; 7:29; 8:42; 10:36; 11:42; 20:21). The disciples' mission is patterned on Jesus's own mission: as the Father sent the Son into the world, so the Son sends the disciples into the world. The same world from which they are not (ouk ek tou kosmou) is the world into which they are sent (eis ton kosmon). They are foreign to the world and simultaneously commissioned to the world. Their sanctification is not for withdrawal but for mission — they are set apart in order to be sent.

This restates in prayer form what will be enacted after the resurrection: "As the Father has sent me, I also send you" (20:21). The kathos ("just as") in both passages (17:18 and 20:21) indicates that the disciples' mission has the same character as Jesus's mission: they go as authorized agents, carrying the sender's message, representing the sender's person, and performing the sender's work.

"For their sakes I sanctify myself, that they themselves also may be sanctified in truth" (kai hyper auton ego hagiazo emauton, hina osin kai autoi hegiasmenoi en aletheia). The phrase "I sanctify myself" (ego hagiazo emauton) is remarkable. In what sense does Jesus sanctify himself? The verb hagiaso, as noted above, means "to set apart, to consecrate for a sacred purpose." In the LXX, the language of self-consecration appears in the context of sacrifice: the one who consecrates himself is the one who sets himself apart for offering. Deuteronomy 15:19-21 uses the verb for setting apart the firstborn for sacrifice. Numbers 6:2-8 describes the Nazirite who "separates himself" (hagiase) to the LORD. The most direct parallel is the consecration of the priests and sacrificial animals — the setting apart for sacred use that culminates in offering.

Jesus sets himself apart for the act of death. The phrase "for their sakes" (hyper auton, "on their behalf, for their benefit") gives the consecration its object: Jesus dedicates himself to the sacrifice that serves the disciples' sanctification. The purpose clause — "that they themselves also may be sanctified in truth" (hina osin kai autoi hegiasmenoi en aletheia) — makes the connection explicit: Jesus's self-consecration (his death) is the means by which the disciples are consecrated. The shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (10:11, 15), and that laying down enables the sheep to be set apart for their own mission. The perfect passive participle hegiasmenoi ("having been sanctified and remaining sanctified") indicates a completed and enduring consecration: the disciples will be sanctified once and remain in that condition. Their sanctification is grounded in Jesus's self-offering, not in their own effort.

Verses 20-23: The Prayer Extends to Future Believers

"I do not ask on behalf of these alone, but for those also who believe in me through their word; that they may all be one; even as you, Father, are in me and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you sent me. The glory which you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one, just as we are one; I in them and you in me, that they may be perfected in unity, so that the world may know that you sent me, and loved them, even as you loved me."

"I do not ask on behalf of these alone, but for those also who believe in me through their word" (ou peri touton de eroto monon, alla kai peri ton pisteuonton dia tou logou auton eis eme). The prayer's scope widens. The "these" (touton) are the disciples present at the supper — the eleven who remain after Judas's departure. The "those who believe in me through their word" (ton pisteuonton dia tou logou auton) are all future believers who will come to faith through the apostolic testimony. The present participle pisteuonton ("believing, those who are believing") describes an ongoing, expanding community. The medium of their belief is the disciples' "word" (logos) — their testimony, their proclamation, the message they carry. This is the fulfillment of 15:27: "You will testify also, because you have been with me from the beginning." The disciples' witness will produce a community of believers that extends beyond those who knew Jesus in the flesh.

The prayer's expansion from the immediate disciples to future believers reveals the scope of Jesus's concern. He does not pray only for those he can see; he prays for the chain of faith that will extend from the apostolic witness across generations and geographies. The word dia ("through") indicates the instrumental cause: future believers will believe because of the disciples' word. The effectiveness of the disciples' mission is presupposed; Jesus prays for the community that their mission will produce.

"That they may all be one; even as you, Father, are in me and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you sent me" (hina pantes hen osin, kathos sy, Pater, en emoi kago en soi, hina kai autoi en hemin osin, hina ho kosmos pisteue hoti sy me apesteilas). The petition is for unity — the same unity requested for the immediate disciples in verse 11 ("that they may be one even as we are"), now extended to the entire community of faith. The standard is again the Father-Son relationship: "even as you, Father, are in me and I in you." The mutual indwelling of Father and Son (10:38; 14:10-11, 20) is the pattern for the community's oneness. The kathos ("even as") does not imply identity of nature but correspondence of quality: the community's unity should reflect the Father-Son unity.

The addition "that they also may be in us" (hina kai autoi en hemin osin) draws the believers into the divine relationship itself. The mutual indwelling of 14:20 ("I am in my Father, and you in me, and I in you") is now the prayer's content: the believers are to be "in us" — in the Father and in the Son. The community's unity is not merely horizontal (believers with one another) but vertical (believers with the Father and Son). The horizontal unity is grounded in the vertical: they are one with each other because they are all in the Father and the Son.

The purpose of the unity is missional: "so that the world may believe that you sent me" (hina ho kosmos pisteue hoti sy me apesteilas). The world — the system that opposes God, that hates Jesus and his followers (15:18-25), that cannot receive the Spirit of truth (14:17) — will believe through the community's visible unity. The world's belief is not produced by argument or signs alone but by the observable reality of a community whose oneness reflects the divine oneness. The unity of believers is the community's testimony: when the world sees people who are one in the way the Father and Son are one, the world recognizes that the one who produced this unity was sent by God. The unity functions as a sign — the community's life together becomes the evidence of Jesus's divine origin.

"The glory which you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one, just as we are one" (kago ten doxan hen dedokas moi dedoka autois, hina osin hen kathos hemeis hen). The glory (doxa) that the Father gave to Jesus has been passed on to the believers. The word doxa has been used throughout the document for the visible manifestation of God's presence and character. In 1:14: "We saw his glory, glory as of the only begotten from the Father." In 2:11: the first sign "manifested his glory." In 12:41: Isaiah "saw his glory." In 17:5: the pre-creational glory. Now this glory is given to the community. The glory is not something kept by Jesus alone but shared with those who belong to him.

What is this glory? The context identifies it as the Father-Son relationship itself — the mutual indwelling, the shared love, the unity of will and purpose. The glory that the Father gave the Son was the unique relationship of oneness between Father and Son. The Son now gives this relational glory to the community: they are drawn into the same unity, the same mutual indwelling, the same love that defines the Father-Son relationship. The purpose of giving them this glory is unity: "that they may be one, just as we are one" (hina osin hen kathos hemeis hen). The glory produces the unity. The community is one because it shares in the same relational reality that makes Father and Son one.

"I in them and you in me, that they may be perfected in unity, so that the world may know that you sent me, and loved them, even as you loved me" (ego en autois kai sy en emoi, hina osin teteleiomenoi eis hen, hina ginoske ho kosmos hoti sy me apesteilas kai egapesas autous kathos eme egapesas). The mutual indwelling is now stated in its fullest form. Jesus is in the believers ("I in them"). The Father is in Jesus ("you in me"). Therefore the Father, through Jesus, is in the believers, and the believers, through Jesus, are in the Father. The chain of indwelling is complete: Father-in-Son-in-believers and believers-in-Son-in-Father. There is no access to the Father except through the Son (14:6), and there is no access to the Son except through the faith produced by the apostolic word (17:20).

The purpose of this mutual indwelling is that the believers may be "perfected in unity" (teteleiomenoi eis hen). The verb teleloo ("to perfect, to bring to completion, to bring to the goal") is from the same root as teleioo in verse 4 ("having accomplished the work") and tetelestai in 19:30 ("It is finished"). The unity is not merely intended or aspired to; it is to be brought to its goal, completed, perfected. The participial form teteleiomenoi (perfect passive: "having been perfected and remaining in that perfected state") describes a unity that has reached its telos — its purpose and completion.

The world's response to this perfected unity is knowledge: "so that the world may know that you sent me, and loved them, even as you loved me" (hina ginoske ho kosmos hoti sy me apesteilas kai egapesas autous kathos eme egapesas). In verse 21, the result was "that the world may believe"; here, the result is "that the world may know." The progression from belief (pisteue) to knowledge (ginoske) may indicate deepening: the world moves from initial belief to settled knowledge as it observes the community's unity. The content of the world's knowledge is twofold: (1) "you sent me" — Jesus's divine origin and mission, and (2) "you loved them even as you loved me" — the Father's love for the community is the same love the Father has for the Son. This is an extraordinary claim: the love the Father has for the believers is not a lesser version of the love the Father has for the Son but the same love (kathos, "even as, just as"). The community lives within the Father's love for the Son.

Verses 24-26: The Final Desire — To Be Where Jesus Is

"Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, be with me where I am, so that they may see my glory which you have given me, for you loved me before the foundation of the world. O righteous Father, although the world has not known you, yet I have known you; and these have known that you sent me; and I have made your name known to them, and will make it known, so that the love with which you loved me may be in them, and I in them."

"Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, be with me where I am" (Pater, ho dedokas moi, thelo hina hopou eimi ego kakeinoi osin met' emou). The word thelo ("I will, I desire, I want") is stronger than a request — it expresses the Son's will, his settled desire. This is not a tentative petition but a declaration of purpose. The Son wills that the community given to him by the Father should be "with me where I am" (hopou eimi ego). This fulfills the promise of 14:3: "If I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and receive you to myself, that where I am you may be also." The "where" is the Father's presence — the dwelling places of 14:2, the glory of 17:5, the bosom of the Father (1:18). Jesus desires the community's ultimate destination to be his own: the presence of the Father.

The phrase "whom you have given me" (ho dedokas moi) continues the pattern of treating the community as the Father's gift (6:37, 39; 10:29; 17:2, 6, 9, 11, 12). The repetition throughout the prayer — "those whom you have given me" — anchors the community's identity in the Father's initiative. They belong because the Father gave them, not because they achieved membership.

"So that they may see my glory which you have given me, for you loved me before the foundation of the world" (hina theorosin ten doxan ten emen hen dedokas moi, hoti egapesas me pro kataboles kosmou). The verb theorosin (present subjunctive of theoreo, "to behold, observe, contemplate") describes sustained, attentive seeing — not a momentary glance but the ongoing contemplation of something that rewards continued attention. The glory they are to see is "my glory which you have given me" — the glory of the Son in the Father's presence, the pre-creational glory of 17:5, the doxa that the Word had "with God" before the world was made (1:1-2).

The basis of this glory is stated: "for you loved me before the foundation of the world" (hoti egapesas me pro kataboles kosmou). The phrase "before the foundation of the world" (pro kataboles kosmou) is parallel to "before the world was" (pro tou ton kosmon einai) in verse 5. The Father's love for the Son precedes creation. The glory the Son possesses is not earned but given in love — the love that existed before anything else existed. The Son's glory is the visible expression of the Father's pre-creational love.

The eschatological dimension of this petition should not be overlooked. While the prayer addresses the immediate situation (the disciples' protection during Jesus's absence), this final desire reaches to the ultimate horizon: the community seeing and sharing in the glory of the Son in the Father's presence. This is the "last day" toward which the entire document points (6:39, 40, 44, 54; 11:24; 12:48) — not merely the survival of the community through persecution but its final destination in the presence of the glorified Son.

"O righteous Father, although the world has not known you, yet I have known you; and these have known that you sent me" (Pater dikaie, kai ho kosmos se ouk egno, ego de se egnon, kai houtoi egnosan hoti sy me apesteilas). The address "righteous Father" (Pater dikaie) introduces the second qualifying adjective for the Father in this prayer (after "Holy Father" in verse 11). The adjective dikaios ("righteous, just, right") in the LXX describes God's faithfulness to his covenant commitments: "The LORD is righteous in all his ways and faithful in all his deeds" (Psalm 145:17, LXX 144:17); "Righteous are you, O LORD, and upright are your judgments" (Psalm 119:137, LXX 118:137). The address invokes the Father's justice — his reliablity, his consistency with his own character — at the moment Jesus distinguishes between the world and the community.

The contrast is drawn with precision. Three parties are named: the world, Jesus, and the disciples. The world "has not known you" (se ouk egno) — the world has failed to know the Father. This failure has been the ground of the world's opposition throughout the document (8:19, 55; 15:21; 16:3). Jesus "has known you" (ego de se egnon) — the Son's knowledge of the Father is the foundation of everything the document has presented (1:18; 7:29; 8:55; 10:15). The disciples "have known that you sent me" (houtoi egnosan hoti sy me apesteilas) — the disciples' knowledge is more limited than Jesus's knowledge; they know not the Father directly (as Jesus does) but the fact of Jesus's sending. Their knowledge is mediated — they know the Father through knowing that Jesus was sent by the Father. Yet this mediated knowledge separates them decisively from the world, which knows neither the Father nor the Son.

"And I have made your name known to them, and will make it known" (kai egnorisa autois to onoma sou kai gnoriso). The verb gnorizo ("to make known, reveal, disclose") appeared in 15:15: "all things that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." Now the same claim appears in the prayer: Jesus has revealed the Father's name — the Father's identity, character, and nature — to the disciples. The addition "and will make it known" (kai gnoriso, future tense) indicates that the revelation is not yet complete. What Jesus has revealed during his earthly ministry will continue to be revealed after his departure — through the Spirit who will "guide you into all the truth" (16:13), who will "take of mine and will disclose it to you" (16:14-15). The making known of the Father's name is an ongoing process that extends beyond the cross and resurrection into the community's life under the Spirit's guidance.

"So that the love with which you loved me may be in them, and I in them" (hina he agape hen egapesas me en autois e kago en autois). The final purpose clause of the prayer — and the final words of the farewell discourse as a whole — states the ultimate goal: the Father's love for the Son is to be "in them" (en autois), and Jesus himself is to be "in them" (kago en autois). The love the Father has for the Son (the pre-creational love of verse 24, the love that is the basis of all the glory and all the giving) is to take up residence within the community of believers. And Jesus himself — the one who is departing, the one who goes to the Father, the one who has manifested the Father's name — is to be within them.

The phrase "I in them" (kago en autois) is the last word of the farewell discourse. It is the answer to the entire crisis that began in 13:33 ("yet a little while I am with you"). Jesus is leaving, but he will be in them. The physical departure is compensated by interior indwelling. The "I" who departs in the flesh will be present within the community — through the Spirit (14:16-17), through the mutual indwelling (14:20, 23), through the abiding in the vine (15:4-5). The prayer ends not with absence but with presence. The last word is not departure but indwelling.

The love and the indwelling are not two separate gifts but aspects of one reality. The Father's love for the Son is the environment within which the community lives, and the Son's presence within the community is the means by which that love is experienced. The community does not generate its own love; it receives the Father's love for the Son, mediated by the Son's presence within them. The chain of love is complete: the Father loves the Son; the Son manifests the Father's name to the community; the community receives the Son's presence within them; and through the Son's indwelling, the Father's own love — the same love that was before the foundation of the world — becomes their lived experience.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. The chapter is the culmination of the farewell discourse (chapters 13-17), recasting the discourse's teaching as petition addressed to the Father. The shift from speaking to the disciples (chapters 14-16) to speaking to the Father (chapter 17) does not introduce new material but transposes the same themes into a different key. The promises made to the disciples become requests made of the Father. The glorification announced in 13:31-32 becomes a prayer for glorification (17:1, 5). The keeping of the commandments urged upon the disciples (14:15, 21; 15:10) becomes a request for the Father's keeping of the disciples (17:11, 15). The unity implied by the vine metaphor (15:1-8) and the love commandment (13:34; 15:12, 17) becomes an explicit petition for oneness (17:11, 21-23). The sanctification by the word (15:3, "you are already clean because of the word") becomes a request for sanctification in the truth (17:17). The farewell discourse is incomplete without this prayer: the instruction prepares the community for what lies ahead; the prayer entrusts the community to the Father's care for what lies ahead.

2. The prayer moves in three concentric circles: Jesus himself (vv. 1-5), the immediate disciples (vv. 6-19), and all future believers (vv. 20-26). The movement from center to circumference reflects the chain of mission: the Father sends the Son, the Son sends the disciples, the disciples' word produces believers. Each circle depends on the one within it: the future believers depend on the disciples' word, the disciples depend on Jesus's revelation, and Jesus depends on the Father's gift. The prayer's structure enacts the chain of authorized sending that the document has been describing since the prologue.

3. The opening petition for glorification (vv. 1-5) reveals the prayer's theology of the cross. The cross is not a defeat to be endured but a glorification to be requested. Jesus asks the Father to glorify him through the very events the hour brings — arrest, trial, crucifixion — because these events are the means by which the Son glorifies the Father (17:1, 4). The work is treated as already accomplished (17:4, teleiosas), even though the cross has not yet occurred, because the prayer is spoken from within the hour. The glorification requested is the restoration of the pre-creational glory the Son shared with the Father "before the world was" (17:5) — the incarnation's self-emptying reversed, the Word returning to the glory he had in the beginning (1:1-2).

4. Eternal life is defined as relational knowledge, not as a quantity of existence. The only definition of eternal life in the document appears in 17:3: "that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent." The present-tense verb (ginoskosin, "that they may be knowing") indicates an ongoing activity, not a one-time acquisition. Eternal life is the continuous knowing of the Father through the Son. This definition is consistent with the document's entire framework: the signs point to the revealer, the revealer points to the Father, and the knowledge of both is the substance of life itself.

5. The prayer's description of the disciples emphasizes the Father's initiative and the disciples' responsive reception. The disciples "were yours" before they were given to the Son (17:6). The Father gave them (17:2, 6, 9, 11, 12, 24). Jesus gave them the Father's words (17:8, 14). They received the words (17:8), recognized Jesus's origin from the Father (17:8), and believed in his sending (17:8). They kept the Father's word (17:6). The initiative belongs to the Father and the Son; the disciples' role is reception and keeping. At the same time, the prayer does not describe this reception as passive or inevitable: the disciples are commended for having kept the word, and the prayer for their protection implies that the keeping must continue and could fail (as it did with "the son of perdition," 17:12).

6. The "son of perdition" (17:12) is treated as a scriptural fulfillment, not as a failure of Jesus's keeping. Jesus kept all whom the Father gave him and lost none except the one whose perishing fulfilled Scripture. The phrase "son of perdition" (ho huios tes apoleias) identifies Judas by his defining characteristic — destruction, perishing — rather than by his name. The document has consistently marked Judas as an anomaly within the group: chosen by Jesus yet called "a devil" (6:70); present at the table yet "not clean" (13:10-11); included in the washing yet entered by Satan (13:27). The prayer's language preserves this distinction: Judas perished, but his perishing was part of the scriptural pattern, not a contradiction of Jesus's protective faithfulness.

7. The prayer asks not for the disciples' removal from the world but for their protection within it (17:15). The community's mission requires its presence in the world. The sanctification requested in 17:17 is not withdrawal but consecration for service — being set apart in truth for the purpose of being sent into the world (17:18). The parallel between the Father's sending of the Son and the Son's sending of the disciples (17:18) makes the point explicit: as the Son entered the world on mission from the Father, so the disciples remain in the world on mission from the Son. Protection from the evil one (17:15) enables mission; removal from the world would end it.

8. Jesus's self-sanctification (17:19) is the consecration of himself as an offering on behalf of the disciples. The language of hagiaso ("to sanctify, to set apart") carries sacrificial connotations: the one who sets himself apart is the one who dedicates himself to the altar. Jesus consecrates himself for the death that will serve the disciples' own consecration. The purpose clause — "that they themselves also may be sanctified in truth" — ties the community's sanctification to the sacrifice of its Lord. The disciples are set apart because Jesus has been set apart as an offering on their behalf.

9. The unity for which Jesus prays (17:11, 21-23) is modeled on the Father-Son relationship, not on organizational uniformity. The standard is "even as we are one" (kathos hemeis, 17:11) and "even as you, Father, are in me and I in you" (17:21). The unity is relational, characterized by mutual indwelling, shared purpose, and the same love that flows between Father and Son. The unity is not an end in itself but has a missional purpose: "so that the world may believe that you sent me" (17:21) and "so that the world may know that you sent me" (17:23). The community's visible oneness is the evidence that persuades the world of Jesus's divine origin. The world cannot see the Father or the Son directly, but it can see the community; when the community's unity reflects the divine unity, the world encounters in the community the reality of the Father-Son relationship.

10. The prayer addresses the Father's love as the ultimate ground of everything. The Father loved the Son "before the foundation of the world" (17:24). The Father loved the disciples "even as you loved me" (17:23) — with the same love. The prayer's final purpose is that "the love with which you loved me may be in them" (17:26). The Father's pre-creational love for the Son is not confined to the Father-Son relationship but is transmitted through the Son to the community. The community lives within the Father's love for the Son. The love is not a distant sentiment but an indwelling reality — it is "in them," carried there by the presence of the Son who is also "in them" (17:26). The prayer ends with this double indwelling: the Father's love in the community, and the Son in the community. The farewell discourse, which began with "having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end" (13:1), closes with the prayer that the Father's own love for the Son may take up residence within those the Son has loved.

11. The prayer's pronouns maintain careful distinctions throughout. "I" refers to Jesus, the Son who has manifested the Father's name, accomplished the work, and is now departing. "You" (singular) refers to the Father, addressed directly throughout. "Them" (autous/autois) refers first to the immediate disciples (vv. 6-19) and then expands to include future believers (vv. 20-26). "The world" (ho kosmos) is the system that has not known the Father (17:25), that hates the disciples (17:14), and that is the object of the community's mission (17:21, 23). "The son of perdition" stands alone as the exception within the group — not truly belonging, though present within it. The pronoun structure maps the relationships: Father and Son in mutual glory and love; the disciples as the Father's gift to the Son, received, kept, and sent; the world as the hostile environment that the community inhabits but does not belong to and that may be reached through the community's visible unity.

12. The prayer occupies a precise structural position as the bridge between the farewell discourse and the Passion narrative. The discourse (chapters 14-16) told the disciples what was coming and how to live through it. The prayer (chapter 17) entrusts the disciples to the Father's keeping for the events that follow. The Passion narrative (chapters 18-19) will enact what the discourse described and the prayer requested. The author explicitly links the prayer to the Passion in 18:9, where Jesus's act of protecting the disciples at the arrest ("let these go their way") is said to fulfill "the word which he spoke, 'Of those whom you have given me I lost not one'" — a direct reference to the prayer's claim in 17:12. The prayer is not a private devotional moment but the interpretive framework for the cross: the events of chapters 18-19 are to be read through the lens of the petitions in chapter 17. The crucifixion is the glorification the prayer requested. The disciples' survival is the keeping the prayer asked for. The community's future mission is the sending the prayer described. The prayer gives meaning to the death before the death occurs.

13. The prayer's final words ("I in them," kago en autois, 17:26) answer the crisis that opened the farewell discourse. In 13:33, Jesus announced: "Yet a little while I am with you." The disciples were troubled (14:1). The entire discourse addressed the question: what happens when Jesus is no longer with them? The prayer's answer is that Jesus will be in them — not merely with them as an external companion but within them as an indwelling presence. The crisis of absence is resolved by the promise of presence. The "with" (meth' hymon) of the earthly ministry gives way to the "in" (en autois) of the post-departure reality. The farewell discourse that began with the announcement of departure ends with the declaration of indwelling. The going away is not the final word; the final word is the being within.
