Acts 18 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 17): Paul and Silas traveled through Amphipolis and Apollonia to Thessalonica, where Paul entered the synagogue and for three Sabbaths "reasoned with them from the Scriptures, explaining and proving that it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus, whom I proclaim to you, is the Christ'" (17:2-3). The synagogue-first pattern continued: "some of them were persuaded and joined Paul and Silas, as did a great many of the devout Greeks and not a few of the leading women" (17:4). But "the Jews were jealous" (17:5) — the same reaction (zelos) as in Pisidian Antioch (13:45) — and they formed a mob, attacked Jason's house, and dragged Jason before the city authorities with the charge: "These men who have turned the world upside down have come here also, and Jason has received them, and they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus" (17:6-7). The political accusation escalated beyond the Philippian charge of "customs not lawful for Romans" (16:21) to the far more dangerous claim of sedition against Caesar. Paul and Silas were sent by night to Berea, where the synagogue members were "more noble" because "they received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (17:11). Many believed, but Thessalonian Jews pursued Paul there and stirred up the crowds (17:13). Paul was sent on alone to Athens while Silas and Timothy remained behind. In Athens, Paul addressed the Areopagus, beginning not from Israel's scriptures but from the Athenians' own religious practice — an altar inscribed "To the unknown God" (17:23) — and working toward the resurrection. At the mention of resurrection, the audience divided: "some mocked, but others said, 'We will hear you again about this'" (17:32). A few believed — Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris among them (17:34) — but the author records no community founded, no church established, no household baptized. The intellectual capital of the Greek world yielded the smallest response of any city in the narrative. Paul departs Athens alone, without companions, without a community behind him, and with the pattern of his Greek mission showing diminishing returns: driven from Thessalonica, pursued to Berea, politely dismissed in Athens.

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 19): Paul returns to Ephesus — the city the Spirit had blocked him from entering in 16:6 — and finds about twelve disciples who knew only the baptism of John. When Paul asks, "Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?" they reply, "No, we have not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit" (19:2). Paul baptizes them in the name of the Lord Jesus, lays hands on them, and the Holy Spirit comes upon them with tongues and prophecy (19:6). This episode directly continues the Apollos situation from 18:24-28 — both Apollos and these Ephesian disciples knew only John's baptism. Paul then enters the synagogue and speaks boldly for three months, but "some became stubborn and continued in unbelief, speaking evil of the Way before the congregation" (19:9). Paul withdraws and moves to the hall of Tyrannus, where he teaches daily for two years, "so that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord, both Jews and Greeks" (19:10). The Ephesian mission becomes the longest and most geographically influential of Paul's career. The chapter culminates with the riot of Demetrius the silversmith, whose livelihood making silver shrines of Artemis is threatened by Paul's preaching that "gods made with hands are not gods" (19:26). The collision between the gospel and the economic structures of pagan religion, glimpsed in the python-spirit episode in Philippi (16:16-19), reaches full expression in Ephesus. The city clerk eventually quiets the mob, and Paul departs for Macedonia.

How chapter 18 connects these: Acts 17 traced a declining trajectory. Paul was driven from Thessalonica, pursued out of Berea, and left Athens with only a handful of converts and no recorded community. He arrives in Corinth alone and, by every external measure, at a low point. Acts 19 will show the Ephesian mission at full power — the Spirit's block lifted, the longest sustained teaching period, the gospel penetrating an entire province. Acts 18 is the turning point between the diminishing returns of chapter 17 and the expansive success of chapter 19. In Corinth, Paul finds co-workers (Aquila and Priscilla, displaced by Claudius's edict), is rejoined by Silas and Timothy, makes the familiar turn from synagogue to Gentiles, and receives a divine vision promising protection and declaring "I have many people in this city." He stays eighteen months — the longest urban residency recorded so far — and a substantial community forms. The Gallio incident resolves the political question that has been building since Philippi: is the Christian message a violation of Roman law? Gallio rules it is not. This legal precedent clears the path for the Ephesian mission and beyond. Paul then deposits Priscilla and Aquila in Ephesus, preparing the ground for his return. The Apollos episode bridges the two chapters directly: Apollos, who knows only John's baptism, is privately corrected by Priscilla and Aquila in Ephesus and sent to strengthen the Corinthian community; in Acts 19, Paul will find more disciples in the same incomplete condition and bring them fully into the community of the Spirit. The chapter moves the narrative from isolation and diminishment to partnership and abundance, from political vulnerability to legal vindication, and from a mission dependent solely on Paul to one served by multiple teachers.

Verses 1-4: Arrival in Corinth, Aquila and Priscilla, Tentmaking

"After this Paul left Athens and went to Corinth" (18:1).

The transition is bare: "after this" (meta tauta). Paul departs Athens without fanfare. The author records no commissioning of a community, no farewell, no encouragement of brothers — none of the markers that accompany Paul's departure from cities where a community has been planted (cf. 14:23, 15:40-41, 16:40, 17:10, 17:14). Athens produced individual believers but no ekklesia. Paul moves on to Corinth alone.

Corinth was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia, a city of enormous commercial significance situated on the narrow isthmus connecting the Peloponnese to mainland Greece. It controlled two ports: Lechaeum on the Corinthian Gulf, facing west toward Italy, and Cenchreae on the Saronic Gulf, facing east toward Asia Minor. Goods and even small ships were dragged across the isthmus to avoid the dangerous voyage around the southern Peloponnese. This geographic advantage made Corinth one of the wealthiest cities in the Roman world. Destroyed by the Roman general Lucius Mummius in 146 BCE and left desolate for a century, it was refounded as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar in 44 BCE and populated largely by Roman freedmen — former slaves who had been manumitted and resettled. By Paul's time, the population was a cosmopolitan mixture of Romans, Greeks, Jews, and traders from across the Mediterranean. The city's reputation for moral license was proverbial in the ancient world — the verb korinthiazomai ("to act like a Corinthian") meant to practice sexual immorality, and the term "Corinthian girl" was a euphemism for a prostitute. Whether this reputation was entirely deserved or partly a literary slander perpetuated by Athens (Corinth's commercial rival) is debated, but the city's wealth, transience, and port culture would have made it markedly different from the more settled communities Paul had visited in Macedonia.

"And he found a Jew named Aquila, a native of Pontus, recently come from Italy with his wife Priscilla, because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to leave Rome. And he went to see them" (18:2).

The verb heurein ("he found") suggests Paul was seeking out members of the Jewish community — a natural first step for a man who consistently begins his mission in the synagogue. Aquila is identified as "a Jew" (Ioudaion) and "a native of Pontus" (Pontikon to genei) — from the region along the southern coast of the Black Sea in Asia Minor. His wife is named Priscilla (Priskilla), a diminutive of the Latin name Prisca. Paul refers to this couple in his letters as Prisca and Aquila (Romans 16:3, 1 Corinthians 16:19, 2 Timothy 4:19), using the formal rather than the diminutive form of the wife's name.

The reason for their presence in Corinth is political: "Claudius had commanded all the Jews to leave Rome" (dia to diatetachenai Klaudion chorizesthai pantas tous Ioudaious apo tes Romes). The Roman historian Suetonius records this event (Claudius 25.4): "Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome." The name "Chrestus" is widely understood as a garbled version of "Christus" — the disturbances were likely caused by disputes within Rome's synagogues over whether Jesus was the Messiah. If this is the case, Aquila and Priscilla may already have been believers before meeting Paul — displaced from Rome precisely because they were part of the Jewish-Christian community whose messianic debates provoked Claudius's expulsion.

The author does not record Paul converting Aquila and Priscilla. Throughout Acts, when someone is converted, the author narrates the event: Lydia's heart is opened (16:14), the jailer asks what he must do to be saved (16:30), Crispus believes (18:8). The absence of a conversion narrative for Aquila and Priscilla, combined with the circumstances of their expulsion from Rome, strongly suggests they arrived in Corinth already believing in Jesus as Messiah. Paul did not find converts in Corinth; he found fellow believers — partners in both trade and faith.

"And because he was of the same trade he stayed with them and worked, for they were tentmakers by trade" (18:3).

The word skenopoioi ("tentmakers") identifies a specific craft. Tents were made from leather or from cilicium, a coarse goat-hair cloth associated with Cilicia — Paul's home province. Whether the term should be understood narrowly (tentmaking) or broadly (leather-working or general textile work) is uncertain, but the point is clear: Paul works with his hands to support himself. This is consistent with what Paul himself writes to the Corinthians: "we labor, working with our own hands" (1 Corinthians 4:12), and "I robbed other churches by accepting support from them in order to serve you" (2 Corinthians 11:8). During the initial period in Corinth, before Silas and Timothy arrive with financial support from Macedonia (v. 5; cf. 2 Corinthians 11:9, Philippians 4:15), Paul supports himself through manual labor.

The shared trade provides the practical basis for shared living arrangements. Paul stays "with them" (par' autois) — he moves into their home and workshop. The connection is simultaneously economic (they share a trade), domestic (they share a house), and — if they are already believers — spiritual (they share a faith). This household becomes the nucleus around which Paul's Corinthian ministry will develop.

"And he reasoned in the synagogue every Sabbath, and tried to persuade Jews and Greeks" (18:4).

The verb dielegeto (from dialegomai, "to reason, to discuss, to argue dialectically") describes Paul's method: it is not simple proclamation but argumentative engagement, discussion, and persuasion from the Scriptures. The same verb was used for Paul's activity in the synagogues of Thessalonica (17:2) and in the marketplace and synagogue of Athens (17:17). Paul's Sabbath-by-Sabbath pattern continues: he enters the synagogue, the established institutional space of Jewish worship and scriptural interpretation, and engages in reasoned argument.

The audience includes "Jews and Greeks" (Ioudaious te kai Hellenas). The "Greeks" (Hellenas) in a synagogue context are God-fearers — Gentiles who worship Israel's God and attend synagogue without having undergone full conversion to Judaism (circumcision, immersion, and acceptance of the full Law). This is the same mixed audience Paul has addressed since Pisidian Antioch (13:16, 26: "Men of Israel and you who fear God"), and the one the Jerusalem Council's decree was designed to accommodate (15:19-20). The verb epithen (from peitho, "to persuade, to convince") is in the imperfect tense, indicating repeated, ongoing effort: Paul kept trying to persuade them, Sabbath after Sabbath.

Verses 5-8: The Arrival of Silas and Timothy, the Turn to Gentiles, Crispus

"When Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, Paul was occupied with the word, testifying to the Jews that the Christ was Jesus" (18:5).

Silas (who has been with Paul since 15:40) and Timothy (who joined at Lystra, 16:1-3) were left behind in Berea (17:14) and had been dispatched to Macedonia — Timothy to Thessalonica (1 Thessalonians 3:1-2, 6) and presumably Silas to Philippi or another Macedonian community. Their arrival "from Macedonia" brings more than companionship. Paul's own letters reveal that the Macedonian churches sent financial support: "the brothers who came from Macedonia supplied my need" (2 Corinthians 11:9); "no church entered into partnership with me in giving and receiving, except you only" — addressed to the Philippians (Philippians 4:15). The financial support freed Paul from the tentmaking shop and allowed him to devote himself entirely to the word.

The verb synecheto (from synecho, "to press together, to constrain, to hold completely, to be wholly absorbed") describes Paul's new condition: he was pressed by the word, occupied with it, driven by it. The word seized him. The dative to logo ("with/by the word") identifies the force that grips Paul — it is the message itself, not merely his sense of duty. When his companions arrive and his material needs are met, the word takes full possession of his activity.

The content of his testimony is stated with economy: "the Christ was Jesus" (einai ton Christon Iesoun — literally "that the Christ is Jesus"). The verb diamartyromenos (from diamartyromai, "to testify solemnly, to bear thorough witness") carries the weight of formal, solemn declaration. This is not casual conversation; it is authoritative witness. The claim is that the Messiah — the anointed one prophesied in Israel's scriptures, the expected deliverer of God's people — is Jesus of Nazareth, the crucified and risen one. This is the same message that has been proclaimed since Peter's Pentecost speech (2:36: "God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified") and Paul's synagogue sermons (9:22, 13:33, 17:3).

"And when they opposed and reviled him, he shook out his garments and said to them, 'Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent. From now on I will go to the Gentiles'" (18:6).

Two verbs describe the synagogue's response. First, antitassomenon (from antitasso, "to range in battle against, to set oneself against, to resist") — this is military language. The Jews did not merely disagree; they arranged themselves in hostile opposition, as troops take up positions against an enemy. Second, blasphemounton (from blasphemeo, "to speak injuriously, to revile, to slander") — the word carries overtones of speech that dishonors God. In the LXX, blasphemia is directed against God himself (Isaiah 52:5, Ezekiel 35:12). Here, the blasphemy is against the message that Jesus is the Christ — and by extension, against God's anointed. The author treats the reviling of the messianic message as an act that touches the honor of God.

Paul's response is a sequence of prophetic gestures and declarations. First, "he shook out his garments" (ektinaxamenos ta himatia). This echoes Jesus's instructions to his disciples: "wherever they do not receive you, when you leave that town shake off the dust from your feet as a testimony against them" (Luke 9:5, 10:11). Paul and Barnabas performed this gesture in Pisidian Antioch when driven from the synagogue (Acts 13:51). Nehemiah made a similar gesture against the nobles who were exploiting the poor: "I also shook out the fold of my garment and said, 'So may God shake out every man from his house and from his labor who does not keep this promise'" (Nehemiah 5:13). The gesture is a physical declaration that the one who has delivered the message is now free of responsibility for those who reject it.

Second, "Your blood be on your own heads!" (to haima hymon epi ten kephalen hymon). This formula draws directly from Ezekiel's watchman passages. In Ezekiel 33:4: "If anyone hears the sound of the trumpet but does not heed the warning, and a sword comes and takes their life, their blood will be on their own head." And Ezekiel 33:8-9: "When I say to the wicked, 'You wicked person, you will surely die,' and you do not speak out to dissuade them from their ways, that wicked person will die for their sin, and I will hold you accountable for their blood. But if you do warn the wicked person to turn from their ways and they do not do so, they will die for their sin, though you yourself will be saved." Paul casts himself in the role of Ezekiel's watchman. He has sounded the trumpet — he has proclaimed that the Christ is Jesus. Those who refuse the warning bear responsibility for their own rejection. The blood-guilt formula also echoes 2 Samuel 1:16, where David says to the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul: "Your blood be on your head, for your own mouth has testified against you." The formula transfers culpability from the one who warned to the one who was warned.

Third, "I am innocent" (katharos ego — literally "clean am I"). The adjective katharos means "clean, pure, free from contamination, guiltless." Paul has discharged his obligation as a watchman; he is clean of their blood.

Fourth, "From now on I will go to the Gentiles" (apo tou nyn eis ta ethne poreusomai). This declaration parallels 13:46, where Paul and Barnabas announced in Pisidian Antioch: "It was necessary that the word of God be spoken first to you. Since you thrust it aside and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we are turning to the Gentiles." The same pattern operates: the word goes first to the synagogue (the established community of Israel's covenant people), is rejected, and then turns to the Gentiles. This is not a permanent, universal abandonment of Jewish evangelism — Paul will enter the synagogue in Ephesus in the very next verse (18:19) and again in 19:8. The declaration applies to this city: in Corinth, Paul's synagogue mission is complete. Those who were to hear have heard. Those who rejected have rejected. The mission now moves to the wider population.

"And he left there and went to the house of a man named Titius Justus, a worshiper of God. His house was next door to the synagogue" (18:7).

Paul moves his base of operations from the synagogue to an adjacent private house. The man's name — Titius Justus (some manuscripts read "Titus Justus") — is Roman in form (a praenomen and cognomen), suggesting he may be a Roman citizen. He is described as "a worshiper of God" (sebomenou ton theon), the technical designation for a God-fearer — a Gentile who attends the synagogue and worships Israel's God without full conversion to Judaism. This is the same term used for Cornelius (10:2), Lydia (16:14), and the God-fearing Gentiles addressed throughout Paul's synagogue preaching (13:16, 26, 43, 50; 17:4, 17).

The detail that his house "was next door to the synagogue" (he oikia en synomorousa te synagoge) is precise and significant. The verb synomoreo means "to border upon, to be adjacent to, to share a boundary with." Paul has not retreated from the synagogue; he has moved next door. His new teaching space is physically adjacent to the old one. Any Jew who wished to hear Paul could still do so by walking a few steps. And any God-fearer who had been attending the synagogue could now hear Paul in a setting that did not require synagogue membership or adherence to synagogue discipline. The new location is a declaration of accessibility: the message is the same, but the institutional framework has changed. The word of God is no longer housed within the synagogue's walls but in an open household next to it.

"Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed in the Lord, together with his entire household. And many of the Corinthians hearing Paul believed and were baptized" (18:8).

The conversion of Crispus is the most dramatic single defection in the Corinthian narrative. He is identified as ho archisynagogos — "the ruler of the synagogue," the presiding official responsible for organizing worship, selecting scripture readings, maintaining order, and inviting speakers to address the assembly (cf. 13:15, where the "rulers of the synagogue" in Pisidian Antioch invited Paul to speak). The archisynagogos was the institutional head of the Jewish community in a given city. His conversion to faith in Jesus is not the defection of a marginal member but of the leader himself. The very man whose role was to oversee the synagogue's life has come to believe the message the synagogue has rejected.

"Together with his entire household" (syn holo to oiko autou) — the household pattern continues. As with Lydia (16:15) and the Philippian jailer (16:33), the head of the household's faith draws the entire household into the community of believers. Paul confirms this detail in his own letter: "I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius" (1 Corinthians 1:14). Crispus was among the very few Paul personally baptized in Corinth — a fact that underscores his significance.

"Many of the Corinthians hearing Paul believed and were baptized" (polloi ton Korinthion akouontes episteuon kai ebaptizonto). The verb forms are revealing. Akouontes ("hearing") is a present participle — the hearing is ongoing, continuous. Episteuon ("believed") is an imperfect tense — they kept believing, one after another, over a sustained period. Ebaptizonto ("were being baptized") is also imperfect — baptisms continued to take place over time. This is not a single event but a sustained process of hearing, believing, and being baptized that extended over Paul's eighteen-month residency. The community grows incrementally, person by person, household by household. The word "Corinthians" (Korinthion) is notable — these are not Jews or God-fearers from the synagogue but residents of Corinth itself, the broader Gentile population.

Verses 9-11: The Lord's Vision and Paul's Extended Stay

"And the Lord said to Paul one night in a vision, 'Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many people in this city'" (18:9-10).

The Lord (ho kyrios) speaks to Paul through a vision (di' horamatos). Throughout Acts, the Lord who speaks in visions is Jesus — the risen one who appeared to Ananias (9:10-17), who spoke to Peter through the sheet vision (10:13-15), who appeared to Paul at his conversion (9:5-6) and will appear to him again in the Jerusalem barracks (23:11). The medium — a nocturnal vision — connects this episode to the Macedonian man vision (16:9-10) that redirected the mission to Europe. As the Macedonian vision launched the Greek mission, this Corinthian vision sustains and extends it.

The Lord's speech contains four elements, each of which draws on deep scriptural precedent.

First, "Do not be afraid" (me phobou). This formula appears throughout the LXX at moments when God addresses servants who face danger or overwhelming circumstances. God spoke it to Abraham: "Fear not, Abram, I am your shield" (Genesis 15:1). To Isaac: "Fear not, for I am with you and will bless you" (Genesis 26:24). To Jacob: "I am God, the God of your father. Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt" (Genesis 46:3). To Moses through the burning bush (the context of Exodus 3, though the explicit formula appears in Deuteronomy 31:8). To Joshua: "Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged" (Joshua 1:9). To Gideon: "Peace! Do not be afraid. You are not going to die" (Judges 6:23). To Jeremiah: "Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (Jeremiah 1:8). To Israel through Isaiah: "Fear not, for I am with you; be not dismayed, for I am your God" (Isaiah 41:10). The formula is not a casual reassurance. It is the standard divine address to those commissioned for tasks that expose them to hostility. Its use here places Paul in the line of Israel's commissioned servants — Abraham, Moses, Joshua, Jeremiah, the exilic community.

The fact that the vision includes "do not be afraid" implies Paul has reason to fear. After being driven from Thessalonica and Berea, after the muted reception in Athens, after the synagogue's hostile opposition in Corinth (v. 6), Paul has grounds for apprehension. The beatings in Philippi (16:22-23), the mob violence in Thessalonica (17:5), and the persistent pursuit by hostile Jews (17:13) constitute a pattern that would make any rational person cautious about continued public speaking. The Lord's command addresses a real and justified fear.

Second, "Go on speaking and do not be silent" (lalei kai me siopseis). Two commands — one positive, one negative — that say the same thing: keep proclaiming. The present imperative lalei ("keep speaking") commands the continuation of what Paul is already doing. The aorist subjunctive with negation me siopseis ("do not be silent, do not ever fall silent") prohibits a decisive cessation. The Lord does not merely permit Paul to continue; he commands it. Silence would be disobedience.

Third, "For I am with you" (dioti ego eimi meta sou). This is the covenant formula — the foundational promise that accompanies every major commission in Israel's history. To Moses: "I will be with you" (Exodus 3:12). To Joshua: "As I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will never leave you nor forsake you" (Joshua 1:5). To Gideon: "The Lord is with you, mighty warrior" (Judges 6:12). To Jeremiah: "I am with you to deliver you" (Jeremiah 1:8). To the exiles through Isaiah: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you" (Isaiah 43:2). The formula is not a vague spiritual presence; it is the specific promise that sustained Israel's leaders through the most dangerous moments of the nation's history. The Lord's self-identification with this formula means: what I promised Moses and Joshua and Jeremiah, I am now promising you. The mission to Corinth carries the same divine backing as the exodus, the conquest, and the prophetic vocation.

Fourth, "No one will attack you to harm you, for I have many people in this city" (oudeis epithesetai soi tou kakosai se, dioti laos estin moi polys en te polei taute). The promise of safety is specific: no one (oudeis) will attack (epithesetai, from epitithemi, "to set upon, to attack") to harm (kakosai, from kakoo, "to do evil to, to harm, to mistreat"). This does not mean Paul will face no opposition — the Gallio incident (vv. 12-16) proves otherwise — but that the opposition will not succeed in injuring him. The attacks will fail.

The reason given is the most theologically significant statement in the chapter: "I have many people in this city" (laos estin moi polys). The word laos ("people") is not the generic word for humans (anthropoi) or for a crowd (ochlos). Laos is the word used throughout the LXX for Israel as God's covenant people — the am Yahweh, the people who belong to God by election and covenant. When Deuteronomy 7:6 declares "you are a people holy to the Lord your God; the Lord your God has chosen you out of all the peoples on the face of the earth to be his people, his treasured possession," the LXX renders "people" as laos. When God says through Hosea, "I will say to Not My People, 'You are my people,' and they shall say, 'You are my God'" (Hosea 2:23), the word is again laos. In Acts, Peter used this word at the Jerusalem Council: "Simeon has related how God first visited the Gentiles, to take from them a people (laon) for his name" (15:14). James confirmed this by citing Amos 9:11-12 (LXX), which spoke of "the remnant of mankind" and "all the Gentiles who are called by my name" (15:17).

The Lord's declaration that he has laos in Corinth — a pagan, Gentile, cosmopolitan city — extends the claim Peter and James articulated at the Jerusalem Council. God has a people in this city who are already his, who belong to him, who will respond when the word is spoken. They exist before Paul's preaching gathers them. They are God's prior possession — a people who need to be found, not created. Paul's continued preaching is necessary not to generate God's people from nothing but to call them together, to bring them from anonymity into community. The Lord's statement is the basis for the command to keep speaking: there are people in this city who are mine, and your speaking is how they will hear and respond. This parallels the logic of Paul's own later argument in Romans 10:14: "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching?"

"And he stayed a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them" (18:11).

Eighteen months is the longest recorded urban residency in Paul's missions up to this point. In Thessalonica, Paul stayed only three Sabbaths before being driven out (17:2). In Athens, the stay was brief. In Philippi, the duration was unspecified but apparently shorter. The eighteen months in Corinth reflects the Lord's promise — Paul can stay because no one will succeed in harming him — and the city's need. A laos polys ("many people") requires sustained teaching, not a passing visit.

The verb ekathisen ("he sat, he settled, he took up residence") suggests established permanence. Paul did not camp in Corinth; he settled there. The content of his teaching is "the word of God" (ton logon tou theou) — the same phrase used throughout Acts for the apostolic message (4:31, 6:7, 8:14, 11:1, 12:24, 13:5, 7, 44, 46). The preposition "among them" (en autois) places Paul within the community, teaching from the inside — not as an itinerant passing through but as a resident teacher embedded in the life of the community he is building.

Verses 12-17: The Gallio Incident

"But when Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews made a united attack on Paul and brought him before the tribunal" (18:12).

The author introduces this episode with a precise political marker: Gallio is proconsul (anthypatos) of Achaia. Lucius Junius Gallio Annaeus was the elder brother of the Stoic philosopher Seneca and the uncle of the poet Lucan. An inscription discovered at Delphi (the "Gallio Inscription") records a letter from the Emperor Claudius referring to Gallio as proconsul of Achaia, dating his tenure to approximately 51-52 CE. This inscription provides one of the most important chronological anchors for reconstructing Pauline chronology and confirms the author's accuracy in identifying both the person and his office.

The title anthypatos ("proconsul") is precisely correct. Achaia was a senatorial province (as opposed to an imperial province), and senatorial provinces were governed by proconsuls, not by legates or prefects. The author of Acts consistently uses the correct administrative titles for officials in different provinces — proconsuls in senatorial provinces (13:7 in Cyprus, 18:12 in Achaia), praetors/strategoi in Roman colonies (16:20 in Philippi), politarchs in Thessalonica (17:6) — a pattern that demonstrates either personal knowledge of Roman provincial administration or access to reliable sources.

"The Jews made a united attack" (katepaestesan homothumadon hoi Ioudaioi) — the verb katephistemi means "to rise up against, to attack, to set upon." The adverb homothumadon ("with one accord, unanimously") is the same word used for the early believers' unity in prayer (1:14, 2:46, 4:24, 5:12). Here it describes a different kind of unanimity: the entire Jewish community of Corinth acts together against Paul. The irony is sharp — the word that described the unity of the Spirit-filled community now describes the unity of those who oppose the Spirit's message.

"Brought him before the tribunal" (egagon auton epi to bema) — the bema was the raised platform in the agora (marketplace) from which the proconsul rendered judicial decisions. The Corinthian bema has been archaeologically identified — a large stone platform in the center of the forum, approached by steps. This is a formal legal proceeding before the highest Roman authority in the province of Achaia. The political stakes are higher here than in any previous confrontation: a proconsular ruling would set precedent for the entire province and potentially for the Empire.

"Saying, 'This man is persuading people to worship God contrary to the law'" (18:13).

The charge is crafted with deliberate ambiguity: "contrary to the law" (para ton nomon). The accusers do not specify whose law. If they mean Roman law, the charge is that Paul promotes an illicit religion — one not recognized as a religio licita under Roman legal categories. Judaism held a recognized legal status in the Empire; if the Christian movement could be classified as something other than Judaism — a new, unauthorized cult — then its promotion could be prosecuted. If they mean Jewish law, the charge is that Paul teaches a distorted form of Judaism, which might interest a Roman magistrate if it led to public disorder. The accusers likely intend the ambiguity: they want Gallio to hear "Roman law" while the substance of their complaint concerns Jewish theological boundaries.

The verb anapeithei (from anapeitho, "to persuade, to incite, to seduce") carries a pejorative connotation — not neutral persuasion but corrupting influence, seduction away from proper practice. The accusers frame Paul as a seducer of the people, one who leads them astray.

"But when Paul was about to open his mouth, Gallio said to the Jews, 'If it were a matter of wrongdoing or vicious crime, O Jews, I would have reason to accept your complaint. But since it is a matter of questions about words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves. I refuse to be a judge of these things'" (18:14-15).

Gallio does not allow Paul to speak. The proconsul renders judgment on the charge itself, before the defendant has offered any defense. This is remarkable: Paul is acquitted not because of what he argues but because of how the proconsul categorizes the charge.

Gallio identifies two categories of offense that would fall within his jurisdiction: adikema ("a wrong, an injustice, a legal tort") and radiourgema poneron ("a wicked crime, reckless villainy"). The word radiourgema derives from radiourgos ("one who acts recklessly or wickedly"), and its coupling with poneron ("evil, wicked") intensifies the description. These are the categories of Roman criminal and civil law — actual injuries, actual crimes. Gallio declares that neither category applies.

Instead, Gallio classifies the dispute as involving "questions about words and names and your own law" (zetamata peri logou kai onomaton kai nomou tou kath' hymas). The "words" (logou) likely refer to the messianic proclamation — the logos, the message about Jesus. The "names" (onomaton) likely refer to the titles at stake — "Christ," "Jesus," "Lord" — and whether they apply to the same person. "Your own law" (nomou tou kath' hymas) makes the classification explicit: this is a matter of Jewish law, not Roman law. The pronoun kath' hymas ("pertaining to you, that is yours") draws a boundary: your law, your problem, not mine.

"See to it yourselves" (opsesthe autoi) — the future middle imperative means "you yourselves look into it, deal with it yourselves." This is not mere indifference; it is a legal ruling. The Roman state, through its authorized representative in the highest provincial court, has determined that the dispute between the synagogue and the followers of Jesus is an internal Jewish matter, outside the jurisdiction of Roman criminal law. The Christian proclamation — that Jesus is the Christ — is not a crime under Roman law. This ruling has implications far beyond Corinth: it establishes a precedent that the proclaiming of Jesus as Messiah does not constitute an offense against the Roman state.

"I refuse to be a judge of these things" (krites ego touton ou boulomai einai). The word order is emphatic: "a judge — I — of these things — am not willing — to be." The pronoun ego ("I") is stressed. Gallio makes this a personal declaration of judicial will: I am unwilling to adjudicate theological disputes. The verb boulomai expresses deliberate choice, not inability.

The significance of this ruling within the narrative of Acts can hardly be overstated. In Philippi (16:20-21), the charge was "customs not lawful for Romans" — but the magistrates acted illegally, and their wrongdoing was exposed by Paul's citizenship claim. In Thessalonica (17:7), the charge was "acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus" — and the authorities imposed security bonds on Jason without a formal hearing. In both cases, the Roman legal system was misused to persecute the missionaries. In Corinth, for the first time, the system works correctly. A competent, high-ranking official hears the charge, evaluates it, and correctly determines that it does not state a Roman legal offense. The gospel is not sedition. Preaching Jesus as Christ is not a crime against Caesar. The theological content of the apostolic message is beyond the jurisdiction of the Roman state.

"And he drove them from the tribunal" (18:16).

The verb apelasen (from apelauno, "to drive away, to expel") is forceful. Gallio does not politely dismiss the accusers; he drives them away. The expulsion from the bema is a public humiliation — the accusers who brought Paul before the tribunal are themselves expelled from it.

"And they all seized Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, and beat him in front of the tribunal. But Gallio paid no attention to any of this" (18:17).

The identity of "they all" (pantes) is debated. Three possibilities exist. First, the Greek bystanders in the agora, emboldened by Gallio's dismissal of the Jewish complaint, seize the opportunity to vent anti-Jewish hostility against the most visible Jewish leader present. Second, the Jewish community itself turns on Sosthenes for failing to secure a conviction — punishing the leader whose legal strategy has been publicly humiliated. Third, some combination of the crowd acts in the confusion following Gallio's forceful dismissal.

Sosthenes is identified as "the ruler of the synagogue" (ton archisynagogon). Crispus held this title earlier (v. 8) but has since become a believer. Sosthenes is apparently his successor — the new synagogue ruler who led the prosecution before Gallio. A man named Sosthenes appears in 1 Corinthians 1:1: "Paul, called by the will of God to be an apostle of Christ Jesus, and our brother Sosthenes." If this is the same Sosthenes, then the synagogue ruler who prosecuted Paul before Gallio later became a believer and Paul's associate — following the same trajectory as Crispus before him. The synagogue in Corinth would have lost two successive leaders to the Jesus movement.

"But Gallio paid no attention to any of this" (ouden touton to Gallioni emelen). The proconsul's indifference completes the episode. Having ruled that the dispute is an internal Jewish matter, Gallio treats its consequences — including violence — with the same disinterest. He does not protect Sosthenes, just as he did not prosecute Paul. The Roman state is neutral: it neither attacks the gospel nor defends the synagogue. This neutrality, while cold toward Sosthenes personally, serves the larger narrative purpose of establishing that the Christian movement operates within a legal space the Roman state does not intend to enter.

Verses 18-23: Departure from Corinth, Ephesus Visit, Return to Antioch

"After this, Paul stayed many days longer and then took leave of the brothers and set sail for Syria, and with him Priscilla and Aquila. At Cenchreae he had cut his hair, for he was under a vow" (18:18).

The phrase "many days longer" (hemeras hikanas) indicates Paul remained in Corinth well beyond the Gallio incident. The Lord's promise — "no one will attack you to harm you" — has been fulfilled; the legal precedent has been set; the community is established. Paul eventually takes leave of "the brothers" (tois adelphois) — the word confirms a community of believers exists in Corinth, addressed with the familial term that characterizes the early movement throughout Acts (1:15, 6:3, 9:30, 11:1, 12:17, 14:2, 15:1, 16:40, 17:6, 10, 14).

Paul's destination is Syria — specifically Antioch, the church that commissioned his mission (13:1-3, 14:26-28). Priscilla and Aquila travel with him. The order of names is notable: in this verse, some manuscripts place Priscilla first (Priskilla kai Akylas), which is the order Luke uses in 18:26 as well. Paul also names Prisca before Aquila in Romans 16:3 and 2 Timothy 4:19 (though the order is reversed in 1 Corinthians 16:19). The frequency with which Priscilla's name precedes her husband's — unusual in Greco-Roman convention, where the husband's name typically comes first — may reflect her greater prominence in the early community, her higher social standing (the name "Prisca" appears in Roman aristocratic families), or simply the early community's recognition of her as the more prominent teacher.

"At Cenchreae he had cut his hair, for he was under a vow" (keiramenos en Kenchreais ten kephalen, eichen gar euchen). Cenchreae was the eastern port of Corinth, facing the Saronic Gulf and the route to Asia. The vow (euchen) is almost certainly a Nazirite vow or a modified form of it. Under Numbers 6:1-21, a Nazirite vow involved abstaining from wine and grape products, avoiding contact with the dead, and letting the hair grow uncut for the duration of the vow. At the vow's completion, the hair was shaved at the door of the tent of meeting and burned in the fire under the peace offering (Numbers 6:18). In the diaspora, where travel to the temple was not always possible, modified observances of Nazirite vows existed. Whether Paul is beginning or completing the vow by cutting his hair (the verb keiro means "to shear, to cut the hair") is debated. The more natural reading is that the cutting marks the conclusion of the vow period, with the formal completion — the offering at the temple — to follow when Paul reaches Jerusalem (cf. v. 22).

The significance of Paul's vow-keeping must not be overlooked. Paul has just declared "from now on I will go to the Gentiles" (v. 6) — a statement that might be misread as a complete break with Jewish practice. The Nazirite vow immediately corrects any such reading. Paul continues to observe Jewish devotional practices even as he proclaims Jesus as Messiah to Gentiles. His turn to the Gentiles is a missional shift, not a religious one. He has not abandoned Judaism; he has concluded that Jesus is the fulfillment of what Judaism anticipated. The vow is evidence that Paul's gospel does not require Jews to cease being Jewish — it requires everyone, Jew and Gentile, to acknowledge Jesus as the Christ.

"And they came to Ephesus, and he left them there, but he himself went into the synagogue and reasoned with the Jews" (18:19).

Paul arrives in Ephesus — the city the Spirit blocked him from entering in 16:6. The prohibition was temporary: the Spirit's "no" in 16:6 was not a permanent exclusion but a matter of timing. The time was not right then; now the door is open, though Paul's visit is brief.

"He left them there" — Priscilla and Aquila are stationed in Ephesus. This is a strategic deployment: Paul places two experienced, capable co-workers in a major city he intends to return to. Their presence will prove critical: they will instruct Apollos (vv. 26-28), they will host a house church (1 Corinthians 16:19: "Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house, send you hearty greetings"), and they will prepare the ground for Paul's extended Ephesian ministry in chapter 19.

Paul enters the synagogue and "reasoned" (dielexato, from dialegomai) with the Jews. The same method, the same setting, the same argumentative engagement — the pattern is unbroken. Paul has just declared in Corinth that he would go to the Gentiles, but in a new city, he begins again in the synagogue. The declaration of v. 6 was city-specific, not programmatic. In each new place, the word goes first to the synagogue.

"When they asked him to stay for a longer time, he declined. But on taking leave of them he said, 'I will return to you if God wills,' and he set sail from Ephesus" (18:20-21).

The Ephesian Jews' request is the opposite of the Corinthian response. Where the Corinthian synagogue opposed, resisted, and blasphemed (v. 6), the Ephesian synagogue invites Paul to stay longer. The reception is warm. But Paul declines — his journey toward Jerusalem and Antioch takes priority. His departure is accompanied by a conditional promise: "I will return to you if God wills" (tou theou thelontos). The phrase reflects Paul's experience of the Spirit's sovereign direction. In 16:6-7, the Spirit overrode Paul's intended itinerary twice. Paul has learned to hold his own plans subordinate to God's will. The promise is genuine — he intends to return — but it is explicitly conditional on divine permission. He will indeed return, and the Ephesian mission will become the longest and most influential of his career (19:1-20:1).

"When he had landed at Caesarea, he went up and greeted the church, and then went down to Antioch" (18:22).

The itinerary is compressed into a single verse. "He landed at Caesarea" — the port city on the Mediterranean coast of Judea. "He went up" (anabas) — the verb anabaino, "to go up," when used without a named destination in connection with Caesarea, conventionally means "went up to Jerusalem." Jerusalem is always "up" in Jewish geography, both because of its elevation in the Judean hill country and because of its theological status as the city of God. "Greeted the church" (aspasamenos ten ekklesian) confirms a visit to the Jerusalem community — the mother church, the community of the apostles and elders. "Then went down to Antioch" (katebe eis Antiocheian) — from Jerusalem, one always "goes down" (katabaino). Paul has returned to his sending church, the community that commissioned him by the Spirit's direction (13:1-3) and that received his report after the first journey (14:26-28). The second missionary journey is complete. The circuit — Antioch to Asia Minor to Macedonia to Greece and back to Antioch — closes.

"After spending some time there, he departed and went from one place to the next through the region of Galatia and Phrygia, strengthening all the disciples" (18:23).

The third journey begins without ceremony. Paul departs Antioch and travels through Galatia and Phrygia — the inland regions of Asia Minor where churches were established on the first and second journeys (13:14-14:23, 16:1-6). The activity is described with a single participle: "strengthening" (stepizon, from sterizo, "to make firm, to establish, to fix securely"). This is pastoral work — consolidating existing communities, not planting new ones. The same verb appeared in 14:22, where Paul and Barnabas returned through Lystra, Iconium, and Pisidian Antioch, "strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith." The pattern holds: missionary advance is followed by pastoral return. The communities Paul plants require ongoing care, and he provides it by revisiting and strengthening them.

The phrase "all the disciples" (pantas tous mathetas) indicates Paul makes a comprehensive visit — not a selective tour but a thorough circuit through every community. This strengthening of existing communities prepares the reader for the new phase about to begin: Paul's extended mission in Ephesus.

Verses 24-28: Apollos in Ephesus and Corinth

"Now a Jew named Apollos, a native of Alexandria, came to Ephesus. He was an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures" (18:24).

The author introduces Apollos with precise identifying details. He is a Jew (Ioudaios tis) — his name, a shortened form of Apollonius, was common among Hellenistic Jews. He is "a native of Alexandria" (Alexandreus to genei) — from the great Egyptian city that was the intellectual capital of the Jewish diaspora. Alexandria was home to the philosopher Philo, to the scholars who produced the LXX (the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures), and to a Jewish community renowned for its learning and its engagement with Greek philosophical and rhetorical traditions. An Alexandrian Jew educated in that tradition would possess a distinctive combination of scriptural mastery and rhetorical sophistication.

He is described as "eloquent" (aner logios). The adjective logios can mean "eloquent, learned, cultured, or skilled in speech." Given Apollos's Alexandrian background, both senses likely apply — he is both learned and articulate. He is also "competent in the Scriptures" (dynatos on en tais graphais). The word dynatos means "powerful, mighty, capable" — this is not casual familiarity but deep mastery. The combination of Alexandrian rhetorical training and scriptural competence makes Apollos a formidable teacher. His later impact on the Corinthian community will confirm this: Paul acknowledges "I planted, Apollos watered" (1 Corinthians 3:6), and some Corinthians will prefer Apollos's style to Paul's own (1 Corinthians 1:12, 3:4).

"He had been instructed in the way of the Lord. And being fervent in spirit, he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, though he knew only the baptism of John" (18:25).

Three positive descriptions are followed by one qualification. First, Apollos "had been instructed in the way of the Lord" (en katechomenos ten hodon tou kyriou). The verb katecheo means "to instruct, to teach, to catechize" — it implies systematic, deliberate instruction, not casual acquaintance. "The way of the Lord" (ten hodon tou kyriou) uses the term hodos ("the way") that designates the Jesus movement throughout Acts (9:2, 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14, 22). Apollos has received formal teaching about the movement.

Second, he is "fervent in spirit" (zeon to pneumati). The verb zeo means "to boil, to seethe, to be hot." Whether pneumati refers to the Holy Spirit or to Apollos's own spirit is ambiguous. The image is of intense, burning conviction — an enthusiasm that drives his teaching and speaking. Paul uses the same phrase in Romans 12:11: "fervent in spirit, serving the Lord."

Third, "he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus" (elalei kai edidasken akribos ta peri tou Iesou). The adverb akribos ("accurately, precisely, with exactness") indicates that what Apollos taught about Jesus was correct. His teaching was not wrong; it was not heretical; it was not confused. It was accurate as far as it went.

The qualification follows: "though he knew only the baptism of John" (epistamenos monon to baptisma Ioannou). The word monon ("only") identifies the limitation. John's baptism was a baptism of repentance in anticipation of the coming one (Luke 3:3, 16; Acts 1:5; 13:24-25; 19:4). Apollos knew that Jesus was the one John pointed to. He could teach accurately about Jesus's identity, his teaching, perhaps his death and resurrection. But his knowledge stopped at the threshold of Pentecost. He did not know about the outpouring of the Spirit, about baptism in the name of Jesus, about the new community constituted by the Spirit's presence. His knowledge was pre-Pentecost knowledge — accurate about Jesus but incomplete about what happened after Jesus's ascension.

This situation raises a historical question: how did Apollos receive instruction about Jesus in Alexandria without learning about Pentecost? The answer lies in the mechanics of early message transmission. The news about Jesus — his life, death, and resurrection — spread through the diaspora networks of the Jewish world. Travelers, merchants, pilgrims returning from Jerusalem festivals would have carried information about the prophet from Nazareth and John's testimony about him. But information about Pentecost and the Spirit's outpouring would have traveled through different channels — specifically through the apostolic community and its emissaries. Alexandria, though connected to Jerusalem by trade and pilgrimage, may not yet have received the full apostolic witness about the Spirit. Apollos knew what had reached Alexandria; Priscilla and Aquila knew what had happened in Jerusalem and beyond.

"He began to speak boldly in the synagogue, but when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (18:26).

Apollos teaches in the Ephesian synagogue with boldness — the verb parresiazesthai ("to speak boldly, to speak freely, with confidence") is the word used for Peter and John's boldness before the Sanhedrin (4:13, 29, 31), for Paul's bold preaching in Damascus (9:27-28), and throughout the apostolic mission. Apollos has the courage that accompanies genuine conviction.

Priscilla and Aquila hear him. They do not confront him publicly. They do not denounce his incomplete teaching. They "took him aside" (proselabonto auton) — the verb proslambano means "to take to oneself, to receive, to draw aside privately." The instruction is personal, private, and respectful. They "explained to him the way of God more accurately" (akribésteron auto exethento ten hodon tou theou). The comparative form akribésteron ("more accurately") acknowledges what he already has: he was accurate (akribos, v. 25); they made him more so. This is correction by completion, not by contradiction. What Apollos knew was true; what he lacked was the rest of the story.

The phrase "the way of God" (ten hodon tou theou) uses "God" rather than "the Lord" (as in v. 25, "the way of the Lord"). The variation may be stylistic, or it may indicate that the full instruction included not only more about Jesus (the Lord) but about God's broader action — the Spirit's outpouring, the inclusion of Gentiles, the Jerusalem Council's decision, the nature of the new community.

Two features of this verse deserve emphasis. First, Priscilla is named before Aquila. In a narrative where word order typically reflects relative prominence, the positioning of Priscilla's name first suggests she is the primary instructor — or at least the more prominent figure in the instructional pair. The author records a woman teaching a man, and not just any man but an eloquent, Scripture-competent Alexandrian Jew. The instruction is private rather than public, but it is real, substantive, and transformative: Apollos goes on to become one of the most effective teachers in the early movement. His effectiveness after this point rests on the instruction he received from Priscilla and Aquila.

Second, the interaction models how the early community handled incomplete knowledge. Apollos is not treated as a heretic or an outsider. He is not rebaptized (contrast the Ephesian disciples in 19:1-7, whose situation is different). He is not publicly corrected or shamed. He is privately instructed, brought to fuller understanding, and then sent forward to serve. The community corrects by adding, not by subtracting.

"And when he wished to cross to Achaia, the brothers encouraged him and wrote to the disciples to welcome him. When he arrived, he greatly helped those who through grace had believed" (18:27).

Apollos desires to go to Achaia — the province whose capital is Corinth, where Paul has just planted and left a community. "The brothers" (hoi adelphoi) in Ephesus — the community that includes Priscilla, Aquila, and the believers who have gathered around them — support his plan. They "encouraged him" (protrepsamenoi) and wrote a letter of recommendation to the Corinthian disciples. The practice of commendatory letters is attested elsewhere in the early movement (cf. Romans 16:1-2, where Paul commends Phoebe; 2 Corinthians 3:1, where Paul refers to the practice). These letters served as the early community's system of mutual accountability and verification — they ensured that unknown teachers arriving in new cities carried the endorsement of a known community.

When Apollos arrives in Achaia, "he greatly helped those who through grace had believed" (synebbaleto poly tois pepisteukosin dia tes charitos). The phrase dia tes charitos ("through grace") modifies the believers' faith: they had come to believe through God's grace. Their faith is not self-generated; it was enabled by grace. This connects to the Lord's declaration in v. 10: "I have many people in this city." The Corinthian believers' faith originated in divine initiative — God's grace preceded and enabled their believing. Apollos arrives to help a community whose existence is rooted in God's prior action.

The verb synebbaleto (from symballo, "to throw together, to contribute, to help") indicates active contribution. Apollos is not merely present; he adds substantially to the community's life. The adverb poly ("greatly, much") quantifies his contribution — it is significant.

"For he powerfully refuted the Jews in public, showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus" (18:28).

Apollos's distinctive contribution is specified. The adverb eutonos ("vigorously, vehemently, with full force") describes his manner. The verb diakatelegcheto (from diakatelegchomai, "to refute thoroughly, to confute completely") describes his method — not mere disagreement but systematic, devastating refutation. The setting is "in public" (demosiai) — in the public forum, in open debate, where everyone can hear and evaluate the arguments. What Paul began in the Corinthian synagogue, Apollos continues in the public sphere.

The content is identical to Paul's message (v. 5): "the Christ was Jesus" (einai ton Christon Iesoun) — that the Messiah prophesied in Israel's scriptures is Jesus of Nazareth. The method is scriptural demonstration: "showing by the Scriptures" (epideiknys dia ton graphon). The verb epideiknymi means "to show, to display, to point out, to prove by pointing to evidence." Apollos does not argue from philosophy or rhetoric alone; he argues from the texts, demonstrating from the graphai (the scriptures, the same word used for the written revelation throughout the LXX and the early movement) that Israel's messianic prophecies are fulfilled in Jesus.

The Alexandrian rhetorical and scriptural training that the author noted in v. 24 now bears fruit in the Corinthian context. Paul had planted the community; Apollos strengthens it with his distinctive gifts. The community that began with Paul's tentmaking and Sabbath reasoning now grows under Apollos's powerful scriptural argumentation. Paul himself will acknowledge this collaboration without rivalry: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:6). The growth does not belong to either worker; it belongs to God, whose grace enabled the believing (v. 27) and whose laos existed in Corinth before either Paul or Apollos arrived (v. 10).

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 18 divides into six movements that together narrate the most sustained and successful period of Paul's urban mission to date and establish critical precedents — legal, ecclesial, and missional — for the remainder of Acts.

The first movement (vv. 1-4) brings Paul to Corinth alone and connects him with Aquila and Priscilla, fellow believers and fellow tentmakers displaced by Claudius's edict against the Jews in Rome. Paul's dual existence — manual laborer during the week, synagogue teacher on the Sabbath — establishes the initial framework of the Corinthian mission. The community that will emerge from this mission begins in a workshop and a synagogue.

The second movement (vv. 5-8) records the arrival of Silas and Timothy from Macedonia, the intensification of Paul's preaching, the synagogue's hostile rejection, and Paul's prophetic declaration that he will turn to the Gentiles. The declaration is city-specific, not universal — Paul will enter synagogues again in Ephesus. The movement culminates in the conversion of Crispus, the synagogue ruler, together with his household, and the sustained growth of a Corinthian believing community. Paul's relocation to the house of Titius Justus, next door to the synagogue, embodies the transition: the message is the same, but the institutional frame has shifted from synagogue to household.

The third movement (vv. 9-11) presents the Lord's nocturnal vision, which addresses Paul's fear with the covenant formula "I am with you" and anchors the entire Corinthian mission in the declaration "I have many people in this city." The word laos — God's people, the covenant community — is applied to an uncollected, as-yet-unbelieving population in a pagan city. God's claim on them precedes Paul's preaching to them. The vision authorizes an eighteen-month stay — the longest urban residency in Acts to this point — and transforms Corinth from a waystation into a settled mission.

The fourth movement (vv. 12-17) narrates the Gallio incident, the political climax of the chapter. For the first time in Acts, a competent Roman official of the highest provincial rank hears the charges against the Christian movement and rules definitively that they do not constitute a Roman legal offense. The dispute is classified as an internal Jewish matter — "questions about words and names and your own law" — and dismissed. This ruling provides the legal framework that will protect the mission going forward: preaching Jesus as Messiah is not sedition, not a crime against Caesar, not a violation of Roman law. The Gallio precedent resolves the political question that has been building since Philippi (16:20-21) and Thessalonica (17:6-7).

The fifth movement (vv. 18-23) compresses Paul's departure from Corinth, his brief visit to Ephesus, his return to Jerusalem and Antioch, and the beginning of his third journey into a rapid narrative summary. Two details stand out: Paul's Nazirite vow, which demonstrates his continued observance of Jewish devotional practice even as he proclaims Jesus to Gentiles; and the strategic placement of Priscilla and Aquila in Ephesus, which prepares the ground for both the Apollos episode and Paul's later extended mission there.

The sixth movement (vv. 24-28) introduces Apollos, an Alexandrian Jew of extraordinary rhetorical and scriptural skill, whose knowledge of Jesus is accurate but incomplete — he knows only John's baptism. Priscilla and Aquila privately instruct him, bringing him to fuller understanding. He then crosses to Corinth, where he "greatly helped those who through grace had believed" by powerfully refuting the Jews from the Scriptures. The Apollos episode demonstrates that the mission is not dependent on Paul alone. Others can teach, correct, and advance the work. The community that Paul planted is strengthened by a different worker with different gifts — a pattern that Paul himself will affirm: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:6).

The chapter's deepest argument concerns the relationship between divine initiative and human mission. The Lord declares "I have many people in this city" before those people have believed. God's grace enables the believing before Apollos arrives to strengthen the believers. The Spirit blocked the Ephesian mission in chapter 16 but opens it in chapter 18. Priscilla and Aquila are displaced by an imperial edict and end up in exactly the cities where they are needed — Corinth to support Paul, Ephesus to instruct Apollos and prepare for the extended mission. The pattern is consistent with everything the author has narrated since the Spirit's direction of the mission in 13:2: God acts first, humans respond, and the convergence of divine initiative with human obedience produces communities of faith in the most unlikely places.

The Gallio ruling introduces a new dimension: the role of the Roman state. In Acts, Rome is neither an ally of the gospel nor a reliable enemy. Roman magistrates beat Paul illegally in Philippi (16:22) and then apologize when confronted (16:39). Roman-adjacent authorities impose bonds in Thessalonica (17:9). In Corinth, a proconsul dismisses the charges and drives the accusers away — but also ignores the beating of Sosthenes. The Roman state is a neutral instrument: it can be misused (Philippi) or correctly applied (Corinth), but it is not the source of either the gospel's power or its opposition. The gospel's power comes from the Lord who says "I am with you"; its opposition comes from those who reject the message that the Christ is Jesus. Rome is the stage, not the actor.

The chapter also advances the theme of the Gentile mission's relationship to Judaism. Paul keeps a Nazirite vow. He begins in the synagogue in every new city. He turns to the Gentiles only after the synagogue rejects the message. Apollos, an Alexandrian Jew, is the one who strengthens the Gentile community in Corinth. Priscilla and Aquila, displaced Jews, are the ones who complete Apollos's instruction. The Gentile mission does not arise from the abandonment of Judaism but from its fulfillment. The message that generates Gentile communities of faith is a Jewish message — that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah of Israel's scriptures — carried by Jewish teachers, argued from Jewish texts, and rooted in the God of Israel's covenant faithfulness to his promise that through Abraham's seed all nations would be blessed (Genesis 12:3, 18:18, 22:18).
