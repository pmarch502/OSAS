Colossians 3 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Colossians 2): Chapter 2 made a single sustained argument: because the Colossian believers possess everything they need in Christ, they must reject any system that claims to supplement, complete, or improve upon what Christ has accomplished. The chapter unfolded in three movements. First (2:1-7), Paul expressed his intense personal concern for the Colossians and other communities he had not met, identified the mystery of God as Christ himself in whom all treasures of wisdom and knowledge reside, warned against persuasive but false reasoning, commended the community's current good order, and commanded them to continue as they began — walking in Christ, rooted, built up, established, abounding in thanksgiving. Second (2:8-15), the chapter stated what Christ is and what God has done through him: all the fullness of deity dwells bodily in Christ (2:9), the Colossians are complete in him who is head of every rule and authority (2:10), they have received a circumcision not made with hands (2:11), they were buried and raised with Christ through faith (2:12), God made them alive and forgave all trespasses (2:13), the debt-record was cancelled and nailed to the cross (2:14), and the rulers and authorities were stripped, publicly shamed, and led in triumphal procession (2:15). Third (2:16-23), the chapter drew practical consequences: let no one judge you about food, drink, or calendar (2:16); these were shadows, Christ is the substance (2:17); let no one disqualify you through asceticism, angel-worship, and visionary claims (2:18-19); if you died with Christ to the stoicheia, stop submitting to their regulations (2:20-21); the regulations deal with perishable things, come from human teaching (2:22), and though they have an appearance of wisdom in self-made religion, cultivated humility, and severity to the body, they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh (2:23).

The final verse of chapter 2 is the immediate launching pad for chapter 3. The ascetic program of the philosophy — its food restrictions, its "do not handle, do not taste, do not touch," its bodily severity — was exposed as impotent: it cannot actually deal with the sarx. The regulations address the body's exterior but leave the flesh's interior operations untouched. The question hanging at the end of chapter 2 is: if the philosophy's program cannot subdue the flesh, what can? Chapter 3 answers that question.

What the author argues immediately after (Colossians 4): Chapter 4 continues the practical instruction that chapter 3 began. The household code that runs from 3:18 through 4:1 reaches its conclusion with the instruction to masters: kyrioi, to dikaion kai ten isoteta tois doulois parechesthe, eidotes hoti kai hymeis echete kyrion en ourano — "Masters, treat your bondservants justly and fairly, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (4:1). The master-slave instruction thus closes the household code by placing the earthly master under the heavenly Master, the same structure that governs the entire section: every human relationship is reframed by the relationship to Christ as Lord.

Following the household code, Paul turns to exhortation about prayer (4:2-4): te proseuche proskarterite, gregorountes en aute en eucharistia — "continue steadfastly in prayer, being watchful in it with thanksgiving" (4:2). The word eucharistia ("thanksgiving") has been a refrain throughout the letter (1:3, 12; 2:7; 3:15, 16, 17), and its reappearance here connects the prayer life of the community to the thanksgiving that marks those who understand their completeness in Christ. Paul asks for prayer for his own ministry — that God would open a door for the word, to speak the mystery of Christ (4:3), the same mysterion identified in 1:26-27 and 2:2 as the heart of Paul's commission. He then gives instruction on conduct toward outsiders (4:5-6): en sophia peripateite pros tous exo — "walk in wisdom toward outsiders" — where the verb peripateo ("walk") continues the ethical walk language from 2:6 and the practical instruction of chapter 3, and sophia ("wisdom") echoes the wisdom claims contested throughout the letter (1:9, 28; 2:3, 23; 3:16). The remainder of chapter 4 (4:7-18) consists of personal greetings, commendations of co-workers, and logistical instructions, including the direction to exchange letters with the Laodicean community (4:16).

How chapter 3 connects these: Chapter 2 established two things: (1) the Colossians are complete in Christ, having died and been raised with him (2:10-13), and (2) the ascetic regulations of the rival philosophy cannot actually deal with the flesh (2:23). Chapter 4 will continue the community's practical outworking of the gospel in prayer, witness, and relational life. Chapter 3 is the bridge: it takes the theological reality of co-death and co-resurrection with Christ (established in chapter 2) and derives from it the actual way of life that replaces the philosophy's failed regulations. The chapter's logic is: because you died and were raised with Christ (3:1-4, restating 2:11-13), you must put to death the earthly things that the philosophy's regulations could not subdue (3:5-11), and you must put on the virtues and practices that characterize the new humanity created in Christ (3:12-17), which then transforms every relationship in the household (3:18-4:1). The chapter answers the question that 2:23 left open: the flesh is not conquered by external regulations but by participation in Christ's death and resurrection, which produces a new self being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator.

Verses 1-4: The Foundation — Raised with Christ, Therefore Seek What Is Above

"If then you were raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God" (3:1).

The conditional particle ei ("if") with the indicative mood synegerthete ("you were raised together with," aorist passive of synegeiro) forms a first-class conditional: the condition is assumed to be true. The author is not expressing doubt; he is drawing a consequence from a reality already established. The word oun ("then, therefore") is the connective that ties this verse to everything in chapter 2: therefore, given that you died with Christ to the stoicheia (2:20), given that you were buried and raised with him through faith (2:12), given that God made you alive together with him (2:13), given that all of this is true — then act accordingly.

The verb synegerthete ("you were raised together with") is the same verb from 2:12, where the Colossians were described as synegerthete dia tes pisteos tes energeias tou theou tou egeirantos auton ek nekron — "raised together with him through faith in the working of God who raised him from the dead." The syn- prefix ("together with") binds their resurrection to Christ's resurrection. The aorist tense treats it as a completed event: this already happened. In 2:12, the location of this co-resurrection was baptism (en to baptismati); in 2:13, it was described as God making them alive from their state of death in trespasses and uncircumcision. The author does not repeat the mechanism here because it was just explained; he simply recalls the fact and draws the consequence.

The imperative follows: ta ano zeteite — "seek the things above." The verb zeteo ("to seek, to strive for, to look for, to desire") is a present imperative, indicating continuous action: keep seeking, go on seeking. The object is ta ano — "the things above," the things in the upper realm. The word ano ("above, upward") establishes a spatial orientation: the Colossians are to direct their seeking upward, toward the heavenly realm where Christ is. This is not a generic aspiration toward "spiritual things" but a specifically located seeking: the things above are defined by a relative clause — hou ho Christos estin en dexia tou theou kathemenos — "where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God."

The phrase en dexia tou theou ("at the right hand of God") draws on Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1): eipen ho kyrios to kyrio mou, kathou ek dexion mou — "The LORD said to my Lord, 'Sit at my right hand.'" This psalm was the most frequently cited Old Testament text in the early communities of Jesus-followers (cf. Mark 12:36, 14:62; Acts 2:34-35; Romans 8:34; 1 Corinthians 15:25; Ephesians 1:20; Hebrews 1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2). The right hand is the position of authority, honor, and power. In ancient Near Eastern royal imagery, to sit at the king's right hand was to share in the king's rule. The participle kathemenos ("seated," present middle participle of kathemai, "to sit, to be seated") indicates an ongoing state: Christ is presently seated there. The significance for the argument is twofold. First, Christ's heavenly session establishes where the Colossians' attention should be directed — not toward the stoicheia tou kosmou (2:8, 20), the elemental forces of the created order, but toward the one who is exalted above them all. Second, the seating at God's right hand confirms Christ's supremacy over the archai kai exousiai ("rulers and authorities") that chapter 2 declared defeated (2:10, 15). The cosmic powers are below; Christ is above at God's right hand. Seeking "the things above" is seeking the reality governed by the victorious Christ, not the reality governed by the defeated powers.

"Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on earth" (3:2).

The command is restated with a different verb: ta ano phroneite — "think on the things above" or "set your minds on the things above." The verb phroneo ("to think, to set one's mind on, to be minded, to have a disposition toward") describes a habitual orientation of thought and attitude, not a single act of concentration. In Philippians 2:5, Paul used the same verb: touto phroneite en hymin ho kai en Christo Iesou — "have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus." In Romans 8:5-6, the verb distinguishes two fundamental orientations: hoi gar kata sarka ontes ta tes sarkos phronousin, hoi de kata pneuma ta tou pneumatos — "those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit." The contrast in Romans 8 between phronein according to the flesh and phronein according to the Spirit illuminates the Colossians passage: the Colossians are to orient their thinking toward the realm where Christ rules, not toward the realm governed by the stoicheia.

The negative command — me ta epi tes ges — "not the things on the earth" — does not mean that the Colossians should ignore their daily lives, their relationships, or their responsibilities in the material world. The very next section (3:5-4:1) will give detailed instructions about exactly those things. Rather, ta epi tes ges ("the things on the earth") is defined by the letter's argument: it refers to the domain governed by the elemental forces, the domain of the old existence from which the Colossians died out (2:20, hos zontes en kosmo, "as though living in the world"). The earthly things are the stoicheia-governed regulations, the ascetic programs, the angel-worship, the visionary claims — everything that chapter 2 rejected. The contrast is not between material life and spiritual escape but between the Christ-governed reality above and the stoicheia-governed reality below.

"For you died, and your life has been hidden with Christ in God" (3:3).

The word gar ("for") introduces the ground of the preceding commands. Why should the Colossians seek and think on the things above? Because apethanete — "you died." The verb is aorist (apethanete, from apothnesko, "to die"), indicating a completed past event. This death was stated in 2:20: ei apethanete syn Christo apo ton stoicheion tou kosmou — "if you died with Christ away from the elemental spirits of the world." And in 2:11-12: they were stripped of the body of the flesh and buried with Christ in baptism. The death is real, accomplished, past. They died. The consequence is that their former existence — the existence governed by the stoicheia, characterized by the trespasses and uncircumcision of 2:13 — is over. Dead people do not submit to the regulations of the domain they left behind.

The second clause introduces a new concept: he zoe hymon kekryptai syn to Christo en to theo — "your life has been hidden with Christ in God." The verb kekryptai ("has been hidden," perfect passive of krypto, "to hide, to conceal, to store away") is in the perfect tense, indicating a completed action with continuing effect: your life was hidden and remains hidden. The word krypto and its cognates have appeared in the letter already: in 1:26, the mystery was described as to mysterion to apokekrymmenon apo ton aionon kai apo ton geneon — "the mystery hidden from the ages and generations." In 2:3, the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are apokryphoi — "hidden" in Christ. The pattern is consistent: what is hidden is not lost or inaccessible but stored, reserved, secured in a place of safekeeping. The Colossians' life is hidden with Christ in God — concealed from the world's sight, stored in the safest possible location.

The phrase syn to Christo en to theo ("with Christ in God") creates a double concealment: the life is with Christ, and Christ is in God. The life is not hidden in an uncertain location; it is with the person who is himself seated at God's right hand (3:1). The location of their true life corresponds to the location of their seeking: they are to seek ta ano because their life is already there. The hiddenness does not mean unreality; it means the present invisibility of a reality that will one day be made visible. The world cannot see the Colossians' true life because it is concealed in a realm the world cannot access. But the concealment is temporary.

"When Christ who is your life is revealed, then you also will be revealed with him in glory" (3:4).

The temporal clause hotan ho Christos phaneroothe, he zoe hymon — "when Christ is revealed, who is your life" — introduces the future disclosure. The verb phaneroo ("to make manifest, to reveal, to make visible") appeared in 1:26 for the manifestation of the mystery: nyn de ephanerathe tois hagiois autou — "but now it has been manifested to his saints." What was hidden (apokekrymmenon, 1:26) was then manifested (ephanerathe). The same pattern applies to the Colossians' life: it is presently hidden (kekryptai, 3:3) and will be manifested (phaneroothe, 3:4). The verb is in the aorist passive subjunctive with hotan ("when, whenever"), expressing a future event whose timing is indefinite but whose occurrence is certain.

The identification of Christ as he zoe hymon — "your life" — is striking. Christ is not merely the source of their life or the one who gives them life; he is their life. The life they possess is not an independent possession that Christ handed them; it is Christ himself. Their existence, in its truest and deepest sense, is Christ. This identification explains the logic: if Christ is their life, and Christ is presently hidden in God, then their life is presently hidden in God. When Christ is revealed, their life is revealed because the two are identical.

The consequence: tote kai hymeis syn auto phanerothasesthe en doxe — "then you also will be revealed with him in glory." The syn auto ("with him") continues the syn- pattern that has governed the entire letter's description of the Colossians' relationship to Christ: they were circumcised with him (peritome tou Christou, 2:11), buried with him (syntaphentes auto, 2:12), raised with him (synegerthete, 2:12), made alive with him (synezoopoiesen syn auto, 2:13), died with him (apethanete syn Christo, 2:20), and now will be revealed with him. Every stage of Christ's experience becomes theirs: his death, his burial, his resurrection, his hiddenness, and finally his revelation.

The phrase en doxe ("in glory") connects to 1:27, where the mystery was identified as Christos en hymin, he elpis tes doxes — "Christ in you, the hope of glory." The glory that was then a hope will at that point be a reality. The hope laid up for them in heaven (1:5) will be manifested.

These four verses (3:1-4) establish the theological foundation for everything that follows in the chapter. The logic is: you died with Christ (past fact, v. 3a); you were raised with Christ (past fact, v. 1a); your true life is hidden with Christ in God (present reality, v. 3b); Christ will be revealed in glory and you with him (future certainty, v. 4). Because this is so, seek and think on the things above (vv. 1b-2). The death and resurrection with Christ that chapter 2 described as the ground for rejecting the philosophy's regulations (2:20) is now presented as the ground for the positive ethical transformation that follows.

Verses 5-11: Putting to Death What Is Earthly and Putting Off the Old Self

"Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry" (3:5).

The word oun ("therefore") draws the first practical consequence from vv. 1-4. The imperative nekrosate ("put to death," aorist active imperative of nekroo, "to put to death, to mortify, to make dead") is a command addressed to people who have already died with Christ (v. 3). The paradox is deliberate: you died (apethanete, v. 3, indicative — a fact), therefore put to death (nekrosate, v. 5, imperative — a command). The indicative precedes and grounds the imperative. The death with Christ was a definitive, completed event; the putting to death of earthly things is the ongoing, active outworking of that event in daily life. The relationship between indicative and imperative is not contradictory but consequential: because you died with Christ, therefore actively put to death what belongs to the old existence. Paul makes the same move in Romans 6:11-13: logizesthe heautous einai nekrous men te hamartia, zontas de to theo — "consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God" (indicative reality), followed by mede paristanete ta mele hymon hopla adikias te hamartia — "do not present your members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness" (imperative command).

What must be put to death is described as ta mele ta epi tes ges — "the members that are on the earth" or "what is earthly in you." The word mele ("members, limbs, body parts") is used metaphorically for the activities and dispositions that belong to the old, earth-bound existence. The phrase ta epi tes ges ("on the earth") echoes v. 2's me ta epi tes ges ("not the things on the earth"). The earthly things they are not to set their minds on include, specifically, the following behaviors and dispositions.

Five items are listed. First: porneia ("sexual immorality, fornication"). The word covers a broad range of sexual conduct outside the boundaries recognized by Jewish ethical teaching. In the LXX, porneia and its cognates are used both literally for prostitution and sexual infidelity (Hosea 1:2, 4:12) and metaphorically for Israel's unfaithfulness to YHWH (Ezekiel 16:15, 22, 25; Jeremiah 3:2, 9). In the first-century Greco-Roman world, sexual norms differed significantly from Jewish ones; for Gentile converts, the prohibition of porneia represented a concrete change of life.

Second: akatharsia ("impurity, uncleanness"). In the LXX, the word is used for ritual and moral impurity. Leviticus uses it for various states of uncleanness (Leviticus 5:3, 7:20-21, 15:3). In Paul's usage, akatharsia refers to moral impurity in a broader sense — the condition of being unclean in God's sight (cf. Romans 1:24, 6:19; 2 Corinthians 12:21; Galatians 5:19; Ephesians 4:19; 5:3).

Third: pathos ("passion, inordinate affection, suffering"). In Stoic philosophy, pathos was a technical term for an irrational movement of the soul — an emotion that overpowers reason. In Paul's usage (cf. Romans 1:26, pathe atimias, "dishonorable passions"; 1 Thessalonians 4:5, me en pathei epithymias, "not in the passion of lust"), the word describes desires that have escaped the governance of the person's higher faculties. The listing here is not a general condemnation of emotion but of disordered, ungoverned desire.

Fourth: epithymia kake ("evil desire"). The word epithymia ("desire, longing, craving") is neutral in itself — it can describe both good and bad desires (cf. Philippians 1:23, ten epithymian echon eis to analysai, "having the desire to depart," where the desire is toward Christ). The adjective kake ("evil, bad") specifies: this is desire turned toward what is harmful, desire set on what God has forbidden. In the LXX, the cognate verb epithymeo appears in the tenth commandment: ouk epithymeseis ("you shall not covet," Exodus 20:17 LXX, Deuteronomy 5:21 LXX).

Fifth: pleonexia ("greed, covetousness, the desire to have more than one's share"). The word is a compound: pleon ("more") + echo ("to have"). It names the disposition that always wants more, that is never satisfied, that grasps beyond what is appropriate. Paul adds a devastating appositive: hetis estin eidololatreia — "which is idolatry." The word eidololatreia ("idol-worship") identifies greed as a form of worship. The greedy person serves the idol of More — the appetite itself has become the god. In the LXX, Israel's idolatry was consistently characterized as the pursuit of other gods (Deuteronomy 6:14, 8:19, 11:16, 28:14; Judges 2:12-13; 1 Kings 11:4-5). The identification of greed with idolatry places it in the category of the most serious sin in Israel's vocabulary: the worship of what is not God. The connection is not arbitrary. Idolatry is giving to something other than God the devotion and trust that belong to God alone. Greed does exactly this: it gives to material acquisition the devotion that belongs to God. The greedy person's functional deity is possession.

The contrast with the philosophy's approach is pointed, though implicit. The philosophy at Colossae attempted to deal with the body through external regulations: do not handle, do not taste, do not touch (2:21). Paul's approach does not regulate the body's contact with external objects; it targets the inner dispositions — the desires, the passions, the cravings that drive the person. The philosophy worked from the outside in; Paul works from the inside out. The philosophy tried to subdue the flesh by imposing bodily severity (apheidia somatos, 2:23); Paul commands the execution of the flesh's internal operations. The verb nekrosate ("put to death") is far more drastic than the philosophy's prohibitions. The philosophy said "avoid"; Paul says "kill."

"On account of these the wrath of God is coming" (3:6).

The phrase di' ha ("on account of which things") refers back to the five vices just listed. The verb erchetai ("is coming," present middle/passive of erchomai) indicates an approaching reality. The wrath of God (he orge tou theou) is not a past event but a coming one. The present tense can indicate certainty of a future event: it is on its way, as sure as if it were already here. In the LXX, the wrath of God is a consistent theme: it falls on those who violate the covenant (Deuteronomy 29:20-28), on the nations who oppose God's purposes (Isaiah 13:9, 13; Jeremiah 10:10; Ezekiel 7:8, 14:19; Zephaniah 1:15, 18), and even on Israel itself when it turns to idolatry (Numbers 25:3-4; 2 Kings 23:26; 2 Chronicles 34:21). The wrath is not an emotional outburst but a settled, judicial response to persistent rebellion against God's order. Paul used the same concept in Romans 1:18 — apokalyptetai gar orge theou ap' ouranou epi pasan asebeian kai adikian anthropon — "for the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men" — and in 1 Thessalonians 1:10, where Jesus delivers "from the coming wrath" (ek tes orges tes erchomenes).

The mention of coming wrath serves a sober function in the argument. The vices listed in v. 5 are not merely undesirable habits; they are the behaviors that provoke the judicial wrath of God. The stakes of the moral transformation Paul commands are not psychological well-being but eschatological judgment. This aligns with the letter's concern about the Colossians' final presentation before God (1:22, "to present you holy and blameless and beyond reproach") and the conditional that governs it (1:23, "if indeed you continue in the faith").

"In these you too once walked, when you were living in them" (3:7).

The verb periepatēsate ("you walked," aorist active of peripateo, "to walk, to conduct one's life") returns the letter's defining verb for the manner of life. In 2:6, the Colossians were commanded: en auto peripateite — "walk in him." Now the past tense describes their former walk: they once walked en toutois — "in these things" — the vices of v. 5. The temporal marker pote ("once, formerly, at one time") places this walk firmly in the past: this was their former existence, not their present identity. The clause hote ezete en toutois — "when you were living in them" — uses the verb zao ("to live") to describe the former condition. The imperfect ezete ("you were living") portrays a continuous past state: they used to live — their ongoing existence was characterized by these things. The phrase en toutois ("in them/these") makes the vices the environment of their former existence: they did not merely commit these acts occasionally; they lived inside them as a fish lives in water.

The verse addresses Gentile believers. The description of their former walk in porneia, akatharsia, pathos, epithymia, and pleonexia corresponds to the standard Jewish characterization of Gentile moral life, as seen in Wisdom of Solomon 14:12-27 and in Paul's own description in Romans 1:24-32. The implication is that the Colossian recipients were Gentile converts whose pre-conversion life was characterized by the standard vices of the pagan world. This is consistent with the letter's identification of them as formerly "alienated and hostile in mind, doing evil deeds" (1:21) and as having been "dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh" (2:13).

"But now you also, put them all aside: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and abusive speech from your mouth" (3:8).

The phrase nyni de ("but now") marks the contrast between the former walk (pote, "once," v. 7) and the present responsibility. The same nyni de appeared in 1:22 to mark the transition from alienation to reconciliation: "yet now he has reconciled you." The temporal shift is decisive: what was true then is no longer operative; what is true now demands a different response.

The imperative apothesthe ("put away, put off, lay aside," aorist middle imperative of apotithemi, "to put away from oneself, to lay aside, to remove") introduces a new metaphor: the removal of garments. The verb apotithemi is used in the LXX and in common Greek for taking off clothing. The image is of stripping off filthy garments and discarding them. The phrase ta panta ("all of them, the whole lot") makes the removal comprehensive: not some of the vices, but all.

A second list follows, different in character from the first. Where vv. 5's list concerned internal desires and drives (sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, greed), this list concerns relational behaviors — the ways the vices of the heart manifest in community life.

First: orge ("anger, settled hostility, lasting indignation"). The word describes a deep-seated disposition of antagonism toward others. In the LXX, orge is used for both divine anger (frequently) and human anger (Proverbs 15:1, 29:8; Ecclesiastes 7:9). While divine orge is just, human orge is repeatedly warned against: Psalm 37:8 (LXX 36:8), pausai apo orges — "cease from anger."

Second: thymos ("wrath, rage, fury, hot temper"). Where orge is settled, thymos is explosive. The word derives from thyo ("to rush, to boil, to burn") and describes the sudden surge of anger, the loss of control. In the LXX, thymos is used for the fierce, consuming anger that burns and destroys (Deuteronomy 29:24 [LXX 29:23]; Psalm 90:7 [LXX 89:7]). The two words together — orge and thymos — cover the full range of anger, from the chronic to the acute.

Third: kakia ("malice, wickedness, ill-will, depravity"). The word describes a general disposition toward doing harm — not a specific act but a settled character that inclines toward evil. In the LXX, kakia names the moral badness that God opposes (Genesis 6:5, where the LXX uses poneria, a near-synonym; Jeremiah 1:16, 4:4; Jonah 1:2).

Fourth: blasphemia ("slander, defamation, evil speaking"). Though the English derivative "blasphemy" has come to refer specifically to speech against God, the Greek word blasphemia has a broader range: any speech that injures the reputation of another, whether divine or human. In community life, blasphemia is the destruction of another person's standing through damaging words.

Fifth: aischrologia ("abusive speech, obscene talk, filthy language"). The word is a compound: aischros ("shameful, base, ugly") + logos ("word, speech"). It names speech that is degrading, coarse, or designed to shame. The phrase ek tou stomatos hymon ("from your mouth") specifies the organ from which these things proceed: the mouth. The emphasis on speech in this second list (blasphemia, aischrologia, and the lying mentioned in v. 9) reflects a deep concern with the communal effects of language. Words build or destroy community, and the new humanity in Christ requires a transformed vocabulary.

"Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices" (3:9).

The prohibition me pseudesthe eis allelous ("do not lie to one another") addresses the community's internal relationships. The verb pseudomai ("to lie, to speak falsely, to deceive") is a present middle imperative with the negative me: stop lying, do not continue lying. The preposition eis ("to, toward") with allelous ("one another") indicates that the lying is directed within the community — member to member. The prohibition of falsehood within the covenant community has deep roots: Leviticus 19:11 (LXX), ouk adeikesete, kai ou pseudesthe — "you shall not deal falsely, and you shall not lie"; Zechariah 8:16, laleite aletheian hekastos pros ton plesion autou — "speak truth each one to his neighbor." The community formed by the truth (1:5, "the word of truth, the gospel") must be characterized by truth in its internal communication.

The ground for the prohibition is stated with a participial clause: apekdysamenoi ton palaion anthropon syn tais praxesin autou — "having stripped off the old self with its practices." The verb apekdyomai ("to strip off completely, to divest oneself of") is the same verb used in 2:11 for the stripping off of the body of the flesh (the circumcision not made with hands) and in 2:15 for God's stripping of the rulers and authorities. The word's recurrence is significant: the same decisive stripping that God performed on the cosmic powers and on the flesh is now described as having been performed on the palaios anthropos — "the old self" or "the old human being." The word palaios ("old, former, worn out") describes what is past its time, what has been superseded. The word anthropos ("human being, person") identifies what was stripped off: not merely bad habits but an entire identity, a whole mode of being human. The phrase syn tais praxesin autou ("with its practices") makes clear that the old anthropos did not come alone; it came with a set of behaviors (praxeis, "practices, deeds, actions") that belonged to it. The practices listed in vv. 5 and 8 are the praxeis of the old self. They were stripped off together with the self that produced them.

The aorist tense of apekdysamenoi indicates a completed past action: the stripping off has already occurred. The tense corresponds to the past tense of apethanete ("you died," v. 3) and synegerthete ("you were raised," v. 1). In baptism, when they died and were raised with Christ, the old self was stripped off. The imperative commands of vv. 5 and 8 are therefore not instructions to do something that has not yet happened; they are commands to live consistently with what has already happened. You stripped off the old self — therefore stop lying, stop slandering, stop raging. The indicative ground of the imperative is unmistakable.

"And have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator" (3:10).

The corresponding positive action: kai endysamenoi ton neon — "and having put on the new self." The verb endyo ("to put on, to clothe oneself with") completes the clothing metaphor. The stripping off of the old garment (v. 9) was accompanied by the putting on of a new one. The word neos ("new, young, fresh, recent") describes what has recently come into existence — the new humanity that replaces the old. The parallelism is exact: apekdysamenoi ton palaion anthropon / endysamenoi ton neon — "having stripped off the old human / having put on the new." The death-and-resurrection pattern maps onto the clothing pattern: dying = stripping off the old; rising = putting on the new.

The new self is not static; it is described with a present passive participle: ton anakainoumenon — "which is being renewed." The verb anakainoo ("to make new again, to renew, to restore") is in the present tense, indicating an ongoing process. The new self, though put on as a completed act (endysamenoi, aorist), continues to undergo renewal. The new self is not a finished product at the moment of baptism; it is a self in process, continually being transformed. The passive voice indicates that the renewal is done to the self, not by the self: God is the agent of the renewal, just as God was the agent of the resurrection (2:12-13).

The renewal has a direction: eis epignosin — "unto knowledge" or "in knowledge." The word epignosis ("full knowledge, thorough recognition") has been central to the letter from the beginning: Paul prayed that the Colossians would be "filled with the knowledge (epignosis) of his will" (1:9), that they would grow "in the knowledge (epignosis) of God" (1:10), and that they would reach "the knowledge (epignosis) of God's mystery, Christ" (2:2). The renewal of the new self moves toward — and is characterized by — this same thorough knowledge of God. The contrast with the philosophy is implicit but powerful: the philosophy offered a form of knowledge (the gnosis and sophia it claimed to possess, 2:8, 23), but the genuine knowledge comes through the renewal of the new self in Christ, not through esoteric practices and visionary claims.

The standard of the renewal is described: kat' eikona tou ktisantos auton — "according to the image of the one who created it." The phrase kat' eikona ("according to the image") recalls Genesis 1:26-27 (LXX): poiesomen anthropon kat' eikona hemeteran — "let us make man according to our image." The new self is being renewed toward the original pattern of humanity — the image of God that was the design from creation. The word eikon ("image") also appeared in 1:15, where Christ was declared eikon tou theou tou aoratou — "the image of the invisible God." The convergence is significant: humanity was originally made kat' eikona theou ("according to God's image," Genesis 1:27); Christ is the eikon tou theou ("the image of God," 1:15); the new self is being renewed kat' eikona tou ktisantos ("according to the image of its creator," 3:10). The renewal moves humanity back toward the image of God, and Christ is the definitive instance of that image. The new self is being transformed into the likeness of the one who made it, and that one is known through Christ who perfectly bears God's image.

The word ktisantos ("of the one who created," aorist active participle of ktizo, "to create") identifies the renewed self as a new creation. The same verb ktizo appeared in 1:16, where ta panta en auto ektisthe — "all things were created in him." The creator of the original cosmos is the creator of the new humanity. The new self is not a human achievement; it is a divine creation.

"Where there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all" (3:11).

The relative adverb hopou ("where") introduces a description of the space in which the new self operates — the reality within the renewed humanity. In this space, ouk eni — "there is not" or "there cannot be." The phrase ouk eni is a strengthened form of ouk estin: it does not merely state an absence but an impossibility. These distinctions do not and cannot exist in the renewed humanity.

The pairs and categories that are dissolved are significant. First: Hellen kai Ioudaios — "Greek and Jew." This is the primary ethnic-religious division of the first-century Mediterranean world. "Greek" (Hellen) designates not merely ethnic Greeks but all non-Jews — the Gentile world. "Jew" (Ioudaios) designates the covenant people of Israel. The division between Jew and Gentile was the deepest fault line in the ancient world from the perspective of Jewish identity: it was the line between those inside the covenant and those outside it. In the new humanity, this line is erased.

Second: peritome kai akrobystia — "circumcision and uncircumcision." This is the same division stated in physical terms. Circumcision was the sign of the covenant (Genesis 17:10-14), the bodily marker that separated Israel from the nations. The uncircumcised were, by definition, outside the covenant community. The mention of circumcision here connects to 2:11, where the Colossians were told they had received "a circumcision not made with hands" — their peritome was not the physical cutting but the stripping off of the body of the flesh. In the new humanity, the physical marker that once defined covenant membership is no longer operative because the reality it pointed to has been fulfilled in Christ.

Third: barbaros, Skythes — "barbarian, Scythian." The word barbaros ("barbarian") was the Greek term for anyone who did not speak Greek — a person whose speech sounded like "bar-bar-bar" to Greek ears. The Scythians (Skythai) were a nomadic people from the steppes north of the Black Sea, regarded in the Greco-Roman world as the most savage and uncivilized of all peoples. Josephus (Against Apion 2.269) and other writers used "Scythian" as shorthand for the extreme of barbarism. The inclusion of these categories extends the dissolution of distinctions beyond the Jewish-Gentile axis to include the cultural hierarchies of the Greco-Roman world itself. Not only is the Jew-Gentile line erased, but the civilized-barbarian line is also erased, and even the barbarian-Scythian line (the line within the "uncivilized" world) is erased.

Fourth: doulos, eleutheros — "slave, free." This is the fundamental social and legal division of the ancient world. A doulos ("slave, bondservant") was property; an eleutheros ("free person") was a citizen with rights. The distinction governed every aspect of daily life: legal standing, economic participation, physical freedom, family structure. The dissolution of this distinction in the new humanity does not necessarily mean the immediate social abolition of slavery (the household code in 3:22-4:1 will address slaves and masters within their existing social roles), but it declares that in the identity of the new self, in the community formed by the renewed humanity, the slave-free distinction has no ultimate standing.

The positive statement replaces all these dissolved categories: alla ta panta kai en pasin Christos — "but Christ is all, and in all." The phrase ta panta ("all things, everything") indicates that Christ fills every role, occupies every space, holds every position. The phrase en pasin ("in all") indicates that Christ is present in every member of the community regardless of their former category. The old identity markers — ethnic, religious, cultural, social — have been replaced by a single identity: Christ. This does not mean that individuals cease to be ethnically Jewish or Greek, or that slaves cease to be physically enslaved. It means that these categories no longer determine identity or standing in the community of the new humanity. The determining factor is Christ, and Christ alone.

The passage parallels Galatians 3:28: ouk eni Ioudaios oude Hellen, ouk eni doulos oude eleutheros, ouk eni arsen kai thely; pantes gar hymeis heis este en Christo Iesou — "there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." The Colossians passage expands the categories (adding barbarian, Scythian) while omitting the male-female pair (which will be addressed differently in the household code). The point in both texts is the same: the unity of the new humanity in Christ transcends every division that the old world imposed.

The implications for the Colossian situation are direct. The philosophy at Colossae was enforcing distinctions: food laws that divided clean from unclean, calendar observances that marked Israel's identity against the nations, circumcision requirements that drew the covenant boundary in flesh, angel-mediated hierarchies that stratified access to the divine. Chapter 2 rejected these practices as shadows whose substance is Christ (2:17). Now 3:11 states the positive reality that those shadows obscured: in the new humanity, the very categories that the philosophy's regulations enforced have been dissolved. The food laws distinguished Jew from Gentile; in Christ, there is no Jew or Gentile. The circumcision requirement drew the covenant line; in Christ, circumcision and uncircumcision are irrelevant. The philosophy's entire program of distinction-enforcement is not merely unnecessary (the argument of chapter 2) but incompatible with the nature of the new humanity (the argument of chapter 3).

Verses 12-17: The Garments of the New Community

"Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, gentleness, and patience" (3:12).

The imperative endysasthe ("put on, clothe yourselves with," aorist middle imperative of endyo) continues the clothing metaphor from vv. 9-10. Having stripped off the old self and put on the new (vv. 9-10), the Colossians are now told what the new garment looks like in practice. The verb is the same one used in v. 10 (endysamenoi), but here it shifts from past fact (you have put on the new self) to present command (now put on these specific virtues). The relationship is consistent: the general act of putting on the new self (v. 10) is lived out through the specific putting on of particular qualities (v. 12).

Before listing the virtues, the author identifies the Colossians with three terms that establish their status. First: eklektoi tou theou — "chosen ones of God" or "God's elect." The word eklektos ("chosen, selected, elect") in the LXX is applied to Israel as a nation. Isaiah 43:20 (LXX): to genos mou to eklekton — "my chosen people." Isaiah 45:4: Iakob ho pais mou, Israel ho eklektos mou — "Jacob my servant, Israel my chosen one." Isaiah 65:9: kai exaxo to ex Iakob sperma kai to ex Iouda, kai kleronomesei to oros to hagion mou, kai kleronomesousin hoi eklektoi mou — "and I will bring forth offspring from Jacob, and from Judah, and my chosen ones shall inherit my holy mountain." The term eklektos is a corporate designation in the LXX: Israel as a people is chosen for God's purposes — chosen to be his priestly nation, his servant among the nations, his light to the Gentiles (Isaiah 42:1, 6; 49:6). The application of this term to the Colossian Gentile community is itself remarkable: the Gentile believers in Colossae are now called what Israel was called. They share in Israel's identity as God's chosen ones.

Second: hagioi — "holy ones, saints." The same word used in 1:2 to address the Colossians and in 1:12 for "the inheritance of the saints in light." The word places them in the category of the consecrated, set apart for God's purposes.

Third: egapemenoi — "beloved." The perfect passive participle of agapao ("to love") indicates that they are and have been loved by God. The same root appeared in 1:13, where the Son was called ho huios tes agapes autou — "the Son of his love." The Colossians are beloved, just as the Son is beloved. Their identity is grounded in God's choice, God's consecration, and God's love — none of which originated in themselves.

The virtues to be "put on" are five. First: splanchna oiktirmou — "compassionate hearts" or "bowels of mercy." The word splanchna ("inner organs, intestines, bowels") was used in Greek to refer to the seat of deep emotion — the gut feeling of compassion. The word oiktirmos ("compassion, pity, mercy") in the LXX translates the Hebrew rachamim, the deep, visceral compassion of God (Psalm 25:6 [LXX 24:6], Psalm 51:1 [LXX 50:3], Psalm 103:4 [LXX 102:4]). The combination describes a deep, gut-level compassion for others — not a superficial sympathy but an internal ache on behalf of another's suffering.

Second: chrestotes — "kindness, goodness, generosity." In the LXX, the word is used for God's kindness: Psalm 31:19 (LXX 30:20), hos to plethos tes chrestotetos sou — "how great is your goodness." Romans 2:4 pairs chrestotes with anoche and makrothymia as attributes of God that lead to repentance. The community is to exhibit the same quality that characterizes God.

Third: tapeinophrosyne — "humility, lowliness of mind." This word appeared in 2:18 and 2:23 as a component of the philosophy's program — the cultivated self-abasement that was part of the ascetic regimen. The reappearance of the word here is striking and deliberate. The philosophy's tapeinophrosyne was a self-imposed, performative humility that fed the fleshly mind (2:18, physioumenos hypo tou noos tes sarkos autou). The tapeinophrosyne Paul commands is genuine: it is one virtue among others in the new humanity, arising from the recognition that one has been chosen, set apart, and loved by God — not from an ascetic program designed to impress spiritual powers or achieve visionary experiences. The same word, two opposite realities. The author's own usage within the same document is the primary guide to meaning, and in this document, the word has two opposite instantiations: false humility that puffs up the fleshly mind (2:18, 23) and true humility that characterizes the new self (3:12).

Fourth: praytes — "gentleness, meekness, mildness." The word describes strength under control — not weakness but the deliberate restraint of power. In the LXX, Moses is described as prays sphodra para pantas tous anthropous — "very meek, more than all people on the face of the earth" (Numbers 12:3 LXX). Moses, the most powerful human leader in Israel's history, was the meekest. Praytes is the quality of a person who has power but does not wield it harshly.

Fifth: makrothymia — "patience, long-suffering, endurance." The word appeared in 1:11, where Paul prayed that the Colossians would be strengthened eis pasan hypomonēn kai makrothymian — "for all steadfastness and patience." The word is a compound: makros ("long") + thymos ("temper, passion"). It describes the quality of being long before the thymos erupts — the capacity to endure provocation without retaliating. In the LXX, makrothymia is an attribute of God: Exodus 34:6, kyrios ho theos oiktirmon kai eleemon, makrothymos — "the LORD God, merciful and gracious, long-suffering." The community is called to exhibit the patience that God himself exhibits.

"Bearing with one another and forgiving each other, if anyone has a complaint against someone. As the Lord forgave you, so you also must forgive" (3:13).

Two participial clauses extend the instruction. First: anechomenoi allelon — "bearing with one another." The verb anechomai ("to bear with, to endure, to put up with") acknowledges that community life involves friction. The members are different from one another, come from different backgrounds (v. 11 dissolved the categories but did not erase the personal histories), and will inevitably irritate, disappoint, and offend one another. The instruction is not that they should pretend the friction does not exist but that they should bear it — carry it, endure it, hold up under it.

Second: charizomenoi heautois — "forgiving one another" or "granting grace to one another." The verb charizomai ("to give freely, to forgive, to grant as a favor") is the same verb used in 2:13 for God's forgiveness: charisamenos hemin panta ta paraptoomata — "having forgiven us all our trespasses." The word is rooted in charis ("grace"): to forgive is to extend grace. The condition for this forgiveness is stated: ean tis pros tina eche momphēn — "if anyone has a complaint against someone." The word momphe ("complaint, blame, cause for fault-finding") acknowledges that the grievances are real, not imagined. The instruction is not to deny that wrong has been done but to forgive the person who did it.

The standard for forgiveness is then stated: kathos kai ho kyrios echarisato hymin, houtos kai hymeis — "as the Lord forgave you, so also you." The word kathos ("just as, in the same way as") establishes a proportion: the community's forgiveness of one another is to correspond to the Lord's forgiveness of them. The Lord's forgiveness was described in 2:13-14: he forgave all the trespasses and cancelled the record of debt. The forgiveness was comprehensive (panta ta paraptoomata, "all the trespasses"), unmerited (charisamenos, "granted as grace"), and costly (nailing the debt-record to the cross). The community is called to forgive in the same manner — not partially, not grudgingly, not conditionally, but as the Lord forgave them. The logic is identical to Jesus's instruction in the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:23-35): the one who has been forgiven an unpayable debt must forgive the smaller debts of others. The experience of being forgiven is both the motivation and the model for forgiving.

"And above all these put on love, which is the bond of perfection" (3:14).

The phrase epi pasin de toutois ("and above all these" or "and over all these") indicates that what follows is the supreme garment, the one that goes over and holds together all the others. The imperative is again endysasthe ("put on"): ten agapen — "love." The word agape ("love") names the self-giving, other-directed commitment that is the hallmark of God's own character (1 John 4:8, ho theos agape estin). In the context of Colossians, agape has been present from the beginning: the Colossians' "love for all the saints" (1:4), Epaphras's report of "your love in the Spirit" (1:8), Paul's desire that their hearts be "knit together in love" (2:2).

Love is described as ho estin syndesmos tes teleiotetos — "which is the bond of perfection" or "which is the bond that produces completeness." The word syndesmos ("bond, ligament, that which binds together") appeared in 2:19, where the body of Christ was described as being supplied and knit together dia ton haphon kai syndesmoon — "through the joints and ligaments." The syndesmos is what holds the body together, what keeps its parts in connection with each other and with the head. Love is this bond for the community: it is what holds the members together in unity. The genitive tes teleiotetos ("of perfection, of completeness, of maturity") identifies the result: the bond of love produces teleiotēs — completeness, maturity, the finished state. The word teleiotēs is cognate with teleios ("complete, mature, perfect"), which appeared in 1:28: hina parastesomen panta anthropon teleion en Christo — "that we may present every person complete in Christ." The completeness that Paul labors for (1:28), the completeness that the Colossians already possess in Christ (2:10, pepleromenoi), is held together and brought to fruition by love. Without love, the other virtues remain disconnected elements; with love, they form a coherent whole — a teleiotēs, a perfection.

"And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, to which indeed you were called in one body. And be thankful" (3:15).

The imperative brabeueto ("let it rule, let it arbitrate, let it be umpire," present active imperative of brabeuo, "to act as umpire, to decide, to award the prize") returns the athletic imagery from 2:18, where katabrabeuo ("to disqualify, to rule against") described the self-appointed judge who tried to rob the Colossians of their prize. There, a false umpire was judging against them; here, the true umpire is identified: he eirene tou Christou — "the peace of Christ." The word brabeuo means "to direct, to rule, to act as referee." The peace of Christ is to function as the deciding authority in the community's internal deliberations and relationships. When disputes arise, when decisions must be made, when tensions emerge between members, the peace of Christ is the arbiter.

The word eirene ("peace") in the LXX translates the Hebrew shalom, which denotes not merely the absence of conflict but the presence of comprehensive well-being — wholeness, harmony, right relationship. In 1:20, God "made peace" (eirenopoiesas) through the blood of the cross. The peace that now rules in the community is the peace that was established at the cross — the reconciliation of all things to God. This peace is not a feeling but a reality: the hostility has been ended, the debt has been cancelled, the powers have been defeated, and the resulting shalom is to govern the community's life.

The phrase en tais kardiais hymon ("in your hearts") locates the operation of this peace: in the kardia, the seat of will, understanding, and decision-making. The phrase eis hen kai eklethete en heni somati ("to which indeed you were called in one body") identifies the peace as the purpose of their calling and the calling as corporate: they were called into one body (hen soma), the body of Christ (1:18, 24; 2:19). The community is not a collection of individuals pursuing peace separately; it is a single body called together into one peace. The "one body" language reinforces the unity theme of v. 11 (where all distinctions were dissolved) and points to the organic connection between the members (2:19, where the body grows from the Head through joints and ligaments).

The final imperative: kai eucharistoi ginesthe — "and be thankful" or "and become thankful." The word eucharistos ("thankful, grateful") is the adjective from which eucharistia ("thanksgiving") derives. Thanksgiving has been a persistent refrain in the letter. In 2:7, the community that grasps its completeness in Christ abounds en eucharistia. In 1:12, Paul urged thanksgiving to the Father who qualified them for the inheritance. The instruction to "be thankful" is not an afterthought but an integral marker of the community's identity: those who know they are chosen, holy, and beloved (v. 12), who have received everything in Christ (2:10), are people characterized by gratitude rather than anxiety. Thanksgiving is the antithesis of the grasping, fearful spirit that the philosophy promoted — the spirit that said "you must do more, observe more, submit to more." Those who are complete have nothing left to grasp; they give thanks.

"Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God" (3:16).

The imperative enoikeito ("let it dwell," present active imperative of enoikeo, "to dwell in, to inhabit, to make one's home in") commands that ho logos tou Christou — "the word of Christ" — take up residence within the community. The verb enoikeo is a compound of en ("in") + oikeo ("to dwell, to live"), and it describes permanent habitation, not a passing visit. The word katoikeo (the intensified form, "to settle permanently") was used in 1:19 and 2:9 for the fullness of deity dwelling in Christ. Now the community is told to let the word of Christ dwell in them. The parallel is suggestive: as the fullness of God dwells in Christ, so the word of Christ is to dwell in the community. The community becomes the residence of Christ's word.

The phrase ho logos tou Christou ("the word of Christ") can mean "the word that Christ spoke" (subjective genitive), "the word about Christ" (objective genitive), or "the word that belongs to Christ" (possessive genitive). In context, all three converge: the message of the gospel, which is about Christ, which originates from Christ, and which belongs to Christ. This is the same word of truth (ho logos tes aletheias) that came to them at the beginning (1:5-6) through Epaphras (1:7). The word is to dwell en hymin — "in you" or "among you" — the pronoun can be understood individually (within each person) or corporately (among the gathered community). The adverb plousios ("richly, abundantly, lavishly") modifies the dwelling: the word is to be present in abundance, not as a sparse visitor but as a wealthy resident.

Two participial phrases describe how the rich indwelling of Christ's word expresses itself. First: didaskontes kai nouthetountes heautous en pase sophia — "teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom." The two verbs didasko ("to teach") and noutheteo ("to admonish, to warn, to counsel") appeared together in 1:28, where Paul described his own ministry: nouthetountes panta anthropon kai didaskontes panta anthropon en pase sophia — "admonishing every person and teaching every person in all wisdom." The same activity that characterized Paul's apostolic ministry is now attributed to the community as a whole: the members teach and admonish one another. The community is not passive recipients of instruction from an outside authority; they are active participants in mutual edification. The phrase en pase sophia ("in all wisdom") connects to the wisdom theme: the genuine sophia that Paul prayed for (1:9), the wisdom hidden in Christ (2:3), and the wisdom that the philosophy claimed but lacked (2:23) is the wisdom in which the community teaches and admonishes. It is available to them because the word of Christ dwells richly among them.

Second: psalmois, hymnois, odais pneumatikais en te chariti adontes en tais kardiais hymon to theo — "singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with grace in your hearts to God." Three terms describe the community's singing. Psalmos ("psalm") in the LXX translates the Hebrew mizmor and designates the compositions of the biblical Psalter and compositions modeled on them. Hymnos ("hymn") designates songs of praise directed to God. Ode ("song") is a broader term for any sung composition; the adjective pneumatikos ("spiritual") specifies that these songs are Spirit-generated or Spirit-directed. Whether these three terms designate three distinct categories or three overlapping descriptions of the same activity, the point is that the community's life together includes singing — and the singing is directed to God (to theo). The phrase en te chariti ("with/in grace" or "with thankfulness") indicates that the singing arises from grace and expresses gratitude. The phrase en tais kardiais hymon ("in your hearts") locates the singing in the center of the person: it is not merely vocal performance but the expression of an inward reality.

The verse as a whole describes a community whose life is shaped by the word of Christ: they teach one another, correct one another, and worship together in wisdom and gratitude. This is the positive alternative to the philosophy's program. Where the philosophy offered regulations, visions, and ascetic discipline, the gospel produces a community rich in Christ's word, active in mutual instruction, and vocal in grateful praise.

"And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him" (3:17).

The verse functions as a comprehensive summary statement. The phrase pan ho ti ean poiete — "whatever you do" — is all-inclusive: every action, without exception. The scope is specified: en logo e en ergo — "in word or in deed" — covering both speech and action, the two modes of human activity. The command: panta en onomati kyriou Iesou — "do all things in the name of the Lord Jesus." The phrase en onomati ("in the name") carries the meaning "under the authority of, as a representative of, in connection with the person of." In first-century usage, doing something "in the name of" a person meant acting as their authorized agent, performing the action as though they were performing it. The Colossians' entire lives — every word, every deed — are to be conducted under the authority of and in connection with the Lord Jesus. Nothing falls outside this scope: not work, not family, not speech, not worship. The comprehensive claim corresponds to the comprehensive reality: if Christ is all and in all (v. 11), then all things are to be done in his name.

The participial phrase eucharistountes to theo patri di' autou — "giving thanks to God the Father through him" — adds the thanksgiving refrain one more time. The thanks are directed to God the Father and offered through Christ (di' autou). Christ is the mediator of thanksgiving as he is the mediator of everything: the fullness dwells in him (1:19, 2:9), the community is complete in him (2:10), and all thanks pass through him to the Father.

Verses 18-25 (through 4:1): The Household — Relationships Reordered by Christ

The section from 3:18 through 4:1 constitutes what is conventionally described as a "household code" (the German term Haustafel is standard in scholarship). Similar instructions appear in Ephesians 5:22-6:9 and 1 Peter 2:18-3:7. The structure addresses paired relationships within the first-century household: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. Each pair receives reciprocal instruction: the one in the subordinate social position is addressed first, followed by the one in the authoritative position. The pattern is not unique to the early Jesus-communities: similar household codes appear in Greco-Roman philosophical literature (Aristotle, Politics 1.1253b; Seneca, Epistulae Morales 94; pseudo-Phocylides; and others) and in Hellenistic Jewish literature (Philo, De Decalogo 165-167; Josephus, Against Apion 2.199-210). The form is adapted from the surrounding culture, but the content is transformed by the repeated reference to the Lord.

What makes this household code distinctively different from its cultural parallels is the phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") and its variations, which appear in every instruction. The household relationships are not governed by custom alone or by philosophical principles alone but by the reality of the community's relationship to Christ as Lord. The entire section is an outworking of v. 17's comprehensive principle: "whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus."

"Wives, submit to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord" (3:18).

The imperative hypotassesthe ("submit yourselves, be subject, place yourselves under," present middle imperative of hypotasso, "to place under, to arrange under, to subordinate") addresses hai gynaikes ("the wives"). The verb hypotasso is a military term in origin: to arrange troops under a commander. In the middle voice, it describes a voluntary placing of oneself under the order of another. The instruction to wives to submit to their husbands reflects the social structure of the first-century household, where the paterfamilias (or its Greek equivalent, the kyrios of the household) held legal authority over the household members.

The qualifying phrase hos aneken en kyrio — "as is fitting in the Lord" — transforms the instruction. The verb aneko ("to come up to, to be proper, to be fitting") describes what is appropriate. But the standard of fitness is not social convention or philosophical principle; it is en kyrio — "in the Lord." The phrase limits and defines the submission: it is fitting in the Lord, which means it operates within the boundaries of what the Lord approves. Any submission that would require violation of what the Lord commands would not be "fitting in the Lord." The Lord, not the husband, sets the ultimate standard.

"Husbands, love your wives, and do not be harsh with them" (3:19).

The reciprocal instruction to husbands is not submission but love: agapate tas gynaikas — "love your wives." The verb agapao ("to love") commands the self-giving commitment that characterizes God's own love (as described in 1:13, the transfer into the kingdom of his beloved Son). The negative command — kai me pikrainesthe pros autas — "and do not be harsh toward them" or "do not be embittered against them" — uses the verb pikraino ("to make bitter, to be harsh, to embitter"). The word pikros ("bitter") in the LXX describes the bitter waters of Marah (Exodus 15:23) and the bitterness of affliction (Ruth 1:20). The instruction prohibits the husband from treating his wife with bitterness, resentment, or cruelty. In a culture where the husband held legal power over the wife, the instruction to love and not to be harsh places a moral constraint on the exercise of that power. Power is to be exercised in love, not in bitterness.

"Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord" (3:20).

The verb hypakouete ("obey, listen to, heed," present active imperative of hypakouo, "to listen, to attend, to obey") is stronger than hypotasso: it describes not voluntary arrangement but active compliance. The scope is en pasin — "in everything." The reason is stated: touto gar euareston estin en kyrio — "for this is pleasing in the Lord." The word euarestos ("well-pleasing, acceptable") describes what is agreeable to the Lord. The verb pleases is not euarestos to the parents (though it may be) but euarestos en kyrio: the Lord is the standard. Children's obedience to parents is framed as a response to the Lord, not merely as a social duty. The commandment to honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12, Deuteronomy 5:16) is the background: the fifth commandment is one of the oldest and most deeply rooted obligations in Israel's ethical tradition.

"Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged" (3:21).

The reciprocal instruction to fathers (hoi pateres, "the fathers") is a negative command: me erethizete ta tekna hymon — "do not provoke your children" or "do not irritate your children." The verb erethizo ("to stir up, to provoke, to irritate") describes actions that arouse resentment, frustration, or anger in the child. The consequence is stated: hina me athymoosin — "lest they become discouraged." The verb athymeo ("to be disheartened, to become discouraged, to lose spirit") describes the inner collapse that occurs when a child is constantly provoked, criticized, or treated unfairly. The father's authority, like the husband's, is real but limited: it must be exercised in a way that does not crush the spirit of the child. The instruction implicitly recognizes that power, even legitimate power, can be used destructively, and places a moral restraint on its exercise.

"Bondservants, obey in everything those who are your earthly masters, not by way of eye-service, as people-pleasers, but with sincerity of heart, fearing the Lord" (3:22).

The instruction to hoi douloi ("the slaves, the bondservants") is the longest section of the household code, receiving three full verses (3:22-25) compared to one verse each for the other groups. The verb is again hypakouete ("obey") with en pasin ("in everything") and the object is tois kata sarka kyriois — "those who are masters according to the flesh" or "your earthly masters." The phrase kata sarka ("according to the flesh") is loaded in the context of Colossians. Throughout the letter, sarx has been associated with the old existence: the body of the flesh (2:11), the uncircumcision of the flesh (2:13), the fleshly mind (2:18, ho nous tes sarkos), the indulgence of the flesh (2:23). The phrase kata sarka kyriois marks the master's authority as belonging to the sphere of the flesh — the earthly, material, temporary order. The earthly master is a kyrios kata sarka; but the true kyrios is Christ (v. 24, to kyrio Christo douleuete — "you serve the Lord Christ"). The slave has two masters: one according to the flesh (temporary, earthly), and one who is the Lord (permanent, heavenly). The slave's obedience to the earthly master is genuine, but it is located within the larger framework of service to the true Lord.

The manner of the obedience is specified with three contrasts. First, negatively: me en ophthalmodouleiais — "not in eye-service." The word ophthalmodouleia ("eye-service, service performed only when being watched") is a compound: ophthalmos ("eye") + douleia ("service, slavery"). It describes the slave who works when the master is watching and stops when the master looks away. The obedience Paul commands is not performance for an audience; it is genuine.

Second, negatively: hos anthropareskoi — "as people-pleasers." The word anthropareskos ("man-pleaser, one who seeks to please people") is a compound: anthropos ("person") + aresko ("to please"). It names the motivation of serving to gain human approval rather than divine approval.

Third, positively: en haploteti kardias — "in sincerity of heart" or "in singleness of heart." The word haplotes ("simplicity, sincerity, singleness, generosity") describes a heart that is undivided — not split between performance and reality, not serving one audience outwardly while serving another inwardly. The qualifying participle phoboumenoi ton kyrion ("fearing the Lord") identifies the ultimate motivation: not the earthly master's eye but the Lord's authority governs the slave's service. The fear (phobos) is not terror but the reverence and awe appropriate to one who serves the true Lord.

"Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men" (3:23).

The phrase ho ean poiete ("whatever you do") echoes v. 17's pan ho ti ean poiete ("whatever you do"). The comprehensive scope is restated within the slave's specific context. The verb ergazesthe ("work," present middle imperative of ergazomai, "to work, to labor") is modified by ek psyches ("from the soul, heartily, wholeheartedly"). The word psyche ("soul, life, inner self") indicates that the work is to come from the whole person, not from the body alone while the soul is elsewhere. The phrase hos to kyrio kai ouk anthropois — "as for the Lord and not for men" — reframes the slave's labor: the ultimate recipient of the work is not the earthly master but the Lord. This does not eliminate the earthly master's authority (v. 22 commanded obedience to the earthly master) but locates it within the larger authority of Christ. The slave serves the earthly master, but in doing so serves the Lord. The work is not diminished by being slave labor; it is elevated by being offered to the Lord.

"Knowing that from the Lord you will receive the inheritance as your reward. You are serving the Lord Christ" (3:24).

The participle eidotes ("knowing") introduces the basis for the slave's wholehearted work. What the slave knows is: apo kyriou apholēmpsesthe ten antapodosin tes kleronomias — "from the Lord you will receive the recompense of the inheritance." The word antapodosis ("recompense, repayment, reward") describes a return corresponding to what was given. The word kleronomia ("inheritance") is the same word used in 1:12, where the Father "qualified you to share in the inheritance (kleros) of the saints in light." The inheritance that belonged to Israel as God's people — the kleros, the allotted portion in God's purposes — is now promised to the slave. In the social world of the first century, a slave could not inherit. A slave was property, not an heir. But in the Lord's economy, the slave receives the inheritance. The social impossibility becomes a spiritual certainty.

The final statement is declarative: to kyrio Christo douleuete — "you are serving the Lord Christ." The verb douleuo ("to serve as a slave, to be in bondage to") is the verb proper to slaves — the doulos does douleia. But the kyrios being served is not the earthly master but the Lord Christ. The slave's service, though rendered to an earthly master, is in reality rendered to Christ. The statement also functions as a command: "serve the Lord Christ" — make your service to the earthly master an act of service to the true Lord.

"For the wrongdoer will be paid back for the wrong he has done, and there is no partiality" (3:25).

The word gar ("for") introduces a warning. The verb komisetai ("will receive back, will be repaid," future middle of komizo, "to carry, to bear, to receive back") states the principle: ho adikon ("the one who does wrong") will receive back ho edikesen ("the wrong he did"). The wrongdoing (adikeo, "to do wrong, to treat unjustly") is not specified — it could apply to the slave who serves dishonestly or to the master who treats the slave unjustly. The ambiguity may be deliberate: the principle applies to both parties. The word prosopolempsia ("partiality, respect of persons") is a compound: prosopon ("face") + lambano ("to receive"). In the LXX, the phrase lambanein prosopon ("to receive the face," i.e., to show favoritism) appears in Leviticus 19:15: ou lēmpsē prosopon ptochou oude thaumaseis prosopon dynatou — "you shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great." Deuteronomy 10:17 declares that God ou thaumazei prosopon oude ou lēmpsetai doron — "does not show partiality and does not take a bribe." The absence of partiality means that God judges impartially — the wrongdoer will be repaid whether slave or free, whether powerful or powerless.

The verse functions as a transition to 4:1, where the instruction to masters will complete the household code. The warning applies to both the slave who wrongs the master and the master who wrongs the slave. In a social system where masters could wrong their slaves with impunity, the declaration that there is no partiality with God would be heard as a leveling principle: the master who treats the slave unjustly will face the same divine judgment as anyone else. The God who qualified both slave and free for the inheritance of the saints in light (1:12) does not favor the master's social position when rendering judgment.

The Argument of the Chapter as a Whole

Colossians 3 translates the theological reality of co-death and co-resurrection with Christ into a comprehensive way of life. The chapter moves through four connected stages, each flowing logically from the one before.

First (3:1-4): the foundation. Because the Colossians died with Christ and were raised with him (restating 2:11-13, 20), their orientation must be upward — toward the things above, where Christ is seated at God's right hand. Their true life is hidden with Christ in God, and when Christ is revealed, they will be revealed with him in glory. This foundation answers the question left open by 2:23: if the philosophy's ascetic program cannot deal with the flesh, what can? The answer is: participation in Christ's death and resurrection, which produces a new orientation and a new identity.

Second (3:5-11): the negative consequence. The death with Christ must be actively applied: the Colossians must put to death the earthly members — the vices that belong to the old existence (vv. 5-7) — and strip off the old self with its practices (vv. 8-9). The old self is replaced by the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator (v. 10). In the new humanity, every division that the old world enforced — ethnic, religious, cultural, social — is dissolved, and Christ is all and in all (v. 11). This section provides the genuine alternative to the philosophy's program. Where the philosophy imposed external regulations to control the body, Paul commands the execution of the internal dispositions that drive sinful behavior. Where the philosophy enforced distinctions (food laws separating clean from unclean, calendar observances marking Jewish identity, circumcision drawing the covenant boundary), the new humanity dissolves every distinction. The philosophy worked from the outside in and preserved the old categories; the gospel works from the inside out and creates a new humanity.

Third (3:12-17): the positive consequence. Having stripped off the old and put on the new, the Colossians must now clothe themselves with specific virtues: compassion, kindness, humility (genuine, not the performative kind the philosophy promoted), gentleness, patience, mutual forbearance, forgiveness modeled on the Lord's forgiveness, and above all, love — the bond that produces completeness. The peace of Christ is to rule in their hearts; the word of Christ is to dwell richly among them; everything is to be done in the name of the Lord Jesus with thanksgiving. This section describes the life of the new community — a community characterized not by regulations and ascetic achievement but by mutual care, honest speech, grateful worship, and pervasive thanksgiving.

Fourth (3:18-4:1): the household application. The principles of the new humanity are applied to the specific relationships of the first-century household: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. Each instruction is grounded in the community's relationship to the Lord (en kyrio, 3:18; euareston en kyrio, 3:20; phoboumenoi ton kyrion, 3:22; hos to kyrio, 3:23; to kyrio Christo, 3:24). The household code does not abolish the social structures of the first-century world, but it reframes every relationship within those structures by placing Christ as the ultimate Lord over every earthly authority. The husband's authority is constrained by the command to love and not to be harsh. The father's authority is constrained by the prohibition of provoking discouragement. The master's authority is relativized by the designation kata sarka ("according to the flesh") and by the warning that God shows no partiality in judgment. The slave's service is elevated by being reframed as service to the Lord Christ, who will grant the inheritance that earthly social structures deny.

The chapter's relationship to what precedes and follows. Chapter 2 dismantled the philosophy's claims and exposed its impotence: it cannot deal with the flesh (2:23). Chapter 3 provides what the philosophy could not: a genuine transformation of life from the inside out, grounded in the death and resurrection with Christ, producing a new humanity where old divisions are dissolved and a new community emerges characterized by love, peace, wisdom, and thanksgiving. Chapter 4 will extend this outward: the community prays, witnesses to outsiders with wisdom, and maintains its connections across cities (the letter exchange with Laodicea, the personal greetings).

Pronoun patterns. The second-person "you" (hymeis, hymas, hymon) addresses the Colossian community throughout the chapter. There is no shift to "we/us" in this chapter comparable to the shift in 2:13-14; the instruction is consistently directed at the Colossian recipients. The transition from "you" (the gathered community, vv. 1-17) to "wives," "husbands," "children," "fathers," "slaves," "masters" (vv. 18-4:1) moves from the whole community to specific groups within it, but all remain within the "you" of the Colossian assembly.

The conditional structure of the community's life. Chapter 3 does not present the Colossians' transformation as automatic or guaranteed. It is constructed entirely of imperatives: seek (v. 1), set your minds (v. 2), put to death (v. 5), put away (v. 8), do not lie (v. 9), put on (v. 12), forgive (v. 13), let the peace of Christ rule (v. 15), let the word of Christ dwell (v. 16), do everything in the name of the Lord (v. 17). These are commands, and commands can be obeyed or disobeyed. The indicative realities — you died (v. 3), you were raised (v. 1), you have put off the old self (v. 9), you have put on the new (v. 10) — ground the imperatives, but they do not replace them. The indicative creates the possibility and the obligation; the imperative calls for the response. The conditional of 1:23 ("if indeed you continue in the faith") continues to operate: the community's standing is real, but their continuation in that standing requires active, ongoing faithfulness. The chapter's cascade of imperatives is the practical content of what "continuing in the faith" looks like. The community that seeks what is above, puts to death what is earthly, puts on the virtues of the new humanity, and conducts all of life in the name of the Lord Jesus is the community that continues in the faith. The community that fails to do these things — that reverts to the old self's practices, that reinstates the old divisions, that lives as though still in the domain of the stoicheia — would be the community that has shifted from the hope of the gospel.

The chapter also bears directly on the question of the philosophy's ascetic program versus the gospel's ethical transformation. The philosophy commanded avoidance of external things: do not handle, do not taste, do not touch (2:21). The gospel commands the death of internal realities: put to death sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, greed (3:5). The philosophy imposed bodily severity (apheidia somatos, 2:23). The gospel puts on love, compassion, kindness, and forgiveness (3:12-14). The philosophy enforced divisions (food laws, calendrical observances, circumcision requirements). The gospel dissolves divisions (3:11). The philosophy had an appearance of wisdom (logon men echonta sophias, 2:23). The gospel teaches in all wisdom (en pase sophia, 3:16). The philosophy left the flesh untouched (ouk en time tini pros plesmonen tes sarkos, 2:23). The gospel puts the flesh to death (nekrosate, 3:5). At every point, the chapter provides the genuine article that the philosophy could only simulate. The new self, renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator, does what the philosophy's self-imposed religion never could: it transforms the person from the inside out, in community, under the lordship of Christ, oriented toward the things above where their true life is hidden with Christ in God.
