Revelation 9 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 8): Chapter 8 opens with the Lamb breaking the seventh seal, producing "silence in heaven for about half an hour" (8:1). After six seals of escalating intensity — horsemen, martyrs' cries, cosmic upheaval — the seventh seal does not bring a climax but a pause. The silence is the hush of the heavenly court before the next phase of judgment begins. Into this silence, seven angels receive seven trumpets (8:2), and a separate angel stands at the golden altar with a golden censer, offering "much incense" with "the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar which was before the throne" (8:3). The smoke of the incense, mingled with the prayers, rises before God (8:4). Then the angel takes the censer, fills it with fire from the altar, and hurls it to the earth, producing "peals of thunder and sounds and flashes of lightning and an earthquake" (8:5) — the Sinai-theophany formula that recurs at each climactic moment in the document (4:5; 8:5; 11:19; 16:18).

The connection between the prayers and the judgments is structural, not incidental. The prayers of the saints — the same prayers held in golden bowls in 5:8, the same cry of "How long?" from the martyrs under the altar in 6:9-10 — are offered on the golden altar, and then fire from that same altar is cast to the earth, initiating the trumpet sequence. The prayers of the suffering faithful are not unanswered; they are ingredient in the judgments that follow.

The first four trumpets (8:7-12) affect the natural world in an escalating pattern that recapitulates the Egyptian plagues at cosmic scale. The first trumpet brings hail and fire mixed with blood, burning a third of the earth, a third of the trees, and all the green grass (8:7) — echoing the seventh Egyptian plague of hail (Exodus 9:23-25). The second trumpet turns a third of the sea to blood and kills a third of sea creatures (8:8-9) — echoing the first Egyptian plague of the Nile turning to blood (Exodus 7:20-21). The third trumpet poisons a third of the rivers and springs with wormwood (8:10-11) — extending the water-corruption plague to freshwater sources. The fourth trumpet darkens a third of the sun, moon, and stars (8:12) — echoing the ninth Egyptian plague of darkness (Exodus 10:21-23). The fraction "a third" governs every trumpet judgment, an escalation from the "fourth" of the seal judgments (6:8) but still a bounded, partial destruction.

Chapter 8 concludes with a pivotal announcement. An eagle flying in midheaven cries out: "Woe, woe, woe to those who dwell on the earth, because of the remaining blasts of the trumpet of the three angels who are about to sound!" (8:13). The phrase "those who dwell on the earth" (tois katoikousin epi tes ges) is the document's recurring designation for humanity in its God-opposing posture (6:10; 11:10; 13:8, 14; 17:2, 8). The eagle's announcement divides the seven trumpets into two groups: the first four, which strike the natural world (earth, sea, rivers, heavenly bodies), and the final three, which are designated as "woes" — direct afflictions on the earth-dwellers themselves. Chapter 9 contains the first two of these three woes.

What the author argues immediately after (Revelation 10-11:14): Between the sixth and seventh trumpets, just as between the sixth and seventh seals (chapter 7), the author inserts an interlude. In 10:1-11, a "strong angel" (angelon ischyron) descends from heaven wrapped in a cloud, with a rainbow over his head, his face like the sun and his feet like pillars of fire — imagery drawn from the chapter 1 vision and from the theophany traditions. He holds an open small scroll (biblaridion) and plants one foot on the sea and one on land, signifying authority over the entire created order. He cries out with a loud voice, and seven thunders speak, but John is told to seal up what the thunders said and not write it (10:4). The angel then swears by the Creator of heaven, earth, and sea that "there will be no more delay, but in the days of the voice of the seventh angel, when he is about to sound, then the mystery of God is finished, as he announced to his servants the prophets" (10:6-7). John is told to take the scroll and eat it — an action drawn from Ezekiel 2:8-3:3, where the prophet ate the scroll of lamentations, mourning, and woe. The scroll is sweet in John's mouth but bitter in his stomach (10:9-10), and he is commissioned to "prophesy again concerning many peoples and nations and tongues and kings" (10:11).

The interlude continues in 11:1-13 with the measuring of the temple and the account of the two witnesses who prophesy for 1,260 days, are killed by the beast from the abyss (to therion to anabainon ek tes abyssou, 11:7 — the same abyss opened in 9:1-2), lie dead in the street for three and a half days, are raised to life, and ascend to heaven. An earthquake kills a tenth of the city (11:13), and "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13) — a response of acknowledgment, contrasting sharply with the refusal to repent in 9:20-21. Then comes the announcement: "The second woe is past; behold, the third woe is coming quickly" (11:14).

How chapter 9 connects the surrounding argument: Chapter 9 is the direct fulfillment of the eagle's announcement in 8:13. The eagle warned of three woes corresponding to the final three trumpets; chapter 9 delivers the first two. The shift from the first four trumpets to the fifth and sixth is marked by a change in target: the first four trumpets struck the natural world (earth, sea, rivers, heavenly bodies); the fifth and sixth trumpets strike humanity directly. The escalation is deliberate. God's preliminary judgments on the created order (trumpets 1-4) did not produce repentance; now the judgments fall on the earth-dwellers themselves.

The chapter also maintains the unbroken chain of divine authority that has governed the entire sequence from chapter 4 onward. The divine passive edothe ("was given") appears repeatedly: the key was given to the star (9:1), power was given to the locusts (9:3), they were told what they could and could not do (9:4-5), the four angels were released by a command from the golden altar (9:13-14). Every destructive force in this chapter operates under the same authorization that governed the seal judgments: God permits, bounds, and directs the destruction. The chain of authority runs unbroken from the throne through the Lamb through the scroll through the trumpets to the agents of judgment.

The chapter's theological climax comes not in the spectacular visions of locust-scorpions or fire-breathing cavalry but in the two concluding verses (9:20-21), where the survivors of these plagues refuse to repent. The purpose of the intensifying judgments — to provoke recognition of God and abandonment of idolatry — is stated by its failure. The survivors continue worshipping demons and idols "which can neither see nor hear nor walk" (9:20) and do not repent of their murders, sorceries, immorality, or thefts (9:21). This refusal mirrors the pattern of Pharaoh during the Egyptian plagues: despite escalating devastation, the heart remains hardened (Exodus 7:22; 8:15, 19, 32; 9:7, 12, 34-35; 10:20, 27). The recapitulation of the Egyptian plagues in the trumpet judgments is not merely formal but structural: the same pattern of divine judgment met by human obstinacy that characterized the exodus is now operating at cosmic scale.

The connection backward to the prayers of the saints is critical. In 8:3-5, the prayers of the saints were offered on the golden altar, and fire from that altar was hurled to earth. In 9:13, the command to release the four angels comes from the four horns of the golden altar. The same altar that received the prayers now issues the command for judgment. The martyrs' cry of 6:10 — "How long, O Lord, holy and true, will you refrain from judging and avenging our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" — is being answered progressively through the trumpet sequence. The judgments of chapter 9 are, in part, the divine response to the prayers of the suffering faithful.

Verses 1-2: The Fifth Trumpet — The Star and the Abyss

"Then the fifth angel sounded, and I saw a star from heaven which had fallen to the earth; and the key of the bottomless pit was given to him. He opened the bottomless pit, and smoke went up out of the pit, like the smoke of a great furnace; and the sun and the air were darkened by the smoke of the pit."

"The fifth angel sounded" (ho pemptos angelos esalpisen). The fifth trumpet continues the sequence begun in 8:7. The first four trumpets were announced in quick succession, each affecting a different domain of the natural world. The fifth trumpet marks the transition to the woe-trumpets announced by the eagle in 8:13. From this point, the targets are no longer trees, seas, rivers, and stars but human beings — specifically, "those who dwell on the earth."

"I saw a star from heaven which had fallen to the earth" (eidon astera ek tou ouranou peptokota eis ten gen). The word peptokota is a perfect active participle of pipto ("to fall"): the star "had fallen" — the fall is already complete when John sees it. This is not a star falling as John watches (as in 8:10, where "a great star fell from heaven," epesen ek tou ouranou) but one that has already descended and now stands on the earth. The masculine pronoun auto ("to him") in the next clause confirms that this "star" is a personal being, not a celestial object. Stars in Israel's scriptures frequently represent angelic beings. In Job 38:7, "the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy" — the parallelism identifies stars with the heavenly court. In Daniel 8:10, the little horn "grew up to the host of heaven and caused some of the host and some of the stars to fall to the earth." In Isaiah 14:12, the taunt against the king of Babylon employs the image of a fallen celestial figure: "How you have fallen from heaven, O star of the morning, son of the dawn!" (LXX: pos exepesen ek tou ouranou ho heosphoros ho proi anatellon).

Whether this fallen star represents a demonic being, a fallen angel assigned a specific task, or an angelic agent of judgment who has "fallen" only in the sense of descending to earth cannot be determined from the text alone with certainty. What the text does make clear is that this figure acts under divine authority: the key is given to him (edothe auto — the divine passive that governs the entire chapter). The fallen star does not seize the key by force; it is entrusted to him by the same sovereign authority that has governed every action since the Lamb opened the first seal.

"The key of the bottomless pit was given to him" (edothe auto he kleis tou phreatos tes abyssou). The word kleis ("key") appeared in 1:18, where the risen Christ declared: "I have the keys of death and of Hades" (echo tas kleis tou thanatou kai tou hadou). In 3:7, the Philadelphia message identifies the speaker as the one who holds "the key of David; who opens and no one will shut, and who shuts and no one opens" — language drawn from Isaiah 22:22. Keys in this document represent authority over access. The risen Christ holds the ultimate keys; this star receives a specific key for a specific purpose. The authority is derivative, granted, bounded.

The "bottomless pit" (to phrear tes abyssou, literally "the shaft/well of the abyss") introduces a location that will recur at critical points in the document. In 11:7, "the beast that comes up out of the abyss" kills the two witnesses. In 17:8, the beast "is about to come up out of the abyss." In 20:1-3, an angel descends from heaven "holding the key of the abyss" and binds the dragon for a thousand years, throwing him into the abyss, shutting it, and sealing it over him. The abyss functions as a prison for hostile spiritual powers — a place of confinement from which they can emerge only when permitted and to which they will ultimately be returned.

In the LXX, abyssos translates the Hebrew tehom — the primordial deep of Genesis 1:2 ("darkness was over the surface of the deep," epi tes abyssou). In later Jewish literature, the abyss became associated with the place of imprisonment for rebellious spiritual beings. In 1 Enoch 18:11-16 and 21:1-10, fallen angels are confined in a fiery abyss. In Jubilees 5:6-10, the watchers are bound "in the depths of the earth." In Luke 8:31, the demons possessing the Gerasene man "were imploring him not to command them to go away into the abyss" (eis ten abysson). A first-century audience familiar with this tradition would understand the abyss as the holding place for hostile spiritual forces — forces that are confined, not autonomous, and released only by divine decision.

"Smoke went up out of the pit, like the smoke of a great furnace; and the sun and the air were darkened by the smoke of the pit" (anebe kapnos ek tou phreatos hos kapnos kaminou megales, kai eskotisthe ho helios kai ho aer ek tou kapnou tou phreatos). The opening of the abyss produces smoke that darkens the sun and the air. The image has multiple scriptural roots.

First, Genesis 19:28: when Abraham looked toward Sodom and Gomorrah after their destruction, "he saw, and behold, the smoke of the land ascended like the smoke of a furnace" (LXX: anebaine he phlox tes ges hosei atmis kaminou). The phrase "like the smoke of a great furnace" (hos kapnos kaminou megales) echoes this image directly. The opening of the abyss produces smoke reminiscent of Sodom's destruction — the smoke of divine judgment on a civilization given over to wickedness.

Second, Exodus 19:18: at Sinai, "the LORD descended upon it in fire; and its smoke ascended like the smoke of a furnace" (anebaine ho kapnos hosei kapnos kaminou). The furnace-smoke image is associated with both judgment (Sodom) and theophany (Sinai). The smoke from the abyss participates in both associations: it is the manifestation of divine judgment and the terrifying effect of God's action in the world.

Third, the darkening of the sun connects to the fourth trumpet (8:12), where a third of the sun was struck, and to the prophetic Day of the LORD language drawn from Joel 2:10: "The sun and the moon grow dark, and the stars lose their brightness." The progressive darkening through the trumpet sequence represents the intensifying withdrawal of created light — the un-making of the first act of creation (Genesis 1:3, "Let there be light").

Verses 3-6: The Locusts from the Abyss

"Then out of the smoke came locusts upon the earth, and power was given them, as the scorpions of the earth have power. They were told not to hurt the grass of the earth, nor any green thing, nor any tree, but only the men who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads. And they were not permitted to kill anyone, but to torment for five months; and their torment was like the torment of a scorpion when it stings a man. And in those days men will seek death and will not find it; they will long to die, and death flees from them."

"Out of the smoke came locusts upon the earth" (ek tou kapnou exelthon akrides eis ten gen). The locusts (akrides) emerge from the smoke of the abyss. Locusts are one of the most devastating natural phenomena known in the ancient Near East. A locust swarm could strip a region of all vegetation in hours, leaving famine in its wake. Locusts constituted the eighth plague on Egypt (Exodus 10:1-20), where they "covered the surface of the whole land, so that the land was darkened; and they ate every plant of the land" (Exodus 10:15). The connection to the Egyptian plagues, which has governed the trumpet sequence from the beginning, continues here.

But the most important scriptural background for this locust vision is Joel 1-2. Joel describes a devastating locust invasion as the vehicle of the Day of the LORD. In Joel 1:6, the locusts are described as "a nation" that "has invaded my land, mighty and without number; its teeth are the teeth of a lion, and it has the fangs of a lioness." Joel 2:2-11 describes the locust horde in military terms: "Their appearance is like the appearance of horses; and like war horses, so they run. With a noise as of chariots they leap on the tops of the mountains" (Joel 2:4-5). Joel 2:10: "Before them the earth quakes, the heavens tremble, the sun and the moon grow dark." Most significantly, Joel 2:11 identifies the locusts as God's own army: "The LORD utters his voice before his army; surely his camp is very great, for strong is he who carries out his word." And Joel 2:25 confirms the identification: "My great army which I sent among you." The locust army in Joel is not a random natural disaster but a divine instrument — God's own military force deployed in judgment.

The author of Revelation draws extensively on Joel's locust-army imagery but transforms it. Joel's locusts devour vegetation; these locusts are forbidden from harming vegetation and instead torment humans. Joel's locusts look like horses and chariots; these locusts will be described with an elaborate composite appearance (9:7-10). The transformation signals that the author is working with Joel's imagery at a heightened, symbolic level: these are not natural locusts but spiritual agents of torment that operate within the prophetic framework Joel established.

"Power was given them, as the scorpions of the earth have power" (edothe autais exousia hos echousin exousian hoi skorpioi tes ges). The divine passive edothe ("was given") again marks the source of authority. The locusts' power is not inherent but granted. The comparison to scorpions introduces a new element not present in Joel or Exodus. A scorpion's sting is not immediately lethal to an adult human but produces intense, burning pain — an agony that is debilitating but temporary. The comparison signals the nature of the locusts' weapon: they inflict torment, not death. In Deuteronomy 8:15, the wilderness through which God led Israel is described as containing "fiery serpents and scorpions" — creatures of the desolate, hostile wasteland. In Ezekiel 2:6, God tells the prophet: "Do not be afraid of them or their words, though briers and thorns are with you, and you sit among scorpions." In Luke 10:19, Jesus tells the seventy: "Behold, I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy." Scorpions represent hostile, painful forces that operate in the domain of darkness and wilderness — appropriate to creatures emerging from the abyss.

"They were told not to hurt the grass of the earth, nor any green thing, nor any tree" (errethe autais hina me adikesousin ton chorton tes ges oude pan chloron oude pan dendron). The verb errethe ("it was said to them, they were told") is a divine passive — the command comes from God, though the speaker is not named. The instruction is remarkable: natural locusts devour vegetation. That is their defining behavior. Joel's locusts stripped the land bare (Joel 1:4, 7). The Egyptian locusts "ate every plant of the land" (Exodus 10:15). But these locusts are specifically prohibited from doing what locusts do. The prohibition separates them decisively from their natural counterparts and confirms that they are symbolic-spiritual agents operating under a commission that inverts the natural locust pattern.

The prohibition also creates a notable tension with 8:7, where the first trumpet burned "a third of the trees" and "all the green grass." The same grass that was burned in the first trumpet is now protected in the fifth. This tension may indicate that the trumpet sequences are not strictly chronological but represent overlapping or recapitulated perspectives on divine judgment — the same reality viewed from different angles. Alternatively, the detail functions within the literary logic of the chapter: the locusts' prohibition against harming vegetation underscores that their sole target is humanity.

The verb adikeo ("to hurt, to harm, to act unjustly toward") appeared in 7:2-3, where the angel with the seal cried out to the four angels: "Do not harm (me adikesete) the earth or the sea or the trees until we have sealed the bond-servants of our God on their foreheads." The same verb now governs the locusts' restrictions. The protection of vegetation and the protection of the sealed are governed by the same divine command structure.

"But only the men who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads" (ei me tous anthropous hoitines ouk echousin ten sphragida tou theou epi ton metopon). The seal (sphragis) of God on the foreheads connects directly to 7:2-8, where 144,000 servants of God were sealed before the trumpet judgments began. The sealing in chapter 7 was not a decorative interlude; it was the necessary precondition for the judgments that follow. The sealed are protected; the unsealed are exposed. The distinction between the sealed and the unsealed mirrors the distinction in the Egyptian plagues between Israel and Egypt. In Exodus 8:22-23 (LXX 8:18-19), God declared: "I will set apart the land of Goshen, where my people are living... I will put a division between my people and your people." In Exodus 9:4: "The LORD will make a distinction between the livestock of Israel and the livestock of Egypt." In Exodus 12:13, the blood on the doorposts was the sign that protected Israel's firstborn from the destroyer. The seal of God in Revelation 7 and 9 functions as the new exodus sign: those marked by God are protected through the judgment, not removed from it.

The precedent in Ezekiel is equally precise. In Ezekiel 9:4-6, God commands the man clothed in linen: "Go through the midst of the city, through the midst of Jerusalem, and put a mark (tav, LXX: semeion) on the foreheads of the men who sigh and groan over all the abominations which are being committed in its midst." The destroying angels that follow are told: "Do not touch any man on whom is the mark" (Ezekiel 9:6). The mark on the forehead in Ezekiel protected the righteous from the divine judgment falling on Jerusalem. The seal of God on the foreheads in Revelation serves the identical function: it identifies those who belong to God and protects them from the judgment falling on the earth-dwellers.

"They were not permitted to kill anyone, but to torment for five months" (edothe autois hina me apokteinousin autous, all' hina basanisthesontai menas pente). The divine passive edothe ("it was given to them," here rendered "they were not permitted") again marks divine limitation. The locusts are given a dual restriction: they may not kill, and they may operate only for five months. The judgment is bounded in both intensity and duration.

The five months (menas pente) corresponds to the natural life cycle of locusts in the Mediterranean region. The locust season runs roughly from late spring to early autumn — approximately five months. The author uses the natural time frame of a locust plague to set the duration of this supernatural torment. The specificity reinforces both the locust imagery and the deliberate limitation of the judgment. This is not endless torment but a defined, temporary affliction with a divinely imposed expiration.

The word basanisthesontai ("they will be tormented") is from basanizo ("to torture, to torment, to test by the touchstone"). The basanos was originally a touchstone used to test the purity of metals — the word carries the connotation of testing, examining, proving. In the LXX and first-century usage, it refers to intense suffering and distress. In Matthew 8:29, the demons cry to Jesus: "Have you come here to torment (basanisai) us before the time?" The torment inflicted by the locusts is severe but explicitly non-lethal — it tests and afflicts but does not destroy.

"Their torment was like the torment of a scorpion when it stings a man" (ho basanismos auton hos basanismos skorpiou, hotan paise anthropon). The scorpion sting is intensely painful. In the Mediterranean and Near Eastern world, scorpion stings were a well-known hazard. The pain is sharp, burning, and debilitating, sometimes producing convulsions, but rarely fatal for a healthy adult. The comparison specifies the quality of the torment: acute, agonizing, but not lethal.

"And in those days men will seek death and will not find it; they will long to die, and death flees from them" (zetesousin hoi anthropoi ton thanaton kai ou me heuresousin auton, kai epithymesousin apothanein kai pheugei ho thanatos ap' auton). The torment is so severe that its victims desire death as escape, but death is denied to them. The image inverts the normal human relationship to death: ordinarily, death is feared and fled from; here, death is sought and it is death that flees. The inversion is deliberate and devastating.

The language echoes Job 3:21, where the afflicted man speaks of those "who long for death, but it does not come, and dig for it more than for hidden treasures." It also recalls Jeremiah 8:3: "And death shall be preferred to life by all the remnant that remains of this evil family." In both Job and Jeremiah, the preference for death over life represents the extremity of suffering. But in both, death is merely preferred; here, death is actively sought and actively withheld. The risen Christ holds "the keys of death and of Hades" (1:18). If death flees from these sufferers, it is because the one who controls death's access has decreed that this torment will not be resolved by death. The purpose of the torment is not destruction but affliction — a chastisement that, by its very refusal to allow escape through death, presses the sufferers toward another response. The response the author intends to highlight — repentance — will be addressed explicitly in verses 20-21.

Verses 7-10: The Appearance of the Locusts

"The appearance of the locusts was like horses prepared for battle; and on their heads appeared to be crowns like gold, and their faces were like the faces of men. They had hair like the hair of women, and their teeth were like the teeth of lions. They had breastplates like breastplates of iron; and the sound of their wings was like the sound of chariots, of many horses rushing to battle. They have tails like scorpions, and stings; and in their tails is their power to hurt men for five months."

"The appearance of the locusts was like horses prepared for battle" (ta homoiomata ton akridon homoia hippois hetoimasmenois eis polemon). The description opens with the comparison that governs the entire portrait: the locusts look like war horses. This image comes directly from Joel 2:4: "Their appearance is like the appearance of horses; and like war horses, so they run." The connection between locusts and horses was a commonplace in the ancient world. An old Arabic proverb observed that the locust's head resembles a horse's head. But the author goes beyond natural observation to construct a composite creature that combines elements of multiple fearsome realities into a single symbolic figure.

The word hetoimasmenois ("prepared, made ready") is the same word used in 9:15 for the four angels "prepared for the hour and day and month and year." The locusts are not a random swarm but an army equipped and deployed for a specific mission.

"On their heads appeared to be crowns like gold" (epi tas kephalas auton hos stephanoi homoioi chryso). The crowns (stephanoi) are described with double approximation: "appeared to be" (hos, "like, as if") crowns "like gold" (homoioi chryso, "resembling gold"). They are not true gold crowns but the appearance of crowns. The stephanos throughout this document signifies victory and authority: the crowns of the twenty-four elders (4:4, 10), the crown of the first horseman (6:2), the crown of life promised to Smyrna (2:10). But these crowns are only "like" gold — they are imitations, parodies of genuine authority. The locusts carry a semblance of royal authority, but it is derivative and counterfeit. This distinguishes them from the genuine crown-bearers in the heavenly court.

"Their faces were like the faces of men" (ta prosopa auton hos prosopa anthropon). The human faces on insect bodies create a disturbing hybrid. The effect is uncanny — these are not mere animals but intelligent agents with human-like awareness. The human face suggests purposefulness, intention, and comprehension. These creatures know what they are doing.

"They had hair like the hair of women" (eichon trichas hos trichas gynaikon). The detail is visually striking and has been interpreted in various ways. Some ancient commentators connected it to the antennae of actual locusts, which can appear hair-like. Others see a connection to the hairstyles of Parthian warriors, who wore their hair long. Within the composite image, the detail adds to the unsettling, boundary-crossing nature of the creatures: they combine the features of horses, men, women, lions, scorpions, and insects into a single being that defies every natural category. The transgression of natural boundaries is itself a mark of their origin — they come from the abyss, not from the created order.

"Their teeth were like the teeth of lions" (hoi odontes auton hos leonton esan). Lion teeth signify tearing, devouring power. Joel 1:6 provides the direct background: "a nation has invaded my land, mighty and without number; its teeth are the teeth of a lion, and it has the fangs of a lioness." Joel described natural locusts in terms of a lion's destructive capacity. The author of Revelation picks up Joel's exact language and incorporates it into the composite portrait.

"They had breastplates like breastplates of iron" (eichon thorakas hos thorakas siderous). The breastplates (thorakas) make the military imagery explicit. Joel 2:7-8 describes the locust army: "They run like mighty men, they climb the wall like soldiers; and they each march in line, nor do they deviate from their paths. They do not crowd each other, they march everyone in his path." Joel portrayed the locusts as disciplined soldiers; the author of Revelation equips them with armor. Iron breastplates signify invulnerability — these creatures cannot be struck down or stopped. In the ancient world, iron was the metal of military might. Daniel 2:40 describes the fourth kingdom as "strong as iron" that "crushes and shatters all things." The iron breastplates make the locusts an unstoppable force.

"The sound of their wings was like the sound of chariots, of many horses rushing to battle" (he phone ton pterygon auton hos phone harmaton hippon pollon trechonton eis polemon). Joel 2:5: "With a noise as of chariots they leap on the tops of the mountains." The connection to Joel continues. The sound of a locust swarm is genuinely terrifying — ancient writers compared it to the roar of an approaching army or a prairie fire. The author draws on this natural observation but amplifies it through Joel's prophetic lens: the sound is of an army charging into battle. The auditory image complements the visual: these creatures look like armored war horses and sound like a cavalry charge.

"They have tails like scorpions, and stings; and in their tails is their power to hurt men for five months" (echousin ouras homoias skorpiois kai kentra, kai en tais ourais auton he exousia auton adikesai tous anthropous menas pente). The scorpion element, introduced in verse 3, is now specified as the tail and sting. The word exousia ("power, authority") is the same word used throughout the document for granted authority (6:8; 9:3; 13:5, 7). Their authority to inflict harm is located specifically in their tails — an unusual detail that will be echoed in the sixth trumpet's cavalry, whose horses also have destructive power in their tails (9:19). The five-month duration is repeated, emphasizing the bounded nature of the judgment.

The composite creature defies literal interpretation and demands symbolic reading. The author is constructing a vision of terrifying spiritual forces through the accumulation of fearsome attributes drawn from the natural and prophetic worlds. No single natural creature is being described. The effect is cumulative: the locust-army of Joel, the destructive teeth of a lion, the intelligence of a human face, the invulnerability of iron armor, the agonizing sting of a scorpion — all combined into a single vision of divinely authorized torment descending on those who lack God's seal.

Verse 11: The King of the Abyss

"They have as king over them, the angel of the abyss; his name in Hebrew is Abaddon, and in the Greek he has the name Apollyon."

"They have as king over them, the angel of the abyss" (echousin ep' auton basilea ton angelon tes abyssou). Natural locusts have no king. Proverbs 30:27 makes this explicit: "The locusts have no king, yet all of them go out in ranks." The detail that these locusts have a king confirms once more that they are not natural creatures but a supernatural army under organized command. Their king is "the angel of the abyss" (ton angelon tes abyssou) — an angelic figure whose domain is the same abyss from which the locusts emerged. Whether this angel is the same as the "star" who received the key in verse 1, or a different figure, the text does not specify. The angel's identity is given through his name.

"His name in Hebrew is Abaddon" (onoma auto Hebraisti Abaddon). The Hebrew word Abaddon derives from the root 'abad ("to perish, to destroy, to be lost"). In the Hebrew scriptures, Abaddon functions as a synonym for Sheol — the realm of the dead, the place of destruction. Job 26:6: "Sheol is naked before him, and Abaddon has no covering." Job 28:22: "Abaddon and Death say, 'With our ears we have heard a report of it.'" Job 31:12: "For it would be a fire that consumes to Abaddon." Psalm 88:11 (LXX 87:12): "Will your steadfast love be declared in the grave, your faithfulness in Abaddon?" Proverbs 15:11: "Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the LORD; how much more the hearts of the children of man." Proverbs 27:20: "Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied." In every occurrence, Abaddon is the place of death, destruction, and the grave — the realm where things perish. By naming the locust king "Abaddon," the author personifies the destroying power of the abyss. The place of destruction has a ruler, and his name is Destruction itself.

"And in the Greek he has the name Apollyon" (kai en te Hellenike onoma echei Apollyon). The Greek name Apollyon means "Destroyer" (from apollymi, "to destroy, to ruin, to kill"). The author provides both the Hebrew and Greek names, a practice that serves two functions. First, it ensures comprehension by both Jewish and Gentile hearers in the assemblies. Second, the Greek name Apollyon carries a phonetic resonance that a first-century audience in the Roman province of Asia would not have missed: Apollyon echoes Apollon — Apollo, the Greek god. Apollo was the patron deity of the emperor Augustus, who claimed Apollo's special protection. The temple of Apollo at Didyma, near Miletus, was one of the most important oracles in Asia Minor — within the immediate geographical sphere of the seven assemblies. Apollo was also associated with plagues (in Homer's Iliad 1.43-53, Apollo sends a plague upon the Greek camp by shooting arrows from his bow) and, paradoxically, with their cure. In some Greek traditions, Apollo bore the epithet Parnopios ("the locust god"), worshipped as a protector against locust plagues.

The author does not make the connection to Apollo explicit. The text does not say "Apollyon is Apollo." But the phonetic echo, combined with the locust imagery and the plague associations, would have been audible to a Greek-speaking audience in Asia Minor. If the hearers registered the resonance, the effect would be devastating: the divine patron of the imperial order is not a heavenly protector but the angel of the abyss, the king of demonic locusts, the Destroyer. The name that the empire reveres belongs, in this vision, to the ruler of the pit.

Verse 12: The Transition Between Woes

"The first woe is past; behold, two woes are still coming after these things."

"The first woe is past" (he ouai he mia apelthen). This editorial note connects back to the eagle's announcement of three woes in 8:13. The fifth trumpet constitutes the first woe; the sixth trumpet (9:13-21) will constitute the second. The third woe corresponds to the seventh trumpet (11:14-15), though it is not announced until 11:14, after the interlude of 10:1-11:13. The announcement serves as a structural marker, orienting the audience within the sequence and emphasizing that the worst is yet to come. The word apelthen ("has passed, has gone") uses the same verb used in 21:4 for the former things that "have passed away." The first woe is complete; it has run its course.

"Behold, two woes are still coming after these things" (idou erchontai eti duo ouai meta tauta). The word idou ("behold, look") calls attention to what follows, signaling that the pace of judgment is not slowing but accelerating. The two remaining woes will be more severe than the first, just as the first four trumpets were more severe than the seals and the sixth trumpet will be more severe than the fifth. The trajectory of the document's judgment sequences is one of intensification: from a fourth (seals) to a third (trumpets), from torment without death (fifth trumpet) to the killing of a third of humanity (sixth trumpet).

Verses 13-15: The Sixth Trumpet — The Four Angels at the Euphrates

"Then the sixth angel sounded, and I heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar which is before God, one saying to the sixth angel who had the trumpet, 'Release the four angels who are bound at the great river Euphrates.' And the four angels, who had been prepared for the hour and day and month and year, were released, so that they would kill a third of mankind."

"I heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar which is before God" (ekousa phonen mian ek ton tessaron keraton tou thysiasteriou tou chrysou tou enopion tou theou). The voice that initiates the sixth trumpet comes from a specific location: the four horns of the golden altar before God. The golden altar (thysiastеrion chrysoun) is the altar of incense, which in the tabernacle and temple stood before the veil of the holy of holies (Exodus 30:1-6). This is the same altar introduced in 8:3, where the angel offered incense "with the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar which was before the throne." The horns of the altar (kerata) were the four projections at the corners of the altar of incense (Exodus 30:2: "You shall make its horns on its four corners; its horns shall be of one piece with it"). In Leviticus 4:7, 18, the priest put blood of the sin offering "on the horns of the altar of fragrant incense" as part of atonement.

The voice issues from the altar where the prayers of the saints were offered and where the fire of judgment was taken (8:3-5). The connection is theologically significant: the judgment announced by this voice is a response to the prayers. The martyrs' cry — "How long, O Lord, holy and true, will you refrain from judging and avenging our blood?" (6:10) — was offered on this altar, and now the altar itself issues the command for a new phase of judgment. The prayers of the suffering faithful are being answered.

"Release the four angels who are bound at the great river Euphrates" (Lyson tous tessaras angelous tous dedemenous epi to potamo to megalo Euphrate). The command is direct: "Release" (lyson, aorist imperative of lyo, "to loose, to release"). Four angels are held bound (dedemenous, perfect passive participle — they have been bound and remain bound until this moment) at the Euphrates. The binding indicates that these are hostile forces that have been restrained — their destructive capacity has been held in check by divine decree, and only now, at the precise appointed moment, are they let loose. The concept of angels bound and awaiting release has precedent in 1 Enoch 18:14-16 and Jude 6, where angels who transgressed their boundaries are kept "in eternal bonds under darkness for the judgment of the great day."

The "great river Euphrates" (to potamo to megalo Euphrate) is the most geographically and historically charged boundary in Israel's scriptures. In Genesis 15:18, God defined the eastern limit of the promised land: "To your descendants I have given this land, from the river of Egypt as far as the great river, the river Euphrates." In Deuteronomy 1:7 and Joshua 1:4, the Euphrates marks the far eastern boundary. Throughout Israel's history, the great military threats came from beyond the Euphrates: Assyria, which destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BCE; Babylon, which destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in 586 BCE; Persia, which conquered Babylon. The Euphrates was the line beyond which hostile empires gathered their strength.

For a first-century audience under Roman rule, the Euphrates carried a more immediate resonance. The Euphrates was the eastern frontier of the Roman Empire, beyond which lay the Parthian Empire — Rome's most feared and unconquered rival. The Parthians had inflicted humiliating defeats on Roman armies, most notably the annihilation of Crassus's legions at Carrhae in 53 BCE. Parthian cavalry — mounted archers and armored horsemen — were legendary for their speed, ferocity, and tactical ingenuity, including the famous "Parthian shot," firing arrows while appearing to retreat. The fear of a Parthian invasion from beyond the Euphrates was a persistent anxiety in the Roman world, particularly in the eastern provinces. The description that follows — a cavalry of inconceivable numbers bearing fire, smoke, and brimstone — would have resonated with this fear while transcending it: the reality the author describes surpasses any earthly army.

"The four angels, who had been prepared for the hour and day and month and year, were released, so that they would kill a third of mankind" (hoi hetoimasmenoi eis ten horan kai hemeran kai mena kai eniauton, hina apokteinousin to triton ton anthropon). The phrase "prepared for the hour and day and month and year" (hetoimasmenoi eis ten horan kai hemeran kai mena kai eniauton) expresses divine foreordination with extraordinary precision. The perfect passive participle hetoimasmenoi ("having been prepared") indicates that the preparation was completed in the past and its result endures. These angels were prepared for a specific moment — not merely a general season but an exact hour, day, month, and year. The quadruple temporal specification (hour, day, month, year, moving from smallest to largest unit) conveys absolute precision. Nothing about this event is improvised, accidental, or contingent. The timing is as fixed as the identity of the agents.

The purpose is stated: "so that they would kill a third of mankind" (hina apokteinousin to triton ton anthropon). The escalation from the fifth trumpet is severe. The locusts were prohibited from killing; the sixth trumpet's forces are commissioned to kill. And the scale is staggering: a third of humanity. The fraction "a third" (to triton) maintains the proportion that has governed the entire trumpet sequence (8:7-12). A third — not all, not half, not everything. The judgment is devastating but still partial, still leaving a surviving remainder. The partial character of the destruction is itself significant: the judgment leaves survivors, and the text will address the response of those survivors in verses 20-21.

Verses 16-19: The Cavalry of Fire, Smoke, and Brimstone

"The number of the armies of the horsemen was two hundred million; I heard the number of them. And this is how I saw in the vision the horses and those who sat on them: the riders had breastplates the color of fire and of hyacinth and of brimstone; and the heads of the horses are like the heads of lions; and out of their mouths proceed fire and smoke and brimstone. A third of mankind was killed by these three plagues, by the fire and the smoke and the brimstone which proceeded out of their mouths. For the power of the horses is in their mouths and in their tails; for their tails are like serpents and have heads, and with them they do harm."

"The number of the armies of the horsemen was two hundred million" (ho arithmos ton strateumaton tou hippikou dismyriades myriadon). The Greek is dismyriades myriadon — literally "two ten-thousands of ten-thousands," which calculates to 200,000,000. The number is beyond any military force in human history. For reference, the entire population of the Roman Empire at its height is estimated at fifty to seventy million. An army of two hundred million exceeds the population of the ancient world. The number signifies an innumerable, overwhelming force — a military reality that no human power could resist or even comprehend. In Daniel 7:10, "a thousand thousands served him, and ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him" — a description of the heavenly court's attendants. The author's number surpasses even Daniel's multitude. The phrase "I heard the number of them" (ekousa ton arithmon auton) parallels 7:4, "I heard the number of those who were sealed." In both cases, John hears a number he could not have counted by sight.

"The riders had breastplates the color of fire and of hyacinth and of brimstone" (echontas thorakas pyrinous kai hyakinthinous kai theiodeis). The breastplates (thorakas) of the riders are described in three colors, each corresponding to one of the three plagues that will kill a third of humanity. "Fiery" (pyrinous, from pyr, "fire") corresponds to fire; "hyacinth-colored" (hyakinthinous — a deep blue or dark blue-purple, the color of the hyakinthos flower) corresponds to smoke; "brimstone-colored" (theiodeis — sulfurous yellow) corresponds to brimstone. The riders wear on their bodies the colors of the destruction they bring. The visual description encodes the nature of the judgment: the army is fire, smoke, and brimstone made mobile and militant.

"The heads of the horses are like the heads of lions" (hai kephalai ton hippon hos kephalai leonton). As with the locusts (9:8), lion imagery characterizes the destructive power of these creatures. Joel 1:6, describing the locust invasion: "Its teeth are the teeth of a lion." The lion's head on a horse's body creates another composite creature — a being that combines the strength and speed of a war horse with the ferocity and devouring power of a lion.

"Out of their mouths proceed fire and smoke and brimstone" (ek ton stomaton auton ekporeuetai pyr kai kapnos kai theion). The three substances that proceed from the horses' mouths are the traditional instruments of divine judgment associated with the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. Genesis 19:24-25: "Then the LORD rained on Sodom and on Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven. And he overthrew those cities and all the valley and all the inhabitants of the cities." The phrase "brimstone and fire" (theion kai pyr, LXX) is the signature of God's judgment on a civilization that had reached the limit of its wickedness. In Psalm 11:6 (LXX 10:6): "Upon the wicked he shall rain snares, fire and brimstone and a burning wind will be the portion of their cup." In Isaiah 30:33: "Its pyre is made deep and large, a burning heap of fire and wood; the breath of the LORD, like a stream of brimstone, kindles it." In Ezekiel 38:22, the judgment on Gog includes "fire and brimstone." The trio of fire, smoke, and brimstone connects the sixth trumpet's judgment to the entire prophetic tradition of divine destruction of the wicked.

"A third of mankind was killed by these three plagues, by the fire and the smoke and the brimstone" (apo ton trion plegon touton apektanthesan to triton ton anthropon, ek tou pyros kai tou kapnou kai tou theiou tou ekporeuomenou ek ton stomaton auton). The word plegon ("plagues") is the same word used in the LXX for the plagues of Egypt (Exodus 11:1, LXX: eti mian plegen). The author explicitly categorizes the three substances as "plagues" — placing them within the exodus-plague framework that has governed the trumpet sequence from the beginning. The fire, the smoke, and the brimstone are three distinct plagues, each contributing to the killing of a third of humanity. The fraction "a third" is maintained: the destruction is massive but not total.

"For the power of the horses is in their mouths and in their tails; for their tails are like serpents and have heads, and with them they do harm" (hai gar exousiai ton hippon en to stomati auton eisin kai en tais ourais auton; hai gar ourai auton homoiai ophesin, echousai kephalas, kai en autais adikousin). The horses inflict harm from both ends: their mouths breathe fire, smoke, and brimstone, and their tails are serpent-like, with heads of their own that do harm. The serpent (ophis) has been a figure of danger and deception since Genesis 3:1. In Numbers 21:6, the LORD sent "fiery serpents" (tous opheis tous thanatountas, LXX) among the people as punishment for their complaining, and the serpents bit the people and many died. In this document, the serpent will be identified explicitly as "the serpent of old who is called the devil and Satan" (12:9; 20:2). The serpent-tails suggest that the destructive power of this cavalry operates through both direct assault (fire from the mouths) and insidious harm (the serpent-tails that strike from behind). The double source of power — mouths and tails — creates a force from which there is no safe approach.

The verb adikousin ("they do harm, they act unjustly") is the same verb used throughout the chapter for the locusts' power to hurt (9:4, 10). The word links the two woe-visions: both the locusts and the cavalry are agents of adikia — harm, injustice, destructive action — operating under divine authorization against those who dwell on the earth.

Verses 20-21: The Refusal to Repent

"The rest of mankind, who were not killed by these plagues, did not repent of the works of their hands, so as not to worship demons, and the idols of gold and of silver and of brass and of stone and of wood, which can neither see nor hear nor walk; and they did not repent of their murders nor of their sorceries nor of their immorality nor of their thefts."

"The rest of mankind, who were not killed by these plagues, did not repent" (hoi loipoi ton anthropon, hoi ouk apektanthesan en tais plegais tautais, ou metenoesan). These two verses are the theological climax of the chapter. Everything that precedes — the opening of the abyss, the locust torment, the cavalry of fire and brimstone, the killing of a third of humanity — leads to this moment. The question implicit in the entire trumpet sequence is: will the judgments produce repentance? The answer is stated with devastating brevity: ou metenoesan — "they did not repent."

The word metanoeo ("to repent, to change one's mind, to turn") is a key word in this document. In the seven messages, the risen Christ repeatedly called the assemblies to repent: Ephesus must repent or lose its lampstand (2:5); Pergamum must repent of tolerating false teaching (2:16); Thyatira is given time to repent (2:21); Sardis must repent (3:3); Laodicea must repent (3:19). Repentance is the appropriate response to divine warning and judgment. The earth-dwellers' refusal to repent stands in direct contrast to the repentance demanded of the assemblies. The assemblies are called to repent of specific failures of faithfulness; the earth-dwellers refuse to repent despite the most severe conceivable warning.

The parallel to the Egyptian plagues is exact. In Exodus 7-12, God sent plague after escalating plague upon Egypt, and after each plague Pharaoh's heart was hardened. The Hebrew text alternates between Pharaoh hardening his own heart (Exodus 8:15, 32; 9:34) and God hardening Pharaoh's heart (Exodus 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10). The result was the same: despite the devastating evidence of God's power and judgment, Pharaoh refused to yield. In Exodus 10:3, God asks through Moses: "How long will you refuse to humble yourself before me?" The survivors of the trumpet plagues face the same question. The judgments are, in effect, God's demand that the earth-dwellers humble themselves and abandon their idolatry. The refusal mirrors Pharaoh's: obstinate, persistent, irrational in the face of overwhelming evidence.

"Did not repent of the works of their hands" (ou metenoesan ek ton ergon ton cheiron auton). The phrase "the works of their hands" (ta erga ton cheiron auton) is the standard prophetic designation for idols in the Hebrew scriptures. Deuteronomy 4:28: "There you will serve gods, the work of human hands (erga cheiron anthropon, LXX), wood and stone, which neither see nor hear nor eat nor smell." 2 Kings 19:18 (LXX 4 Kingdoms 19:18): "They have cast their gods into the fire, for they were not gods but the work of human hands (erga cheiron anthropon), wood and stone." Psalm 115:4 (LXX 113:12): "Their idols are silver and gold, the work of human hands (erga cheiron anthropon)." Psalm 135:15 (LXX 134:15): "The idols of the nations are silver and gold, the work of human hands (erga cheiron anthropon)." Isaiah 2:8: "Their land has also been filled with idols; they worship the work of their hands (ta erga ton cheiron auton), that which their fingers have made." Jeremiah 1:16: "I will pronounce my judgments on them concerning all their wickedness in forsaking me, and in burning incense to other gods and in worshipping the works of their own hands (tois ergois ton cheiron auton)."

The phrase is prophetic shorthand for idolatry — the worship of things humans have manufactured. The prophetic critique is consistent and devastating: the idol is the product of the worshipper's own labor, yet the worshipper treats it as a power greater than himself. The inversion is absurd: the maker worships the made thing.

"So as not to worship demons, and the idols of gold and of silver and of brass and of stone and of wood" (hina me proskynesousin ta daimonia kai ta eidola ta chrysa kai ta argyra kai ta chalka kai ta lithina kai ta xylina). The author adds an interpretive layer that the Hebrew prophets occasionally made explicit: behind the idols stand demons. Deuteronomy 32:17: "They sacrificed to demons (daimoniois, LXX) who were not God, to gods whom they have not known." Psalm 106:37 (LXX 105:37): "They sacrificed their sons and their daughters to the demons (tois daimoniois)." 1 Corinthians 10:20, in a text likely known to the assemblies: "The things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons (daimoniois) and not to God." The worship of idols is not merely a cognitive error — a mistake about the nature of the statue. It involves actual spiritual engagement with hostile powers. The earth-dwellers are not simply confused; they are entangled with demonic forces.

The catalogue of idol materials — gold, silver, brass, stone, and wood — follows a descending order from most precious to least valuable. A nearly identical list appears in Daniel 5:4, 23, describing the idolatrous feast of Belshazzar: "They drank the wine and praised the gods of gold and silver, of bronze, iron, wood, and stone." In Daniel 5, Belshazzar's feast ended with the handwriting on the wall and his kingdom's fall that very night. The echo of Belshazzar's materials reinforces the Danielic framework: like Belshazzar, the earth-dwellers persist in idolatry even as divine judgment closes in.

"Which can neither see nor hear nor walk" (ha oute blepein dynantai oute akouein oute peripatein). This phrase recapitulates the prophetic polemic against idols with devastating precision. Psalm 115:5-7 (LXX 113:13-15): "They have mouths, but they do not speak; they have eyes, but they do not see. They have ears, but they do not hear; they have noses, but they do not smell. They have hands, but they do not feel; they have feet, but they do not walk; they make no sound in their throat." Deuteronomy 4:28: "gods... which neither see nor hear nor eat nor smell." Isaiah 46:7: "They lift it to their shoulders and carry it; they set it in its place, and it stands there; it does not move from its place. Though one cries out to it, it does not answer; it cannot deliver him from his distress." The idols cannot see the worshipper's need, cannot hear the worshipper's prayer, cannot walk to the worshipper's aid. They are inert, powerless, dead things worshipped by those who refuse to worship the living God.

The contrast with the God who sits on the throne in chapters 4-5 is total. The God of this document has eyes "like a flame of fire" (1:14; 2:18) that see everything. He has ears that receive the prayers of the saints (5:8; 8:3-4). He acts — he comes, he judges, he rewards, he creates anew. The idols can do none of these things. The absurdity of the earth-dwellers' choice is exposed: they have witnessed the power of the living God displayed in plague after plague and still cling to objects that are incapable of any response whatsoever.

"And they did not repent of their murders nor of their sorceries nor of their immorality nor of their thefts" (kai ou metenoesan ek ton phonon auton oute ek ton pharmakon auton oute ek tes porneias auton oute ek ton klemmaton auton). The double refusal to repent — stated in verse 20 regarding idolatry and restated in verse 21 regarding moral conduct — creates a comprehensive catalogue of the earth-dwellers' sins. The four sins listed in verse 21 — murders (phonoi), sorceries (pharmaka), immorality (porneia), and thefts (klemmata) — correspond roughly to violations of the second table of the Decalogue: "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13), the prohibition of sorcery (Exodus 22:18; Deuteronomy 18:10-11), "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14), and "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15).

The word pharmaka ("sorceries") derives from pharmakon, which means "drug, potion, poison" and by extension "sorcery, magic." In the LXX, pharmakon and its cognates are associated with magical practices, potions, and occult manipulation. In Exodus 7:11, 22; 8:7 (LXX 8:3), Pharaoh's magicians (pharmakoi, LXX of Exodus 7:11) replicated Moses's signs through their "secret arts." In Galatians 5:20, pharmakeia appears in a list of "works of the flesh." In Revelation 18:23, Babylon's sorcery (pharmakeia) is the means by which "all the nations were deceived." The word carries associations of deception, manipulation, and the use of substances and rituals to access or manipulate spiritual powers apart from God.

The word porneia ("immorality, sexual immorality, fornication") functions throughout this document on two levels. Literally, it refers to sexual conduct outside the boundaries established by the Torah. Figuratively, it represents idolatrous unfaithfulness — the metaphor used by Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel for Israel's unfaithfulness to God (Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 3:1-10; Ezekiel 16, 23). In this document, the two senses merge: the "great harlot" of chapters 17-18 embodies both literal sexual exploitation and idolatrous seduction. The earth-dwellers' porneia is their comprehensive unfaithfulness — sexual, spiritual, and political — to the one who sits on the throne.

The structure of verses 20-21 is chiastic in theme: the outer frame is religious (idolatry, verse 20; sorcery, included in verse 21 as a religious practice), and the inner core is ethical (murders, immorality, thefts). The two dimensions — worship and conduct — are presented as inseparable. Wrong worship produces wrong behavior. The refusal to repent of idolatry is bound to the refusal to repent of violence, sexual exploitation, and theft. The prophetic tradition consistently linked these: Hosea 4:1-2: "There is no faithfulness or steadfast love, and no knowledge of God in the land; there is swearing, deception, murder, stealing, and adultery. They employ violence, and bloodshed follows bloodshed." Jeremiah 7:9: "Will you steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, offer sacrifices to Baal, and walk after other gods that you have not known?" The pairing of idolatry with ethical violation is not a secondary observation but the prophets' central diagnosis: the root sin (wrong worship) produces the fruit of sin (wrong conduct).

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. Chapter 9 delivers the first two of the three woes announced by the eagle in 8:13. The shift from the first four trumpets (which struck the natural world) to the fifth and sixth trumpets (which strike humanity directly) represents a deliberate escalation. God's preliminary judgments on creation did not produce repentance; the woe-trumpets now fall on the earth-dwellers themselves. The fifth trumpet torments without killing; the sixth trumpet kills a third of humanity. The intensification is purposeful, moving from warning to devastation.

2. The divine passive edothe ("was given") continues to govern every destructive action in the chapter. The key to the abyss was given to the star (9:1). Power was given to the locusts (9:3). They were told what they could and could not do (9:4-5). The four angels were released by a command from the golden altar (9:13-14). The four angels were prepared for an exact moment (9:15). Every agent of destruction in this chapter operates under divine authorization, within divine limits, and according to divine timing. The chain of authority running from the throne through the Lamb through the scroll through the trumpets to the agents of judgment remains unbroken.

3. The fifth trumpet draws extensively on the locust-army imagery of Joel 1-2 and the plague of locusts in Exodus 10. Joel described a locust invasion as the Day of the LORD and identified the locust horde as God's own army: "The LORD utters his voice before his army" (Joel 2:11); "My great army which I sent among you" (Joel 2:25). The author of Revelation transforms Joel's imagery: the locusts emerge not from the natural world but from the abyss, they are forbidden from harming vegetation (their natural target) and instead torment humans, and they are equipped with scorpion stings rather than devouring mouths. The transformation elevates the vision from natural disaster to supernatural judgment while maintaining the Joeline framework of the locust army as a divine instrument.

4. The protection of the sealed (9:4) connects the trumpet judgments directly to the sealing vision of chapter 7. The 144,000 sealed servants of God were marked before the judgments began; now, the locusts are explicitly prohibited from harming those who bear God's seal. The pattern replicates the exodus: as the blood on the doorposts protected Israel's firstborn from the destroyer (Exodus 12:13), and as the mark on the foreheads protected the righteous from the destroying angels in Ezekiel 9:4-6, so the seal of God protects the faithful through the trumpet judgments. The sealed are not removed from the scene of judgment; they are protected within it.

5. The five-month duration (9:5, 10) reinforces the bounded, temporary nature of the judgment. The five months correspond to the natural life cycle of locusts in the Mediterranean world. The judgment is severe but has a divinely imposed expiration. The prohibition against killing (9:5) further limits the torment: it afflicts but does not destroy. The sufferers desire death but are denied it (9:6), suggesting that the purpose of the torment is not annihilation but the creation of conditions under which repentance might occur.

6. The composite description of the locusts (9:7-10) defies literal interpretation and demands symbolic reading. The creatures combine features of horses, humans, women, lions, scorpions, and insects into a single form that violates every natural category. The accumulation of fearsome attributes — armor, lion teeth, scorpion stings, cavalry sound — constructs a vision of overwhelming, inescapable spiritual torment. The author signals through the genre (esemanen, 1:1 — the revelation is "signified," communicated through signs) that these are symbolic representations of realities that exceed the capacity of literal description.

7. The name Abaddon/Apollyon (9:11) identifies the king of the locust army as "Destruction" personified. In the Hebrew scriptures, Abaddon is a synonym for Sheol — the realm of the dead (Job 26:6; 28:22; Psalm 88:11; Proverbs 15:11; 27:20). The Greek name Apollyon ("Destroyer") carries a phonetic echo of Apollo (Apollon), the patron deity of the Roman emperors and a god associated with plagues in Greek tradition. For an audience in the Roman province of Asia, where Apollo was prominently worshipped, the resonance would suggest that the divine patron of the imperial order is, in truth, the ruler of the abyss — the king of destructive forces, not a heavenly protector.

8. The sixth trumpet's command originates from the golden altar (9:13), linking it directly to the prayers of the saints (8:3-5) and the martyrs' cry (6:9-10). The same altar that received the prayers now issues the command for judgment. The prayers of the suffering faithful are not decorative but operative: they participate in the heavenly process that produces earthly judgment. The cry "How long?" is being answered through the trumpet sequence.

9. The Euphrates (9:14) activates the full weight of Israel's historical memory and the first-century geopolitical imagination. In Israel's scriptures, the Euphrates marks the boundary beyond which the great destroying empires gathered: Assyria, Babylon, Persia (Genesis 15:18; Isaiah 7:20; 8:7-8). For a first-century audience, the Euphrates was the Roman Empire's eastern frontier, beyond which lay the Parthian threat. The four angels bound at the Euphrates represent destructive forces held in check at the boundary of the known world, now released by divine command. The imagery is simultaneously scriptural and contemporary: the author deploys Israel's historical geography to describe a judgment that transcends any single historical moment.

10. The cavalry of 200 million (9:16-19) exceeds any conceivable human army. The number surpasses the population of the ancient world. The horses breathe fire, smoke, and brimstone — the traditional instruments of divine judgment associated with Sodom's destruction (Genesis 19:24). The riders wear the colors of the destruction they carry: fire-red, smoke-blue, brimstone-yellow. The serpent-tails echo the serpent of Genesis 3 and the fiery serpents of Numbers 21:6. The imagery combines military terror with the full vocabulary of divine wrath, producing a vision of judgment that is both militaristic and supernatural.

11. The refusal to repent (9:20-21) is the theological center of the chapter. Everything that precedes it — the torment of the locusts, the killing of a third of humanity — serves a purpose that is now revealed by its failure. The judgments were designed to produce repentance, and the survivors refuse to repent. The pattern replicates Pharaoh's response to the Egyptian plagues: escalating judgment met by unyielding obstinacy. The sins the survivors cling to are comprehensive: idolatry (the root) and murders, sorceries, immorality, and thefts (the fruit). The prophetic tradition (Hosea 4:1-2; Jeremiah 7:9) consistently presented wrong worship and wrong conduct as inseparable. The catalogue of idol materials (gold, silver, brass, stone, wood) echoes Daniel 5:4, 23 — the feast of Belshazzar, who praised dead idols on the night his kingdom fell. The idols "can neither see nor hear nor walk" (Psalm 115:5-7; Deuteronomy 4:28), a withering prophetic polemic that exposes the absurdity of preferring inert objects to the living God whose power has just been demonstrated in plague after plague.

12. The chapter's position within the document's larger argument reinforces the conditional and urgent nature of the assemblies' situation. The earth-dwellers refuse to repent despite the most severe judgments. The assemblies, by contrast, are called to repent (2:5, 16, 21-22; 3:3, 19) while the opportunity exists. The contrast between the two groups — those who hear and repent, those who experience judgment and do not — is the document's central pastoral concern. The assemblies are being shown what happens to those who persist in idolatry and unfaithfulness, and the vision functions as both warning and exhortation: repent now, while the risen Christ still walks among the lampstands and offers the promise to those who conquer. The refusal to repent in 9:20-21 is the dark counterpart to the repentance the messages demand. The document does not assume that its audience will automatically be among the saved; it places the choice before them with unflinching clarity.
