Acts 6 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 5): The narrative of Acts 5 operates on two tracks — internal integrity and external opposition — and both intensify. Internally, the Ananias and Sapphira episode (5:1-11) demonstrated that the community's shared life, described in the ideal terms of 4:32-37, could be counterfeited: a couple sold property and secretly withheld a portion while presenting the gift as the full amount. Peter's confrontation — "you have not lied to men but to God" (5:4) — and the sudden death of both husband and wife established that the Holy Spirit's presence within the community was not merely empowering but also discerning and judging. "Great fear came upon all the church, and upon as many as heard these things" (5:11). Externally, the apostles continued performing "many signs and wonders among the people" in Solomon's Portico (5:12), and "believers were the more added to the Lord, multitudes both of men and women" (5:14). This growth provoked the Sadducean leadership to arrest all the apostles (5:17-18) — their second arrest, escalating from the two arrested in chapter 4 to the whole group. An angel released them from prison (5:19), and they resumed teaching in the temple (5:21). When brought before the Council again, the high priest charged them: "ye have filled Jerusalem with your doctrine, and intend to bring this man's blood upon us" (5:28). Peter's response sharpened the earlier declaration of 4:19: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (5:29). He then rehearsed the core proclamation: God raised Jesus, whom "ye slew and hanged on a tree," and exalted him as "a Prince and a Saviour, for to give repentance to Israel, and forgiveness of sins" (5:30-31). The response within the Council was murderous ("they were cut to the heart, and took counsel to slay them," 5:33), but was restrained by Gamaliel's counsel — a Pharisee who argued that if this movement is from God, "ye cannot overthrow it; lest haply ye be found even to fight against God" (5:39). The apostles were beaten and commanded to stop speaking in Jesus's name, but "departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (5:41). The chapter closes: "And daily in the temple, and in every house, they ceased not to teach and preach Jesus Christ" (5:42).

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 7): Stephen's speech before the Council is a sustained retelling of Israel's history from Abraham through Moses to Solomon, structured around a recurring pattern: God sends an agent of deliverance, and Israel rejects that agent. The patriarchs sold Joseph into Egypt, but "God was with him" (7:9). Moses was rejected the first time he tried to help his brethren — "Who made thee a ruler and a judge over us?" (7:27) — but God sent him back as "a ruler and a deliverer" (7:35). In the wilderness, "our fathers would not obey, but thrust him from them, and in their hearts turned back again into Egypt" (7:39), making the golden calf while Moses was on Sinai receiving "the lively oracles" (7:38). Stephen then traced the tabernacle and temple history, culminating in the declaration that "the most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands" (7:48), supported by Isaiah 66:1-2. The speech reached its climax with a direct accusation: "Ye stiffnecked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye do always resist the Holy Ghost: as your fathers did, so do ye" (7:51). "Which of the prophets have not your fathers persecuted?" (7:52). The Council's response was fury: they "were cut to the heart, and they gnashed on him with their teeth" (7:54). Stephen, "full of the Holy Ghost," saw "the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God" (7:55-56). They stoned him. The witnesses "laid down their clothes at a young man's feet, whose name was Saul" (7:58).

How chapter 6 connects these: Acts 6 is the hinge between the community's internal life under apostolic leadership (chapters 2-5) and the explosive confrontation between Stephen and the Council (chapter 7) that will fracture the movement outward from Jerusalem. The chapter traces a path from an internal administrative problem (the Hellenist complaint about food distribution) through an organizational solution (the appointment of the Seven) to the emergence of Stephen as a figure whose Spirit-empowered speech provokes external opposition, arrest, and a hearing before the same Council that has already twice confronted the apostles. The transition is seamless: the community's growth creates a practical strain (v. 1), the strain is resolved by delegating responsibility (vv. 2-6), the resolution enables further growth (v. 7), and the new leaders — particularly Stephen — take up ministry that extends beyond table-service into public proclamation, miracles, and disputation (vv. 8-10), which brings them into conflict with Hellenistic synagogues and ultimately the Council (vv. 11-15). The chapter also serves as the narrative bridge between Jerusalem-centered growth and the geographic expansion that begins after Stephen's death, when "they that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word" (8:4) and Philip — the second of the Seven named in 6:5 — carries the message to Samaria (8:5). Everything in this chapter prepares for two consequences: the death that closes chapter 7 and the scattering that opens chapter 8.

Verses 1-2: The Complaint and the Crisis of Growth

"Now at this time, as the disciples were increasing in number, a complaint developed on the part of the Hellenistic Jews against the native Hebrews, because their widows were being overlooked in the daily serving of food" (6:1).

The phrase "at this time" (en de tais hemerais tautais, "in these days") situates the episode within the period described at the end of chapter 5: the apostles teaching daily in the temple and in every house, the community continuing to grow despite the Council's opposition and beatings. The opening clause — "as the disciples were increasing in number" (plethynonton ton matheton) — identifies the cause of the problem before stating the problem. The verb plethyno ("to multiply, to increase") is used in the LXX for the multiplication of Israel in Egypt: "the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied" (Exodus 1:7, LXX: eplethynan). The author's word choice places the community's growth in the same category as Israel's growth before the Exodus — a people multiplying under God's blessing, and multiplication creating new challenges.

The word for "complaint" is goggysmos, and it is loaded with scriptural resonance. In the LXX, the verb goggyzo and its noun goggysmos are the distinctive vocabulary of Israel's murmuring in the wilderness. Israel murmured against Moses and Aaron about food: "the whole congregation of the children of Israel murmured against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness" when they wanted bread and meat (Exodus 16:2, LXX: diegoggysen... epi Mousen kai Aaron). They murmured at Marah over bitter water (Exodus 15:24). They murmured at Rephidim over lack of water (Exodus 17:3). They murmured at the report of the spies (Numbers 14:2). In every instance, the murmuring was directed at Moses, the leader responsible for the community's provision, and it concerned material needs — food, water, survival. The author's choice of goggysmos for the Hellenists' complaint is not incidental. He is signaling that the community of disciples is experiencing the same kind of crisis that Israel experienced in the wilderness: a growing people, a shortage in daily provision, and a complaint directed at the community's leaders.

The division is between "the Hellenistic Jews" (ton Helleniston) and "the native Hebrews" (tous Hebraious). Both groups are Jewish. The Hellenists (Hellenistai) are Greek-speaking Jews, most likely from the diaspora — Jews who grew up in cities like Alexandria, Cyrene, Antioch, or the cities of Asia Minor where Greek was the primary language and Hellenistic culture the dominant environment. The Hebrews (Hebraioi) are Aramaic- and Hebrew-speaking Jews native to Palestine. The division is linguistic and cultural, not ethnic or theological. Both groups are disciples (mathetai). Both belong to the community. But the cultural and linguistic gap between a Greek-speaking Jew from Alexandria and an Aramaic-speaking Jew from Galilee would have been significant — different primary languages, different cultural assumptions, different synagogue traditions, different levels of engagement with the Greek intellectual world.

The specific complaint is that "their widows were being overlooked in the daily serving of food" (paretheorunto en te diakonia te kathemerine hai cherai auton). The verb paratheoreo means "to overlook, to neglect, to pass over." The imperfect passive (paretheorunto) indicates that this was not a single incident but an ongoing pattern: the Hellenist widows were being consistently neglected. "The daily serving" (te diakonia te kathemerine) refers to a regular, organized distribution of food — a daily practice (kathemerine, "daily, day by day") of communal provision. This connects directly to the earlier descriptions of communal life: "distribution was made unto every man according as he had need" (4:35) and "breaking bread from house to house, did eat their meat with gladness and singleness of heart" (2:46). The idealized community of chapters 2 and 4, where "neither was there any among them that lacked" (4:34), is now experiencing a fracture along cultural lines. The ideal has not been abandoned, but its execution is failing.

The widows are the specific victims. In Israelite law and prophetic tradition, widows (along with orphans and foreigners) were the paradigmatic vulnerable population. Deuteronomy 10:18: God "doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow." Deuteronomy 24:17-21 mandated provisions for the widow in the harvest. Isaiah 1:17: "judge the fatherless, plead for the widow." James 1:27 (later): "Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction." A community that neglects its widows is failing one of the most fundamental obligations of Israelite covenant faithfulness. The author's specification that the neglected parties are widows heightens the seriousness of the failure: this is not merely an administrative glitch; it is a violation of the community's covenant identity.

"So the twelve summoned the congregation of the disciples and said, 'It is not desirable for us to neglect the word of God in order to serve tables'" (6:2).

"The twelve" (hoi dodeka) — this is the first time in Acts that the apostles are referred to collectively by this numerical designation since the restoration of the number in 1:26 with the selection of Matthias. The number itself carries weight: twelve corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel. The Twelve function as the leadership of the reconstituted Israel, the new community gathered around the risen Jesus. Their action here is corporate: they summon the whole assembly.

"The congregation of the disciples" (to plethos ton matheton) — the word plethos means "the full number, the whole body, the assembly." This is a formal gathering of the entire community, not a meeting of a leadership committee. The decision-making process is communal. The Twelve propose; the congregation decides.

The Twelve's statement — "It is not desirable for us to neglect the word of God in order to serve tables" (ouk areston estin hemas kataleipsantas ton logon tou theou diakonein trapezais) — establishes a practical distinction, not a hierarchical one. The verb kataleipo means "to leave behind, to abandon, to forsake." The Twelve are saying that they cannot abandon the proclamation of God's word in order to manage the food distribution. The word areston ("desirable, pleasing, fitting") indicates that this is a matter of proper order, not of the inherent worth of the tasks. The issue is not that serving tables is beneath them; it is that attempting to do everything will result in doing nothing well.

The parallel to the Exodus narrative is striking and almost certainly deliberate. In Exodus 18:13-26, Jethro observed Moses sitting as the sole judge of the people "from morning unto evening" (Exodus 18:13) and told him: "The thing that thou doest is not good. Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone" (Exodus 18:17-18). The solution was delegation: "Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them" (Exodus 18:21). Moses retained the major matters; the appointed leaders handled the daily administration. The structural logic is identical in Acts 6: the leaders cannot bear the whole burden; the community must select qualified people; the leaders retain their primary task (word and prayer) while the selected ones handle the practical administration.

An even closer parallel exists in Numbers 11:10-17, where Moses complained to God: "I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me" (Numbers 11:14). God's response: "Gather unto me seventy men of the elders of Israel... and I will take of the spirit which is upon thee, and will put it upon them; and they shall bear the burden of the people with thee, that thou bear it not thyself alone" (Numbers 11:16-17). In that case, the Spirit was explicitly shared — distributed from Moses to the seventy. In Acts 6, the criterion for those selected is that they be "full of the Spirit" (v. 3). The Spirit is already upon them before they are chosen, but the pattern of sharing the burden of leadership and the Spirit's role in equipping those who share it is consistent.

The author is constructing the early community as the new Israel in its wilderness phase, and the apostles as operating in a role analogous to Moses — overwhelmed by the burden of a growing people, guided to delegate, and enabled by the Spirit's distribution among qualified leaders.

Verses 3-6: The Selection and Commissioning of the Seven

"Instead, brothers and sisters, select from among you seven men of good reputation, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom we may put in charge of this task" (6:3).

The verb "select" (episkepsasthe) is a second-person plural imperative: "you [all] select." The Twelve do not choose the seven; they instruct the congregation to do so. The authority to propose the structure comes from the apostles; the authority to choose the individuals comes from the community. This two-part process — apostolic design, communal election — reflects a pattern of shared governance.

The criteria the Twelve establish are threefold. First, "of good reputation" (martyroumenous, literally "being witnessed to, attested, having a good testimony from others"). These must be men whose character is recognized by those around them — people who can vouch for them. Second, "full of the Spirit" (plereis pneumatos). The same quality that marks the apostles (2:4, 4:8, 4:31) must mark these leaders. The Spirit's presence is not an optional enhancement; it is a prerequisite for service. Third, "of wisdom" (kai sophias, "and of wisdom"). Practical wisdom — the ability to make sound judgments, to administer fairly, to navigate the very kind of cultural tension that has created the current crisis. The combination of spiritual fullness and practical wisdom echoes the description of Bezalel, whom God filled "with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge" to oversee the construction of the tabernacle (Exodus 31:3, 35:31).

"Whom we may put in charge of this task" (hous katastesomen epi tes chreias tautes) — the verb kathistemi means "to appoint, to set in place, to establish in a position." The Twelve will formalize the appointment; the community chooses the people. The word chreia ("need, task, necessity") is practical — this is about meeting a concrete need, not creating an abstract office.

The number seven has significance in Jewish communal organization. Josephus records that Moses appointed seven magistrates in every city (Antiquities 4.214), and the Mishnah later describes boards of seven for town administration (Megillah 1:3). The number is not arbitrary; it reflects an established pattern of civic governance in Jewish communities. The author may be signaling that the community is organizing itself along the lines of a recognized Jewish civic structure.

"But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (6:4).

The Twelve define their own role: prayer (proseuche) and "the ministry of the word" (te diakonia tou logou). The word diakonia ("service, ministry") appears in both verse 1 and verse 4, but with different objects. In verse 1, it is "the daily serving" (te diakonia te kathemerine) — the food distribution. In verse 4, it is "the ministry of the word" (te diakonia tou logou) — the proclamation of God's message. The same root (diakon-) is used for both activities. The author is not establishing a hierarchy of sacred versus secular work; he is distinguishing between two forms of diakonia, both necessary, both legitimate, requiring different people so that neither is neglected. The verb proskartero ("to devote oneself to, to persist in, to attend constantly to") is the same word used in 1:14 ("these all continued with one accord in prayer") and 2:42 ("they continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship"). It denotes persistent, focused commitment.

"The announcement found approval with the whole congregation; and they chose Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit, and Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolas, a proselyte from Antioch" (6:5).

"Found approval with the whole congregation" (eresen... enopion pantos tou plethous) — the proposal pleased everyone. The word plethos ("the whole body") again emphasizes that this is a communal decision. There is no recorded dissent. The Hellenists' complaint is being addressed, and the solution satisfies the entire assembly.

The names are significant. All seven names are Greek: Stephanos, Philippos, Prochoros, Nikanor, Timon, Parmenas, Nikolaos. While it was common for Palestinian Jews to carry both a Hebrew and a Greek name, the author records only the Greek names. This strongly suggests that all seven are from the Hellenist community — the very community that raised the complaint. The apostles do not merely address the grievance; they give the aggrieved party full responsibility over the distribution. The people who were being overlooked are now the people managing the provision. This is not a token gesture of inclusion; it is a transfer of operational authority to those who experienced the failure.

Stephen is named first and described individually: "a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit" (andra plere pisteos kai pneumatos hagiou). The word pleres ("full") has already appeared in the criteria (v. 3: "full of the Spirit and of wisdom"). Stephen not only meets the criteria but exceeds them — he is full of faith as well. This introductory description marks him as the most prominent of the seven and prepares the reader for his emergence as a major figure in the verses that follow.

Nicolas is identified last and with a distinctive qualifier: "a proselyte from Antioch" (proseluton Antiochea). A proselyte (proselytos) is a Gentile convert to Judaism — not born Jewish but having undergone circumcision and full incorporation into the Jewish community. Nicolas is the outsider among outsiders: not merely a diaspora Jew, but a Gentile who became a Jew, from Antioch — a major Hellenistic city in Syria. His inclusion among the Seven signals the breadth of the community's membership. The mention of Antioch also foreshadows: Antioch will become the base of operations for the Gentile mission (Acts 11:19-26, 13:1-3). A proselyte from Antioch serving in the Jerusalem community anticipates the trajectory that will carry the message from Jerusalem to Antioch and beyond.

"And they brought these men before the apostles; and after praying, they laid their hands on them" (6:6).

The laying on of hands (epithesis ton cheiron) is a well-established practice in the Hebrew Bible for commissioning, transferring authority, and consecrating for service. Moses laid hands on Joshua to commission him as his successor: "And he laid his hands upon him, and gave him a charge" (Numbers 27:23; cf. Deuteronomy 34:9, where Joshua is "full of the spirit of wisdom; for Moses had laid his hands upon him"). The act has precedent in the transfer of blessing (Genesis 48:14, Jacob blessing Ephraim and Manasseh) and in the consecration of the Levites (Numbers 8:10, where "the children of Israel shall put their hands upon the Levites"). The apostles' laying on of hands connects the Seven's appointment to this tradition of formal commissioning: they are being publicly authorized for their task, and the authority of the apostles stands behind them.

The prayer (proseuxamenoi) before the laying on of hands indicates that the commissioning is not merely a human transaction but a seeking of divine endorsement. The apostles pray first, then lay on hands — the act of commissioning is wrapped in dependence on God.

Verse 7: The Third Growth Summary

"The word of God kept spreading; and the number of the disciples continued to increase greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests were becoming obedient to the faith" (6:7).

This verse is one of the periodic summary statements that punctuate the narrative of Acts (cf. 2:47, 5:14, 9:31, 12:24, 16:5, 19:20). These summaries function as narrative hinges, marking the end of one phase and the transition to the next. This summary is the most emphatic yet. The author makes three claims, each escalating in significance.

First: "the word of God kept spreading" (ho logos tou theou euxanen, literally "the word of God was growing"). The verb auxano means "to grow, to increase." The author personifies the word as something that grows like a living organism — a seed, a plant, an expanding reality. This is consistent with the parable of the sower (Luke 8:4-15), where the word is the seed that produces growth. The word is not merely repeated; it grows. Its scope widens, its impact deepens, its reach extends.

Second: "the number of the disciples continued to increase greatly in Jerusalem" (eplethyneto ho arithmos ton matheton en Ierousalem sphodra). The verb plethyno ("to multiply") is the same word used in verse 1 for the growth that caused the problem. The problem has been solved, and the growth continues — indeed, accelerates. The adverb sphodra ("greatly, exceedingly, enormously") intensifies the growth beyond anything previously described. The resolution of internal conflict has not slowed the community; it has unleashed it.

Third, and most striking: "a great many of the priests were becoming obedient to the faith" (polys te ochlos ton hiereon hypekouon te pistei). This is a remarkable claim. The priests (hiereis) are the functionaries of the temple system. They serve in the daily sacrifices, maintain the sacred precincts, and occupy positions within the institutional structure of second-temple Judaism. "A great many" (polys ochlos, literally "a great crowd") of them are now "becoming obedient to the faith" (hypekouon te pistei). The verb hypakouon ("to obey, to submit, to respond to") is in the imperfect tense, indicating an ongoing process — priests were continuously, progressively responding. The dative "to the faith" (te pistei) treats "the faith" as a body of conviction and commitment that demands response — not merely intellectual assent but obedience, a reorientation of life.

The placement of this detail is significant. It sits between the appointment of the Seven and the arrest of Stephen. The charge against Stephen will be that he speaks "against this holy place and the Law" (6:13) — that is, against the temple and against Moses. But the author has just told the reader that the temple's own functionaries, the priests themselves, are joining the movement. The people closest to the temple system are not threatened by the message; they are obeying it. The charge of temple-blasphemy, when it comes, will arrive in a context where the reader already knows that many priests have found the message compelling, not destructive.

Verses 8-10: Stephen's Public Ministry and the Dispute

"And Stephen, full of grace and power, was performing great wonders and signs among the people" (6:8).

Stephen was appointed to manage food distribution (v. 3), but his ministry immediately overflows that assignment. The author describes him in terms that parallel the apostles and, more importantly, parallel Moses. He is "full of grace and power" (pleres charitos kai dynameos). The word pleres ("full") has now been used for Stephen three times in this chapter: full of faith and the Holy Spirit (v. 5), full of the Spirit and wisdom (the criterion of v. 3 that he meets), and now full of grace and power (v. 8). The accumulation of "fullness" language sets Stephen apart as a figure of extraordinary spiritual concentration.

"Great wonders and signs" (terata kai semeia megala) — this phrase directly echoes the description of Moses in Deuteronomy 34:11: "in all the signs and the wonders, which the Lord sent him to do in the land of Egypt." The same phrase is used for what God did "by the hands of the apostles" in Acts 2:43 and 5:12. Stephen is now doing what the apostles do, and what Moses did before them. The author is building a Moses typology around Stephen that will reach its climax in the final verse of the chapter.

"But some men from what was called the Synagogue of the Freedmen, including both Cyrenians and Alexandrians, and some from Cilicia and Asia, rose up and argued with Stephen" (6:9).

The "Synagogue of the Freedmen" (tes synagoges tes legomenes Libertinon) identifies a specific congregation in Jerusalem. The Libertinoi (Latin: libertini, "freedmen") were Jews or descendants of Jews who had been captured and enslaved — likely by Pompey during his conquest of Jerusalem in 63 BCE — and subsequently freed. Freed slaves in the Roman world were called liberti or libertini, and their descendants retained that designation. These freed Jews, or their descendants, had formed their own synagogue in Jerusalem. They came from multiple diaspora locations: Cyrene (in North Africa), Alexandria (in Egypt), Cilicia (in southeast Asia Minor — notably, this is Paul's home region, Acts 21:39, 22:3), and Asia (the Roman province in western Asia Minor). Whether these represent one synagogue with members from all these regions or multiple synagogues is debated by the grammar, but the point is clear: these are Hellenistic Jews from the diaspora, just as Stephen himself is.

This is a crucial detail. The opposition to Stephen does not come from the Aramaic-speaking Palestinian establishment (they will be drawn in later, v. 12). It comes from fellow Hellenists — Greek-speaking diaspora Jews who have their own synagogue(s) in Jerusalem. The conflict is intra-Hellenistic. Stephen, a Hellenist, is debating with other Hellenists. The dispute is not between insiders and outsiders, or between Palestinian and diaspora Judaism. It is between Hellenistic Jews who have embraced Jesus as Messiah and Hellenistic Jews who have not. The battle line runs through the middle of the Hellenistic community itself.

"But they were unable to cope with his wisdom and the Spirit by whom he was speaking" (6:10).

"Unable to cope" (ouk ischyon antostenai) — the verb anthistemi means "to stand against, to resist, to withstand." Combined with the negative, the statement is absolute: they could not withstand him. The reason is twofold: "his wisdom" (te sophia) and "the Spirit by whom he was speaking" (to pneumati ho elalei). Wisdom and Spirit appear together as the criteria for the Seven in verse 3. Stephen possesses exactly what the apostles required, and his opponents cannot overcome it.

This fulfills Jesus's promise in Luke 21:15: "For I will give you a mouth and wisdom, which all your adversaries shall not be able to gainsay nor resist." The verb anthistemi in Acts 6:10 corresponds to the verb anteipon ("gainsay, contradict") and antistenai ("resist") in Luke 21:15. The promise was that Jesus's followers, when brought into conflict, would receive a wisdom that their opponents could not refute. Stephen is the first specific instance of this promise being fulfilled in an individual outside the circle of the Twelve.

Verses 11-14: The False Charges

"Then they secretly induced men to say, 'We have heard him speak blasphemous words against Moses and God'" (6:11).

Unable to defeat Stephen in open argument (v. 10), his opponents shift to covert action. The verb hypoballo ("to secretly induce, to suborn, to put forward surreptitiously") indicates that the men who make the accusation are not speaking from their own observation but have been coached. They are planted witnesses. The author wants the reader to understand from the outset that the charges are manufactured — the product of a conspiracy that arose because honest debate failed.

The accusation is that Stephen speaks "blasphemous words against Moses and God" (rhemata blasphema eis Mousen kai ton theon). The charge is twofold: against Moses and against God. In Israelite law, blasphemy against God was a capital offense (Leviticus 24:10-16). The addition of "against Moses" extends the charge: Stephen is accused not merely of speaking against God but of speaking against the lawgiver himself, the mediator of God's covenant with Israel. The coupling of Moses and God together in the accusation sets up the irony that will pervade the rest of the chapter and all of chapter 7: Stephen is accused of opposing Moses, but Stephen's entire defense will be built on Moses's own story, and Stephen's face will shine like Moses's face.

"And they stirred up the people, the elders, and the scribes, and they came up to him and dragged him away, and brought him before the Council" (6:12).

The verb synkineo ("to stir up, to move together, to agitate") describes the deliberate manipulation of public opinion. The suborned witnesses do not merely make their accusation in a legal proceeding; they use it to agitate three groups: "the people" (ton laon), "the elders" (tous presbyterous), and "the scribes" (tous grammateis). This is the first time in Acts that "the people" (laos) are turned against the movement. Until now, the people have consistently been favorable: "having favour with all the people" (2:47), "the people magnified them" (5:13), the authorities "feared the people" (5:26). The Council's inability to punish the apostles in chapters 4 and 5 was partly due to popular support. Now, for the first time, the people are stirred against a member of the community. This marks a turning point. The popular protection that shielded the apostles is being eroded.

The word synerpasen ("dragged away, seized") indicates violence. Stephen is physically seized and brought before "the Council" (to synedrion) — the Sanhedrin, the same body that tried Jesus (Luke 22:66), confronted Peter and John (Acts 4:5-7), and interrogated all the apostles (Acts 5:27).

"They put forward false witnesses who said, 'This man does not stop speaking against this holy place and the Law'" (6:13).

The author explicitly labels these as "false witnesses" (martyras pseudeis). The reader is not left to decide whether the charges have merit; the author declares them false before stating them. This is the same language used for the trial of Jesus: "the chief priests and all the council sought false witness against Jesus" (Matthew 26:59). The pattern is being replicated: just as the Council used false witnesses against Jesus, they now use false witnesses against Stephen.

The charge is that Stephen "does not stop speaking against this holy place and the Law" (ou pauetai lalon rhemata kata tou topou tou hagiou kai tou nomou). "This holy place" (tou topou tou hagiou) is the temple. "The Law" (tou nomou) is the Torah, the Law of Moses. The charge thus directly parallels the earlier accusation of speaking "against Moses and God" (v. 11), but with more specificity: the temple is the concrete expression of God's presence, and the Law is the concrete expression of Moses's authority. Stephen is accused of a sustained attack (ou pauetai, "he does not stop") on the two pillars of second-temple Jewish identity: the temple as the place of God's dwelling and the Torah as the charter of God's people.

"For we have heard him say that this Nazarene, Jesus, will destroy this place and change the customs which Moses handed down to us" (6:14).

The charge becomes more specific. Stephen is alleged to have said two things. First, "Jesus the Nazarene will destroy this place" (Iesous ho Nazoraios houtos katalysei ton topon touton). The verb katalyo means "to destroy, to tear down, to demolish." "This place" (ton topon touton) is the temple. Second, Jesus will "change the customs which Moses handed down to us" (allaxei ta ethe ha paredoken hemin Moyses). The verb allasso means "to change, to alter, to exchange one thing for another." The "customs" (ethe) are the traditions and practices associated with the Mosaic law.

The author has already told us these are false witnesses (v. 13), but the content of the accusation deserves scrutiny. Jesus did predict the temple's destruction. In Luke 21:5-6, when some spoke of the temple's adornment, Jesus said: "As for these things which ye behold, the days will come, in the which there shall not be left one stone upon another, that shall not be thrown down." The prediction of the temple's destruction is part of the gospel tradition. What makes the witnesses "false" is not necessarily that Stephen never mentioned this topic, but that they distort its meaning and framing. Jesus predicted what God would do to the temple as judgment; the false witnesses recast this as Stephen claiming that Jesus himself will demolish the temple — a very different matter. Predicting divine judgment on an institution is prophetic; claiming that a person will personally destroy the sacred house of God sounds like blasphemous presumption. The distortion turns prophecy into threat.

Similarly, "changing the customs Moses handed down" takes what the movement actually claims — that Jesus fulfills the Law and therefore reframes how it is understood — and distorts it into an accusation of abolition. There is a kernel of reality (the movement does claim that Jesus has inaugurated something new in relation to the Law), but it is framed as a hostile act of destruction rather than a claim of fulfillment.

Verse 15: Stephen's Face

"And all who were sitting in the Council stared at him, and they saw his face, which was like the face of an angel" (6:15).

The verb atenizo ("to gaze intently, to fix one's eyes upon, to stare") indicates sustained, focused attention. Every member of the Council is looking at Stephen's face. The word pantos ("all") ensures the reader understands this is not observed by a few — the entire body sees it.

"They saw his face, which was like the face of an angel" (eidon to prosopon autou hosei prosopon aggelou). The word prosopon ("face") appears twice, and the comparison is to the face of an aggelos ("messenger, angel"). The allusion to Moses is unmistakable and devastating.

When Moses descended from Sinai after receiving the Law, "the skin of his face shone" (Exodus 34:29, LXX: dedoxastai he opsis tou prosopou autou, "the appearance of his face was glorified"). Aaron and the Israelites "were afraid to come nigh him" (Exodus 34:30). Moses had been in God's presence, receiving God's word, and his face bore the visible evidence of that encounter. The shining face was the mark of divine authorization — proof that Moses had been with God and spoke from God.

Stephen is accused of speaking "against Moses and God" (v. 11), "against this holy place and the Law" (v. 13). The charge is that Stephen opposes Moses, opposes God, opposes the temple, opposes the Torah. But in the climactic moment, as Stephen stands before the Council charged with anti-Mosaic blasphemy, God marks his face in the manner of Moses's face. The irony is absolute. The man accused of opposing Moses is confirmed by God in the very way God confirmed Moses. The Council is looking at a Moses-like face and preparing to condemn the man who wears it — just as Israel in the wilderness repeatedly rejected Moses despite the visible evidence of his divine commission.

The word aggelos ("angel, messenger") also carries forward into Stephen's own speech. In Acts 7:38, Stephen will describe Moses as the one "in the church in the wilderness with the angel which spake to him in the mount Sina." In Acts 7:53, he will accuse the Council of having "received the law by the disposition of angels, and have not kept it." The angel who spoke to Moses on Sinai, the angelic face that marks Stephen before the Council, and the angelic mediation of the Law all form a connected web of imagery. Stephen's face announces what his speech will argue: God is present and speaking through him, just as God was present and speaking through Moses, and the Council is about to do to Stephen what their fathers did to Moses — reject the messenger while claiming to honor the message.

The chapter ends here, suspended. Stephen has not spoken yet. The last image is his face — shining, angel-like, before a hostile court. The reader turns to chapter 7 knowing two things: the witnesses are false, and the accused bears the visible mark of God's presence. The question is not whether Stephen has spoken against God; the question is whether the Council will recognize God's messenger when they see one.

Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. The chapter demonstrates that growth produces new challenges that require structural adaptation. The community's increase in numbers (v. 1) created the very conditions for the Hellenist complaint. The author does not present growth as an unmixed blessing; it strains existing structures and exposes cultural fault lines. The response is not to suppress the complaint or slow the growth but to reorganize — to create new leadership structures adequate to the community's actual complexity. The Exodus parallel reinforces this: just as Moses could not bear the burden alone and needed to delegate (Exodus 18, Numbers 11), so the Twelve cannot serve tables and tend the word. Growth demands new structures, and the Spirit works through organizational adaptation, not only through miracles.

2. The wilderness murmuring language (goggysmos) frames the community's experience as a recapitulation of Israel's wilderness phase. The author's choice of this loaded term is deliberate. Israel murmured about food in the wilderness; the Hellenists murmur about food distribution in Jerusalem. Israel's murmuring tested Moses's leadership; the Hellenists' murmuring tests the Twelve's leadership. In both cases, the crisis is resolved through delegation and the sharing of the Spirit's burden among more leaders. The community of disciples is living through its own wilderness period — growing, struggling with provision, reorganizing under inspired leadership, and moving toward what lies ahead.

3. The selection of seven men with Greek names from the complaining community reveals the apostles' approach to internal conflict. They do not merely address the grievance; they empower the aggrieved. Every name is Greek. Nicolas is a proselyte — a Gentile convert. The community that felt overlooked is given operational control of the distribution. This is not conflict suppression but conflict resolution through empowerment. The practical result is that the very people best positioned to understand and correct the Hellenist widows' neglect are the ones charged with the task.

4. The detail that "a great many of the priests were becoming obedient to the faith" (v. 7) pre-undermines the charge that Stephen opposes the temple. The temple's own functionaries are joining the movement. The people most invested in the temple system are not hearing the message as an attack on the temple but as something they can obey. When the false witnesses accuse Stephen of speaking "against this holy place" (v. 13), the reader already knows that the holy place's own servants are finding the faith compelling. The charge is contradicted before it is made.

5. The opposition to Stephen arises from within the Hellenistic Jewish community, not from the Palestinian establishment. The Synagogue of the Freedmen, with members from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, and Asia, consists of diaspora Jews — Hellenists, like Stephen himself. The first sustained persecution of the movement's expansion beyond the apostles comes from people who share the victims' cultural background. The battle line does not fall between insiders and outsiders but cuts through the middle of the Hellenistic Jewish world. This patterns what will happen repeatedly in Acts: in city after city, it is often the synagogue community that is divided by the message, with some embracing it and others opposing it violently.

6. The false-witness structure replicates the trial of Jesus. Suborned witnesses, distorted charges, a hearing before the Council — the pattern from Jesus's trial is being reproduced with Stephen. The author wants the reader to see the continuity: the institutional opposition that killed Jesus now turns on Jesus's followers, using the same methods. Jesus predicted this (Luke 21:12-15), and it is happening. The community is not merely remembering Jesus; it is reliving his experience.

7. The charges against Stephen contain a distorted kernel of truth, and the author's identification of the witnesses as "false" targets the distortion, not the underlying reality. Jesus did predict the temple's destruction (Luke 21:5-6). The movement does claim that Jesus fulfills and therefore reinterprets the Law. What makes the witnesses false is not that Stephen never mentioned these topics but that they reframe prophetic warning as blasphemous threat, and fulfillment as abolition. The false witnesses turn "the temple will be judged" into "Jesus will destroy the temple," and "Jesus fulfills Moses" into "Jesus will change what Moses commanded." The difference between prophecy and blasphemy, between fulfillment and destruction, is the hinge on which the charges turn — and the witnesses are on the wrong side of that hinge.

8. Stephen's shining face (v. 15) is the author's decisive commentary on the charges. Accused of opposing Moses, Stephen is marked by God in the manner of Moses. The allusion to Exodus 34:29-35 is unmistakable: Moses's face shone because he had been in God's presence, receiving God's word. Stephen's face shines because he is full of the Spirit, full of grace and power, and stands in God's presence even while standing before a hostile court. The visual evidence contradicts the verbal accusation. The Council is looking at the proof that God is with Stephen while preparing to condemn him for being against God. This is precisely the pattern Stephen's speech will expose in chapter 7: Israel has always rejected God's chosen agents while claiming to serve God.

9. The chapter introduces the narrative mechanism that will carry the message beyond Jerusalem. The Seven are appointed; Stephen and Philip are the two named with any detail. Stephen's death (chapter 7) and the resulting persecution will scatter the Hellenists (8:1-4), and Philip will be the one who carries the message to Samaria (8:5). Later, the scattering "upon the persecution that arose about Stephen" will reach "as far as Phenice, and Cyprus, and Antioch" (11:19), and "some of them were men of Cyprus and Cyrene, which, when they were come to Antioch, spake unto the Grecians, preaching the Lord Jesus" (11:20). The appointment of the Seven in Acts 6 is the preparation of the instrument through which the promise of Acts 1:8 — "ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth" — begins to be fulfilled. The administrative solution to a food-distribution problem becomes the staging ground for the movement's geographic explosion.

10. The Moses typology that pervades the chapter functions as the theological infrastructure for Stephen's speech in chapter 7. The wilderness murmuring language (v. 1), the delegation of leadership (vv. 2-6), the "wonders and signs" performed by Stephen (v. 8, echoing Moses's wonders and signs in Deuteronomy 34:11 and Acts 7:36), and the shining face (v. 15, echoing Exodus 34:29) all build a sustained identification between the community's experience and Israel's wilderness experience, and between Stephen and Moses. Stephen's speech will make this typology explicit: Moses was the prophet whom Israel rejected, and God has raised up a prophet "like" Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15, cited in Acts 3:22 and 7:37) whom Israel is rejecting again. The chapter is constructed so that by the time Stephen opens his mouth in 7:2, every element of the Moses parallel is already in place. The author has shown the reader what Stephen's speech will argue before Stephen says a word.
