John 3 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (John 2): John 2 narrates two events connected by location, timing, and theological weight. First, at a wedding in Cana of Galilee, Jesus turns water into wine — described as "the beginning of his signs" (archen ton semeion), which "revealed his glory" (ephanerosen ten doxan autou) and produced belief among his disciples (2:11). The water was contained in six stone jars set aside "for the Jewish rites of purification" (kata ton katharismon ton Ioudaion, 2:6). Jesus transforms the water of Jewish purification into wine, and the master of the feast notes that the best wine has been kept until now (2:10). The sign communicates that something superior has arrived to replace what came before — a theme the prologue established with "grace upon grace" (1:16) and "the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (1:17).

Second, Jesus goes up to Jerusalem for Passover and drives the merchants from the temple, declaring "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19). The author notes that "he was speaking of the temple of his body" (2:21). The one who "tabernacled among us" (1:14) confronts the existing temple because he himself is its replacement — the true locus of God's presence.

The chapter then closes with a passage that is the direct antecedent to chapter 3: "Now when he was in Jerusalem at the Passover, during the feast, many believed in his name, observing his signs which he was doing. But Jesus himself was not entrusting himself to them, because he knew all people, and because he had no need for anyone to testify concerning man, for he himself knew what was in man" (2:23-25). This passage creates a category of belief that Jesus does not trust — belief based on observing signs without deeper understanding. The phrase "he knew what was in man" (autos eginosken ti en to anthropo) is the hinge on which the door to chapter 3 swings open.

What the author argues immediately after (John 4): Jesus leaves Judea for Galilee and "had to" (edei) pass through Samaria, where he encounters a Samaritan woman at Jacob's well. The conversation moves from physical water to "living water" (hydor zon, 4:10), just as the chapter 3 conversation moved from physical birth to birth "from above." Jesus tells the woman that "the hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father" (4:21) — continuing the temple-replacement theme from 2:19-21. The woman's misunderstanding of Jesus's offer parallels Nicodemus's misunderstanding of birth from above: both interlocutors hear physical language and miss the spiritual reality. But unlike Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman moves from confusion to testimony, bringing her whole town to encounter Jesus. The Samaritans declare Jesus "the Savior of the world" (4:42) — a title that extends beyond Israel and fulfills 3:16-17's declaration that God sent the Son not to judge the world but to save it. The chapter ends with Jesus returning to Cana and performing his "second sign" (deuteron semeion, 4:54) — healing a royal official's son from a distance, producing belief in the official and "his whole household" (4:53).

How chapter 3 connects these: John 2 ended by establishing that Jesus knows "what is in man." Chapter 3 immediately presents a specific man — "Now there was a man of the Pharisees" (en de anthropos ek ton Pharisaion, 3:1) — who comes to Jesus and receives a diagnosis of what that man lacks. The word anthropos ("man") in 3:1 directly echoes the anthropos of 2:25. Nicodemus is the test case, the specific instance of the general category the author has just introduced. He is one who has seen the signs and concluded something about Jesus, but Jesus knows what is in him — and what is in him is not yet sufficient. What follows in chapter 3 is the author's exposition of what is actually required: not merely observing signs and drawing correct conclusions, but being born from above by water and Spirit, believing in the lifted-up Son of Man, and coming to the light. Chapter 4 will then demonstrate this pattern in action through the Samaritan woman's encounter, extending the scope of the offer from a teacher of Israel (Nicodemus) to a Samaritan outsider, and from there to the whole world.

The chapter also continues the purification/replacement theme. In chapter 2, Jesus replaced purification water with wine and the physical temple with his body. In chapter 3, a dispute arises between John's disciples and a Jew "about purification" (peri katharismou, 3:25), directly echoing the purification jars of 2:6. The old categories of purification are being superseded by what Jesus brings. And the chapter as a whole presents the transition from John's ministry to Jesus's ministry — from the baptism of water to the baptism of Spirit — completing the subordination of the forerunner that chapter 1 began.

Verses 1-2: Nicodemus Comes at Night

"Now there was a man of the Pharisees, named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews. This man came to him at night and said to him, 'Rabbi, we know that you have come from God as a teacher, for no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him.'"

The conjunction de ("now, and") links this passage directly to the preceding statement about Jesus knowing "what was in man" (2:25). The author does not begin a new topic; he provides the first illustration of it. "There was a man" (en de anthropos) — the same word anthropos from 2:25 — signals that Nicodemus is the concrete example of the general principle just stated.

Nicodemus's identity. He is identified three ways: he is "of the Pharisees" (ek ton Pharisaion), his name is Nicodemus (a Greek name, Nikodemos, meaning "conqueror of the people" — common among Hellenized Jews), and he is "a ruler of the Jews" (archon ton Ioudaion). The word archon designates a member of the ruling council, the Sanhedrin. In this document, "the Jews" (hoi Ioudaioi) typically refers to the Jerusalem religious establishment. Nicodemus is therefore not an ordinary inquirer but a man of authority, education, and status within Israel's leadership. He will appear twice more in the document: in 7:50-51, where he challenges the council's condemnation of Jesus by asking "Does our law judge a man without first hearing him?", and in 19:39, where he brings an enormous quantity of myrrh and aloes for Jesus's burial. His trajectory through the document — from nighttime inquiry, to partial defense, to burial devotion — traces its own narrative arc.

"He came to him at night" (elten pros auton nyktos). The detail "at night" (nyktos) carries both practical and symbolic weight. Practically, a member of the Sanhedrin visiting a controversial Galilean teacher might prefer discretion. But for the author of this document, "night" is never merely a clock reading. The prologue declared that "the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not overcome it" (1:5). Later, when Judas leaves the last supper to betray Jesus, the author notes: "it was night" (en de nyx, 13:30). Nicodemus comes out of the darkness toward the light — but as the conversation will reveal, he does not yet fully enter the light. He is between the two realms.

"Rabbi, we know that you have come from God as a teacher." Nicodemus addresses Jesus as "Rabbi" (the same title the first disciples used in 1:38, 49), acknowledging him as a teacher. The plural "we know" (oidamen) may indicate that Nicodemus speaks for a group — perhaps other Pharisees or council members who have observed Jesus's signs — or it may be a communal expression of what the Jerusalem audience generally recognized. His conclusion is that Jesus "has come from God" (apo theou elelythas), because "no one can do these signs unless God is with him" (ean me e ho theos met' autou). The reasoning is straightforward: miraculous signs require divine backing. This echoes the logic of 2:23, where "many believed in his name, observing his signs." Nicodemus's assessment is accurate as far as it goes — Jesus has indeed come from God — but the conversation will expose how far short of full understanding this assessment falls. Nicodemus has drawn the right inference from the signs but has not yet grasped what the signs point to.

The phrase "God is with him" (ho theos met' autou) appears in the LXX as a marker of divine favor and empowerment. Genesis 21:22: Abimelech says to Abraham, "God is with you in all that you do." Genesis 39:2-3: "The LORD was with Joseph, and he became a successful man." 1 Samuel 3:19: "The LORD was with Samuel." Isaiah 7:14 (LXX): the child shall be called "Immanuel" — "God with us." Nicodemus places Jesus in the category of one whom God accompanies and empowers. What he does not yet understand is the prologue's claim: this is not merely a man accompanied by God but the Word who was God and has become flesh.

Verses 3-8: Born from Above

"Jesus answered and said to him, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born from above, he cannot see the kingdom of God.'"

Jesus does not respond to Nicodemus's statement by accepting, qualifying, or correcting the compliment. He bypasses it entirely and addresses what Nicodemus actually needs. The double "amen" (amen amen lego soi), characteristic of this author (appearing twenty-five times in the document), introduces a solemn pronouncement.

"Born from above" (gennethe anothen). The word anothen is the crux of the passage. It has two established meanings in Greek:

1. "From above." This is the spatial/directional meaning — from a higher place. This is how the author uses the word elsewhere in this document: in 3:31, "The one who comes from above (anothen) is above all"; in 19:11, "You would have no authority over me unless it had been given you from above (anothen)"; in 19:23, Jesus's tunic was "woven from the top (anothen)." The author's consistent usage of anothen as "from above" strongly indicates this is the primary meaning intended here.

2. "Again, anew." This is the temporal meaning — a second time, once more. This is the meaning Nicodemus hears, as his response in verse 4 makes clear: "How can a man be born when he is old? He cannot enter his mother's womb a second time (deuteron) and be born, can he?"

The ambiguity is not accidental. The author constructs the conversation so that Nicodemus hears one meaning (again) while the reader, who has read the prologue, hears the other (from above). The prologue already established this concept: those who become children of God are "born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a man, but of God" (1:13). Birth "from above" is birth "of God" — the same reality, stated now to Nicodemus in language he should recognize but does not.

"Cannot see the kingdom of God" (ou dynatai idein ten basileian tou theou). The phrase "the kingdom of God" (he basileia tou theou) appears in this document only here and in verse 5. This is remarkable because the kingdom of God is a dominant theme in the other three accounts of Jesus's life. This author virtually never uses the phrase, preferring instead "eternal life" (zoe aionios) as the equivalent concept. The use of "kingdom of God" here, in conversation with a Pharisee, suggests Jesus is using the language Nicodemus would recognize from Israel's prophetic tradition. The kingdom of God is what the prophets promised — the time when God's sovereign rule would be fully established on earth, when the promises to Abraham and David would be fulfilled, when Israel would be restored and the nations would acknowledge Israel's God. Daniel 2:44: "The God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed." Daniel 7:14: the Son of Man receives "dominion and glory and a kingdom." Obadiah 1:21: "the kingdom will be the LORD's."

The verb "see" (idein) here means more than visual perception; it means to experience, to enter into, to participate in (as confirmed by the parallel in v. 5, where "see" is replaced by "enter into"). Nicodemus cannot access, cannot participate in, cannot experience the kingdom of God without being born from above. His Pharisaic learning, his position on the council, his acknowledgment that Jesus comes from God — none of these grant entry. Something radically different is required.

Verse 4: Nicodemus's misunderstanding. "Nicodemus said to him, 'How can a man be born when he is old? He cannot enter his mother's womb a second time and be born, can he?'"

The misunderstanding follows a pattern this author uses repeatedly throughout the document: Jesus makes a statement with layered meaning; the interlocutor takes it at the most literal, physical level; Jesus then explains the deeper meaning. The same pattern occurs with the Samaritan woman and "living water" (4:10-15), with the crowd and "the bread of God" (6:32-34), with the disciples and "Lazarus has fallen asleep" (11:11-14). Each time, the misunderstanding creates the opening for Jesus to unfold what he actually means.

Nicodemus hears "born again" (a second physical birth) and objects that this is impossible. His objection is correct on its own terms — physical rebirth is indeed impossible. But his inability to hear the other meaning ("from above") is itself diagnostic. He is "the teacher of Israel" (ho didaskalos tou Israel, v. 10) and should know the prophetic texts that describe God giving new life, new hearts, new beginnings to his people.

Verse 5: "Born of water and the Spirit." "Jesus answered, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God.'"

Jesus restates the requirement with greater specificity. The single birth "from above" is now described as birth "of water and the Spirit" (ex hydatos kai pneumatos). The kai ("and") links water and Spirit as components of a single birth, not two separate births. What does "water and Spirit" mean?

The most direct background is Ezekiel 36:25-27, a passage any teacher of Israel would know: "Then I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean; I will cleanse you from all your filthiness and from all your idols. Moreover, I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you; and I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. I will put my Spirit within you and cause you to walk in my statutes." In this prophetic text, water (cleansing) and Spirit (new inner life) are joined in God's promise of restoration. God would cleanse his people with water and renew them with his Spirit. This was a promise for the future — for the time when God would act decisively to restore Israel. Jesus is telling Nicodemus that this promised time has arrived, that the Ezekiel 36 renewal is now operative, and that without it, one cannot enter the kingdom.

Isaiah 44:3 similarly joins water and Spirit: "For I will pour water on the thirsty land, and streams on the dry ground; I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring, and my blessing on your descendants." Joel 2:28-29 (LXX 3:1-2) promises the outpouring of the Spirit on "all flesh." These prophetic promises form the interpretive background that Nicodemus, as a teacher of Israel, should have recognized.

The water component may also connect to the baptismal practice that John has been performing — a baptism in water as a sign of repentance and preparation for the one who would "baptize in the Holy Spirit" (1:33). Water baptism and Spirit baptism, already distinguished in John the Baptist's testimony, are here joined as the twin elements of the new birth. The reader who has heard 1:33 ("He upon whom you see the Spirit descending and remaining upon him, this is the one who baptizes in the Holy Spirit") now learns what that Spirit-baptism accomplishes: it constitutes the birth from above without which no one enters the kingdom.

Verse 6: "That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit." Jesus draws a categorical distinction. Flesh (sarx) produces flesh; Spirit (pneuma) produces spirit. These are two different orders of existence. Physical birth produces physical life; birth from the Spirit produces spiritual life. The categories do not overlap. No amount of fleshly birth — no genealogy, no ethnic identity, no human effort — can produce what only the Spirit can produce. This restates the prologue's declaration about the children of God being born "not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a man, but of God" (1:13). The three excluded sources in 1:13 all belong to the category of "flesh." The one sufficient source — God — operates through the Spirit.

Verse 7: "Do not be amazed that I said to you, 'You must be born from above.'" The verb thaumases ("be amazed, marvel") indicates that Nicodemus is visibly astonished. Jesus's response is: do not marvel at this. The requirement of new birth should not surprise a teacher of Israel. The prophets taught it. Ezekiel promised it. The dei ("must" — implied in the necessity of the requirement) is the divine necessity that runs through this document and this author's argument.

The pronoun shift in verse 7 is significant. In verse 3, Jesus said "unless one is born from above" (third person singular, ean me tis gennethe anothen). In verse 7, he says "you must be born from above" (hymas dei gennethenai anothen) — second person plural. The "you" (hymas) extends the requirement beyond Nicodemus as an individual. Jesus is not saying that Nicodemus personally has an unusual deficiency; he is saying that everyone — "you all" — must be born from above. The plural includes Nicodemus, his fellow Pharisees, the rulers, all of Israel, and by extension every human being. No one is exempt from this requirement.

Verse 8: "The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from and where it is going; so is everyone who is born of the Spirit." The word pneuma means both "wind" and "spirit" in Greek (as ruach does in Hebrew). Jesus exploits the double meaning: the wind/spirit blows (pnei) where it wills (thelei), you hear its sound (phonen) but cannot track its origin or destination. So it is with the Spirit-born person. The point is not that the new birth is arbitrary or random but that it operates beyond human control and comprehension. You cannot manufacture it, schedule it, or constrain it. You can perceive its effects (you hear the sound) but cannot master its process. This directly challenges Nicodemus's assumption that understanding God's ways is a matter of study and expertise. The Spirit is sovereign; the teacher of Israel does not command it.

Verses 9-13: Earthly and Heavenly Things

"Nicodemus said to him, 'How can these things be?' Jesus answered and said to him, 'Are you the teacher of Israel and do not understand these things?'"

Nicodemus's third question — "How can these things be?" (pos dynatai tauta genesthai) — echoes Mary's question to Gabriel: "How will this be?" (pos estai touto, Luke 1:34). But where Mary asked how and received an answer, Nicodemus asks how and receives a rebuke. The difference lies in what each should already know. Nicodemus is ho didaskalos tou Israel — "THE teacher of Israel," the definite article indicating a recognized and authoritative position. The rebuke is that someone who holds this position should understand these things. The prophets — Ezekiel, Isaiah, Joel — described the new birth, the new heart, the Spirit's outpouring. A teacher of Israel should recognize what Jesus is describing. His failure to do so reveals that his knowledge of scripture, though extensive, has not penetrated to the level of comprehension that the prophetic texts demand.

Verse 11: "Truly, truly, I say to you, we speak of what we know and testify of what we have seen, and you do not receive our testimony." A significant pronoun shift occurs. Jesus moves from "I" to "we" (legomen, oidamen, heorakamen, martyroumen — all first person plural). Who is "we"? Several possibilities exist: Jesus and the Father who sent him, Jesus and his disciples, Jesus and the prophets who testified before him, or a plurality within Jesus's own identity (in line with the prologue's presentation of the Word who was with God and was God). Whatever the referent, the "we" places Jesus's teaching within a corporate testimony that Nicodemus and those he represents ("you" is also plural — hymeis — "you all do not receive our testimony") are rejecting. The dynamic is the prologue enacted: "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him" (1:11). The testimony is offered; the reception is refused.

Verse 12: "If I told you earthly things and you do not believe, how will you believe if I tell you heavenly things?" Jesus distinguishes between "earthly things" (ta epigeia) and "heavenly things" (ta epourania). What are the "earthly things" he has told Nicodemus? The most natural referent is the analogy of birth from above — the wind, the flesh/spirit distinction, the observable effects of the Spirit's work. These are earthly analogies for heavenly realities, drawn from common experience and prophetic tradition. If Nicodemus cannot grasp these, how will he receive the heavenly realities themselves — the direct revelation of God's nature, purposes, and action? The progression from earthly to heavenly anticipates what Jesus is about to say in verses 13-21, where the discourse moves from analogies (the serpent lifted up) to the most extraordinary claim in the document (God's love sending the Son for the world's salvation).

Verse 13: "No one has ascended into heaven, but he who descended from heaven: the Son of Man." The claim is exclusive. No one has gone up to heaven to bring back direct knowledge of heavenly things — not Moses (though later Jewish tradition speculated about Moses's ascent), not Enoch (though Genesis 5:24 says God took him), not Elijah (though 2 Kings 2:11 narrates his ascent in a whirlwind). The only one qualified to speak of heavenly things is the one who descended from heaven: the Son of Man. This connects directly to the prologue: the Word was with God (1:1-2), the Word became flesh (1:14), and therefore the Word-become-flesh is the only one who can reveal what is in heaven. The claim is the same as 1:18: "No one has ever seen God; the only-begotten God, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has made him known." The Son of Man has come from the realm of heavenly things and therefore speaks from direct knowledge.

The title "Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthropou) connects to its use in 1:51, where Jesus told Nathanael: "You will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." The Son of Man is the point of connection between heaven and earth. He descended from heaven and is therefore the one through whom heavenly realities are communicated to earthly people.

Verses 14-15: The Serpent Lifted Up

"And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up, so that whoever believes in him will have eternal life."

This passage anchors its claim in a specific event from Israel's recorded history. Numbers 21:4-9 narrates the episode: during the wilderness wandering, the people "spoke against God and against Moses" (Numbers 21:5), saying "Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness?" God sent fiery serpents (nachashim seraphim) among them, and the serpents bit the people, and many died. The people confessed their sin and asked Moses to intercede. God instructed Moses: "Make a fiery serpent and set it on a pole; and it shall be that everyone who is bitten, when he looks at it, shall live" (Numbers 21:8). Moses made a bronze serpent and set it on a pole, "and it came about, that if a serpent bit anyone, when he looked to the bronze serpent, he lived" (Numbers 21:9).

Several features of this episode are directly relevant to what Jesus claims:

1. The condition was looking. Survival was not automatic. Those who were bitten had to look at (hibit el in Hebrew, epeblepsen in the LXX) the bronze serpent. The looking was an act of trust in God's provision — God said "look and live," and those who looked did live. Those who refused to look, or who did not look, died. The condition was real: looking was necessary. Wisdom of Solomon 16:6-7, a first-century Jewish text, interprets this episode explicitly: "For they were troubled for a little while as a warning, and received a token of salvation to remind them of the commandment of your law. For the one who turned toward it was saved, not by the thing that was beheld, but by you, the Savior of all." The Wisdom of Solomon makes clear that the bronze serpent itself did not save; God saved. But the looking was required as the response through which salvation was received.

2. The lifting up was necessary. The serpent had to be placed on a pole (nes in Hebrew, semeion/semasian in the LXX) — it had to be elevated, made visible, made accessible to all who would look. If it had not been lifted up, no one could have seen it and lived.

3. The crisis was universal among those in the camp. Everyone who had been bitten was in danger of death. The remedy was available to everyone — "whoever looks, lives." The scope was not limited to certain tribes or families but extended to everyone in the camp who had been afflicted.

Jesus draws a direct typological parallel: "as" (kathos) Moses lifted up the serpent, "even so" (houtos) must (dei — divine necessity) the Son of Man be lifted up. The verb "lifted up" (hypsothenai) is the first of three uses in this document (3:14, 8:28, 12:32-34). In 8:28, Jesus tells the religious leaders: "When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he." In 12:32-34: "'And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself.' But he was saying this to indicate the kind of death by which he was about to die." The author's own gloss in 12:33 makes explicit that "lifted up" refers to crucifixion. But the word hypsoo also means "to exalt, to elevate to a position of honor" (as in the LXX of Isaiah 52:13: "Behold, my servant shall prosper, he shall be exalted [hypsothsetai] and lifted up and shall be very high"). This author appears to use the word with deliberate double meaning: the Son of Man is lifted up on the cross, and in that very act is exalted. The crucifixion is not a defeat that must be overcome by the resurrection; it is itself the moment of glorification.

"So that whoever believes in him will have eternal life" (hina pas ho pisteuon en auto eche zoen aionion). The purpose (hina, "so that") of the Son of Man's lifting up is stated: whoever believes will have eternal life. Two elements require attention:

First, "whoever believes" (pas ho pisteuon). The word pas ("every, all, whoever") echoes the scope of the serpent narrative — everyone who looked, lived. The scope is universal in availability: not limited to a particular group but open to every person (pas) who responds. The participle pisteuon is present tense — "the one believing," "the one who goes on believing" — denoting continuous, ongoing trust, not a single past act. This is consistent with the author's use of pisteuo throughout the document as an active, sustained orientation toward Jesus.

Second, "eternal life" (zoe aionios). This is the first occurrence of this phrase in the document. It will appear seventeen times, always in connection with believing in Jesus (3:15, 16, 36; 4:14, 36; 5:24, 39; 6:27, 40, 47, 54, 68; 10:28; 12:25, 50; 17:2, 3). The word aionios derives from aion ("age") and in the LXX context refers to the life of the age to come — the life that belongs to God's restored creation, the life that the prophets promised for the messianic age. Daniel 12:2 (LXX): "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake, some to everlasting life (zoen aionion), others to disgrace and everlasting contempt." The phrase does not merely mean life that lasts forever (though it includes that); it means the quality and kind of life that belongs to God's age, God's realm, God's own existence. In 17:3, the author will define it explicitly: "This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent." Eternal life is relational knowledge of God through Christ.

The typological parallel is precise: the bitten Israelites who looked at the lifted serpent received physical life; those who believe in the lifted-up Son of Man receive eternal life. In both cases, the provision comes from God, the lifting up is necessary, and the human response (looking/believing) is the condition through which the provision is received.

Verses 16-18: The Purpose of the Son's Sending

A question of voice arises here. In the Greek text, there are no quotation marks, and scholars have debated whether verses 16-21 are a continuation of Jesus's words to Nicodemus or the author's own commentary on the dialogue. Several features suggest the author's voice: the shift from "I" to third-person references to God and the Son, the summary-like quality of the statements, and the thematic parallels with the prologue. However, the question of speaker does not change the meaning, since the author presents both Jesus's words and his own commentary as authoritative revelation. The argument is continuous regardless of who is speaking it.

Verse 16: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only-begotten Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish, but have eternal life."

This verse is frequently extracted from its context and treated as a freestanding summary. In context, it is the explanation (gar, "for") of why the Son of Man must be lifted up (v. 14) and why believing in him produces eternal life (v. 15). The logic flows directly: the Son of Man is lifted up because God loved the world and gave his Son for this purpose.

"God so loved" (houtos gar egapesen ho theos). The word houtos ("so, thus, in this way") does not primarily indicate the degree of God's love (how much God loved) but the manner of it (this is how God loved, this is what God's love looked like in action). God's love is demonstrated not by an emotion but by an act: he gave.

"The world" (ton kosmon). In 1:10, "the world" was the creation that came into being through the Word but did not recognize him. In 1:29, Jesus was identified as "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world." The scope is the same here: the object of God's love is the entire created human order, not a select portion of it. The "world" in this author's vocabulary includes those who will reject the light (3:19-20) as well as those who will come to it (3:21). God's love is directed toward the world in its entirety; the world's response to that love varies.

"He gave his only-begotten Son" (edoken ton huion ton monogene). The verb edoken ("gave") can mean both "gave" as in "provided, sent" and "gave up, handed over." The latter sense connects to the crucifixion — God gave the Son to death. The word monogenes ("only-begotten, unique, one-of-a-kind"), as noted in the analysis of 1:14 and 1:18, echoes the LXX of Genesis 22:2, where God commands Abraham to take "your son, your only son" (ton monogene sou) — Isaac. The Aqedah (binding of Isaac) looms over this verse: as Abraham was willing to give his only son, so God gives his only Son. But where Abraham's sacrifice was halted and a substitute provided (Genesis 22:12-13), God's giving of the Son is completed. The parallel that was foreshadowed in the Aqedah reaches its fulfillment in the cross.

"That whoever believes in him shall not perish, but have eternal life" (hina pas ho pisteuon eis auton me apoletai all' eche zoen aionion). The purpose of the giving is salvation, not judgment. Two outcomes are set in contrast: perishing (apoletai, from apollymi — "to be destroyed, to be lost, to perish") and having eternal life. Between these two outcomes stands the condition: believing (pisteuon, present tense participle — again, ongoing trust and commitment, not a single moment of assent). The one who goes on believing does not perish but has (present tense, eche) eternal life. The present tense of "has" is striking — eternal life is not only a future hope but a present possession for the one who believes.

Verse 17: "For God did not send the Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him."

The verb krinein ("to judge") will become central later in the document. In 5:22, "the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son." In 5:27, "he gave him authority to execute judgment, because he is the Son of Man." In 8:15-16, Jesus says "I judge no one. But even if I do judge, my judgment is true." In 12:47-48, "If anyone hears my sayings and does not keep them, I do not judge him; for I did not come to judge the world but to save the world. The one who rejects me and does not receive my sayings has a judge; the word that I spoke will judge him at the last day." The picture across the document is complex: the Son has authority to judge (5:22, 27), but his purpose in coming was not judgment but salvation (3:17, 12:47). Nevertheless, judgment occurs as a consequence of the response to the Son — those who refuse the light judge themselves (3:18-19).

The verb sothe ("might be saved") echoes the LXX's soteria ("salvation"), the comprehensive term for God's rescue of his people. Isaiah 45:22: "Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth." Isaiah 49:6: "I will make you a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth." The scope again is "the world" — God's intent in sending the Son was the salvation of the whole world, not its condemnation.

Verse 18: "He who believes in him is not judged; he who does not believe has been judged already, because he has not believed in the name of the only-begotten Son of God."

Two present realities are stated. The one who believes (ho pisteuon — present participle, ongoing belief) is not judged (ou krinetai — present tense, "is not being judged, does not come under judgment"). The one who does not believe (ho me pisteuon) has been judged already (ede kekritai — perfect tense, "has already been judged, stands in a state of judgment"). The perfect tense indicates a completed state with ongoing effect. The person who does not believe is not awaiting a future judgment; the judgment is already operative. The refusal to believe constitutes the judgment itself.

The reason is stated: "because he has not believed in the name of the only-begotten Son of God" (hoti me pepisteuken eis to onoma tou monogenous huiou tou theou). The verb pepisteuken is also perfect tense — "he has not come to believe and does not stand in a state of belief." The name (onoma) of the Son carries the full reality of the Son's identity. To believe "in the name" is to commit to the person the name identifies — the only-begotten Son of God, the one the prologue described as the Word who was God and became flesh.

The logic is stark: the sending of the Son creates a dividing line. Before the Son's coming, the world existed in a general condition of darkness. The Son's coming introduces light, and light forces a decision. To believe is to step into the light and escape judgment. To refuse to believe is to remain in darkness, and the darkness is itself the judgment.

Verses 19-21: Light, Darkness, and the Verdict

"This is the judgment, that the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light, for their deeds were evil. For everyone who does evil hates the light and does not come to the light, lest his deeds be exposed. But he who practices the truth comes to the light, so that his deeds may be manifested as having been wrought in God."

"This is the judgment" (haute de estin he krisis). The word krisis ("judgment, verdict, separation") defines what judgment means in this context. It is not a future tribunal but a present reality: the light has entered the world (the incarnation described in 1:9-14 and everything that flows from it), and human beings have responded by preferring darkness. The judgment consists of the choice itself. The light does not need to condemn; the preference for darkness is self-condemning.

"Men loved the darkness rather than the light" (egapesan hoi anthropoi mallon to skotos e to phos). The verb egapesan ("they loved") is aorist, describing the characteristic human response. It echoes the prologue's "the world did not know him... his own did not receive him" (1:10-11). The love of darkness is not presented as a philosophical position but as a moral preference: "for their deeds were evil" (en gar auton ponera ta erga). The word ponera ("evil, wicked, worthless") describes actions, not merely thoughts. People love the darkness because they are doing things that the light would expose. The flight from the light is a flight from exposure.

Verse 20: "Everyone who does evil hates the light." The verb phaula prasson ("doing worthless/base things") intensifies the description. Such a person "does not come to the light" (ouk erchetai pros to phos) — the same directional language used of Nicodemus in verse 2 (elthe pros auton, "came to him"). Coming to the light or remaining in the darkness is the fundamental human choice this passage describes. The reason for avoiding the light is "lest his deeds be exposed" (hina me elegchthe ta erga autou). The verb elegcho means "to expose, convict, reprove, bring to light." It does not merely mean "criticized" but "shown for what they truly are." The light functions as revelation — it makes visible what darkness conceals.

Verse 21: "But he who practices the truth comes to the light." The phrase "practices the truth" (poion ten aletheian) is arresting. Truth (aletheia) is not merely spoken or believed; it is done, practiced, performed. This is an Hebraic idiom. In the LXX and Jewish literature, "doing truth" (Hebrew: 'asah emet) means acting faithfully, living with integrity. Genesis 47:29 (LXX): Jacob asks Joseph to "deal with me in kindness and truth" (poieseis met' emou eleemosyne kai aletheian). Nehemiah 9:33: "You are just in all that has come upon us, for you have dealt truthfully" (aletheian epoiesas). Tobit 4:6 and 13:6: "If you do what is true, your ways will prosper." Truth in this framework is not an abstraction to be grasped intellectually but a way of living to be enacted.

The one who does truth comes to the light "so that his deeds may be manifested as having been wrought in God" (hina phanerothe autou ta erga hoti en theo estin eirgasmena). The phrase "wrought in God" (en theo eirgasmena) means that the person's deeds have been done in communion with God, in dependence on God, in alignment with God. The person who practices truth does not fear the light because the light will reveal that their deeds have their source in God. The contrast is not between perfect people and sinful people but between those who live in openness to God's revelation and those who hide from it.

This passage completes the Nicodemus scene by explaining why Nicodemus came "at night." He came in the darkness, drawn toward the light but not yet fully entering it. His position between night and light mirrors the position described in these verses: the light has come, and the question is whether a person will come to it or remain in the darkness. The author leaves Nicodemus's response unresolved at this point, allowing the reader to track his trajectory through the rest of the document (7:50-51, 19:39).

Verses 22-24: Jesus and John Baptizing

"After these things Jesus and his disciples came into the land of Judea, and there he was spending time with them and baptizing. John also was baptizing in Aenon near Salim, because there was much water there; and people were coming and were being baptized. For John had not yet been thrown into prison."

The scene shifts from Jerusalem (where the Nicodemus conversation took place) to the Judean countryside. Jesus and his disciples are baptizing — though 4:2 will clarify that "Jesus himself was not baptizing, but his disciples were." The detail is significant: Jesus's ministry involves baptism, an activity parallel to John's and inevitably inviting comparison.

"Aenon near Salim, because there was much water there." Aenon (from the Hebrew/Aramaic 'ayin, "spring") is a location with abundant water. The geographical detail serves the narrative but also carries symbolic weight in a document that will repeatedly use water as a sign of what Jesus offers (4:10-14, 7:37-39). The juxtaposition of two baptizing ministries — John's and Jesus's — sets the stage for the discussion that follows about who is greater and what each baptism means.

"For John had not yet been thrown into prison." This chronological note distinguishes this document's timeline from those of the other three accounts, where John's imprisonment precedes the bulk of Jesus's public ministry (Matthew 4:12, Mark 1:14). This author places the early phase of Jesus's ministry as overlapping with John's, allowing for the comparison and contrast that follows.

Verses 25-30: John's Final Testimony and the Bridegroom

"Therefore there arose a discussion on the part of John's disciples with a Jew about purification. And they came to John and said to him, 'Rabbi, he who was with you beyond the Jordan, to whom you have testified — behold, he is baptizing, and all are coming to him.'"

"A discussion... about purification" (zetesis... peri katharismou). The word katharismos ("purification, cleansing") directly echoes 2:6, where the water jars at Cana were set aside "for the Jewish rites of purification." The dispute about purification provides the context for the question about Jesus's baptizing activity. The connection is deliberate: the old purification system (represented by the water jars and by John's water baptism) is being superseded by what Jesus brings. The dispute about purification leads to the question about Jesus, because Jesus is the answer to the purification question.

"He is baptizing, and all are coming to him." John's disciples report what they perceive as a competitive threat. The one to whom John testified is now attracting everyone. Their tone implies concern: John's prominence is declining. The word pantes ("all") may be hyperbolic, but it captures the shift in momentum.

Verse 27: "John answered and said, 'A man can receive nothing unless it has been given him from heaven.'" John's response reframes the situation. No one takes a role for himself; every role is assigned from heaven. John's ministry was given to him by God; Jesus's ministry was given to him by God. There is no competition because neither one chose his position.

Verse 28: "You yourselves are my witnesses that I said, 'I am not the Christ, but I have been sent before him.'" John recalls his own prior testimony (see 1:20: "He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed, 'I am not the Christ'"). He explicitly reaffirms his subordinate role. He is not the Christ but the one sent before the Christ. His role is preparatory, not ultimate.

Verses 29-30: "He who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices greatly because of the bridegroom's voice. So this joy of mine has been made full. He must increase, but I must decrease."

John employs the bridegroom-friend metaphor. In first-century Jewish wedding custom, the "friend of the bridegroom" (ho philos tou nymphiou) was the shoshbin — the best man who arranged the wedding, facilitated the match, and presented the bride to the groom. The shoshbin's role was essential but temporary; once the groom and bride were united, his work was complete. His joy was not diminished by the wedding's consummation but fulfilled by it.

The bridegroom imagery carries deep scriptural resonance. In Israel's prophetic tradition, God is consistently portrayed as the bridegroom of Israel. Hosea 2:16 (LXX 2:18): "In that day, declares the LORD, you will call me 'My husband.'" Isaiah 54:5: "For your Maker is your husband, the LORD of hosts is his name." Isaiah 62:5: "As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you." Jeremiah 2:2: "I remember the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride." Ezekiel 16 portrays the entire history of Jerusalem as a marriage between God and his people. By calling Jesus the bridegroom, John places him in the role that the prophets assigned to God himself. The bridegroom of Israel has arrived, and the shoshbin's work is finished.

"He must increase, but I must decrease" (ekeinon dei auxanein, eme de elattousthai). The dei ("must") again invokes divine necessity. This is not John's personal preference or resignation; it is the ordained pattern. The forerunner's diminishment and the bridegroom's exaltation are both necessary, both planned, both part of God's design. John's ministry is not failing; it is completing its purpose.

Verses 31-36: The One from Above

As with verses 16-21, the question of speaker arises. Are these words John the Baptist's continued speech, or the author's own theological commentary? The content shifts from John's concrete, first-person testimony (vv. 27-30) to sweeping declarations about the one from above, the one from earth, testimony, the Spirit, the Father's love for the Son, and the wrath that remains on the disobedient. The style and vocabulary are closer to the author's own voice (especially the prologue and vv. 16-21) than to John the Baptist's typical speech in this document. But again, whether John or the author speaks, the argument is continuous with everything that precedes it.

Verse 31: "He who comes from above is above all; he who is of the earth is from the earth and speaks of the earth. He who comes from heaven is above all." This restates the categorical distinction of verse 6 (flesh/Spirit) and verse 13 (the one who descended from heaven). Two origins produce two kinds of speech. The one "of the earth" (ek tes ges) speaks "of the earth" (ek tes ges lalei) — earthly speech, limited by earthly perspective. The one "from above" (anothen — the same word from verse 3) is "above all" (epano panton estin). The word anothen links this verse directly to the earlier dialogue: to be born anothen is to be born from the realm of the one who comes anothen. The birth from above connects the believer to the one who is himself from above.

Verse 32: "What he has seen and heard, of that he testifies; and no one receives his testimony." The one from above testifies based on direct observation — he has seen (heoraken) and heard (ekousen) heavenly realities. This echoes verse 11: "We speak of what we know and testify of what we have seen." The lament "no one receives his testimony" (oudeis lambanei autou ten martyrian) restates the prologue's theme of rejection (1:10-11) and echoes the experience described in verses 11-12, where Nicodemus and those he represents do not receive the testimony. The "no one" (oudeis) is hyperbolic — verse 33 immediately names an exception — but it captures the prevailing response.

Verse 33: "He who has received his testimony has set his seal to this, that God is true." The person who does receive the testimony thereby confirms (esphragisen, "has set a seal on") a fundamental truth: God is truthful (ho theos alethes estin). The act of believing the Son's testimony is simultaneously an affirmation of God's truthfulness, because the Son speaks God's words (v. 34). To believe the Son is to trust God; to reject the Son is to call God a liar. This logic will recur in 5:37-38: "The Father who sent me has himself testified about me... you do not have his word remaining in you, because you do not believe the one he sent."

Verse 34: "For he whom God has sent speaks the words of God; for he gives the Spirit without measure." The one God sent (hon gar apesteilen ho theos) speaks "the words of God" (ta rhemata tou theou) — not his own words but God's. The authority of the Son's speech derives from the Father's commissioning. The phrase "he gives the Spirit without measure" (ou gar ek metrou didosin to pneuma) indicates that the Spirit is given to the Son without limitation. In the LXX and Jewish tradition, the Spirit was given to prophets for specific tasks and in limited measure — the Spirit came upon Saul and departed (1 Samuel 16:14); the Spirit rested on the seventy elders temporarily (Numbers 11:25). The Spirit descended on Jesus and "remained" (emeinen, 1:33) — the permanence was already established. Now the author adds that the Spirit is given "without measure" — without the limitation that characterized every prior gift of the Spirit in Israel's history. The fullness of the Spirit on the Son corresponds to the fullness of grace and truth (1:14) that the prologue attributed to the Word-made-flesh.

Verse 35: "The Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand." The verb agapa ("loves") describes the Father's relationship to the Son — the same verb used in verse 16 for God's love for the world. The gift is comprehensive: "all things" (panta) have been placed "into his hand" (en te cheiri autou). In the LXX, having something "in one's hand" signifies authority and control. Genesis 39:4, 6, 8: Potiphar "placed everything he had in Joseph's hand." Daniel 7:14: the Son of Man receives "dominion and glory and a kingdom." The Father's love for the Son expresses itself in granting the Son total authority. This prepares for 5:22 ("the Father has given all judgment to the Son"), 13:3 ("the Father had given all things into his hands"), and 17:2 ("you have given him authority over all flesh").

Verse 36: "He who believes in the Son has eternal life; but he who does not obey the Son will not see life, but the wrath of God abides on him."

This verse serves as the summation of the entire chapter's argument, and its word choices demand careful attention.

"He who believes in the Son has eternal life" (ho pisteuon eis ton huion echei zoen aionion). This restates verse 15 and verse 16. The present tense participle (pisteuon, "the one believing") and the present tense verb (echei, "has") are both continuous: the one who goes on believing presently possesses eternal life. Eternal life is a present reality for the believing person, not merely a future hope.

"He who does not obey the Son" (ho de apeithon to huio). The critical word here is apeithon, not apiston. The author does not use apisteo ("to not believe, to disbelieve") but apeitheo ("to disobey, to be disobedient, to refuse compliance"). The word appears in the LXX for active rebellion and refusal to submit. In the LXX of Numbers 14:43, Israel is told they will fall by the sword "because you have turned away from the LORD, and the LORD will not be with you" — the word for their turning away is related to this same root of disobedience. The LXX of Deuteronomy 1:26: "But you were not willing to go up and you disobeyed (epeithesate) the command of the LORD your God." Isaiah 3:8: "Their deeds and their words are against the LORD, to disobey (apeithein) the eyes of his glory." The opposite of "believing in the Son" is not merely intellectual skepticism but active disobedience — a refusal to comply with what the Son demands. Believing, in this author's framework, is not merely cognitive assent but is inseparable from obedience. Unbelief manifests as disobedience; disobedience reveals unbelief.

"Will not see life" (ouk opsetai zoen). The verb opsetai ("will see") echoes the verb idein ("to see") from verse 3: "unless one is born from above, he cannot see the kingdom of God." The same exclusion applies: without the new birth (v. 3), without believing/obeying (v. 36), one will not see life.

"But the wrath of God abides on him" (all' he orge tou theou menei ep' auton). The verb menei ("remains, abides") is the same word that pervades this document: the Spirit "remained" on Jesus (1:33), the disciples asked where Jesus was "staying" (1:38), they "stayed" with him (1:39), and the great discourse of chapter 15 will be about "remaining" in Jesus. Here, the word describes the opposite condition: instead of remaining in the Son and having life, the disobedient person has the wrath of God remaining on him. The present tense of menei indicates not a future imposition of wrath but a present, ongoing condition. The wrath does not arrive as a new punishment; it is already there and stays. The implication is that apart from the Son, the default condition of humanity is one of existing under God's wrath (a concept rooted in the prophetic tradition: Isaiah 9:12, 19; 10:4; Zephaniah 1:15, 18; 2:2-3; Psalm 78:31). The Son's coming offers escape from this condition; refusing the Son means remaining in it.

The word orge ("wrath") in the LXX describes God's active response to covenant violation, idolatry, and injustice. It is not arbitrary anger but the settled opposition of a holy God to everything that opposes his purposes. Deuteronomy 29:27 (LXX): "The anger of the LORD burned against this land." Psalm 78:31: "The anger of God rose against them." Isaiah 13:9: "The day of the LORD is coming, cruel, with wrath and fierce anger." The "wrath of God" is not an abstraction but has concrete precedent in Israel's history: the wilderness judgments, the exile, the prophetic warnings. What the author says is that this same wrath — the settled divine opposition to human rebellion — remains on those who disobey the Son.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. The chapter is constructed as a continuous argument about what is required to enter the kingdom of God and receive eternal life. The Nicodemus dialogue (vv. 1-21) establishes the requirement (birth from above, by water and Spirit) and the means (believing in the lifted-up Son of Man). John's testimony (vv. 25-36) confirms Jesus's supremacy and restates the stakes. Together, the two halves present a single, coherent claim: the Son has come from above, must be lifted up, and believing/obeying him is the condition for eternal life.

2. Nicodemus is presented as the first test case of 2:23-25. He is "a man" (anthropos, 3:1) who has observed the signs and drawn correct but insufficient conclusions. He represents those who believed because of signs but to whom Jesus would not entrust himself because "he knew what was in man" (2:25). The dialogue reveals what Nicodemus lacks: not information, not sincerity, not religious devotion, but birth from above — a transformation that no human effort, education, or status can produce.

3. The "born from above" requirement restates the prologue's redefinition of God's children. In 1:12-13, the author declared that those who become children of God are born "not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a man, but of God." Chapter 3 explains how that birth occurs (by water and Spirit, in fulfillment of Ezekiel 36:25-27) and insists that it is universally necessary (the plural "you must" of v. 7). No exemption exists for Pharisees, teachers, rulers, or any category of person.

4. The serpent typology grounds the atonement in Israel's historical experience. The author does not present the Son of Man's lifting up as an abstract theological concept but as the fulfillment of a pattern already established in Israel's history. In Numbers 21, God's provision for dying Israelites required an act of looking, of turning toward the remedy God provided. The remedy was lifted up for all to see. Those who looked lived; those who did not look died. Jesus identifies himself as the fulfillment of this pattern. The condition of looking/believing is not optional or trivial; in the wilderness, failure to look meant death.

5. God's love for the world is presented as the motive for the Son's sending, and the sending is presented as the definitive act of that love. Verse 16 does not describe a generalized divine benevolence but a specific, costly action: God gave his only-begotten Son. The Aqedah parallel (Genesis 22) supplies the emotional weight — this is the father offering the beloved, unique son. The purpose is salvation (v. 17), not condemnation. But the salvation is conditional on believing (v. 16), and the refusal to believe constitutes its own judgment (v. 18).

6. The light-darkness motif from the prologue is developed into a moral framework. Judgment is not an arbitrary verdict imposed from outside but the natural consequence of preferring darkness over light (vv. 19-21). The light exposes, and those whose deeds are evil flee from exposure. Those who practice truth come to the light because they have nothing to hide. Nicodemus's coming "at night" places him on the boundary between these two responses, and the author leaves his ultimate decision unresolved until later in the document.

7. John the Baptist's final testimony completes his subordination and identifies Jesus as the bridegroom of Israel. By calling Jesus the bridegroom and himself the bridegroom's friend, John places Jesus in the role that the prophets assigned to God: the husband of Israel (Hosea 2:16, Isaiah 54:5, 62:5). John's decrease and Jesus's increase is not a matter of personal fortune but divine necessity (dei). The forerunner's purpose is fulfilled when the one he heralded takes center stage.

8. The chapter ends with a stark binary that defines the condition of every human being. Verse 36 divides humanity into two categories: those who believe/obey the Son and have eternal life, and those who disobey the Son and remain under God's wrath. The verb apeithon ("disobeying") rather than apiston ("disbelieving") makes clear that the response to the Son is not merely intellectual but behavioral — it involves submission and obedience, not just mental agreement. The wrath of God "remains" (menei) on the disobedient, indicating not a future threat but a present condition from which the Son offers rescue. The chapter that began with a man coming to Jesus at night ends with a description of those who will not come to the light. The question the author leaves with the reader is: which response will you choose?

9. The present-tense grammar of believing is consistent throughout the chapter. The participle pisteuon ("the one believing") appears in verses 15, 16, 18 (twice), and 36. In each case, it is present tense, describing an ongoing activity rather than a completed one-time event. Likewise, the present tense echei ("has") describes a present possession of eternal life that corresponds to present, ongoing belief. The grammar presents the relationship between believing and having life as continuous and concurrent: the person who goes on believing goes on having eternal life. The text does not address directly whether one who stops believing loses what was had, but the present-tense construction consistently pairs ongoing believing with ongoing possession of life, and the contrasting condition in verse 36 — the wrath remaining on the disobedient — describes a continuing state, not a past verdict.

10. The chapter as a whole serves as the author's foundational exposition of how the Word-made-flesh accomplishes the salvation the prologue announced. The prologue declared that the Word became flesh, that his glory was beheld, that he was full of grace and truth, and that to those who received him he gave the right to become children of God. Chapter 3 explains the mechanism: birth from above by water and Spirit, believing in the Son who was lifted up as Moses lifted up the serpent, and coming to the light rather than remaining in the darkness. Every element of the prologue's announcement finds its operational explanation in this chapter.
