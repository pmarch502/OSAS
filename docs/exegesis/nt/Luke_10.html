Luke 10 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 9): Luke 9 is densely packed and marks a major structural turning point in the Gospel. It opens with Jesus sending the twelve apostles on mission — giving them "power and authority over all the demons and to heal diseases" and sending them to "proclaim the kingdom of God and to perform healing" (9:1-2). He instructs them to take nothing for the journey and to stay in whatever house receives them, shaking the dust off their feet against towns that reject them (9:3-5). They go out and return, and Jesus withdraws with them to Bethsaida, where the feeding of the five thousand takes place (9:10-17). Peter confesses Jesus as "the Christ of God" (9:20), followed immediately by the first prediction of suffering, death, and resurrection (9:22), and the declaration that following Jesus requires taking up one's cross daily (9:23-27). The transfiguration (9:28-36) reveals Jesus's glory to Peter, James, and John, with Moses and Elijah speaking with him about his "departure" (exodon) that he is about to accomplish in Jerusalem. The chapter's second half accelerates the movement: a failed exorcism by the disciples (9:37-43a), a second prediction of suffering (9:43b-45), a dispute among the disciples about who is greatest (9:46-48), and a rebuke of John for trying to stop someone casting out demons in Jesus's name who was "not following with us" (9:49-50). Then the narrative pivots sharply: "When the days were approaching for His being taken up, He resolutely set His face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51). This initiates Luke's extended travel narrative. The first event on the road is a Samaritan village refusing to receive Jesus because "His face was set toward Jerusalem" (9:53). James and John ask whether they should "command fire to come down from heaven and consume them" (9:54) — an explicit allusion to Elijah's act in 2 Kings 1:10-12 — and Jesus rebukes them. The chapter closes with three encounters about the cost of discipleship: a man who volunteers to follow but is warned that the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head (9:57-58), a man called to follow who asks first to bury his father (9:59-60), and a man who wants to say goodbye to his family first (9:61-62). Jesus's response to the last — "No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" — establishes that the journey to Jerusalem demands total commitment.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 11): Luke 11 opens with the disciples observing Jesus praying and asking him to teach them to pray (11:1). Jesus provides the prayer that begins "Father, hallowed be Your name. Your kingdom come" (11:2-4). He then teaches on persistence in prayer through the parable of the friend at midnight (11:5-8), followed by the assurance that those who ask will receive, those who seek will find, and those who knock will have the door opened (11:9-13). The chapter then moves to the Beelzebul controversy: Jesus casts out a demon and some accuse him of doing so by the power of "Beelzebul, the ruler of the demons" (11:15). Jesus responds with the argument that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand (11:17-20) and declares that "if I cast out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (11:20). The chapter concludes with a sustained series of woes against the Pharisees and the lawyers (11:37-54), denouncing them for concern with external purity while being "full of robbery and wickedness" within (11:39), for loving the seat of honor in the synagogues (11:43), for loading people with burdens they will not touch (11:46), and for "taking away the key of knowledge" — neither entering themselves nor allowing others to enter (11:52). Chapter 11 thus moves from the disciples' learning to pray (the proper posture before God) to open confrontation with the religious establishment (which resists what God is doing through Jesus).

How chapter 10 connects these: Luke 9 ended by setting the journey to Jerusalem in motion and establishing its cost. The Samaritan village rejected Jesus; those who wanted to follow faced radical demands. Luke 11 will address the nature of prayer (the disciples' inner life), the source of Jesus's power (the Beelzebul controversy), and the failure of the religious leaders (the woes). Chapter 10 functions as the bridge in three movements. First, the sending of the seventy(-two) extends the mission that began with the twelve in chapter 9 — widening the scope from the inner circle to a much larger body of workers, and making explicit what is at stake in the acceptance or rejection of their message: "The one who listens to you listens to Me, and the one who rejects you rejects Me; and the one who rejects Me rejects the One who sent Me" (10:16). Second, the parable of the Samaritan directly addresses the Samaritan rejection of chapter 9: a village of Samaritans refused to receive Jesus, and James and John wanted to destroy them, but in chapter 10 it is a Samaritan who embodies what the Torah actually requires — loving one's neighbor — while a priest and a Levite fail to do so. The ethnic category that was rejected in chapter 9 becomes the exemplar in chapter 10. Third, the episode of Martha and Mary closes the chapter by showing the proper response to Jesus's presence — sitting at his feet and listening to his word — which prepares for the disciples' request to learn to pray in chapter 11. The chapter thus moves from sending (mission), to defining (what does faithfulness to God's command look like?), to receiving (what is the posture of a true disciple?).

Verses 1-4: The Sending of the Seventy(-Two)

"Now after this the Lord appointed seventy others, and sent them in pairs ahead of Him to every city and place where He Himself was going to come. And He was saying to them, 'The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore plead with the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into His harvest. Go; behold, I am sending you out as lambs in the midst of wolves. Carry no money belt, no bag, no shoes; and greet no one on the way.'"

The author designates Jesus as "the Lord" (ho kyrios), the same narrator's title used at key moments in the Gospel (7:13, the raising at Nain). The number seventy (some manuscripts read seventy-two) carries significant scriptural resonance from two directions.

First, in Numbers 11:16-17, 24-25, Moses gathered seventy elders of Israel at the tent of meeting, and God "took of the Spirit who was upon him and placed it upon the seventy elders; and when the Spirit rested upon them, they prophesied." Two additional men, Eldad and Medad, also received the Spirit and prophesied in the camp, prompting Joshua to object: "Moses, my lord, restrain them!" Moses replied: "Are you jealous for my sake? Would that all the LORD's people were prophets, that the LORD would put His Spirit on them!" (Numbers 11:28-29). The seventy elders were an extension of Moses's authority — they carried the same Spirit and exercised delegated leadership. If this is the primary background, the seventy sent by Jesus are an extension of his own authority, carrying his commission as the elders carried Moses's.

Second, in the LXX of Genesis 10, the table of nations lists seventy-two peoples (the Masoretic Text has seventy). These represent the totality of the world's nations. If this is the background, the number signals that Jesus's mission extends beyond Israel to all the peoples of the earth. The twelve apostles correspond to the twelve tribes of Israel; the seventy(-two) correspond to the nations.

Both backgrounds may be operative simultaneously. The mission is both an extension of authority (as with Moses) and a signal of universal scope (as with the nations). The fact that they are sent "ahead of Him to every city and place where He Himself was going to come" makes them advance messengers — heralds preparing for the arrival of the one who sent them.

The harvest metaphor — "the harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few" — draws on prophetic imagery. In Joel 3:13 (4:13 LXX), "Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe" is a call to divine judgment on the nations. In Isaiah 27:12, God's gathering of Israel is described as a harvest. The metaphor can signify either judgment or salvation, and here it carries both dimensions: the harvest is the gathering of people who will respond to the kingdom proclamation, but the rejection by others will bring consequences (as vv. 10-15 will make clear). The instruction to "plead with the Lord of the harvest" (deēthēte oun tou kyriou tou therismou) places God as the owner of the harvest and the one who sends laborers — including, by implication, the seventy themselves, whose sending is an answer to this very prayer.

"Lambs in the midst of wolves" (arnas en mesō lykōn) describes vulnerability, not aggression. The missionaries carry no power of their own except the message and authority entrusted to them. The wolf imagery appears in Ezekiel 22:27 for Israel's princes who "devour lives" and in Zephaniah 3:3 for Jerusalem's judges. The danger is real, but the lambs are not told to arm themselves. They are told to go.

The instructions about what not to carry — no money belt (ballantion), no bag (pēra), no extra sandals — replicate the instructions given to the twelve in 9:3. The prohibition against greeting anyone on the way (mēdena kata tēn hodon aspasēsthe) is not about rudeness; it reflects the urgency of the mission. Eastern greetings could involve lengthy exchanges and hospitality obligations. When Elisha sent Gehazi to lay his staff on the dead child of the Shunammite woman, he instructed him: "If you meet anyone, do not greet him, and if anyone greets you, do not answer him" (2 Kings 4:29). The mission is too urgent for social convention.

Verses 5-9: Instructions for Reception

"Whatever house you enter, first say, 'Peace be to this house.' If a man of peace is there, your peace will rest on him; but if not, it will return to you. Stay in that house, eating and drinking what they give you; for the laborer is worthy of his wages. Do not keep moving from house to house. Whatever city you enter and they receive you, eat what is set before you; and heal those in it who are sick, and say to them, 'The kingdom of God has come near to you.'"

The greeting "peace be to this house" (eirēnē tō oikō toutō) is the standard Hebrew shalom — but here it is more than a social convention. The peace is presented as something with substance and direction: it can "rest on" (epanapahēsetai) a receptive person or "return" (anakampsei) to the sender. The word epanapauomai means "to rest upon, to settle on" — it is the word used of the Spirit resting on the seventy elders in Numbers 11:25-26 (LXX: kai epanepausato to pneuma ep' autous). Peace here functions almost as a spiritual reality with agency, not merely a wish. A "son of peace" (huios eirēnēs) — literally "a child of peace" — is a Hebraism meaning a person whose character is defined by peace, one who is receptive to the shalom being offered.

The instruction to stay in one house and not move from house to house prevents the missionaries from seeking better accommodations or creating competition among hosts. It also keeps them in the posture of dependence rather than consumers shopping for comfort.

The declaration "the laborer is worthy of his wages" (axios gar ho ergatēs tou misthou autou) grounds the missionaries' acceptance of hospitality in a principle of justice, not charity. Those who work deserve to be supported by those who benefit from their work. This is the same principle underlying the Torah's provision that the ox should not be muzzled while threshing (Deuteronomy 25:4).

The instruction "eat what is set before you" (esthiete ta paratithemena hymin) is significant in context. If the seventy represent a mission that extends beyond Jewish boundaries (the table-of-nations connection), this instruction implicitly relaxes dietary restrictions — eat whatever your hosts provide, without inquiry about its preparation. This would be a radical instruction if addressed to Jews entering Gentile territory.

The message they carry is twofold: healing ("heal those in it who are sick") and proclamation ("the kingdom of God has come near to you," ēngiken eph' hymas hē basileia tou theou). The verb engizō means "to draw near, to approach, to be at hand." The kingdom is not a distant future event but an imminent reality arriving in the persons of the messengers and, through them, in the person of the one who sent them. The healings are not incidental — they are the kingdom's arriving presence made visible in human bodies.

Verses 10-12: Instructions for Rejection

"But whatever city you enter and they do not receive you, go out into its streets and say, 'Even the dust of your city which clings to our feet we wipe off in protest against you; yet be sure of this, that the kingdom of God has come near.' I say to you, it will be more tolerable in that day for Sodom than for that city."

The response to rejection is a public symbolic act: wiping off the dust (ton koniorton ton kollēthenta hēmin ek tēs poleōs hymōn apomassometha hymin). Jews returning to the land of Israel from Gentile territory would shake off the dust of pagan soil from their feet, a purification gesture marking the boundary between unclean and clean territory. When the missionaries perform this act against a Jewish city, they are treating it as pagan ground — declaring that the city has placed itself outside the sphere of God's people by refusing God's messengers.

The critical declaration — "yet be sure of this, that the kingdom of God has come near" — means that the kingdom's nearness is not contingent on acceptance. The kingdom has arrived whether the city welcomes it or not. What changes is the city's relationship to that arrival: for those who receive the message, the kingdom is salvation; for those who reject it, the kingdom is judgment. The same event is both.

The comparison to Sodom is devastating. Sodom's destruction (Genesis 19:24-25) was the paradigmatic example of divine judgment in Israel's memory. Yet Jesus says that "it will be more tolerable" (anektoteron estai) for Sodom than for the city that rejects his messengers. The logic is clear: Sodom never had the opportunity these cities have. Sodom was destroyed for its wickedness, but these cities are rejecting the kingdom of God itself, announced by God's own messengers. Greater privilege entails greater accountability. This principle is deeply rooted in the prophetic tradition: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2).

Verses 13-16: Woes on the Cities of Galilee

"'Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the miracles had been performed in Tyre and Sidon which occurred in you, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes. But it will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon in the judgment than for you. And you, Capernaum, will not be exalted to heaven, will you? You will be brought down to Hades! The one who listens to you listens to Me, and the one who rejects you rejects Me; and the one who rejects Me rejects the One who sent Me.'"

Jesus pronounces prophetic woes (ouai) on three Galilean cities. The form is that of the prophetic judgment oracle — the same genre used by Isaiah, Amos, Habakkuk, and others against cities and nations that failed to respond to God's revealed will.

Chorazin and Bethsaida are towns in the region of Galilee where Jesus concentrated his ministry. The author has not narrated specific episodes in Chorazin, but the woe presumes that the cities witnessed the "miracles" (dynameis, literally "powers, mighty works") and did not repent.

The comparison with Tyre and Sidon is pointed. These Phoenician cities were objects of extended prophetic denunciation. Isaiah 23 pronounces judgment on Tyre, describing its pride and commerce. Ezekiel 26-28 devotes three full chapters to Tyre's judgment, culminating in the declaration that its king said "I am a god, I sit in the seat of gods" (Ezekiel 28:2) and will be brought down. Ezekiel 28:21-23 pronounces judgment on Sidon as well. Joel 3:4-8 (4:4-8 LXX) condemns Tyre and Sidon for selling the people of Judah and Jerusalem as slaves. These cities represent Gentile arrogance, commercial exploitation, and hostility to Israel.

Yet Jesus declares that Tyre and Sidon would have repented if they had witnessed what Chorazin and Bethsaida saw. The image of repentance — "sitting in sackcloth and ashes" — is the conventional posture of mourning and repentance throughout the Hebrew scriptures (Job 42:6; Isaiah 58:5; Jonah 3:6; Daniel 9:3). The argument is counterfactual but devastating: pagan cities notorious for their wickedness would have responded to what Israel's own cities have ignored. This continues the pattern established in the Nazareth synagogue (4:25-27), in the centurion's faith (7:9), and throughout the Gospel: those outside Israel may be more responsive than those inside.

The woe on Capernaum is the sharpest. Jesus asks rhetorically whether Capernaum expects to be "exalted to heaven" (heōs ouranou hypsōthēsē) and answers with its opposite: "you will be brought down to Hades" (heōs hadou katabēsē). The language is drawn from Isaiah 14:13-15, part of the taunt against the king of Babylon: "You said in your heart, 'I will ascend to heaven; I will raise my throne above the stars of God'... But you will be brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit." In Isaiah's oracle, the king of Babylon represents human pride that claims divine prerogatives and is therefore cast down. Jesus applies this same language to Capernaum — his own base of operations, the city that hosted more of his ministry than any other. Capernaum's sin is not the flagrant wickedness of Babylon but the failure to respond to the unprecedented privilege of having the kingdom of God proclaimed in its streets. Proximity to Jesus without repentance is worse than distance from him in ignorance.

The concluding statement (v. 16) establishes a chain of representation: the seventy represent Jesus, and Jesus represents God. To listen to the messengers is to listen to Jesus; to listen to Jesus is to listen to the One who sent him. Conversely, to reject the messengers is to reject Jesus, and to reject Jesus is to reject God. This is the logic of the biblical shaliach — the authorized agent who fully represents the one who sent him. The principle has roots in the words of Moses: "the man who will not listen to My words which he shall speak in My name, I Myself will require it of him" (Deuteronomy 18:19). The seventy are not independent operators; they are extensions of Jesus's own mission, which is itself an extension of God's purpose.

Verses 17-20: The Return of the Seventy

"The seventy returned with joy, saying, 'Lord, even the demons are subject to us in Your name.' And He said to them, 'I was watching Satan fall from heaven like lightning. Behold, I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing will injure you. Nevertheless do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are recorded in heaven.'"

The seventy return with joy (meta charas), reporting that "even the demons are subject to us in Your name" (kai ta daimonia hypotassetai hēmin en tō onomati sou). The "even" (kai) suggests that this exceeded their expectations. They were sent to heal the sick and proclaim the kingdom; the subjection of demons was an unexpected extension of their authority. The phrase "in Your name" (en tō onomati sou) specifies the source of their power — they act not on their own authority but as representatives of Jesus, whose name carries the authority described in verse 16.

Jesus's response — "I was watching Satan fall from heaven like lightning" (etheōroun ton Satanan hōs astrapēn ek tou ouranou pesonta) — is one of the most compressed and significant statements in the Gospel. The imperfect tense etheōroun ("I was watching") suggests continuous or repeated observation, not a single past event. Several scriptural backgrounds converge.

Isaiah 14:12 (LXX): "How you have fallen from heaven, O morning star, son of the dawn!" — the taunt against the king of Babylon that Jesus has just applied to Capernaum (v. 15). The falling from heaven represents the overthrow of a power that claimed exalted status.

Job 1:6-12 and 2:1-7 present Satan (ha-satan, "the adversary") as a figure in the divine council who has access to heaven and who accuses the righteous before God. The "fall from heaven" could indicate a loss of this accusatory standing — the adversary's power to accuse is being broken.

The "like lightning" (hōs astrapēn) conveys suddenness and visibility. Lightning is instantaneous, dramatic, and unmistakable. Whatever is happening to Satan's power is not gradual but decisive.

The timing is significant. Jesus says he "was watching" this happen — correlating Satan's fall with the mission of the seventy. As they proclaimed the kingdom and demons submitted to them, Jesus perceived a cosmic shift: the authority of the adversary was being overthrown. The kingdom's advance through the seventy's mission is not merely a local phenomenon; it has consequences in the heavenly realm. The proclamation of the kingdom and the subjection of demons on earth correspond to the displacement of the accuser's authority in heaven.

Jesus then confirms and extends the authority: "I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy" (dedōka hymin tēn exousian tou patein epanō opheōn kai skorpiōn, kai epi pasan tēn dynamin tou echthrou). The imagery of treading on serpents and scorpions draws from Psalm 91:13 (90:13 LXX): "You will tread upon the lion and cobra, the young lion and the serpent you will trample down." In Psalm 91, this is a promise of divine protection for the one who dwells in the shelter of the Most High. The serpent imagery also reaches back to Genesis 3:15, where the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head. Scorpions appear in Deuteronomy 8:15 as one of the dangers of the wilderness through which God brought Israel safely. The combined image is of total authority over hostile spiritual forces — "all the power of the enemy" (pasan tēn dynamin tou echthrou), where "the enemy" (ho echthros) is Satan.

But Jesus immediately redirects their joy: "Nevertheless do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are recorded in heaven" (plēn en toutō mē chairete hoti ta pneumata hymin hypotassetai, chairete de hoti ta onomata hymōn engegraptai en tois ouranois). The contrast is between power exercised and identity secured. The subjection of spirits is real but temporary — it is tied to the exercise of mission. The recording of names in heaven speaks to permanent standing before God. The image of names written in a heavenly register appears in Exodus 32:32-33, where Moses pleads with God: "If You will, forgive their sin — and if not, please blot me out from Your book which You have written!" God replies: "Whoever has sinned against Me, I will blot him out of My book." It appears again in Daniel 12:1: "everyone who is found written in the book will be rescued." The register is not a permanent guarantee independent of faithfulness — as Exodus 32:33 makes explicit, names can be blotted out. But Jesus's point is that their deepest ground for joy is not what they can do (exercise authority over demons) but who they are before God (people whose names God has recorded). Power is delegated and can be revoked; relationship with God is the enduring reality.

Verses 21-24: Jesus's Thanksgiving and the Disciples' Privilege

"At that very time He rejoiced greatly in the Holy Spirit, and said, 'I praise You, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that You have hidden these things from the wise and intelligent and have revealed them to infants. Yes, Father, for this way was well-pleasing in Your sight. All things have been handed over to Me by My Father, and no one knows who the Son is except the Father, and who the Father is except the Son, and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal Him.'"

"Turning to the disciples, He said privately, 'Blessed are the eyes which see the things you see, for I say to you, that many prophets and kings wished to see the things which you see, and did not see them, and to hear the things which you hear, and did not hear them.'"

The note that Jesus "rejoiced greatly in the Holy Spirit" (ēgalliasato tō pneumati tō hagiō) is unusual. Luke rarely describes Jesus's emotional state in this way; the verb agalliaō means "to exult, to be overjoyed." This is the same verb used by Mary in the Magnificat: "my spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior" (1:47). The Spirit is identified as the source or sphere of this exultation, connecting Jesus's joy to the same Spirit who empowered his ministry (4:1, 14, 18).

The prayer addresses God as "Father" (pater) and "Lord of heaven and earth" (kyrie tou ouranou kai tēs gēs) — combining intimate relationship with sovereign authority. The substance of the prayer is a thanksgiving for a pattern of divine revelation that inverts human expectations: God has "hidden these things from the wise and intelligent" (apekrypsas tauta apo sophōn kai synetōn) and "revealed them to infants" (apekalypsas auta nēpiois).

The "wise and intelligent" (sophoi kai synetoi) in context are those who possess religious expertise and learning — the scribes, Pharisees, and lawyers who have rejected God's purpose (7:30) and whose woes will fill chapter 11. The "infants" (nēpioi) — literally, those who do not yet speak, the untaught, the unsophisticated — are the disciples, the seventy, the tax collectors and sinners, the common people who accepted John's baptism. The pattern is consistent with the Magnificat: God "has scattered those who were proud in the thoughts of their heart. He has brought down rulers from their thrones, and has exalted those who were humble" (1:51-52). It echoes Psalm 8:2 (LXX): "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies You have prepared praise." The hiddenness is not arbitrary cruelty; it is the consequence of sophistication that cannot receive what does not fit its categories, while simplicity can.

The declaration "all things have been handed over to Me by My Father" (panta moi paredothē hypo tou patros mou) asserts comprehensive authority delegated from the Father to the Son. The verb paradidōmi ("to hand over, to entrust") indicates a transfer of authority. This is the language of Daniel 7:14: "To him was given dominion, glory, and a kingdom." What has been handed over is "all things" (panta) — not a partial jurisdiction but total authority.

The mutual knowledge statement — "no one knows who the Son is except the Father, and who the Father is except the Son" — describes an exclusive reciprocal relationship. The word "knows" (ginōskei) denotes intimate, personal knowledge, not merely informational awareness. Only the Father truly knows the Son's identity, and only the Son truly knows the Father's character. The addition "and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal Him" (kai hō ean boulētai ho huios apokalypsai) places the Son in the position of mediator of divine knowledge. Access to the Father comes through the Son's choice to reveal. This connects directly to the preceding thanksgiving: the hiding from the wise and the revealing to infants is the Son's revelatory activity, carried out according to the Father's good pleasure.

Turning privately to the disciples, Jesus pronounces them "blessed" (makarioi) for what they see and hear. The comparison is with "prophets and kings" (prophētai kai basileis) — figures who longed to see what the disciples are witnessing. This is not a general statement about the superiority of the present over the past. It is a specific claim about the moment in Israel's story: the prophets spoke of what God would do; the kings yearned for the kingdom's restoration; the disciples are seeing it happen. Isaiah wrote of the blind seeing and the lame walking; the disciples have witnessed these things. David was promised an everlasting throne; the disciples are in the presence of the one who claims the Father's authority. The privilege is not earned; it is the accident of being present at the right moment in the divine plan and having eyes to see it.

Verses 25-29: The Lawyer's Question

"And a lawyer stood up and put Him to the test, saying, 'Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?' And He said to him, 'What is written in the Law? How does it read to you?' And he said, 'You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength, and with all your mind; and your neighbor as yourself.' And He said to him, 'You have answered correctly; do this and you will live.' But wishing to justify himself, he said to Jesus, 'And who is my neighbor?'"

A "lawyer" (nomikos) — an expert in the Torah, the Mosaic law — stands and asks a test question (ekpeirazōn auton): "What shall I do to inherit eternal life?" (ti poiēsas zōēn aiōnion klēronomēsō). The verb ekpeirazō means "to test, to put to the proof." The lawyer is not a sincere seeker but an examiner testing Jesus's competence. The question itself concerns "eternal life" (zōē aiōnios) — the life of the age to come, the age the prophets promised when God's kingdom would be fully established. The word klēronomeō ("to inherit") carries covenantal overtones: Israel was to "inherit" the promised land (Deuteronomy 4:1; 16:20), and the language was extended to the eschatological inheritance of life in God's restored creation (Daniel 12:2).

Jesus responds with a counter-question, sending the expert back to his own expertise: "What is written in the Law? How does it read to you?" (en tō nomō ti gegraptai; pōs anaginōskeis). This is pedagogically significant. Jesus does not supply an answer from outside the Torah; he directs the lawyer to find it within the text the lawyer claims to know.

The lawyer answers by combining two Torah passages:

First, Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength" — the Shema, the foundational confession of Israel recited daily. The lawyer adds "and with all your mind" (kai en holē tē dianoia sou), which may reflect the LXX rendering or an interpretive expansion. The Shema defines the relationship between Israel and God as total, undivided love.

Second, Leviticus 19:18: "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (agapēseis ton plēsion sou hōs seauton). In its original context in Leviticus 19, the "neighbor" (Hebrew: rea) is defined within a framework that begins with "the sons of your people" (Leviticus 19:18) but extends to "the stranger who resides with you" (Leviticus 19:34: "you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt").

The lawyer's combination of these two texts as the summary of the Torah is not unique to him; there is evidence that linking these two commandments was a recognized approach in first-century Judaism. Jesus affirms it: "You have answered correctly; do this and you will live" (orthōs apekrithēs; touto poiei kai zēsē). The phrase "do this and you will live" echoes Leviticus 18:5: "You shall keep My statutes and My judgments, by which a man may live if he does them; I am the LORD." The answer to inheriting eternal life is not a mystery — the Torah itself provides it. The question is whether the person asking will actually do it.

The lawyer's follow-up — "wishing to justify himself" (thelōn dikaiōsai heauton) — reveals his motive. The verb dikaioō means "to declare righteous, to vindicate." He wants to draw a boundary around the command that will place himself safely inside it. If "neighbor" can be defined narrowly enough, the command becomes manageable. The question "who is my neighbor?" (kai tis estin mou plēsion) is a request for a boundary: who counts and who does not? Who must I love and who am I free to ignore?

Verses 30-35: The Parable

"Jesus replied and said, 'A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and fell among robbers, and they stripped him and beat him, and went away leaving him half dead. And by chance a priest was going down on that road, and when he saw him, he passed by on the other side. Likewise a Levite also, when he came to the place and saw him, passed by on the other side. But a Samaritan, who was on a journey, came upon him; and when he saw him, he felt compassion, and came to him and bandaged his wounds, pouring oil and wine on them; and he put him on his own animal, and brought him to an inn and took care of him. On the next day he took out two denarii and gave them to the innkeeper and said, "Take care of him; and whatever more you spend, when I return I will repay you."'"

The parable is set on a specific road: the descent from Jerusalem to Jericho, a distance of about seventeen miles through desolate, rocky terrain that dropped approximately 3,300 feet in elevation. This road was notoriously dangerous, known for banditry. The setting is not abstract; it is a particular place that Jesus's audience would recognize as realistically perilous.

The victim is identified only as "a man" (anthrōpos tis). He has no ethnic, religious, or social markers. After the robbers strip him (ekdysantes), beat him (plēgas epithentes), and leave him "half dead" (hēmithanē), he is unrecognizable. His clothing, which would indicate his social status and possibly his religious identity, is gone. He cannot speak to identify himself. He is simply a human being in need. The stripping of identifying markers is essential to the parable's logic: the passersby cannot determine in advance whether this man is someone they are obligated to help.

The first passerby is a priest (hiereus). Priests served in the Jerusalem temple on a rotating basis and then returned home; many lived in Jericho. The priest is "going down" (katabainōn) — traveling from Jerusalem toward Jericho, meaning he has completed his temple service and is heading home. He sees the man and "passed by on the other side" (antiparēlthen). The verb antiparerchomai means to pass by on the opposite side — a deliberate detour to avoid contact.

Why would a priest avoid a man lying by the road? Several factors in first-century Jewish purity law are relevant. Contact with a dead body rendered a person ritually unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11-16) and required an elaborate purification process involving the ashes of a red heifer. A priest who became corpse-contaminated could not serve in the temple until purified. The man appears to be dead or nearly so. If the priest touched him and the man died, or was already dead, the priest would be contaminated. Leviticus 21:1-3 restricts priestly contact with the dead to immediate family members only. The high priest may not approach even his parents' bodies (Leviticus 21:11). The priest may therefore be acting in accordance with his understanding of Torah obligations — preserving his ritual purity takes precedence over the uncertain status of an unknown man.

However, there is a counter-obligation. The Torah also commands: "You shall not stand idly by the blood of your neighbor" (Leviticus 19:16, immediately preceding the "love your neighbor" command in 19:18). The question of which command takes priority — purity or mercy — is exactly the kind of question the rabbis debated. The parable forces the listener to confront this tension.

The second passerby is a Levite (Leuitēs). Levites were a subsidiary priestly class who assisted in temple worship — musicians, gatekeepers, administrators. The same purity concerns apply, though Levites were not held to quite as strict a standard as priests. The Levite's response is identical: he sees the man and passes by on the other side. The repetition establishes a pattern: the two figures most associated with Israel's temple and worship — priest and Levite — both fail to act.

The third traveler is a Samaritan (Samaritēs). For Jesus's audience, this would have been jarring. The hostility between Jews and Samaritans was deep and longstanding. It originated in the Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom (722 BC) and the subsequent settlement of foreign peoples in the territory (2 Kings 17:24-41). The Samaritans maintained their own temple on Mount Gerizim (destroyed by John Hyrcanus in 128 BC), rejected the authority of the Jerusalem temple, and accepted only the Pentateuch as scripture. From the Jewish perspective, Samaritans were at best schismatics and at worst idolatrous half-breeds. The hostility was mutual: the Samaritan village in Luke 9:52-53 refused to receive Jesus precisely because he was traveling to Jerusalem. Sirach 50:25-26 (a pre-Christian Jewish text) calls the people of Shechem (Samaritans) "the foolish people that live in Shechem" and says they are "not even a people."

The Samaritan's response contrasts with the priest and Levite at every point. He "felt compassion" (esplanchnisthē) — the same visceral, gut-level compassion attributed to Jesus at the raising of the widow's son (7:13) and to the father in the parable of the lost son (15:20). Where the priest and Levite saw and avoided, the Samaritan sees and is moved in his depths.

His actions are detailed and extravagant: - He "came to him" (prosēlthen) — he approaches rather than crossing to the other side. - He "bandaged his wounds" (katedēsen ta traumata autou) — providing direct medical care. - He poured "oil and wine" (elaion kai oinon) on the wounds — oil to soothe and wine as an antiseptic, standard first-century medical treatment. - He "put him on his own animal" (epibibasas auton epi to idion ktēnos) — sacrificing his own comfort and walking so the injured man could ride. - He "brought him to an inn" (ēgagen auton eis pandocheion) — providing shelter and continuing care. - He "took care of him" (epemelēthē autou) — personally tending to him through the night. - The next day, he gave two denarii (approximately two days' wages) to the innkeeper with instructions to care for the man, promising to reimburse any additional expense on his return.

Each action costs the Samaritan something: time, comfort, money, safety (stopping on a dangerous road makes him vulnerable too). His compassion is not a feeling that passes; it translates into sustained, costly action across an extended period. He does not simply drop the man at the inn; he stays overnight, pays in advance, and promises to return.

Verses 36-37: The Reversal of the Question

"'Which of these three do you think proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell into the robbers' hands?' And he said, 'The one who showed mercy toward him.' Then Jesus said to him, 'Go and do the same.'"

Jesus's question back to the lawyer is decisive, and it transforms the lawyer's original question in a way the lawyer did not anticipate. The lawyer asked "who is my neighbor?" — seeking to define the object of the command, to draw a circle around the people he is obligated to love. Jesus's counter-question asks "which of these three proved to be a neighbor?" (tis toutōn tōn triōn plēsion dokei soi gegonenai) — making the subject of neighborliness the one who acts, not the one who is acted upon.

The lawyer asked: who qualifies as a neighbor to me? Jesus answers: you become a neighbor by showing mercy. The question is not "whom must I love?" but "am I the kind of person who loves?" The command "love your neighbor as yourself" is not a directive to identify qualifying recipients and then love them. It is a call to become the kind of person who, when confronted with need, acts with mercy regardless of the sufferer's identity.

The lawyer's answer is revealing: "the one who showed mercy toward him" (ho poiēsas to eleos met' autou). He cannot bring himself to say "the Samaritan." He describes the action rather than naming the ethnicity. But Jesus does not press the point. He simply commands: "Go and do the same" (poreuou kai su poiei homoiōs).

The parable thus accomplishes several things simultaneously. It answers the lawyer's question about inheriting eternal life (v. 25) by making "do this and you will live" concrete rather than abstract. It dismantles the ethnic and religious boundaries that the lawyer's question implicitly assumed — a Samaritan, the most despised outsider, is the one who fulfills the Torah's command while the priest and Levite, the insiders par excellence, fail. It reverses the categories of clean and unclean: the priest and Levite preserve their ritual purity but violate the Torah's deeper command; the Samaritan, who is ritually unacceptable to the Jewish system, is the one who enacts the mercy that the Torah requires. And it continues the pattern that runs through Luke's Gospel: the outsiders who should be furthest from God's purposes are the ones who embody them, while the insiders who should be closest are the ones who fail.

The connection to the Samaritan rejection in Luke 9:52-56 is unmistakable. James and John wanted to destroy the Samaritan village that rejected Jesus; Jesus rebuked them. Now, one chapter later, a Samaritan is the hero of a story about what the Torah actually demands. The juxtaposition is deliberate: the impulse to condemn and destroy the Samaritan (James and John's reaction) is the opposite of the Torah's command to love and show mercy. The Samaritan who was rejected is now the one who shows what covenant faithfulness looks like.

Verses 38-42: Martha and Mary

"Now as they were traveling along, He entered a village; and a woman named Martha welcomed Him into her home. She had a sister called Mary, who was seated at the Lord's feet, listening to His word. But Martha was distracted with all her preparations; and she came up to Him and said, 'Lord, do You not care that my sister has left me to do all the serving alone? Then tell her to help me.' But the Lord answered and said to her, 'Martha, Martha, you are worried and bothered about so many things; but only one thing is necessary, for Mary has chosen the good part, which shall not be taken away from her.'"

The transition "as they were traveling along" (en de tō poreuesthai autous) keeps the episode within the journey to Jerusalem. The unnamed village is later identified in John's Gospel as Bethany (John 11:1), but Luke does not name it here. Martha "welcomed Him into her home" (hypedexato auton) — the verb hypodechomai means to receive as a guest, to offer hospitality. Martha is the householder and host.

Mary's posture is described with precision: she "was seated at the Lord's feet" (parakathestheisa pros tous podas tou kyriou). To sit at someone's feet is the posture of a disciple learning from a teacher. When Paul describes his education, he says he was "brought up at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3). The phrase "listening to His word" (ēkouen ton logon autou) completes the picture: Mary has taken the position of a student receiving instruction from a rabbi. In the first-century Jewish world, this was an unusual posture for a woman. Women were not typically accepted as students of Torah. The Mishnah (Sotah 3:4) records a debate about whether a man should teach his daughter Torah, with Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus declaring that "whoever teaches his daughter Torah is as though he taught her obscenity." While this text is later, it reflects attitudes that were likely present in the first century. Mary's action transgresses a social boundary.

Martha, meanwhile, is "distracted with all her preparations" (periespato peri pollēn diakonian). The word periespato means "to be pulled in different directions, to be overwhelmed." The word diakonia means "service, ministry" — it is the same word used for the practical service of the community. Martha's work is not trivial; hospitality was a sacred duty in Jewish culture (Genesis 18:1-8, Abraham's reception of the three visitors). She is doing what a good host is supposed to do.

Her complaint to Jesus is direct: "Lord, do You not care that my sister has left me to do all the serving alone?" (kyrie, ou melei soi hoti hē adelphē mou monēn me katelipen diakonein). She addresses Jesus as "Lord" and asks whether he cares (melei) — the same verb used in Mark 4:38 when the disciples wake Jesus during the storm ("Teacher, do You not care that we are perishing?"). Martha is not merely frustrated; she feels abandoned and wants Jesus to intervene on her behalf. She asks Jesus to "tell her to help me" (eipe oun autē hina moi synantilabētai) — she wants Jesus to exercise his authority to redirect Mary back to the kitchen.

Jesus's response is gentle but clear. The doubled name "Martha, Martha" (Martha, Martha) signals affection and concern, not rebuke — the repetition of a name in direct address appears elsewhere as a sign of intimate urgency (Genesis 22:11, "Abraham, Abraham!"; Exodus 3:4, "Moses, Moses!"; 1 Samuel 3:10, "Samuel, Samuel!").

"You are worried and bothered about so many things" (merimnās kai thorybazē peri polla). The verb merimnaō means "to be anxious, to worry" — it is the same word Jesus uses in 12:22 ("do not worry about your life, as to what you will eat") and that appears in the parable of the sower for the cares that choke the word (8:14). The verb thorybazō means "to be disturbed, to be in turmoil." Martha's activity, though well-intentioned, has produced anxiety and inner turbulence.

"But only one thing is necessary" (henos de estin chreia). The "one thing" stands in contrast to the "many things" that have fragmented Martha's attention. What is that one thing? The context identifies it: Mary's choice — sitting at Jesus's feet and listening to his word. This is not a permanent declaration that practical service is worthless. It is a statement about priorities in this particular moment: when Jesus is present and teaching, hearing his word takes precedence over preparing food for him.

"Mary has chosen the good part" (Maria gar tēn agathēn merida exelexato). The word meris means "portion, share, part" — it is the word used for a share of an inheritance or a portion of food at a meal. Ironically, while Martha is preparing portions of food, Mary has chosen the better "portion." The phrase echoes Psalm 16:5 (15:5 LXX): "The LORD is the portion of my inheritance and my cup." Mary's chosen portion is the Lord's word itself.

"Which shall not be taken away from her" (hētis ouk aphairethēsetai autēs) — this is a promise of permanence. The food Martha prepares will be consumed and gone. The word Mary receives will remain. This connects to the parable of the sower in Luke 8, where the seed that falls on good soil represents those who "hear the word with a noble and good heart, and hold it fast, and bear fruit with perseverance" (8:15). Mary is the good soil, and Jesus will not take the word back from her.

The episode also connects backward to the sending of the seventy. The seventy were sent to proclaim the kingdom and to heal. When they entered a house, the household was to receive them and listen. Martha and Mary represent two responses to the arrival of the kingdom in one's home: service (which is good but can become anxiety) and receptive listening (which Jesus identifies as the necessary thing). After a chapter about mission, confrontation with rejection, the nature of true neighborliness, and the question of what the Torah requires, the chapter closes with the simplest and most fundamental answer: sit at the feet of the one who speaks, and listen.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 10 is organized around the question of what faithful response to the kingdom of God looks like, explored from multiple angles.

The sending of the seventy (vv. 1-16) extends the mission beyond the twelve apostles to a larger body, possibly symbolizing the nations through the number seventy(-two). The missionaries carry Jesus's own authority and represent him as he represents the Father. The stakes of acceptance and rejection are absolute: cities that reject the message face a judgment worse than Sodom's, and the cities where Jesus did most of his work — Chorazin, Bethsaida, Capernaum — are condemned for their failure to repent despite the privilege they received. Greater proximity to the kingdom means greater accountability.

The return and Jesus's response (vv. 17-24) interpret the mission's success as a cosmic event: Satan's authority is being overthrown as the kingdom advances. But Jesus redirects the disciples' joy from the power they exercise to the relationship they possess — their names recorded in heaven. His prayer reveals that the pattern of divine disclosure favors the simple over the sophisticated, and his private words to the disciples declare that they are witnessing what prophets and kings longed to see. The privilege is not their accomplishment but their placement at this moment in God's plan.

The lawyer's question and the parable (vv. 25-37) confront the attempt to limit the scope of the Torah's demand. The lawyer knows the right answer — love God and love your neighbor — but wants to draw the circle small enough to be comfortable inside it. Jesus's parable demolishes every boundary the lawyer might construct. The neighbor is not defined by ethnicity, religion, or social standing. The priest and Levite — the Torah's institutional guardians — fail to do what the Torah commands, while the Samaritan — the despised outsider — embodies the mercy the Torah requires. The reversal is complete: the question is not "who qualifies as my neighbor?" but "will I be a neighbor to whoever needs me?" This directly subverts the ethnic hostility of Luke 9:52-56, where the impulse was to destroy the Samaritan village. Here the Samaritan is the one who knows what the Torah means.

Martha and Mary (vv. 38-42) close the chapter with the most intimate setting: a home, two sisters, and Jesus's word. Martha's service is not condemned, but Mary's listening is declared the necessary thing. After a chapter about sending, speaking, rejection, acceptance, mercy, and the Torah's demand, the final image is of a woman sitting at Jesus's feet in silence, receiving his word. The chapter that began with seventy being sent out ends with one person sitting still. Both are faithful responses: the seventy go because they are sent; Mary stays because Jesus is present. The common element is attention to Jesus's word — whether carrying it to others or receiving it oneself.

The chapter thus connects Luke 9 and Luke 11 by enacting the transition from calling (the cost of discipleship in 9:57-62) to formation (what the life of a disciple looks like in practice). The seventy learn that their identity matters more than their power. The lawyer learns that the Torah demands mercy without boundaries. Mary learns — or rather, demonstrates that she already knows — that the one necessary thing is to hear the word. This prepares for Luke 11, where the disciples will ask Jesus to teach them to pray (the deepest form of attending to God) and where the Pharisees and lawyers will be condemned for precisely the failure the lawyer and the priest and Levite exhibited: knowing the Torah's demands and not doing them.
