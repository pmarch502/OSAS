Romans 6 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Romans 5): Paul had moved from the demonstration that justification comes through faith (chapters 3-4) to an exploration of what that justification produces. The chapter opened: "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:1). The verb dikaiothentes ("having been justified") is aorist passive participle — a completed action: justification has already happened for those who have faith. What follows from it is eirene ("peace") — not an emotional state but a relational reality, the cessation of hostility between God and those who were formerly his enemies. Paul developed this through a sequence: justification produces peace, peace gives access to grace, grace produces hope, hope does not disappoint because God's love has been poured out through the Holy Spirit (5:1-5). He then amplified the magnitude of God's love: "while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly" (5:6). The word asebeis ("ungodly, impious") is the same word used in 1:18 for those against whom God's wrath is revealed — the gospel is the power of God directed precisely at those who were under wrath. Paul pressed the point: "God demonstrates his own love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (5:8). The logic ran forward: if God reconciled us while we were enemies (echthroi, 5:10), how much more will we be saved through Christ's life now that we are reconciled. The "how much more" (pollo mallon) argument established that the future completion of salvation is more certain than its initiation, because the harder thing (reconciling enemies) has already been accomplished.

In the second half of chapter 5 (5:12-21), Paul introduced the Adam-Christ typology. Through one man (Adam), sin entered the world, and through sin, death, and death spread to all human beings (5:12). Paul's key term here was basileuein ("to reign, to rule as king"). Death reigned (ebasileusen ho thanatos, 5:14, 17). Sin reigned in death (ebasileusen he hamartia en to thanato, 5:21). These are sovereignty claims: sin and death are described not merely as acts or events but as ruling powers, tyrants exercising dominion over human beings. Against this reign of sin and death, Paul set the counter-reign: "those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness will reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ" (5:17). Grace reigns through righteousness leading to eternal life (5:21). The chapter ended on this note of superabundance: "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (5:20, hou de epleonasen he hamartia, hyperperisseusen he charis). The verb hyperperisseusen ("super-abounded, overflowed beyond measure") is intensified by the prefix hyper- — grace did not merely match sin's increase but overwhelmed it.

This conclusion created a logical opening that Paul knew his hearers would notice. If grace increases in proportion to sin, then more sin produces more grace. Paul had already anticipated this objection in 3:8, where he noted that some slanderously accused him of teaching "let us do evil that good may come." Having now stated the principle of superabounding grace in its strongest form (5:20), Paul must address the objection directly. Romans 6 is that address.

What the author argues immediately after (Romans 7): Romans 7 extends the death metaphor from chapter 6 into a new domain: the believer's relationship to the Torah. Paul opens with a legal analogy drawn from marriage: "Do you not know, brothers — for I am speaking to those who know the law — that the law is binding on a person only as long as he lives?" (7:1). The principle is that death dissolves legal obligations. A married woman is bound to her husband while he lives; if he dies, she is released from the law of the husband (7:2-3). Paul applies this to the believer's situation: "you also have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another, to him who has been raised from the dead, in order that we may bear fruit for God" (7:4). The death that chapter 6 described as dying to sin is now described as dying to the law. The purpose of this death is stated as belonging to another (the risen Christ) and bearing fruit for God — precisely the fruit of holiness that 6:22 anticipated. Paul then contrasts two modes of existence: "when we were living in the flesh, our sinful passions, aroused by the law, were at work in our members to bear fruit for death" (7:5), versus "now we are released from the law, having died to that which held us captive" (7:6). The vocabulary of fruit (karpos), members (mele), death (thanatos), and slavery/captivity carries directly from chapter 6.

Chapter 7 then turns to the question this arrangement provokes: "Is the law sin?" (7:7) — just as chapter 6 addressed "shall we continue in sin that grace may abound?" Both questions are objections that Paul's argument seems to invite, and both receive the same initial answer: me genoito. Paul then develops the complex relationship between the law, sin, and the human being through the rest of chapter 7, culminating in the cry "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" (7:24) — answered by "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (7:25), which in turn sets up the life-in-the-Spirit exposition of chapter 8.

How chapter 6 connects these: Romans 5 ended with the reign of grace through righteousness. Romans 7 will address the believer's death to the law. Romans 6 is the necessary bridge: it explains how grace's superabundance does not license sin (answering the objection from 5:20), and it establishes the mechanism of death-with-Christ that chapter 7 will apply to the law. The death metaphor is chapter 6's central contribution: the believer has died to sin (6:2), was buried with Christ through baptism (6:4), was crucified with Christ (6:6), and has died to the regime under which sin exercised authority. This same death-mechanism will be applied to the law in 7:4-6. Chapter 6 also introduces the slavery metaphor (6:15-23) that reframes the question of human existence as a question of lordship: every person serves either sin or righteousness, and the transfer from one master to the other occurred at a definite point. The chapter thus answers the moral objection raised by the end of chapter 5 (does grace encourage sin?), grounds that answer in the concrete reality of baptism (the believer's identification with Christ's death and resurrection), and prepares for chapter 7's discussion of the law by establishing that death dissolves prior obligations and allegiances. The three chapters form a continuous argument: grace reigns (ch. 5), grace does not license sin because the believer has died to sin and is alive to God (ch. 6), and by the same death the believer has been released from the law's jurisdiction to serve in the new way of the Spirit (ch. 7).

Verses 1-4: The Question, the Answer, and the Baptismal Reality

"What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" (6:1).

The phrase ti oun eroumen ("what then shall we say?") is Paul's characteristic diatribe formula for introducing an objection that his own argument has provoked (cf. 3:5, 4:1, 7:7, 8:31, 9:14, 9:30). The question is not rhetorical filler; it voices precisely the conclusion that a hearer of 5:20 would be tempted to draw. The verb epimenomen (present active subjunctive of epimeno, "to remain upon, to persist in, to continue in") is stronger than a simple "shall we sin." Epimeno means to stay, to abide, to persevere in a condition or activity. The prefix epi- intensifies the staying: shall we keep on persisting in sin? The dative te hamartia ("in sin") treats sin as a sphere or domain in which one dwells — the same spatial metaphor Paul used in 3:9 (hyph' hamartian, "under sin"). The purpose clause hina he charis pleonase ("in order that grace may increase") completes the logic: if the ratio of grace to sin described in 5:20 holds, then increasing sin increases grace, and maximizing sin maximizes grace.

The objection is not frivolous. It is the logical consequence of Paul's own statement. If a hearer takes 5:20 in isolation, the conclusion is inescapable. Paul's answer will not be to qualify or retract 5:20 but to show that the objection rests on a misunderstanding of what happened to the person who received grace.

"By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it?" (6:2).

Me genoito — the same emphatic negation used in 3:4, 6, 31. Paul deploys it at precisely the moments when his argument might be distorted into its opposite.

The answer is not a moral exhortation ("you shouldn't") or a doctrinal qualification ("grace doesn't work that way"). It is a statement of fact about the condition of the people in question: hoitines apethanomen te hamartia ("we who died to sin"). The relative pronoun hoitines ("we who," "we who are of the kind that") is qualitative — it identifies the group by a defining characteristic. The characteristic is death: apethanomen (aorist active of apothnesko, "to die"). The aorist tense points to a specific, completed event: we died. This is not a gradual process or an aspiration; it is something that has already happened. The dative te hamartia ("to sin") indicates the relationship that death dissolved: the bond between the person and sin has been severed by death.

The question pos eti zesomen en aute ("how shall we still live in it?") uses the future indicative zesomen ("shall we live") and the adverb eti ("still, yet, any longer"). The logic is: if we have died to sin, then our existence in sin is over. Living in something you have died to is a contradiction. A dead person does not continue the activities of life. Paul's argument is not "you must not sin" but "you cannot go on living in the domain of sin because you have exited that domain through death." The question "how?" is not a moral appeal but a logical impossibility: how can a corpse still inhabit the house it died in?

The critical question for the first-century hearer is: when did this death occur? Paul answers immediately.

"Or do you not know that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?" (6:3).

The phrase e agnoeite ("or are you ignorant? or do you not know?") assumes the audience does know — it is a rhetorical formula that appeals to shared knowledge (cf. 7:1, "do you not know, brothers, for I am speaking to those who know the law"; 1 Corinthians 3:16, 6:2, 6:3, 6:9, 6:15, 6:19). Paul is not introducing new teaching but reminding them of what they should already understand from their baptismal instruction.

The verb ebaptisthemen (aorist passive of baptizo, "to immerse, to dip, to plunge, to wash ritually") occurs twice in the verse. The word baptizo in non-religious Greek usage means to plunge, to sink, to submerge — it is used of ships sinking, of cloth being dyed by immersion, of being overwhelmed by disaster. In the LXX, the related noun baptismos does not appear, but ritual washing (immersion in water for purification) is prescribed throughout the Torah: the purification of priests (Exodus 29:4, 40:12; Leviticus 8:6), the cleansing of lepers (Leviticus 14:8-9), the purification from contact with the dead (Numbers 19:7-19), and various other states of ritual impurity (Leviticus 15). In all these cases, immersion in water marks a transition from one state to another — from unclean to clean, from outside the community to within it, from the domain of death-contamination to the domain of life. The proselyte baptism practiced in first-century Judaism (attested in later rabbinic sources but with roots traceable to this period) functioned similarly: a Gentile's immersion marked the transition from being outside the covenant people to being within them. The convert was said to be "like a newborn child" — the old identity was dissolved and a new one began.

Paul's construction "baptized into Christ Jesus" (ebaptisthemen eis Christon Iesoun) uses the preposition eis ("into, toward, unto") — not en ("in") or epi ("upon"). The preposition indicates direction and incorporation: baptism moves the person into Christ, into union with him, into participation in his story. This is not merely a symbolic ceremony; Paul treats it as the event in which the believer's story becomes fused with Christ's story. The second clause specifies what part of Christ's story: "into his death" (eis ton thanaton autou ebaptisthemen). The death of Christ is the reality into which the baptized person has been plunged.

"Therefore we were buried with him through baptism into death, in order that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life" (6:4).

The verb synetaphemen (aorist passive of synthaptein, "to bury together with, to be co-buried") is the first of several syn- ("with") compound verbs in this chapter (cf. synestauroothe, 6:6; syzesomen, 6:8). Each describes a shared experience between the believer and Christ. The aorist tense again points to a completed event: the burial already happened. The medium was baptism: dia tou baptismatos ("through the baptism"). The purpose is stated with eis ton thanaton ("into the death") — the burial was into death, sharing in the reality of Christ's death.

The purpose clause (hina hosper egerthe Christos ek nekron dia tes doxes tou patros, houtos kai hemeis en kainoteti zoes peripatesomen) draws a parallel between Christ's experience and the believer's. The connector hosper... houtos ("just as... so also") establishes proportional correspondence. Just as Christ was raised (egerthe, aorist passive of egeiro, "to raise, to awaken") from the dead (ek nekron) through the glory of the Father (dia tes doxes tou patros), so also we might walk (peripatesomen, aorist active subjunctive of peripateo, "to walk around, to conduct one's life, to live") in newness of life (en kainoteti zoes).

The phrase dia tes doxes tou patros ("through the glory of the Father") is significant. The word doxa ("glory") in the LXX is the standard translation of the Hebrew kavod — the weighty, radiant, manifest presence of God. God's doxa was the pillar of cloud and fire that led Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 16:10, 40:34-38), the radiance that filled the tabernacle and later the temple (1 Kings 8:10-11), the vision that Ezekiel saw departing from and returning to the temple (Ezekiel 10:4, 18-19; 43:2-5). God raised Christ by the same power that manifested itself throughout Israel's history as the kavod — the visible, active presence of God in the world.

The word kainotes ("newness, new quality") combined with zoe ("life") describes not just a continuation of the old life with minor adjustments but an entirely new quality of existence. The word kainos (as opposed to neos) in Greek emphasizes qualitative newness — something unprecedented in kind, not merely recent in time. The verb peripateo ("to walk") is the standard LXX metaphor for the conduct of life, translating the Hebrew halak ("to walk"): "walk in my statutes" (Leviticus 26:3), "walk before me" (Genesis 17:1), "walk in the way of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 5:33; Judges 2:22). To "walk in newness of life" is to conduct one's entire existence in a new mode.

The verb peripatesomen is subjunctive (hina... peripatesomen — "in order that we might walk"), not indicative. This is a purpose clause, not a simple prediction. The baptismal death-and-burial occurred so that a new walk might result. The subjunctive carries the force of intended outcome: the point of the death was the new life. But the subjunctive also leaves the walking as something to be enacted, not merely experienced passively. The believer has been buried with Christ; the purpose is that the believer would now walk accordingly.

A structural observation: Paul's sequence follows the sequence of Christ's own experience: death (v. 3), burial (v. 4a), resurrection (v. 4b). The believer's baptism enacts the same trajectory: plunged into Christ's death, buried with him, raised to walk in new life. But there is a notable asymmetry. Christ's resurrection is stated in the indicative (egerthe — "was raised," a fact). The believer's corresponding new life is stated in the subjunctive (peripatesomen — "might walk," a purpose). Christ's resurrection is accomplished; the believer's new walk is the intended result that must be lived out.

Verses 5-7: United in Death, United in Resurrection; the Crucified Old Humanity

"For if we have been united with him in the likeness of his death, we will certainly also be united with him in the likeness of his resurrection" (6:5).

The word symphytoi (from symphytos, "grown together, planted together, conjoined by nature") is a botanical metaphor. It describes two things that have been grafted or grown into one another — like a scion grafted onto rootstock that becomes organically one with the tree. The word occurs only here in the New Testament. Its force is organic union, not mere association. The believers have not simply witnessed Christ's death or agreed to its significance; they have been fused with it, grafted into it, made organically one with it.

The phrase to homoiomati tou thanatou autou ("in the likeness of his death") uses homoioma ("likeness, form, resemblance, that which is made similar"). Paul does not say the believers literally died the same physical death Christ died. Their baptism was a death in the likeness of his death — an enactment that participates in the reality without being identical to it. The same word homoioma appears in 8:3 ("God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh") where it indicates genuine participation in human existence without identity with sin.

The conditional structure (ei gar... alla kai) moves from the established condition to its necessary consequence. If the union with his death has happened (gegonamen, perfect active of ginomai, "we have become" — the perfect tense indicates a past event with continuing results), then union with his resurrection will certainly follow (esometha, future middle of eimi, "we will be"). The word alla ("but, indeed, certainly") here functions to strengthen the consequence: we will certainly also be. The future tense esometha is important: while death with Christ is a past event (gegonamen, perfect), the full participation in his resurrection is cast as future. Paul is not saying the resurrection life is entirely future — the "newness of life" in verse 4 is a present reality — but the full, bodily resurrection that corresponds to Christ's own bodily resurrection remains ahead. The believer currently walks in new life; the believer will be fully united with the resurrection.

"Knowing this, that our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be rendered powerless, so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin" (6:6).

The phrase touto ginoskontes ("knowing this") introduces the content of the believers' understanding — what they are to recognize about what happened.

The subject is ho palaios hemon anthropos ("our old man," "our old self," "the old humanity that was ours"). The word palaios means "old, ancient, worn out, obsolete" — it is the opposite of kainos (the "newness" of verse 4). The anthropos is not merely an individual person but a mode of human existence. Paul uses the phrase only here and in Colossians 3:9 and Ephesians 4:22, but the concept pervades his thought. The "old humanity" corresponds to the Adamic humanity described in 5:12-21 — the humanity that exists under the reign of sin and death, the humanity inherited from Adam. This old humanity synestauroothe ("was crucified with," aorist passive of systauroo, "to crucify together with") Christ. The syn- prefix again indicates shared experience: the old Adamic mode of existence was nailed to the cross with Christ. The aorist tense locates this as a completed event.

The purpose (hina) is twofold, stated in sequence. First: hina katargethe to soma tes hamartias ("in order that the body of sin might be rendered powerless, abolished, done away with"). The verb katargeo is the same word Paul used in 3:3 (will human unfaithfulness "nullify" God's faithfulness?), in 3:31 (do we "overthrow" the law by faith?), and which will appear in 7:2 and 7:6. It means to render inoperative, to deprive of power, to make ineffective. The subject being rendered powerless is to soma tes hamartias — "the body of sin." This phrase does not mean that the physical body is inherently sinful. The genitive tes hamartias ("of sin") indicates the body insofar as it belongs to sin, is under sin's control, serves as sin's instrument. In Paul's preceding argument, the "members" (mele) of the body served as instruments of unrighteousness (the language he will use explicitly in 6:13). The "body of sin" is the human body in its function as sin's tool, sin's vehicle, sin's property. The crucifixion of the old humanity was aimed at stripping sin of its instrument — not destroying the body itself but destroying sin's ownership of the body.

The second purpose clause follows from the first: tou meketi douleuein hemas te hamartia ("so that we would no longer serve sin as slaves"). The verb douleuo ("to serve as a slave, to be in bondage to, to be enslaved") is the verbal form of doulos ("slave"). The infinitive douleuein with the negative meketi ("no longer") describes the intended outcome: the enslaving relationship is terminated. The adverb meketi ("no longer") is critical — it presupposes that they were formerly enslaved. The crucifixion of the old self was the act of manumission, the liberation of the slave from the slave-master.

The slavery metaphor here connects directly to Israel's foundational narrative. The Exodus was the paradigmatic act of liberation from bondage. In Egypt, Israel was enslaved (douleuein is used in the LXX for Israel's slavery: Exodus 1:13-14, edouloun... en erga sklera, "they enslaved them with hard labor"; Exodus 6:5, hous hoi Aigyptioi katadouloontai autous, "whom the Egyptians hold in bondage"). God's act of redemption was to free them from that slavery: "I am the LORD, and I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, and I will deliver you from slavery to them, and I will redeem you" (Exodus 6:6, LXX: lytroosomai hymas). The redemption language of 3:24 (apolytrosis, "redemption, release by ransom payment") already drew on this Exodus vocabulary. Now Paul develops the other side of the Exodus paradigm: liberation from bondage. The believer's situation mirrors Israel's: formerly enslaved (to sin, as Israel was to Egypt), liberated through a decisive act of God (the death of Christ, as Israel was freed through the Passover and the sea crossing), and now called to walk in a new mode of existence (newness of life, as Israel was called to walk in God's statutes in the wilderness and the promised land).

"For the one who has died has been freed from sin" (6:7).

The Greek reads ho gar apothanon dedikaiootai apo tes hamartias. The verb dedikaiootai is the perfect passive of dikaioo ("to justify, to declare righteous, to acquit, to set free"). The perfect tense indicates a completed action with continuing effect: the one who has died stands in a permanent state of having been justified/freed from sin. The preposition apo ("from, away from") indicates separation: justified away from sin, released from sin's claim.

The legal metaphor is precise. In the ancient world, death dissolved all legal claims. A dead slave could not be compelled to serve. A dead debtor owed nothing. A dead criminal could not be punished further. The principle appears in rabbinic teaching: "When a person dies, he is freed from the Torah and the commandments" (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 30a; Niddah 61b — though these texts are later, the principle they articulate was already operative in first-century Jewish legal reasoning). Paul applies this principle to the believer's relationship with sin: the one who has died with Christ (the old humanity crucified, v. 6) has been acquitted from sin's jurisdiction. Sin's legal claim over the dead person is void.

The use of dikaioo here rather than eleutheroo ("to set free") connects this verse to the letter's running argument about justification. The believer is not only declared righteous (the courtroom metaphor of chapters 3-4) but is also freed from sin's mastery (the slavery metaphor emerging in chapter 6). The two images — the acquitted defendant and the liberated slave — converge in this single verb.

Verses 8-11: The Logic of Co-Death and Co-Life, and the Command to Reckon

"Now if we have died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him" (6:8).

The conditional ei de ("but if, now if") introduces a premise Paul treats as established: we died with Christ (apethanomen syn Christo, aorist — a completed event). The conclusion draws from that premise: pisteuomen hoti kai syzesomen auto ("we believe that we will also live with him"). The verb pisteuomen ("we believe, we trust") is present active — it describes the current, ongoing stance of the believers. The verb syzesomen ("we will live with," future active of syzao, "to live together with") is future tense. The future here may encompass both the present experience of new life (already described in v. 4 as "walking in newness of life") and the ultimate future resurrection. The syn- prefix ("with") maintains the theme of shared experience with Christ: just as the death was shared, so the life will be shared.

"We know that Christ, having been raised from the dead, no longer dies; death no longer has dominion over him" (6:9).

The participle eidotes ("knowing") introduces another element of the believers' knowledge (parallel to ginoskontes in v. 6). The content of this knowledge is about Christ's resurrection and its permanence.

The phrase Christos egertheis ek nekron ("Christ having been raised from the dead") uses the aorist passive participle of egeiro: Christ was raised by another (God the Father, as implied by 6:4, "through the glory of the Father"). The consequence: ouketi apothneskeei ("he no longer dies"). The adverb ouketi ("no longer") is the same construction as meketi in verse 6 — it marks a permanent change from a prior condition. Christ entered death once; he exited permanently. The present tense apothneskeei ("dies") with the negative ouketi creates a statement of permanent condition: there is no future dying for Christ.

The second statement — thanatos autou ouketi kyrieuei ("death no longer exercises lordship over him") — introduces a key verb: kyrieuei (from kyrieuo, "to lord over, to exercise dominion over, to be master of"). The word is built on kyrios ("lord, master"). Death, which once exercised lordship over Christ (he was dead, in the tomb, under death's power), no longer does so. The resurrection was the termination of death's dominion. Paul is using the sovereignty language of chapter 5: death "reigned" (ebasileusen, 5:14, 17). Here the vocabulary shifts from basileuein ("to reign as king") to kyrieuein ("to lord over"), but the concept is the same: death is a power that held dominion, and that dominion has been broken. This same verb kyrieuein will reappear in verse 14 in the statement "sin shall not have dominion over you" — connecting Christ's liberation from death's dominion to the believer's liberation from sin's dominion.

"For the death he died, he died to sin once for all; but the life he lives, he lives to God" (6:10).

The construction ho gar apethanen, te hamartia apethanen ephapax ("for what he died, he died to sin once for all") uses the neuter relative pronoun ho ("what, that which") as an accusative of respect or reference: "as for the dying he died." The dative te hamartia ("to sin") parallels the dative te hamartia in verse 2 ("we who died to sin"). Christ's relationship to sin has been definitively terminated by his death, just as the believers' relationship to sin was terminated by their co-death with him.

The word ephapax ("once for all, once and for all time") is compound: epi + hapax ("upon + once"). It denotes a single, unrepeatable, permanently effective event. The word appears in the LXX in contexts of finality, and Paul uses it here to emphasize that Christ's death was not a repeatable ritual (like the annual Day of Atonement, where the high priest entered the Holy of Holies each year with blood) but a single act with permanent effect. The contrast with the Yom Kippur ritual is implicit: the old hilasterion (3:25) required annual repetition; Christ's death as the hilasterion was ephapax — once for all.

The contrasting clause: ho de ze, ze to theo ("but what he lives, he lives to God"). The same grammatical structure describes Christ's present life. The dative to theo ("to God") is the counterpart of te hamartia: as his death terminated his relationship to sin, so his life is oriented entirely toward God. The relationship is exclusive: to God and to God alone.

"In the same way, consider yourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus" (6:11).

The phrase houtos kai hymeis ("so also you") applies the pattern of Christ's experience (vv. 9-10) to the believers. The verb logizesthe (present middle/passive imperative of logizomai, "to reckon, to count, to consider, to regard") is the same verb used throughout chapter 4 for God's "reckoning" of faith as righteousness (4:3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23, 24). In chapter 4, God does the reckoning; here, the believers are commanded to reckon. The imperative mood makes this a command, not a suggestion: you are to regard yourselves, count yourselves, calculate yourselves to be dead to sin and alive to God.

The object of the reckoning: heautous nekrous men te hamartia zontas de to theo ("yourselves as dead indeed to sin, but alive to God"). The adjective nekrous ("dead") and the participle zontas ("living, alive") describe the two-sided reality: dead in one direction, alive in another. The datives te hamartia ("to sin") and to theo ("to God") are parallel — they identify the two poles of the believer's existence. The relationship with sin has the character of death (termination, non-response, cessation). The relationship with God has the character of life (responsiveness, activity, vitality).

The phrase en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") locates this dual reality. It is not a status the believer achieves independently but one that exists "in Christ" — within the sphere of Christ's own death-to-sin and life-to-God. The believer's death and life are derivative of and dependent upon Christ's death and life.

A pronoun observation: Paul shifts from the first person plural ("we" in vv. 2-10: "we who died," "we were baptized," "we were buried," "we have been united," "our old self," "we believe") to the second person plural ("you" in v. 11: hymeis, "you... consider yourselves"). The shift marks a transition from description (what happened to us) to command (what you must do about it). The indicative gives way to the imperative. Paul has established what is true; now he tells them how to live in light of what is true.

Verses 12-14: The Command Not to Let Sin Reign, and the Promise

"Therefore do not let sin reign in your mortal body so that you obey its desires" (6:12).

The word oun ("therefore") draws the imperative from the indicative: because you have died to sin and are alive to God, therefore act accordingly. The verb basileueto (present active imperative of basileuo, "to reign, to rule as king") connects directly to the reign-language of chapter 5. Sin "reigned" (ebasileusen) in 5:14, 17, 21 — it exercised royal dominion over humanity. Now Paul commands: me basileueto he hamartia ("do not let sin reign") — stop sin from continuing its royal rule. The present imperative with the negative me can mean either "stop doing what you are already doing" or "do not let this begin." In either case, the command presupposes that the believers have agency in the matter: sin's reign is something they can resist, prevent, or terminate.

The location of sin's potential reign is specified: en to thneto hymon somati ("in your mortal body"). The adjective thnetos ("mortal, subject to death") describes the body in its present condition — not yet resurrected, still subject to death and decay. Paul does not say sin reigns in the "body of sin" (as in v. 6, where that body was rendered powerless) but in the "mortal body" — the physical body the believer currently inhabits. The distinction matters: the "body of sin" (sin's instrument) has been rendered powerless through co-crucifixion, but the "mortal body" (the physical body) remains the arena in which the battle is fought. The believers have not yet received resurrection bodies; they live in mortal flesh. And it is in this mortal flesh that sin attempts to reassert its reign.

The purpose or result of sin's reign: eis to hypakouein tais epithymiais autou ("so that you obey its desires, so as to obey its lusts"). The verb hypakouein ("to obey, to be subject to, to comply with") is built on akouo ("to hear") with the prefix hypo ("under") — to hear under, to listen in submission. The word epithymia ("desire, craving, longing, lust") is not inherently negative in Greek (it can mean any strong desire), but in Paul's usage it regularly describes the desires of the unredeemed humanity — the cravings that drive sinful behavior (cf. 1:24, "God gave them up to the desires [epithymias] of their hearts to impurity"; 7:7-8, "I would not have known what it is to desire [epithymian] if the law had not said 'You shall not desire [epithymeseis]'"; 13:14, "make no provision for the flesh to gratify its desires [epithymias]"). The pronoun autou ("its") is ambiguous — it could refer to the body's desires or to sin's desires. If it refers to sin (which has been the subject throughout), then the desires are sin's own cravings which seek expression through the mortal body.

"And do not go on presenting your members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness, but present yourselves to God as those alive from the dead, and your members to God as instruments of righteousness" (6:13).

The verb paristanete (present active imperative of paristemi, "to present, to offer, to place beside, to make available") is a word with cultic and military associations. In the LXX, paristemi is used for presenting offerings before the Lord (Leviticus 16:7, "he shall take the two goats and present them [stesei] before the LORD"; Numbers 3:6, "bring the tribe of Levi near and present [steseis] them before Aaron the priest"). In military contexts, it means to place troops at a commander's disposal. Both senses are operative here: the believers' members (mele — "limbs, body parts, organs") are being either offered to sin or offered to God.

The word hopla ("instruments, weapons, tools, implements") has a primary meaning of "weapons" or "armor" — the equipment a soldier carries. In 2 Corinthians 6:7, Paul uses hopla dikaiosynes ("weapons of righteousness"); in 2 Corinthians 10:4, "the weapons (hopla) of our warfare." The military sense is appropriate here: the body's members are weaponry, deployed either in sin's army or in God's. The contrast is between hopla adikias ("weapons of unrighteousness") and hopla dikaiosynes ("weapons of righteousness") — the same root (dik-) that has governed the letter's argument from the beginning. The members that were formerly enlisted in the service of adikia are now to be transferred to the service of dikaiosyne.

The middle clause — alla parastesate heautous to theo hosei ek nekron zontas ("but present yourselves to God as those alive from the dead") — shifts from the present imperative (paristanete, "stop presenting," continuous) to the aorist imperative (parastesate, "present," decisive action). The aorist imperative calls for a definitive act: make the presentation, make the transfer, enlist now. The phrase hosei ek nekron zontas ("as those alive from the dead") describes the believers' self-understanding in this act: they are to present themselves as people who have come back from death. The conjunction hosei ("as, as though, like") does not indicate pretense — it indicates the reality from which their action proceeds. They are in fact people who have died (with Christ) and are now alive (in newness of life). Their self-presentation to God flows from that identity.

"For sin shall not have dominion over you, for you are not under law but under grace" (6:14).

The verb kyrieusei (future active of kyrieuo, "to exercise lordship, to have dominion") echoes the kyrieuei of verse 9, where death no longer "lords over" Christ. The same verb, applied to the believers: sin will not lord it over you. The future tense carries promissory force: this is not merely a wish but a declaration of what will be the case.

The reason given is: ou gar este hypo nomon alla hypo charin ("for you are not under law but under grace"). The preposition hypo ("under") with the accusative indicates subjection — being under the authority or regime of something. The contrast is between two regimes: nomos ("law") and charis ("grace"). Paul does not explain here why being "under law" would result in sin's dominion — that argument will be developed in chapter 7, where he shows that the law, though good in itself, becomes sin's instrument when it encounters the flesh (7:7-13). What he asserts here is the baseline: the regime has changed. The believers are no longer under the Torah-regime but under the grace-regime. And under grace, sin does not exercise lordship.

This verse is the hinge of the chapter. It concludes the first half (vv. 1-14), which argued from the reality of baptismal co-death with Christ, and sets up the second half (vv. 15-23), which will argue from the metaphor of slavery and lordship. The word kyrieusei in verse 14 becomes the bridge: the question of dominion — who is lord, who is master, whose authority governs — is the question of the entire chapter, and the slavery metaphor of the second half is simply the dominion question stated in social terms.

Verses 15-19: The Second Question and the Slavery Metaphor

"What then? Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace? By no means!" (6:15).

The question ti oun? hamartesomen hoti ouk esmen hypo nomon alla hypo charin? reprises the pattern of verse 1 but from a different angle. Verse 1 asked: shall we continue in sin so that grace increases? That question arose from the superabundance of grace in 5:20. Verse 15 asks: shall we sin because we are under grace rather than law? This question arises from the freedom described in verse 14. If the regime has changed from law to grace, and if law was the enforcement mechanism against sin, then does the removal of law mean sin is now permitted? The question shifts from "does grace reward sin?" (v. 1) to "does grace permit sin?" (v. 15). Both receive the same answer: me genoito.

"Do you not know that when you present yourselves to someone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one you obey, either of sin resulting in death, or of obedience resulting in righteousness?" (6:16).

The formula ouk oidate hoti ("do you not know that") again appeals to common knowledge. The principle Paul states is drawn from everyday social reality in the first-century world, where slavery was ubiquitous. Every person in Paul's audience would have known slaves, been slaves, or owned slaves. The principle is: if you present yourself (paristanete heautous, using the same verb from v. 13) to someone as a slave for obedience (doulous eis hypakoen, "slaves for the purpose of obeying"), then you are that person's slave (douloi este, "you are slaves"). The identity follows the practice. You are the slave of whomever you obey (ho hypakouete, "the one you obey").

Paul presents two options, and only two. There is no third category, no neutral ground, no state of freedom from all masters. The options are: etoi hamartias eis thanaton e hypakoes eis dikaiosynen ("either of sin leading to death, or of obedience leading to righteousness"). The word etoi ("either") with the correlative e ("or") presents a strict alternative. The first option: slavery to sin, with death as its destination (eis thanaton). The second option: slavery to obedience, with righteousness as its destination (eis dikaiosynen). The word hypakoe ("obedience") here stands in as the master in place of an explicit name (God, or righteousness). The asymmetry is deliberate: "sin" is named as a master, but the opposing master is described by the posture of the slave ("obedience") rather than by the name of the lord. This may be because Paul wants to emphasize the active, volitional character of the believer's service: the alternative to slavery to sin is not passive reception of grace but active obedience.

The point Paul is making is that the absence of Torah does not create freedom from all authority. It creates a choice between two authorities. Every human being is serving something. The question is not whether you are a slave but whose slave you are. Removing the law does not remove obligation; it changes the framework within which obligation operates.

"But thanks be to God that though you were slaves of sin, you became obedient from the heart to that form of teaching to which you were committed" (6:17).

The phrase charis de to theo ("but thanks be to God") is a doxological interjection. The past tense ete douloi tes hamartias ("you were slaves of sin") uses the imperfect — you were formerly, you used to be. The slavery to sin is in the past. The contrast: hypekousate de ek kardias ("but you obeyed from the heart"). The verb hypekousate (aorist active of hypakouo, "to obey, to listen under") describes a specific, decisive act of obedience. The phrase ek kardias ("from the heart") indicates that this obedience was not external compliance but inward, genuine, heartfelt response. The heart (kardia) in Jewish anthropology is the center of the whole person — will, understanding, and affection. Obedience ek kardias echoes the Deuteronomic call: "Love the LORD your God with all your heart (kardia)" (Deuteronomy 6:5, LXX); "circumcise the foreskin of your heart" (Deuteronomy 10:16, LXX).

The phrase eis hon paredothete typon didaches ("to that form of teaching to which you were delivered/entrusted") is unusual. The verb paredothete (aorist passive of paradidomi, "to hand over, to deliver, to entrust, to transmit") is typically used for the transmission of tradition: Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 11:2, 23 and 15:3 for the handing on of apostolic teaching. But here the passive is striking: it is not that the teaching was handed over to them, but that they were handed over to the teaching. The believers were entrusted to the teaching, delivered into its custody. The word typon ("form, pattern, mold, stamp, type") suggests a mold into which they were placed — the teaching shaped them as a mold shapes metal or clay. The didache ("teaching, instruction") is the apostolic message about Christ, the gospel as a body of teaching that includes ethical instruction. The image is of being transferred from one master (sin) to another authority (the pattern of teaching) that now shapes the believer's life.

"And having been freed from sin, you became slaves of righteousness" (6:18).

The verb eleutheroothentes (aorist passive participle of eleutheroo, "to set free, to liberate") describes the act of manumission — being freed from a slave-master. The passive voice indicates that someone else freed them; they did not free themselves. The liberation was from sin (apo tes hamartias). The result: edouloothete te dikaiosyne ("you were enslaved to righteousness"). The verb edouloothete (aorist passive of douloo, "to enslave, to make a slave of") is again passive: they were made slaves. The dative te dikaiosyne ("to righteousness") identifies the new master. Freedom from sin was not freedom from all service; it was transfer to a new service. The freed slave of sin became the slave of righteousness.

The structure of the Exodus narrative again underlies Paul's language. God freed Israel from Egypt (eleutheroo — liberation) not to give them autonomous existence but to bring them into his own service: "Let my people go that they may serve me" (Exodus 7:16, 8:1, 8:20, 9:1, 9:13, 10:3, LXX: exaposteilon ton laon mou hina moi latreusosin — "send out my people that they may worship/serve me"). The verb latreuein ("to serve, to worship") in the Exodus command is the same kind of total-life service that Paul describes with douleuein. Israel's liberation from Pharaoh's service was simultaneously their conscription into God's service. So with the believer: freedom from sin is entrance into the service of righteousness.

"I am speaking in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh. For just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to lawlessness, resulting in further lawlessness, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness, resulting in sanctification" (6:19).

The parenthetical anthropinon lego dia ten astheneian tes sarkos hymon ("I speak in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh") is Paul's acknowledgment that the slavery metaphor is imperfect. The phrase anthropinon lego parallels kata anthropon lego in 3:5 — Paul is speaking according to human analogy, using a metaphor drawn from social life rather than offering a precise theological definition. The "weakness of the flesh" (astheneia tes sarkos) explains why Paul resorts to analogy: the hearers' limited capacity requires concrete, familiar imagery. The word sarx ("flesh") here carries the sense of human limitation and weakness, the capacity that struggles to grasp spiritual realities without earthly parallels.

The comparison uses the same verb as verse 13: paristanete/parastesate ("present, offer"). The command is structured as a symmetrical transfer. Previously: you presented your members (ta mele hymon) as slaves (doula) to impurity (te akatharsia) and to lawlessness (te anomia) leading to further lawlessness (eis ten anomian). The word akatharsia ("impurity, uncleanness") in the LXX is a cultic term for ritual defilement — the opposite of the holiness required for approach to God (Leviticus 5:3, 7:20-21; Numbers 19:13). The word anomia ("lawlessness, transgression, violation of law") is the negation of nomos: a-nomia, the state of being without law or acting against law. The phrase eis ten anomian ("leading to more lawlessness") describes the escalating character of sin: one act of lawlessness produces conditions for further lawlessness. Sin is not self-limiting; it is self-multiplying.

Now: present your members as slaves to righteousness (te dikaiosyne) leading to sanctification (eis hagiasmon). The word hagiasmos ("sanctification, holiness, the process of being made holy") is built on hagios ("holy, set apart, consecrated"). In the LXX, holiness (hagiotes, hagiasmos, hagiosyne) is fundamentally a relational term: to be holy is to belong to God, to be set apart for God's purposes. The temple vessels are holy because they belong to God. The Sabbath is holy because it is set apart for God. The priests are holy because they are consecrated to God's service. Holiness is not primarily a moral quality (though moral behavior follows from it) but a status of belonging. Paul's statement that obedience to righteousness leads to hagiasmos means that it leads to an ever-deepening state of belonging to God — the opposite of the ever-deepening anomia that slavery to sin produced.

Verses 20-23: The Two Outcomes — Death and Eternal Life

"For when you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness" (6:20).

The logic is inverted mirror. When sin was their master, righteousness had no claim on them — they were "free" (eleutheroi) in relation to righteousness. The word eleutheroi ("free, unbound") is bitterly ironic: their "freedom" from righteousness was not liberty but bondage to sin. They were free from the good, which is to say they were captive to the bad. The verse establishes a principle of exclusivity: you cannot serve both masters simultaneously. When you belonged to sin, righteousness was absent from your life. This echoes the teaching attributed to Jesus: "No one can serve two masters" (Matthew 6:24; Luke 16:13).

"What fruit did you have then? Things of which you are now ashamed, for the end of those things is death" (6:21).

The word karpos ("fruit") is a common metaphor for the outcome or product of a way of life. In the LXX, "fruit" regularly describes the consequences of one's choices: "the fruit of the righteous is a tree of life" (Proverbs 11:30); "Say to the righteous that it will go well with them, for they will eat the fruit of their deeds" (Isaiah 3:10, LXX); "You have plowed wickedness, you have reaped injustice, you have eaten the fruit of lies" (Hosea 10:13). Paul asks the rhetorical question: what fruit did your former slavery produce? The implied answer: nothing of value. The things they produced are things eph' hois nyn epaischynesthe ("of which you are now ashamed"). The verb epaischynomai ("to be ashamed of, to be embarrassed by") indicates a present assessment of past behavior — from their current vantage point (in Christ, under grace), they look back on their former "fruits" with shame.

The final verdict on that fruit: to gar telos ekeinon thanatos ("for the end of those things is death"). The word telos ("end, goal, outcome, completion, purpose") can mean either "end" in the sense of termination or "end" in the sense of goal/purpose. Both senses may apply: death is both the outcome and the inherent destination of sin's fruits. The harvest of sin is always death. This connects to the reign-of-death language in 5:14, 17, 21: sin's reign was a reign "in death" — death was the domain in which sin exercised its power, and death was the product of sin's rule.

"But now having been freed from sin and having become slaves of God, you have your fruit resulting in sanctification, and the outcome, eternal life" (6:22).

The phrase nyni de ("but now") marks the temporal contrast, just as it did in 3:21 ("but now the righteousness of God has been manifested"). The "now" is the present era inaugurated by Christ's death and the believer's baptismal participation in it. The two aorist passive participles — eleutheroothentes apo tes hamartias ("having been freed from sin") and douloothentes to theo ("having been enslaved to God") — describe completed events. The shift from verse 18 is notable: in verse 18, the new master was "righteousness" (dikaiosyne); here, the new master is "God" (theo). The progression reveals what Paul means by "slavery to righteousness" — it is slavery to God himself. Righteousness is not an abstract principle but the character of God, and to serve righteousness is to serve the God whose character it is.

The fruit of this new slavery: eis hagiasmon ("resulting in sanctification") — the same word from verse 19. The sanctification is the present, ongoing fruit of belonging to God. And the final outcome: to de telos zoen aionion ("and the end/outcome, eternal life"). The word telos here parallels its use in verse 21 (where the telos of sin's fruit was death). The destination of God's service is zoe aionios — "life of the age, eternal life, life of the coming age." In the LXX and in first-century Jewish usage, zoe aionios is the life of the age to come, the life that belongs to the eschatological future when God's reign is fully established. Daniel 12:2 (LXX): "many of those sleeping in the dust of the earth will arise, some to eternal life (eis zoen aionion)." Paul places this life as the telos — the ultimate outcome, the final destination — of the path that begins with liberation from sin and service to God.

"For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (6:23).

This verse functions as the summary of the entire chapter, compressing its argument into a single antithetical statement.

The word opsonia ("wages, rations, pay, soldier's wages") is a military term. In classical Greek and in the papyri, opsonia refers to the provisions or pay given to soldiers — their rations, their salary for service. The word appears in Luke 3:14, where John the Baptist tells soldiers to "be content with your wages (opsoniois)"; in 1 Corinthians 9:7, "who serves as a soldier at his own expense (opsoniois)?"; and in 2 Corinthians 11:8, "I robbed other churches, taking wages (opsonion) from them." The metaphor is exact: sin is a commanding officer, and those who serve under sin's command receive sin's pay. The pay (opsonia) that sin distributes to its soldiers is thanatos — death. This is not arbitrary punishment; it is the earned compensation for service rendered. Death is what sin pays.

The contrasting term: charisma tou theou ("the free gift of God," "the grace-gift of God"). The word charisma is built on charis ("grace") — it is the thing that grace gives, the concrete expression of grace's generosity. Unlike opsonia, which is earned compensation, charisma is by definition unearned. A charisma cannot be a wage because it comes from charis, and charis is unmerited. The deliberate contrast between opsonia and charisma is the rhetorical climax of the chapter: sin pays a wage (death); God gives a gift (eternal life). The wage is earned; the gift is free. The wage comes from a master who exploits; the gift comes from a God who gives.

The gift is zoe aionios en Christo Iesou to kyrio hemon ("eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord"). The phrase en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") reprises the locative formula from verse 11 and from throughout Paul's letters: eternal life exists within the sphere of Christ, within union with him. The title to kyrio hemon ("our Lord") closes the chapter with the word kyrios — "lord, master." The chapter began with the question of dominion (shall sin reign?) and ends with the declaration of who actually is Lord: Christ Jesus. He is the kyrios under whose authority the believers live, and his lordship is the source of the gift of eternal life.

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Romans 6 divides into two parallel halves, each opened by an objection and each developing a metaphor to answer it.

The first half (6:1-14) is opened by the objection of verse 1: "Shall we continue in sin that grace may abound?" Paul's answer draws on the metaphor of death and resurrection through baptism. The believers died to sin when they were baptized into Christ's death (vv. 3-4). The old Adamic humanity was crucified (v. 6). The dead person is freed from sin's jurisdiction (v. 7). Christ's death to sin was once for all; his life is to God (v. 10). The believers are to reckon themselves accordingly (v. 11) and not let sin reign in their mortal bodies (v. 12). The half concludes with the promissory declaration that sin will not have dominion because the believers are under grace, not under law (v. 14).

The second half (6:15-23) is opened by the objection of verse 15: "Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace?" Paul's answer draws on the metaphor of slavery and lordship. Every person serves a master (v. 16). The believers were formerly slaves of sin but have obeyed from the heart and been transferred to the service of righteousness (vv. 17-18). Their members, once instruments of impurity and lawlessness, are now to be instruments of righteousness leading to holiness (v. 19). The fruit of the old slavery was shame and death (v. 21); the fruit of the new slavery is holiness and eternal life (v. 22). The half concludes with the compressed summary: sin pays wages (death); God gives a gift (eternal life) in Christ Jesus our Lord (v. 23).

The two halves are parallel in structure. Each begins with an objection (vv. 1, 15). Each answers me genoito (vv. 2, 15). Each develops a metaphor for the believer's transfer from one domain to another (death-and-resurrection in the first half, change-of-master in the second). Each contains imperatives (vv. 11-13, 19). Each climaxes with a statement about the believer's position relative to sin's dominion (v. 14, v. 23). The two metaphors — death and slavery — illuminate the same reality from different angles. Death explains why the believer is no longer under sin's power (a dead person is beyond a tyrant's reach). Slavery explains the believer's ongoing obligation (a freed slave who has been acquired by a new master owes that master service).

The pronouns in the chapter trace a consistent pattern. Paul uses "we" (first person plural) in the doctrinal exposition (vv. 1-10): "we who died," "we were baptized," "we were buried," "we have been united," "our old self," "we died with Christ," "we believe we will also live." This "we" includes Paul himself among those who have undergone the baptismal death and share in the new life. Beginning at verse 11, Paul shifts to "you" (second person plural) for the imperatives: "consider yourselves," "do not let sin reign in your body," "do not present your members," "present yourselves." The shift from "we" to "you" marks the transition from shared identity to direct exhortation. In verses 17-22, Paul uses "you" in the past tense to describe their former condition ("you were slaves of sin," "you obeyed from the heart," "you were freed") and their present condition ("you have your fruit resulting in sanctification"). The second-person address makes the application personal and direct. Verse 23 returns to a general, aphoristic statement that applies universally.

The imperatives in the chapter are significant for the logic of the argument. If the believer's death to sin were automatic and irresistible in its effects, there would be no need for commands. Paul would not need to say "do not let sin reign" (v. 12) if sin could not possibly reign. He would not need to say "do not present your members as instruments of unrighteousness" (v. 13) if such presentation were impossible. He would not need to say "present your members as slaves to righteousness" (v. 19) if the slavery to righteousness were self-executing. The presence of imperatives indicates that the reality established by baptism (death to sin, life to God) must be enacted, lived out, and maintained by the believer's active participation. The indicative (you have died to sin) grounds the imperative (therefore do not let sin reign), but the imperative is genuine: the believer can fail to live in accordance with what is true. The death to sin is real, but the mortal body remains (v. 12), and in that mortal body sin attempts to reassert its dominion. The believer's task is to live out the death that has already occurred and not to return to the slavery from which they have been freed.

This point is reinforced by verse 16, which states the principle with uncomfortable clarity: you are the slave of whomever you obey. The identity follows the practice. If a person freed from sin's slavery begins obeying sin again, that person becomes sin's slave again, and sin's wage is death. Paul does not treat the believer's transfer from sin's service to God's service as a transaction that cannot be reversed. He treats it as a reality that must be maintained through ongoing obedience. The Exodus parallel is instructive: Israel was freed from Egypt, but the wilderness generation repeatedly turned back in their hearts to Egypt and was consequently barred from the promised land (Numbers 14:28-35; Psalm 95:7-11, cited in Hebrews 3:7-19). Liberation is real, but the liberated must walk in their liberation.

The chapter's argument within the larger structure of Romans is essential to Paul's case. In chapters 1-3, Paul established that all humanity is under sin and that justification comes through faith, not through law. In chapter 4, he demonstrated this from Abraham. In chapter 5, he showed the scope and certainty of grace's triumph over sin and death through Christ. But the argument of chapter 5, especially its climax in 5:20 ("where sin increased, grace abounded all the more"), left an open question: does this grace-theology produce moral indifference or even moral license? Chapter 6 answers: no, because the person who received grace is not the same person who was under sin. The person who was under sin died. The person now alive is a new creation, alive to God in Christ Jesus, liberated from sin's dominion, enslaved to righteousness. The objection assumes continuity between the person under sin and the person under grace — as if the same person simply switched from one payment system to another while continuing the same work. Paul denies this continuity: a death intervened. The person who might have benefited from continuing in sin no longer exists. The person who now exists has a different master, a different allegiance, a different destiny.

Chapter 6 thus serves as the moral foundation for the rest of Paul's argument. It establishes that justification by faith through grace does not produce antinomianism. It does so not by reintroducing law as the enforcement mechanism (that would contradict everything Paul argued in chapters 3-5) but by showing that the grace that justifies also transforms — it kills the old and raises the new, it liberates from one master and binds to another, it produces a new walk in a new life. The law could not do this (a point Paul will develop in chapter 7). The Spirit does this (a point Paul will develop in chapter 8). Chapter 6 states the reality; chapters 7 and 8 explain the mechanism.
