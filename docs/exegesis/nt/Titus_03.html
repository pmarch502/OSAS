Titus 3 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Titus 2:11-15): The closing section of chapter 2 provided the theological engine for all the behavioral instructions that preceded it. The grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, and this grace actively trains believers to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age (2:11-12). The community awaits the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works (2:13-14). The chapter closed with the charge to Titus: "Declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you" (2:15). The theological statement established that the behavioral transformation of the community is not a human achievement but the product of divine grace operating through Christ's redemptive self-giving. The community's identity as the laos periousios — the people of God's own possession, echoing the Exodus covenant — was defined not by external regulation but by zealous devotion to good works.

What follows (the letter's conclusion, 3:12-15): The letter closes with personal instructions about travel arrangements (Artemas or Tychicus will be sent to relieve Titus, who is to join Paul at Nicopolis), a charge to provision Zenas the lawyer and Apollos for their journey, and a final exhortation that "our people" must learn to devote themselves to good works for pressing needs, so that they may not be unfruitful. The final greeting extends grace to "all of you." The closing instructions are practical applications of the letter's central theme: good works are not abstract moral obligations but concrete acts of provision, hospitality, and community support.

How chapter 3 connects both: Chapter 3 completes the letter's three-movement structure. Chapter 1 identified the crisis (false teachers who profess to know God but deny him by their works, rendering themselves unfit for any good work). Chapter 2 provided the positive content (sound doctrine producing transformed conduct in every social category) and the first theological grounding statement (2:11-14, the appearing of grace that trains believers). Chapter 3 now extends the behavioral instruction outward from the internal life of the community to its relationship with the surrounding civic order (3:1-2), provides a second theological grounding statement that parallels the first (3:3-7, the appearing of God's goodness and mercy that saves through the washing of regeneration), draws the practical conclusion that believers must devote themselves to good works (3:8), and closes the ring the letter opened by returning to the warning against the kind of controversies the false teachers promote (3:9-11). The chapter's architecture mirrors the whole letter in miniature: behavior (3:1-2) grounded in theology (3:3-7) producing good works (3:8) and requiring the rejection of what opposes them (3:9-11).

The two theological statements — 2:11-14 and 3:3-7 — function as twin pillars supporting the same structure. Both describe a divine appearing or manifestation. Both describe salvation as God's initiative, not human achievement. Both connect salvation to transformed living. Both culminate in a people defined by good works. The repetition is not redundant but reinforcing: the letter says the same thing twice from different angles to ensure the message is unmistakable.

Verses 1-2: Civic Conduct — The Community's Posture Toward the Outside World

"Remind them to be submissive to rulers and authorities, to be obedient, to be ready for every good work, to speak evil of no one, to avoid quarreling, to be gentle, and to show perfect courtesy toward all people."

(Hypomimneske autous archais exousiais hypotassesthai, peitharchein, pros pan ergon agathon hetoimous einai, medena blasphemein, amachous einai, epieikeis, pasan endeiknumenous praoteta pros pantas anthropous.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: Titus was charged to declare, exhort, and rebuke with all authority; no one is to disregard him (2:15). What the author argues immediately after: A reminder of the community's former condition — foolish, disobedient, enslaved to passions (3:3). How these verses connect the two: The charge to Titus at 2:15 authorized the delivery of instruction; 3:1-2 is the next installment of that instruction. The content shifts from the internal life of the community (how older men, younger women, slaves, etc. are to live) to the community's relationship with the civic order. And the connection to 3:3 is causal: the community is to treat the surrounding world with gentleness and courtesy because they themselves were once in the same condition as the outsiders they now encounter. The behavioral instruction of 3:1-2 will be grounded in the theological reminder of 3:3-7 just as the behavioral instruction of 2:2-10 was grounded in the theological statement of 2:11-14.

"Remind them" (hypomimneske autous). The verb hypomniskein ("to remind, to bring to remembrance, to call to mind") is composed of hypo ("under") + mimneskein ("to remember, to call to mind"). The present imperative indicates ongoing action: Titus is to keep reminding them. The verb implies that what follows is not new instruction but something the community has already been taught and needs to hear again. In 2 Timothy 2:14, Paul uses the same verb: "Remind them (hypomnimneske) of these things." In 2 Peter 1:12: "I intend always to remind (hypomniskein) you of these qualities, though you know them." The need for reminder is not a sign of failure but a recognition that communities need repeated instruction, especially when cultural pressures push in the opposite direction. The pronoun autous ("them") refers to the community members Titus is responsible for — the believers in the Cretan towns.

"To be submissive to rulers and authorities" (archais exousiais hypotassesthai). The verb hypotassesthai ("to submit, to be subject to, to place oneself under") is the same verb used in 2:5 for younger women toward their husbands and in 2:9 for slaves toward their masters. Its appearance here extends the submission language from the household sphere to the civic sphere. The objects of submission are archais kai exousiais — "rulers and authorities." The noun arche ("beginning, origin, ruler, magistrate, principality") in its political sense designates the ruling authority, the person or office that holds governing power. The noun exousia ("authority, power, right, jurisdiction") designates the sphere or mandate of that authority. Together, the two nouns comprehensively describe the governing structures of the Roman world — from local magistrates to provincial governors to imperial officials.

The instruction to submit to governing authorities parallels Romans 13:1-7, where Paul writes: "Let every person be subject (hypotassestho) to the governing authorities (exousiais hyperechousais). For there is no authority (exousia) except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God." The theological basis in Romans 13 is that God has established the governing structures. The instruction in Titus does not develop this theological basis but assumes it. For a community on Crete — a Roman province governed by a proconsul, with local magistrates in each town — submission to the political order is a practical instruction with concrete implications: paying taxes, obeying laws, honoring civic obligations, and not engaging in the kind of political agitation that would bring the community into conflict with Rome.

The instruction also has an apologetic dimension, consistent with the letter's repeated concern that the community's conduct commend the gospel to outsiders. In 2:5, the younger women's conduct prevents "the word of God" from being reviled. In 2:8, Titus's sound speech ensures that "an opponent may be put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us." In 2:10, the slaves' conduct adorns "the doctrine of God our Savior." The community's civic submission serves the same purpose: a community that lives as a responsible participant in the civic order removes a potential accusation from opponents and demonstrates that the gospel produces good citizens, not seditious agitators.

"To be obedient" (peitharchein). The verb peitharchein ("to obey, to comply, to submit to authority") is composed of peithesthai ("to obey, to be persuaded") + arche ("ruler, authority"). The word specifically describes obedience to those in authority — it is the natural companion to "submissive to rulers and authorities." In Acts 5:29, Peter and the apostles declare: "We must obey (peitharchein) God rather than men." In Acts 27:21, the same verb describes the sailors' obedience to Paul's counsel. The combination of hypotassesthai and peitharchein reinforces the instruction: the community is both to arrange itself under the civic order and to comply with its directives.

"To be ready for every good work" (pros pan ergon agathon hetoimous einai). The adjective hetoimos ("ready, prepared, willing, at hand") describes a state of preparedness — the community is to be in a condition of readiness for good works, not waiting to be compelled but standing ready to serve. The phrase pan ergon agathon ("every good work") is the positive counterpart of the verdict on the false teachers in 1:16: they are pros pan ergon agathon adokimoi — "unfit for every good work." The verbal echo is precise and deliberate: where the false teachers are disqualified from every good work, the community is to be ready for every good work. The contrast defines the letter's vision of authentic versus counterfeit faith: authentic faith produces readiness for good works; counterfeit faith produces disqualification from them.

The phrase "every good work" also anticipates 3:8 ("those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works") and 3:14 ("our people must learn to devote themselves to good works"). The good-works theme runs through the entire letter as its central practical concern, appearing at 1:16 (false teachers unfit for good works), 2:7 (Titus as model of good works), 2:14 (Christ purified a people zealous for good works), 3:1 (ready for every good work), 3:8 (devote themselves to good works), and 3:14 (learn to devote themselves to good works). The repetition establishes good works as the letter's defining test of genuine faith.

"To speak evil of no one" (medena blasphemein). The verb blasphemein ("to slander, to speak evil of, to revile, to defame, to blaspheme") here takes a human object: medena — "no one." The same verb appeared in 2:5, where the younger women's conduct prevents the word of God from being blasphemed. Here the community is forbidden from directing blasphemia toward any person. The prohibition is universal: not "speak evil of no fellow believer" but "speak evil of no one" — the scope extends to all people, including the pagan neighbors, the civic authorities, and even the opponents of the community. In a culture where the Cretans' own prophet described them as "always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12), the temptation for the community to adopt a posture of verbal contempt toward their surrounding culture must have been strong. The instruction forbids it. The community that has been transformed by grace (2:11-14) must not speak with contempt about those who have not yet experienced it.

"To avoid quarreling" (amachous einai). The adjective amachos ("peaceable, not quarrelsome, not contentious") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) and mache ("fight, battle, conflict, quarrel"). The word describes a person who does not engage in fights, who avoids contention. In James 4:1: "What causes quarrels (machais) and what causes fights (polemous) among you?" The community is to be characterized by the absence of combativeness. This instruction connects to 3:9, where Titus will be told to "avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law" — the false teachers' program generates precisely the kind of contentiousness that the community is to avoid.

"To be gentle" (epieikeis). The adjective epieikes ("gentle, yielding, forbearing, reasonable, fair, equitable") describes a quality that goes beyond strict justice to show consideration and clemency. In Philippians 4:5: "Let your reasonableness (to epieikes) be known to everyone." In James 3:17: "the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle (epieikes), open to reason." In 1 Timothy 3:3, the overseer must be epieikes — "gentle." In 1 Peter 2:18, slaves are to be subject not only to good and gentle (epieikesin) masters but also to unjust ones. The quality describes a disposition that does not insist on its own rights to the last measure but yields, accommodates, and shows grace. It is the opposite of the quarrelsome, combative, self-assertive posture that characterizes the false teachers.

"To show perfect courtesy toward all people" (pasan endeiknumenous praoteta pros pantas anthropous). The verb endeiknysthai ("to show, to demonstrate, to display, to prove") in the middle voice means "to display from oneself." The noun praotes ("gentleness, meekness, mildness, courtesy, humility") describes a controlled strength — not weakness but power under restraint. In Galatians 5:23, praotes appears as a fruit of the Spirit. In Galatians 6:1: "you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness (praotetos)." In 2 Timothy 2:25: "correcting his opponents with gentleness (praoteti)." In Matthew 5:5: "Blessed are the meek (praeis), for they shall inherit the earth" — citing Psalm 37:11 (LXX 36:11). In Numbers 12:3 (LXX), Moses is described as praos — "very meek, more than all people who were on the face of the earth." The praos person is not a pushover but a person whose strength is governed by consideration for others.

The qualifier pasan ("all, complete, every kind of") intensifies: the gentleness is to be comprehensive, covering every situation. And the scope is defined by pros pantas anthropous — "toward all people." As with the prohibition against slander (medena blasphemein), the scope extends to all people without restriction. The community's gentleness is not reserved for fellow believers or for people who deserve it; it is extended to all people — the pagan neighbor, the difficult magistrate, the hostile opponent, the false teacher's follower. The universal scope matches the universal scope of God's saving grace in 2:11: "the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people (pasin anthropois)." The community's courtesy toward all people mirrors God's grace toward all people.

Verse 3: The Reminder — What We Once Were

"For we ourselves were once foolish, disobedient, led astray, slaves to various passions and pleasures, passing our days in malice and envy, hated by others and hating one another."

(Emen gar pote kai hemeis anoetoi, apeitheis, planomenoi, douleuontes epithymiais kai hedonais poikilais, en kakia kai phthono diagontes, stygeetoi, misountes allelous.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The community is to show perfect courtesy toward all people (3:2). What the author argues immediately after: The appearing of God's goodness and loving kindness that saved them (3:4-7). How this verse connects the two: The conjunction gar ("for") makes the connection explicit: the reason the community is to show gentleness and courtesy toward all people is that they themselves were once in the same condition as those people. The verse provides the motivational ground for the behavioral instructions of 3:1-2. And it simultaneously sets up the theological statement of 3:4-7 by establishing the "before" picture that makes the "after" picture intelligible. The structure is: behave this way toward outsiders (3:1-2) because you were once like them (3:3) until God saved you (3:4-7). The argument requires no change of subject — the same community whose civic conduct is being shaped in 3:1-2 is now reminded of the raw material from which that conduct has been formed.

"For we ourselves were once" (emen gar pote kai hemeis). The imperfect verb emen ("we were") describes a past, sustained condition — not a single act but a way of life. The adverb pote ("once, formerly, at one time") specifies that this was the previous condition, now changed. The emphatic pronoun hemeis ("we ourselves") and the intensive particle kai ("also, even") include Paul in the description: this is not a distant "they" but an intimate "we." Paul is not lecturing the community about their past from a position of superiority; he places himself in the same former condition. The same self-inclusion appears in Ephesians 2:3: "Among whom we all (hemeis pantes) once lived in the passions of our flesh." The rhetorical effect is to eliminate any ground for condescension toward outsiders: "we ourselves" were exactly what they are now.

The catalog that follows describes the former condition with seven terms:

(1) Foolish (anoetoi). The adjective anoetos ("without understanding, senseless, foolish, unintelligent") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) + noein ("to perceive, to understand, to think"). The anoetos person is one whose nous (mind) is not functioning properly — the faculty of understanding is inactive or disabled. In Galatians 3:1, Paul addresses the Galatians: "O foolish (anoetoi) Galatians! Who has bewitched you?" In Luke 24:25, the risen Jesus says to the Emmaus disciples: "O foolish ones (anoetoi), and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken!" In Romans 1:21, those who did not honor God "became futile in their thinking, and their foolish (asynetos) hearts were darkened." The foolishness described is not a lack of intelligence but a failure of spiritual perception — the inability to see what is actually true about God and about one's own condition.

(2) Disobedient (apeitheis). The adjective apeithes ("disobedient, unpersuadable, resistant to authority") appeared in 1:16 as part of the final verdict on the false teachers: "they are detestable, disobedient (apeitheis)." The same word now describes the community's former condition. The implication is uncomfortable but deliberate: what the false teachers currently are, the community once was. The difference between them is not that the community was inherently better but that God intervened (3:4-7). This connection reinforces the argument for gentleness toward outsiders: the community has no ground for contempt because they once shared the very disobedience they now see in others.

(3) Led astray (planomenoi). The present passive participle planomenoi (from planasthai, "to wander, to go astray, to be led astray, to be deceived") describes a condition of being misled — not merely choosing wrongly but being actively deceived, carried off course by error. In Hebrews 5:2, the high priest can deal gently with "the ignorant and wayward (planomenois)." In 1 Peter 2:25: "For you were straying (planomenoi) like sheep, but have now returned to the Shepherd." In Isaiah 53:6 (LXX): "All we like sheep have gone astray (eplanemethemen)." The passive voice is significant: those who wander are not simply choosing to wander; they are being led astray by forces, influences, and deceptions that operate upon them. The imagery echoes the shepherd-and-sheep language of the prophets, where Israel's wandering is attributed both to the people's own stubbornness and to the false shepherds who misled them (Ezekiel 34:1-6).

(4) Slaves to various passions and pleasures (douleuontes epithymiais kai hedonais poikilais). The present participle douleuontes (from douleuein, "to serve as a slave, to be enslaved, to be in bondage") describes active enslavement. The language echoes 2:3, where the older women must not be "enslaved (dedoulomenas) to much wine." In Romans 6:6, Paul writes: "our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be brought to nothing, so that we would no longer be enslaved (douleuein) to sin." In Romans 6:17-18: "you who were once slaves (douloi) of sin have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching." The slavery is to epithymiais kai hedonais poikilais — "various passions and pleasures." The noun epithymia ("desire, craving, longing, lust") is the same word used in 2:12 for the "worldly passions" (kosmikas epithymias) that grace trains believers to renounce. The noun hedone ("pleasure, enjoyment, delight") appears in James 4:1: "Is it not this, that your passions (hedonon) are at war within you?" In Luke 8:14, the seed among thorns is choked by "pleasures (hedonon) of life." The adjective poikilos ("various, diverse, manifold, of many kinds") indicates the diversity of the enslaving desires: not a single addiction but a comprehensive bondage to a shifting array of cravings. The picture is of a life governed not by any coherent purpose but by whatever desire happens to dominate at the moment.

(5) Passing our days in malice and envy (en kakia kai phthono diagontes). The present participle diagontes (from diagein, "to spend time, to pass one's life, to live") describes the ongoing manner of life. The same verb appeared in 1 Timothy 2:2: "that we may lead (diagomen) a peaceful and quiet life." There the manner of life is peaceful; here the manner is defined by two vices. The noun kakia ("malice, wickedness, evil, badness, depravity") describes an active disposition toward harm — not merely the absence of goodness but the presence of ill will. In Romans 1:29: "filled with all manner of unrighteousness, evil (kakia)..." In 1 Corinthians 5:8: "not with the old leaven, the leaven of malice (kakias) and evil." In 1 Peter 2:1: "Put away all malice (kakian)." The noun phthonos ("envy, jealousy, grudging ill-will") describes resentment at another's advantage. In Romans 1:29: "full of envy (phthonou)." In Galatians 5:21: "envy (phthonoi)" appears among the works of the flesh. In Philippians 1:15, some preach Christ "from envy (phthonon)." The pairing of malice and envy describes a social posture: people who wish harm on others and resent others' good fortune. This is the opposite of the epieikeia ("gentleness") and praotes ("courtesy") that 3:1-2 prescribed.

(6) Hated by others (stygetoi). The adjective stygetos ("hateful, detestable, odious") appears only here in the New Testament. It is related to the verb stygein ("to hate, to abhor, to detest"). The adjective is passive in force: they were hated, found detestable by those around them. The word echoes bdelyktos ("detestable") from 1:16, where the false teachers are described as detestable. The condition of being hated is the natural consequence of the preceding vices: a person governed by malice and envy naturally provokes hostility in return. The hateful person becomes the hated person. This is the social fruit of the life described in the preceding terms: foolishness, disobedience, enslavement to passions, and malice inevitably produce relationships marked by mutual hostility.

(7) Hating one another (misountes allelous). The present participle misountes (from misein, "to hate, to detest, to regard with hostility") describes active hatred directed toward one another (allelous, the reciprocal pronoun). The final item in the catalog completes the picture: not only were they hated by those around them, but they hated each other. The reciprocal pronoun indicates mutual, multidirectional hostility — a community of people who despised and were despised, who attacked and were attacked. In 1 John 2:9-11: "Whoever says he is in the light and hates his brother is still in the darkness." In 1 John 3:15: "Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer." The mutual hatred described here is the complete antithesis of the community life Paul has been prescribing throughout the letter — the life of mutual instruction (2:3-4), service (2:9-10), and gentleness (3:2).

The overall effect of the catalog is to establish that the community's former condition was identical to the condition of the surrounding world they now live among. Every quality that might tempt the community to look down on outsiders was once their own defining characteristic. The "we once were" eliminates any basis for spiritual superiority and grounds the instruction to show gentleness toward all people (3:2) in shared experience: you must be gentle toward them because you were them.

Verses 4-7: The Second Theological Statement — Salvation by Mercy Through the Washing of Regeneration

"But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Savior, so that being justified by his grace we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life."

(Hote de he chrestotes kai he philanthropia epephane tou soteros hemon theou, ouk ex ergon ton en dikaiosyne ha epoiesamen hemeis alla kata to autou eleos esosen hemas dia loutrou palingenesias kai anakainoseos pneumatos hagiou, hou execheen eph' hemas plousios dia Iesou Christou tou soteros hemon, hina dikaiothentes te ekeinou chariti kleronomon genethomen kat' elpida zoes aioniou.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The community's former condition — foolish, disobedient, enslaved (3:3). What the author argues immediately after: The "faithful saying" and the conclusion that believers must devote themselves to good works (3:8). How this section connects the two: The adversative de ("but") in verse 4 creates the pivot from the "before" picture (3:3) to the divine intervention that changed everything. The structure is identical to Ephesians 2:1-5, where Paul describes the former condition of being "dead in trespasses and sins" (2:1-3) and then pivots: "But God, being rich in mercy... made us alive together with Christ" (2:4-5). The same "once you were X, but God did Y" pattern operates here. The theological statement explains how the community moved from the condition of 3:3 to the life prescribed in 3:1-2, and it grounds the conclusion of 3:8: because God saved them by his mercy, those who have believed must devote themselves to good works. Salvation by mercy is the source, not the substitute, for good works.

Verse 4: The Appearing of God's Goodness and Loving Kindness

"But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared" (hote de he chrestotes kai he philanthropia epephane tou soteros hemon theou).

The conjunction hote ("when") marks a temporal point: the intervention occurred at a specific moment. The adversative de ("but") creates the contrast with 3:3: we were once X, but when Y happened, everything changed.

Two divine attributes are named as the subjects of the appearing:

(1) Goodness (chrestotes). The noun chrestotes ("goodness, kindness, generosity, uprightness") describes a disposition of active benevolence. In Romans 2:4: "Do you presume on the riches of his kindness (chrestotetoes) and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God's kindness (chreston) is meant to lead you to repentance?" In Romans 11:22: "Note then the kindness (chrestoteta) and the severity of God: severity toward those who have fallen, but God's kindness (chrestoteta) to you, provided you continue in his kindness (chrestoteti)." In Ephesians 2:7: "so that in the coming ages he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness (chrestoteti) toward us in Christ Jesus." In Galatians 5:22, chrestotes appears as a fruit of the Spirit. The word describes God's disposition as fundamentally generous, actively beneficent — a God whose nature inclines toward doing good to those who do not deserve it.

(2) Loving kindness (philanthropia). The noun philanthropia ("love of humanity, love for people, benevolence, kindness") is composed of philos ("loving, fond of") + anthropos ("human being, person"). The word appears only here and in Acts 28:2 in the New Testament, where the natives of Malta showed "unusual kindness (philanthropian)" to the shipwrecked travelers. In Greek political and philosophical usage, philanthropia was a cardinal virtue of kings and benefactors — the generous disposition of a ruler toward his subjects. Josephus uses the word frequently to describe acts of royal clemency and generosity (Antiquities 6.348; 12.22). In the LXX, the word appears in the Epistle of Aristeas (208, 265, 290) to describe the ideal disposition of a king. By applying philanthropia to God, Paul attributes to God the highest quality that the Greco-Roman world expected of its best rulers: a genuine, generous love for the people under his care. The word would resonate powerfully in a Roman province where imperial propaganda claimed the emperor's philanthropia as justification for his rule. Paul declares that the true philanthropos — the true lover of humanity — is not Caesar but God.

The verb epephane ("appeared, was made manifest, became visible") is the same verb used in 2:11 for the appearing of grace: "For the grace of God has appeared (epephane), bringing salvation for all people." The parallel is exact and deliberate. In 2:11, what appeared was he charis tou theou — "the grace of God." Here in 3:4, what appeared was he chrestotes kai he philanthropia tou soteros hemon theou — "the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior." The two statements describe the same event from different angles. Grace appeared (2:11). Goodness and loving kindness appeared (3:4). The appearing is singular: both describe the same divine intervention in history — the coming of Christ and the gospel's proclamation. But the vocabulary differs to illuminate different facets of that intervention. Grace emphasizes the unmerited character of God's favor. Goodness emphasizes the beneficent character of God's disposition. Loving kindness emphasizes God's personal affection for humanity.

The title tou soteros hemon theou ("of God our Savior") has run through the letter as a signature designation. It appeared in 1:3 in the greeting, in 2:10 in the context of slaves adorning "the doctrine of God our Savior," and now here in 3:4. As noted throughout the analysis, the LXX uses soter ("savior") for God specifically in connection with his demonstrated acts of deliverance. Isaiah 45:21 (LXX): "Who declared this from the beginning?... Was it not I, the LORD? And there is no other god besides me, a righteous God and a Savior (soter); there is none besides me." The title soter applied to God anchors the saving action about to be described in the history of Israel's God, who has always been the one who saves.

Verse 5a: Not by Our Works, but by His Mercy

"He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy" (ouk ex ergon ton en dikaiosyne ha epoiesamen hemeis alla kata to autou eleos esosen hemas).

The verb esosen ("he saved") is the aorist of sozein ("to save, to deliver, to rescue, to preserve"). The aorist describes a completed act: God saved us. The pronoun hemas ("us") includes Paul, Titus, and the community — the same "we" who were described in verse 3 as foolish, disobedient, and enslaved. The subject of the saving act is God our Savior from verse 4. The structure is stark: God saved us. The initiative belongs entirely to God; the community contributes nothing to the saving act.

The basis of salvation is specified by negation and then by affirmation:

Negation: ouk ex ergon ton en dikaiosyne ha epoiesamen hemeis — "not from works in righteousness that we did." The preposition ek ("from, out of, on the basis of") with the genitive ergon ("works") indicates the basis or ground of salvation: it does not arise from works. The phrase ton en dikaiosyne ("those in/of righteousness") further qualifies: these are works performed in the sphere of righteousness, works aimed at achieving a righteous standing. The relative clause ha epoiesamen hemeis ("which we ourselves did") makes the exclusion comprehensive and emphatic: the pronoun hemeis ("we ourselves") at the end of the clause is emphatic — whatever works we ourselves performed, they are not the basis of salvation.

The parallel with Ephesians 2:8-9 is close: "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works (ouk ex ergon), so that no one may boast." In Romans 3:20: "For by works of the law (ex ergon nomou) no human being will be justified in his sight." In Romans 3:28: "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law (choris ergon nomou)." In 2 Timothy 1:9: God "saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works (ou kata ta erga hemon) but because of his own purpose and grace, which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began." The consistency across the Pauline corpus is clear: the saving act is not grounded in human performance. This statement does not devalue good works — the entire letter is dedicated to producing them — but it locates them properly: good works are the fruit of salvation, not the root of it.

Affirmation: alla kata to autou eleos — "but according to his own mercy." The adversative alla ("but, rather, on the contrary") creates a sharp contrast between what salvation is not based on (our works) and what it is based on (his mercy). The noun eleos ("mercy, compassion, pity, steadfast love") in the LXX translates the Hebrew hesed ("covenant loyalty, steadfast love, mercy, loving-kindness") and rachamim ("compassion, tender mercy"). The word carries the full weight of Israel's covenant vocabulary for God's faithful, compassionate disposition toward his people. Exodus 34:6 (LXX): "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful (oiktirmon) and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love (polyeleemon) and faithfulness." Psalm 103:8 (LXX 102:8): "The LORD is merciful (oiktirmon) and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love (polyeleeos)." Psalm 51:1 (LXX 50:3): "Have mercy (eleeson) on me, O God, according to your steadfast love (to mega eleos sou); according to your abundant mercy (ton oiktirmon sou) blot out my transgressions." The eleos of God is not a spontaneous, undirected emotion but a covenant attribute — the reliable, persistent compassion that God exercises toward his people because of who he is, not because of what they have done.

The reflexive possessive autou ("his own") emphasizes the source: the mercy originates entirely within God. It is not elicited by human merit or provoked by human need; it flows from God's own character. The phrase kata to autou eleos ("according to his own mercy") identifies mercy as the operative principle of salvation: God saved according to the standard of his own mercy, not according to the standard of human performance.

Verse 5b: The Washing of Regeneration and Renewal of the Holy Spirit

"By the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit" (dia loutrou palingenesias kai anakainoseos pneumatos hagiou).

The preposition dia ("through, by means of") with the genitive indicates the instrument or means of salvation. God saved us through two coordinate operations:

(1) The washing of regeneration (loutrou palingenesias). The noun loutron ("bath, washing, the act of washing") appears only here and in Ephesians 5:26 in the New Testament: "that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing (loutro) of water with the word." In the LXX, the noun and its verbal cognate louein ("to wash, to bathe") are used for ritual washings that effect purification. Leviticus 15:13: "he shall wash (lousetai) his body in running water and be clean." Numbers 19:7: "the priest shall wash (lousetai) his clothes and bathe (lousetai) his body in water." Song of Songs 4:2 (LXX): "Your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes that have come up from the washing (loutrou)." Ezekiel 16:4: "On the day you were born... you were not washed (elousthes) with water to cleanse you." The noun loutron describes a thorough washing — not a sprinkling but a bath, a complete cleansing.

The genitive palingenesias ("of regeneration, of rebirth, of new creation") specifies the character of this washing. The noun palingenesia ("regeneration, new birth, renewal, re-creation") is composed of palin ("again") + genesis ("birth, origin, coming into being"). The word appears only here and in Matthew 19:28 in the New Testament, where Jesus speaks of "the regeneration (palingenesia), when the Son of Man will sit on his glorious throne." In Matthew's usage, palingenesia describes the cosmic renewal — the restoration of all things at the end of the age. In Stoic philosophy, palingenesia described the cyclical regeneration of the cosmos. But in the present context, the word describes an individual or communal regeneration: the washing effects a new birth, a new beginning, a re-creation of the person.

The concept has deep roots in Israel's prophetic tradition. Ezekiel 36:25-27 provides the closest Old Testament parallel: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses, and from all your idols I will cleanse you. And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes and be careful to obey my rules." The Ezekiel passage combines precisely the elements present in Paul's statement: (a) cleansing with water, (b) internal renewal/new creation, and (c) the gift of God's Spirit. A first-century hearer steeped in the prophetic tradition would recognize Paul's language as the fulfillment of Ezekiel's promise: the washing of regeneration is the clean water that God promised to sprinkle on his people, and the renewal of the Holy Spirit is the new spirit that God promised to put within them.

Isaiah 44:3 (LXX) reinforces the connection: "For I will pour water on the thirsty land, and streams on the dry ground; I will pour out my Spirit upon your offspring, and my blessing on your descendants." The pouring of water and the pouring of the Spirit are parallel images for the same divine act of renewal. Jeremiah 31:31-34 (LXX 38:31-34) adds the covenant dimension: "I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel... I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts." The washing of regeneration in Titus 3:5 is the concrete realization of what the prophets described: the new covenant act by which God cleanses and remakes his people from within.

(2) Renewal of the Holy Spirit (anakainoseos pneumatos hagiou). The noun anakainosis ("renewal, renovation, making new") appears only here and in Romans 12:2 in the New Testament: "be transformed by the renewal (anakainosei) of your mind (tou noos)." The related verb anakainoun ("to renew, to make new") appears in 2 Corinthians 4:16: "our inner self is being renewed (anakainoutai) day by day," and in Colossians 3:10: "the new self, which is being renewed (anakainoumenon) in knowledge after the image of its creator." The noun describes the act of making something new — not repairing the old but producing a genuinely renewed reality. The connection to Romans 12:2 is particularly significant: in Romans, the renewal of the mind (nous) is the means by which believers are transformed and able to discern God's will. In Titus 3:3, the community's former condition included foolishness (anoetoi) — a failure of the nous. The renewal effected by the Spirit restores the very faculty that was formerly disabled.

The genitive pneumatos hagiou ("of the Holy Spirit") can be read as either subjective (the renewal that the Holy Spirit performs) or appositional (the renewal that consists in receiving the Holy Spirit). Both readings are grammatically possible, and both may be in view. The Spirit is both the agent of renewal and the content of the renewal: God renews by giving his Spirit, and the Spirit's presence is itself the renewal. This dual function echoes the Ezekiel 36:26-27 promise: God puts a new spirit within them (the renewal) and puts his Spirit within them (the agent who effects and sustains the renewal).

The grammatical relationship between loutrou palingenesias and anakainoseos pneumatos hagiou is debated: do they describe two distinct operations or two aspects of a single operation? The single preposition dia ("through") governing both genitives, and the single article-less construction, suggest a hendiadys — two expressions describing a single complex act: the washing-that-regenerates-and-the-renewal-that-the-Spirit-performs are one divine operation, not two sequential stages. God saves through a single act that cleanses, regenerates, and renews by the Spirit.

Verse 6: The Outpouring of the Spirit Through Christ

"Whom he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Savior" (hou execheen eph' hemas plousios dia Iesou Christou tou soteros hemon).

The relative pronoun hou ("whom") refers back to the Holy Spirit (pneumatos hagiou). The verb ekchein ("to pour out, to shed, to spill") in the aorist (execheen) describes a completed act of pouring. The imagery is liquid and abundant — not a measured dispensation but a flooding outpouring. The language is drawn directly from the prophetic tradition of Spirit-pouring. Joel 2:28-29 (LXX 3:1-2): "And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out (ekcheo) my Spirit on all flesh." This is the text Peter quoted at Pentecost in Acts 2:17: "And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out (ekcheo) my Spirit on all flesh." In Acts 2:33, Peter declares that Jesus, "having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, has poured out (execheen) this that you yourselves are seeing and hearing." In Acts 10:45, when the Spirit fell on the Gentiles: "the gift of the Holy Spirit was poured out (ekkechytai) even on the Gentiles."

The verb execheen in Titus 3:6 is identical in form to the verb used in Acts 2:33 — both are aorist active third person singular of ekchein. The linguistic connection to Pentecost and its prophetic background in Joel is unmistakable. Paul is describing the community's experience as the fulfillment of the same prophetic promise that Peter announced on the day of Pentecost: God has poured out his Spirit, as Joel prophesied.

The adverb plousios ("richly, abundantly, generously") qualifies the outpouring. In Colossians 3:16: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly (plousios)." In 2 Peter 1:11: "there will be richly (plousios) provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom." The outpouring is not stingy or measured but abundant and generous — consistent with the philanthropia ("loving kindness") attributed to God in verse 4. A God characterized by philanthropia pours out his Spirit plousios.

The preposition dia ("through") with the genitive Iesou Christou ("Jesus Christ") identifies Christ as the agent or mediator through whom the Spirit is poured out. The title tou soteros hemon ("our Savior") now appears applied to Christ, completing the letter's characteristic alternation. In 3:4, "God our Savior" was the source of salvation. Here in 3:6, "Jesus Christ our Savior" is the one through whom the Spirit is poured out. The alternation follows the same pattern as 1:3-4 (God our Savior / Christ Jesus our Savior), 2:10/2:13 (God our Savior / our great God and Savior Jesus Christ). The saving work belongs to God and is executed through Christ. The two are partners in the single work of salvation, sharing the same title because they share the same purpose.

Verse 7: Justified by Grace, Heirs of Eternal Life

"So that being justified by his grace we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (hina dikaiothentes te ekeinou chariti kleronomon genethomen kat' elpida zoes aioniou).

The conjunction hina ("so that, in order that") introduces the purpose of the entire sequence: God saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Spirit in order that we might be justified and become heirs. The purpose clause looks forward, not backward: the saving act aims at a future outcome.

"Being justified by his grace" (dikaiothentes te ekeinou chariti). The aorist passive participle dikaiothentes (from dikaioun, "to justify, to declare righteous, to vindicate, to acquit") describes an act of being declared righteous. The passive voice indicates that the justification is done to the believer, not by the believer: God is the one who justifies. In Romans 3:24: "being justified (dikaioumenoi) freely by his grace (te autou chariti) through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." In Romans 5:1: "Therefore, since we have been justified (dikaiothentes) by faith, we have peace with God." In Romans 8:30: "those whom he called he also justified (edikaiosen)." In Galatians 2:16: "a person is not justified (dikaioutai) by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ." The language of justification in Paul consistently describes God's act of declaring the believer righteous on the basis of grace through faith, not on the basis of works.

The instrumental dative te ekeinou chariti ("by his grace, by the grace of that one") specifies the means: justification is effected by grace. The demonstrative pronoun ekeinou ("of that one, his") points back to God our Savior (3:4) or Christ our Savior (3:6) — the antecedent is ambiguous and may be deliberately so, since the entire passage has presented God and Christ as co-actors in the single work of salvation. The noun charis ("grace, favor, gift") returns from 2:11, where "the grace of God has appeared." The two theological statements are now linked by the same vocabulary: grace appeared (2:11); we are justified by grace (3:7). The appearing of grace and the experience of justification are connected as cause and effect.

"We might become heirs" (kleronomoi genethomen). The noun kleronomos ("heir, one who inherits, one who receives an allotted portion") in the LXX and in Pauline usage carries profound covenantal weight. The kleronomos is the person who receives the kleronomia — the inheritance, the allotted portion, the promised possession. In the LXX, the inheritance language is tied to the land promise: God promised Abraham and his descendants an inheritance (kleronomia) — the land of Canaan (Genesis 15:7; Deuteronomy 1:8; 4:1). In Paul, the inheritance concept is expanded beyond the land to encompass the fullness of God's promise. Romans 4:13: "For the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir (kleronomos) of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith." Romans 8:17: "and if children, then heirs (kleronomoi) — heirs of God and fellow heirs (synkleronomoi) with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him." Galatians 3:29: "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs (kleronomoi) according to promise." Galatians 4:7: "So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir (kleronomos) through God."

The verb genethomen (aorist middle subjunctive of ginesthai, "to become") indicates a change of status: from what they once were (3:3) to what they now are — heirs. The people who were foolish, disobedient, enslaved, malicious, hated, and hateful have become heirs of God's promise. The transformation is not gradual self-improvement but a divine act of re-creation that changes the person's fundamental identity and standing.

"According to the hope of eternal life" (kat' elpida zoes aioniou). The phrase returns to the letter's opening: in 1:2, Paul's apostleship was grounded ep' elpidi zoes aioniou — "on the hope of eternal life." The same phrase closes the theological ring: the hope that grounded Paul's mission in the greeting now defines the inheritance that the justified heirs await. The preposition kata ("according to, in correspondence with") indicates that the inheritance corresponds to the hope: what has been hoped for is what will be inherited. The noun elpis ("hope") is not a vague wish but a confident expectation grounded in the promise of the God who cannot lie (1:2). The phrase zoes aioniou ("of eternal life, of the life of the age to come") identifies the content of the inheritance: life that belongs to the coming age, life beyond death and corruption, the life that Daniel 12:2 promised and that Jesus spoke of as the goal of faithful living (Mark 10:30; John 3:16; 5:24; 17:3).

The theological statement of 3:4-7 is now complete, and its structure can be mapped:

  • The divine initiative: God's goodness and loving kindness appeared (3:4)
  • The exclusion of human merit: not by works of righteousness that we did (3:5a)
  • The operative principle: according to his own mercy (3:5a)
  • The means: through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit (3:5b)
  • The mediator: poured out richly through Jesus Christ our Savior (3:6)
  • The purpose: that, justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life (3:7)

The correspondence between this statement and the one in 2:11-14 is systematic:

| 2:11-14 | 3:4-7 | |---|---| | The grace of God appeared (2:11) | The goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared (3:4) | | Bringing salvation for all people (2:11) | He saved us (3:5) | | Training us to renounce ungodliness (2:12) | Not by works of righteousness (3:5) | | To live self-controlled, upright, godly lives (2:12) | According to his own mercy (3:5) | | Waiting for the blessed hope (2:13) | Heirs according to the hope of eternal life (3:7) | | Christ gave himself to redeem and purify (2:14) | Through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Spirit (3:5) | | A people zealous for good works (2:14) | Justified by his grace (3:7) |

The two statements are complementary: 2:11-14 emphasizes the training and transformative function of grace, culminating in a people zealous for good works. 3:4-7 emphasizes the mercy and regenerative function of grace, culminating in heirs of eternal life. Together they describe the complete work of God in salvation: God's grace trains for transformed living, and God's mercy regenerates for eternal inheritance. Neither statement is complete without the other.

Verse 8: The Faithful Saying — Believers Must Devote Themselves to Good Works

"The saying is trustworthy, and I want you to insist on these things, so that those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works. These things are excellent and profitable for people."

(Pistos ho logos, kai peri touton boulomai se diabebaiousthai, hina phrontizousin kalon ergon proistasthai hoi pepisteukotes theo. Tauta estin kala kai ophelima tois anthropois.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The theological statement on salvation by mercy through regeneration and the Spirit (3:4-7). What the author argues immediately after: The warning against foolish controversies and quarrels about the law (3:9). How this verse connects the two: Verse 8 draws the practical conclusion from the theological statement: because God has saved by mercy and made believers heirs of eternal life, those who have believed must devote themselves to good works. The verse then transitions to 3:9 by implying the contrast: good works are "excellent and profitable," while the controversies of the false teachers (3:9) are "unprofitable and worthless." The verse is the hinge between the theology (3:4-7) and the practical warning (3:9-11).

"The saying is trustworthy" (pistos ho logos). The phrase pistos ho logos ("faithful is the saying, trustworthy is the word") is a formula that appears five times in the Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8). Each occurrence marks a statement of particular importance and reliability. The adjective pistos ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") declares the statement worthy of confidence. The question of which "saying" is being endorsed — whether it refers to what precedes (3:4-7) or what follows (the exhortation about good works) — is best resolved by recognizing that the formula validates the entire theological-practical complex: the saving work of God (3:4-7) and its necessary consequence in good works (3:8b) form a single trustworthy teaching.

"And I want you to insist on these things" (kai peri touton boulomai se diabebaiousthai). The verb boulesthai ("to will, to wish, to desire, to intend") expresses Paul's deliberate intention. The verb diabebaiousthai ("to insist, to affirm strongly, to speak confidently, to assert emphatically") appears only here and in 1 Timothy 1:7 in the New Testament. In 1 Timothy 1:7, those who want to be teachers of the law "do not understand (nooutes) either what they are saying or the things about which they make confident assertions (diabebaiountai)." The false teachers make confident assertions about the wrong things; Titus is to make confident assertions about the right things — the theological truths of 3:4-7 and their practical consequences. The phrase peri touton ("concerning these things") refers to the theological statement just concluded. Titus is not merely to mention these truths in passing but to insist on them, to affirm them with force and confidence.

"So that those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works" (hina phrontizousin kalon ergon proistasthai hoi pepisteukotes theo). The hina clause states the purpose of Titus's insistent teaching. The subject is hoi pepisteukotes theo — "those who have believed in God." The perfect tense of the participle (pepisteukotes, from pisteuein, "to believe, to trust, to have faith") describes persons who have come to faith and remain in that state of faith — they are believers, established in their trust in God. The fact that Paul specifies "believed in God" (theo) rather than "believed in Christ" is notable in a letter that has carefully alternated between God and Christ as the agents of salvation. The believing is directed toward God because the theological statement of 3:4-7 began with God our Savior (3:4) and traced the entire saving work to God's initiative.

The verb phrontizein ("to be careful, to give thought to, to take care, to be intent on") appears only here in the New Testament. It describes deliberate, purposeful attention — not casual or accidental engagement but careful, intentional focus. What the believers are to be careful about is kalon ergon proistasthai — "to devote themselves to good works." The verb proistasthai ("to manage, to devote oneself to, to maintain, to practice, to be occupied with, to engage in") in the middle voice describes active engagement. In 1 Thessalonians 5:12, leaders are described as those who proistamenous — "those who lead/manage." In Romans 12:8, the one who leads is to do so with zeal. In 1 Timothy 3:4-5, the elder must proistamenon — "manage" his household well. The verb carries the sense of giving active, sustained attention to something, overseeing it, being responsible for it. The believers are to treat good works as a domain requiring their active management and sustained investment.

The phrase kalon ergon ("good works") is the climax of the letter's central theme. The adjective kalos ("beautiful, noble, fine, good, excellent") describes works that are not merely morally correct but visibly admirable. The phrase has appeared at every critical juncture of the letter: 1:16 (false teachers unfit for every good work), 2:7 (Titus as model of good works), 2:14 (Christ purified a people zealous for good works), 3:1 (ready for every good work), and now 3:8 (believers devoting themselves to good works). The recurrence is a drumbeat: the letter insists, again and again, that genuine faith produces visible, admirable, beneficial action. The theological statement of 3:4-7 — salvation by mercy, not by works — does not diminish this insistence but grounds it: precisely because salvation is by mercy, those who have been saved must devote themselves to good works. The works are not the means of earning salvation but the evidence of having received it and the proper response to the mercy that granted it.

"These things are excellent and profitable for people" (tauta estin kala kai ophelima tois anthropois). The demonstrative tauta ("these things") refers to the good works just mentioned, or more broadly to the entire theological-practical teaching. The adjective kalos ("good, fine, excellent, beautiful") evaluates the teaching as excellent. The adjective ophelimos ("profitable, useful, beneficial, advantageous") appears only here, in 1 Timothy 4:8, and in 2 Timothy 3:16 in the New Testament. In 1 Timothy 4:8: "godliness is profitable (ophelimos) in every way, as it holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come." In 2 Timothy 3:16: "All Scripture is... profitable (ophelimos) for teaching." The teaching about good works grounded in God's mercy is declared both excellent in itself and beneficial for people. The dative tois anthropois ("for people") may refer to the believers who practice them, or to the broader human community that benefits from them, or to both.

Verse 9: The Warning Against Foolish Controversies

"But avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law, for they are unprofitable and worthless."

(Moras de zeteseis kai genealogias kai ereis kai machas nomikas periistaso; eisin gar anopheleis kai mataioi.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: Good works are excellent and profitable for people (3:8). What the author argues immediately after: The instruction about the divisive person — warn twice, then reject (3:10-11). How this verse connects the two: The contrast between 3:8 and 3:9 is pointed and deliberate. Good works are kala kai ophelima — "excellent and profitable." The controversies are anopheleis kai mataioi — "unprofitable and worthless." The pairing of positive and negative evaluations creates a choice: Titus (and the community) must pursue what is profitable and avoid what is unprofitable. The verse also circles back to the letter's opening crisis: the false teachers of 1:10-16, who promoted Jewish myths and human commands, are the source of the controversies, genealogies, and quarrels about the law described here. The ring is closing.

The adversative de ("but") creates the contrast with the preceding instruction about good works. The verb periistasthai ("to avoid, to shun, to turn away from, to stand aside from") in the middle imperative means "to place oneself at a distance from, to go around." In 2 Timothy 2:16, Paul uses the same verb: "But avoid (periistaso) irreverent babble." The instruction is to maintain distance: do not engage, do not participate, do not be drawn into these disputes.

Four items are to be avoided:

(1) Foolish controversies (moras zeteseis). The adjective moros ("foolish, stupid, dull") describes what lacks understanding or sense. In 2 Timothy 2:23: "Have nothing to do with foolish (moras), ignorant controversies; you know that they breed quarrels." The noun zetesis ("controversy, debate, question, inquiry, investigation") describes the kind of dispute that consumes energy without producing resolution. The combination describes debates that are not merely unresolved but fundamentally lacking in intellectual substance.

(2) Genealogies (genealogias). The noun genealogia ("genealogy, tracing of descent, record of ancestry") appeared in 1 Timothy 1:4, where the false teachers devoted themselves to "myths and endless genealogies (genealogiais aperantois), which promote speculations rather than the stewardship from God that is by faith." The genealogies in view are most likely the elaborate ancestral speculations found in Second Temple Jewish literature — the expansion and embellishment of biblical genealogies with invented details about patriarchs, angels, and cosmic events. Works like the Book of Jubilees and portions of 1 Enoch contain extensive genealogical elaborations that served as frameworks for speculative theology. These genealogies distract from the gospel's core message and generate the kind of factional disputes that divide communities.

(3) Dissensions (ereis). The noun eris ("strife, discord, contention, rivalry") describes interpersonal conflict driven by competitive or adversarial motives. In Romans 1:29: "full of envy, murder, strife (eridos)." In 1 Corinthians 1:11: "there is quarreling (erides) among you." In 1 Corinthians 3:3: "there is jealousy and strife (eris) among you." In Galatians 5:20, eris appears among the works of the flesh. The dissensions are the social fruit of the foolish controversies: when people argue about matters of no substance, personal rivalries and factional loyalties emerge.

(4) Quarrels about the law (machas nomikas). The noun mache ("fight, battle, quarrel, conflict") is the noun from which the adjective amachos ("peaceable," 3:2) was derived. The adjective nomikos ("pertaining to the law, legal, about the law") specifies the subject matter: these are quarrels about the nomos — the Torah, the law of Moses. The phrase identifies the specific content of the controversies with precision: the false teachers who are "especially those of the circumcision party" (1:10) are generating disputes about the application, interpretation, and requirements of the Mosaic law. These are not debates about whether the law is good (Paul elsewhere affirms that it is, Romans 7:12) but factional quarrels about its binding authority on Gentile believers, its dietary and purity requirements, and its role in the life of the community. Paul's verdict on these quarrels is unequivocal.

"For they are unprofitable and worthless" (eisin gar anopheleis kai mataioi). The conjunction gar ("for") provides the reason for avoiding them. Two adjectives deliver the verdict:

The adjective anopheles ("unprofitable, useless, producing no benefit") is the direct negation of ophelimos ("profitable") from verse 8. The contrast is explicit: good works are ophelima (profitable); these controversies are anopheleis (unprofitable). The community's energy is a finite resource, and it must be invested in what produces benefit, not wasted on what produces none.

The adjective mataios ("vain, empty, futile, worthless, purposeless") echoes the description of the false teachers in 1:10 as mataiologoi ("empty talkers"). The root mataios runs through the characterization of the false teaching: their talk is empty (1:10, mataiologoi), and their controversies are empty (3:9, mataioi). The emptiness is the defining quality of what the false teachers produce: it generates heat but no light, conflict but no resolution, activity but no fruit.

Verses 10-11: The Divisive Person — Warning and Rejection

"As for a person who stirs up division, after warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him, knowing that such a person is warped and sinful; he is self-condemned."

(Hairetikon anthropon meta mian kai deuteran nouthesian paraitou, eidos hoti exestraptai ho toioutos kai hamartanei, on autokatakridos.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The instruction to avoid foolish controversies (3:9). What the author argues immediately after: Personal travel instructions and the charge to provision Zenas and Apollos (3:12-13). How these verses connect the two: The instruction moves from the general (avoid controversies) to the specific (how to deal with the person who promotes them). The false teachers of 1:10-16 generate the controversies of 3:9; the hairetikos anthropos of 3:10 is the individual embodiment of that problem. The transition to 3:12-13 completes the letter's practical business: after addressing the final disciplinary instruction, Paul turns to personal logistics.

"As for a person who stirs up division" (hairetikon anthropon). The adjective hairetikos ("factious, divisive, promoting division, sectarian") appears only here in the New Testament. It is derived from the noun hairesis ("choice, sect, party, faction, opinion"). In Acts 5:17, the party of the Sadducees is called a hairesis. In Acts 15:5, "the sect (haireseōs) of the Pharisees." In Acts 24:5, Paul's opponents call the Christian movement a hairesis. In Acts 24:14, Paul responds: "according to the Way, which they call a sect (hairesin), I worship the God of our fathers." In 1 Corinthians 11:19: "there must be factions (haireseis) among you in order that those who are genuine among you may be recognized." In Galatians 5:20, haireseis appears among the works of the flesh alongside eritheia ("selfish ambition") and dichostasiai ("divisions").

The hairetikos anthropos is a person who promotes haireseis — factions, divisions, parties. The term does not describe someone who holds a private opinion but someone whose activity creates factions within the community, splitting the body into competing groups. Given the letter's context, the hairetikos anthropos is the individual who promotes the foolish controversies, genealogies, and quarrels about the law described in 3:9 — the person whose teaching and behavior divide the community. This is not a merely intellectual disagreement but a social disruption: the divisive person fractures the community's unity.

"After warning him once and then twice" (meta mian kai deuteran nouthesian). The noun nouthesia ("warning, admonition, instruction, correction") is composed of nous ("mind") + tithemi ("to place, to put"). The word literally means "placing in the mind" — putting something into a person's understanding, correcting their thinking. In Ephesians 6:4: "bring them up in the discipline and instruction (nouthesia) of the Lord." In 1 Corinthians 10:11: "these things... were written down for our instruction (nouthesian)." The related verb nouthetein appears in Acts 20:31, where Paul reminded the Ephesian elders: "for three years I did not cease night or day to admonish (nouthetōn) every one with tears." In Colossians 1:28: "warning (nouthetountes) everyone and teaching everyone with all wisdom." In 1 Thessalonians 5:14: "admonish (noutheteite) the idle."

The instruction specifies a limited process: mian kai deuteran — "one and then a second" warning. The number is deliberately limited. The divisive person is not to be endlessly engaged in dialogue but given two opportunities to hear and respond to correction. The limit recognizes that repeated engagement with a factious person often feeds the very division it seeks to cure: the more the community debates with the divisive individual, the more attention and platform the individual receives, and the more the community's energy is consumed.

The process echoes Jesus' instruction in Matthew 18:15-17 about dealing with a sinning community member: first a private confrontation, then witnesses, then the community as a whole, and finally exclusion — "let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector." The principle is the same: correction is attempted before separation, and separation comes only after correction has been offered and refused.

"Have nothing more to do with him" (paraitou). The verb paraiteisthai ("to refuse, to reject, to decline, to have nothing to do with, to excuse oneself from") in the present middle imperative means "keep refusing, keep declining." In 1 Timothy 4:7: "Have nothing to do with (paraitou) irreverent, silly myths." In 1 Timothy 5:11: "refuse (paraitou) to enroll younger widows." In 2 Timothy 2:23: "Have nothing to do with (paraitou) foolish, ignorant controversies." The verb does not necessarily describe formal excommunication in a developed ecclesiastical sense but a withdrawal of engagement: after two warnings, the community ceases to interact with the divisive person as though there were still a dispute to be resolved. The divisive person has been warned, has refused the correction, and is now declined — the community moves on without him.

"Knowing that such a person is warped and sinful; he is self-condemned" (eidos hoti exestraptai ho toioutos kai hamartanei, on autokatakridos).

The participle eidos ("knowing") provides the epistemic ground for the rejection: Titus can act with confidence because he knows what follows.

The verb ekstrephein ("to turn inside out, to pervert, to twist, to warp") in the perfect passive (exestraptai) describes a settled condition: the divisive person has been turned from his proper course and remains in that twisted state. The perfect tense indicates a completed action with ongoing results: the warping happened in the past and persists. The word appears only here in the New Testament. The image is of a thing bent out of its proper shape — like a piece of wood warped by moisture, no longer able to serve its intended purpose. The divisive person's thinking and character have been distorted to the point where they no longer function as they should.

The verb hamartanein ("to sin, to miss the mark, to err") in the present tense indicates ongoing sinful activity: the divisive person continues to sin. The present tense suggests that the sin is not a past mistake but an ongoing pattern of behavior — the divisive activity continues.

The adjective autokatakridos ("self-condemned, condemned by himself") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of autos ("self") + katakrinein ("to condemn, to pass judgment against"). The word describes a person who has condemned himself — his own actions serve as the judgment against him. By refusing the correction offered in the first and second warnings, the divisive person has demonstrated his own commitment to division. He has been given the opportunity to repent, and his refusal of that opportunity constitutes his own verdict against himself. The community does not condemn him; he condemns himself by his persistent refusal of correction. The word removes any burden of guilt from the community's decision to separate: the divisive person's own behavior is the judgment.

Verses 12-14: Personal Instructions and the Final Charge About Good Works

"When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you, do your best to come to me at Nicopolis, for I have decided to spend the winter there. Do your best to speed Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way; see that they lack nothing. And let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful."

(Hotan pempso Arteman pros se e Tychikon, spoudason elthein pros me eis Nikopolin; ekei gar kekrika parachimasai. Zenan ton nomikon kai Apollon spoudaios propempson, hina meden autois leipe. Manthanetosan de kai hoi hemeteroi kalon ergon proistasthai eis tas anankaias chreias, hina me osin akarpoi.)

What the author was arguing immediately before: The instruction about the divisive person (3:10-11). What the author argues immediately after: The final greeting (3:15). How these verses connect the two: The letter transitions from its final disciplinary instruction to the practical logistics of Paul's mission. The personal instructions are not mere postscripts but embodiments of the letter's themes: the provision for Zenas and Apollos exemplifies the good works the letter has been urging, and the final exhortation about good works (3:14) restates the letter's central practical concern one last time before the closing.

Verse 12: Travel Plans

"When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you" (hotan pempso Arteman pros se e Tychikon). The conjunction hotan ("when, whenever") with the subjunctive pempso ("I send") indicates a future event that is planned but not yet executed. Paul intends to send one of two persons to Crete as a replacement for Titus. The name Artemas (Arteman, accusative) appears only here in the New Testament. Nothing further is known about this individual. The name Tychicus (Tychikon) is better attested: in Acts 20:4, Tychicus of Asia accompanied Paul on his journey to Jerusalem. In Ephesians 6:21-22, Paul describes Tychicus as "the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord" and says he is sending him to the Ephesians. In Colossians 4:7-8, the same description appears: "Tychicus will tell you all about my activities. He is a beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord." In 2 Timothy 4:12: "Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus." Tychicus is a trusted courier and representative — a person Paul relies on for sensitive assignments. The disjunction e ("or") indicates that Paul has not yet decided which of the two will be sent.

"Do your best to come to me at Nicopolis" (spoudason elthein pros me eis Nikopolin). The verb spoudazein ("to make haste, to be diligent, to do one's best, to make every effort") in the aorist imperative commands urgent action: once the replacement arrives, Titus is to make every effort to join Paul. The destination is Nicopolis ("city of victory"). Several cities bore this name in the Roman world; the most likely candidate is Nicopolis in Epirus, on the western coast of Greece, founded by Augustus to commemorate his victory at the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE. It was a major city on the route between Rome and the eastern Mediterranean.

"For I have decided to spend the winter there" (ekei gar kekrika parachimasai). The verb krinein ("to decide, to judge, to determine") in the perfect active (kekrika) indicates a settled decision. The verb paracheimazein ("to winter, to spend the winter") describes the practice of remaining in one location during the winter months when sea travel was suspended (the mare clausum, roughly November through March). The detail is practical: Paul will be at Nicopolis through the winter, and he wants Titus to join him there before travel becomes impossible.

Verse 13: Provision for Zenas and Apollos

"Do your best to speed Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way; see that they lack nothing" (Zenan ton nomikon kai Apollon spoudaios propempson, hina meden autois leipe).

The verb propempein ("to send forward, to escort, to help on one's journey, to provision for travel") describes a specific act of hospitality: providing a departing traveler with supplies, money, companionship, and whatever else is needed for the journey. In Romans 15:24: "I hope to see you in passing and to be sent on my way (propemphthenal) there by you." In 1 Corinthians 16:6: "so that you may help me on my journey (propempsete)." In 3 John 6: "You will do well to send them on their journey (propempsas) in a manner worthy of God." The propempsis was a concrete expression of hospitality and community support — not merely saying goodbye but actively equipping the traveler for the road ahead.

The adverb spoudaios ("diligently, eagerly, with haste") intensifies the instruction: Titus is to provision them with urgency and generosity.

Two persons are named. Zenas (Zenan) is identified as ton nomikon — "the lawyer." The adjective nomikos ("pertaining to the law, legal, learned in the law") in the Gospels describes those expert in the Mosaic law (Luke 7:30; 10:25; 11:45-46, 52; 14:3). Zenas may be a Jewish legal expert who has come to faith in Christ, or the term may here refer to a Roman jurist — a person trained in Roman civil law. The name Zenas appears only here in the New Testament. Apollos (Apollon) is well known from Acts and Paul's letters. In Acts 18:24-28, Apollos is described as "a Jew, born in Alexandria, an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures" who taught accurately about Jesus, was further instructed by Priscilla and Aquila, and became a powerful apologist for the gospel in Achaia. In 1 Corinthians 1:12 and 3:4-6, Apollos appears as one of the teachers around whom factions formed in Corinth. In 1 Corinthians 3:6: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth." In 1 Corinthians 16:12, Paul mentions Apollos's travel plans. The pairing of Zenas and Apollos suggests that they may be the bearers of this letter or fellow workers passing through Crete on their way to another destination.

The purpose clause hina meden autois leipe — "so that they lack nothing" — specifies the standard of provision: the community is to ensure that Zenas and Apollos depart fully equipped, with every need met. The verb leipein ("to lack, to be deficient, to fall short") recalls ta leiponta ("the things remaining, what was left unfinished") in 1:5 — the unfinished work that Titus was left to complete. There, organizational work was lacking; here, the travelers must not lack. The community that is being organized toward wholeness must also provide wholeness to those who serve the mission.

Verse 14: The Final Good-Works Charge

"And let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful" (manthanetosan de kai hoi hemeteroi kalon ergon proistasthai eis tas anankaias chreias, hina me osin akarpoi).

The verb manthanein ("to learn, to be taught, to acquire the practice of") in the present imperative (manthanetosan, third person plural) commands ongoing learning: the community must keep learning this practice. The learning is not merely intellectual but practical: they are to learn by doing, to acquire the habit of good works through practice. The conjunction kai ("also, even") suggests an addition: in addition to what has already been said, our people too must learn this.

The possessive pronoun hoi hemeteroi ("our own people") is affectionate and identifies: these are Paul's own community members, the believers in Crete. The phrase distinguishes them from outsiders and creates a sense of communal responsibility.

The phrase kalon ergon proistasthai — "to devote themselves to good works" — repeats exactly the language of 3:8. The repetition is the letter's final statement of its central practical concern. The first occurrence (3:8) drew the conclusion from the theological statement (3:4-7). This second occurrence (3:14) applies the principle to the concrete situation of provisioning traveling workers. The good works that the letter has been urging are not merely a general aspiration but a specific, immediate practice: helping fellow workers who have practical needs.

The phrase eis tas anankaias chreias — "for cases of urgent need" or "for pressing needs" — specifies the occasions for good works. The adjective anankaios ("necessary, pressing, urgent, indispensable") indicates that the needs are not trivial but genuine. The noun chreia ("need, necessity, requirement, use") identifies what requires attention: real needs of real people. The good works that the letter envisions are responsive to concrete human situations, not performed for display or merit.

"And not be unfruitful" (hina me osin akarpoi). The final hina clause states the negative purpose: the community must practice good works so that they do not become akarpos — "unfruitful, barren, producing nothing." The adjective akarpos ("unfruitful, barren, without fruit") is composed of the alpha-privative (a-) + karpos ("fruit, produce, result"). In Matthew 13:22, the seed choked by thorns becomes "unfruitful" (akarpos). In Mark 4:19, worldly concerns make the word "unfruitful" (akarpon). In 2 Peter 1:8: "if these qualities are yours and are increasing, they keep you from being ineffective or unfruitful (akarpous) in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ." In Jude 12, the false teachers are described as "fruitless trees in late autumn, twice dead, uprooted." The agricultural metaphor treats the community as a planting that must bear fruit. A community that does not produce good works is like a tree that does not produce fruit — it occupies space without fulfilling its purpose. The God who saved them by mercy (3:5), poured out his Spirit on them richly (3:6), and made them heirs of eternal life (3:7) did not do so in order to produce a barren community but a fruitful one.

The connection to Jesus' teaching in John 15:1-8 is audible: "Every branch in me that does not bear fruit he takes away... By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit" (John 15:2, 8). The expectation of fruit-bearing is not an optional addendum to faith but an inherent dimension of it. A faith that does not produce fruit is a faith that has failed to fulfill its purpose.

Verse 15: The Final Greeting

"All who are with me send greetings to you. Greet those who love us in the faith. Grace be with you all."

(Aspazontai se hoi met' emou pantes. Aspasai tous philountas hemas en pistei. He charis meta panton hymon.)

"All who are with me send greetings to you" (aspazontai se hoi met' emou pantes). The verb aspazesthai ("to greet, to welcome, to embrace, to salute") is the standard epistolary greeting formula. The phrase hoi met' emou pantes ("all who are with me") indicates that Paul is surrounded by fellow workers and community members who join in the greeting. The pronoun se ("you") is singular, addressed to Titus personally.

"Greet those who love us in the faith" (aspasai tous philountas hemas en pistei). The verb philein ("to love, to have affection for, to be fond of") describes personal affection. The participle tous philountas hemas ("those who love us") identifies a specific group among the Cretan believers: those who have personal affection for Paul and his mission. The phrase en pistei ("in the faith") qualifies the love: this is love that operates within the sphere of faith, love that is grounded in and expressed through the shared faith of the community. The same phrase koinen pistin ("common faith") appeared in 1:4 to describe the faith that Paul and Titus share. The love that is grounded in this common faith is the relational bond of the community.

"Grace be with you all" (he charis meta panton hymon). The final benediction extends grace — the same charis that appeared in 2:11 (the grace of God has appeared) and 3:7 (justified by his grace) — to the entire community. The pronoun hymon ("you") is now plural, expanding the address from Titus alone (se, singular, earlier in the verse) to the whole community (panton hymon, "all of you"). The letter that opened with "grace and peace" (1:4) closes with "grace" — the word that has carried the letter's theological weight from beginning to end. The grace that appeared, that trained, that saved, that justified, that made heirs is now invoked as a continuing presence: may it remain with all of you.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Titus 3 completes the letter by extending its behavioral instruction outward, providing its second and final theological grounding, and closing the ring that the letter opened.

(1) Verses 1-2: The community's posture toward the outside world. The behavioral instructions move from the internal life of the community (chapter 2's instructions by social category) to the community's relationship with the civic order. Believers are to submit to rulers and authorities, obey, be ready for every good work, speak evil of no one, avoid quarreling, be gentle, and show complete courtesy toward all people. The instruction is both civic and missionary: the community's conduct in the public sphere either commends or discredits the gospel.

(2) Verse 3: The motivational ground for gentleness. The reason the community must treat outsiders with courtesy is that the community was once in the same condition as those outsiders. The catalog of the former life — foolish, disobedient, led astray, enslaved to passions and pleasures, passing days in malice and envy, hated and hating — eliminates any basis for spiritual superiority and grounds the instruction to show gentleness in shared experience.

(3) Verses 4-7: The second theological statement — salvation by mercy through regeneration. The core of the chapter is the theological statement that parallels 2:11-14. God's goodness and loving kindness appeared (3:4), and he saved us — not because of works done in righteousness but according to his own mercy (3:5a) — through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit (3:5b), whom he poured out richly through Jesus Christ our Savior (3:6), so that, justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life (3:7). Every element of the statement emphasizes divine initiative and excludes human merit as the basis of salvation. The means of salvation — the washing of regeneration and the Spirit's renewal — fulfills the prophetic promise of Ezekiel 36:25-27, where God pledged to sprinkle clean water on his people, give them a new heart, and put his Spirit within them. The purpose of salvation is the creation of justified heirs — people whose status before God is secured by grace and whose future is defined by the hope of eternal life first announced in the letter's greeting (1:2).

(4) Verse 8: The practical conclusion. The theological statement issues in a practical demand: those who have believed in God must be careful to devote themselves to good works. The declaration that these things are "excellent and profitable for people" sets up the contrast with the next verse.

(5) Verse 9: The warning against foolish controversies. In direct contrast to the excellent and profitable good works, the foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law promoted by the false teachers are declared "unprofitable and worthless." The instruction to avoid them closes the ring with 1:10-14: the insubordinate, empty-talking, mind-deceiving members of the circumcision party who teach Jewish myths and human commands have been the source of the controversies the community must now avoid.

(6) Verses 10-11: The divisive person. The general instruction to avoid controversies becomes specific: the person who promotes faction is to be warned once, then twice, and then rejected. The divisive person is self-condemned — his refusal of correction constitutes his own judgment against himself. The community is freed from the obligation of endless engagement with a person whose activity destroys unity.

(7) Verses 12-14: Personal instructions and the final good-works charge. The letter closes with practical logistics — travel plans, provision for fellow workers — that embody the good works the letter has been urging. The final exhortation restates the central theme one last time: "let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful." The community's purpose is fruit-bearing — the production of visible, concrete, beneficial acts that demonstrate the reality of the faith they profess and the mercy they have received.

(8) Verse 15: The greeting and benediction. Grace — the word that has carried the letter's theological weight from the appearing of grace (2:11) to justification by grace (3:7) — is invoked as a continuing presence for the entire community.

The letter's overall architecture is now visible in its completeness. Chapter 1 diagnosed the crisis: false teachers who profess to know God but deny him by their works, rendering themselves unfit for every good work. Chapter 2 prescribed the remedy: sound teaching that produces transformed conduct in every social category, grounded in the appearing of grace that trains believers to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives as a people zealous for good works. Chapter 3 extended the remedy outward to civic life, grounded it in a second theological statement about salvation by mercy through regeneration and the Spirit, drew the conclusion that believers must devote themselves to good works, and warned against the controversies that the false teachers promote. The letter began with the hope of eternal life promised by the God who cannot lie (1:2) and ends with heirs of that same eternal life, justified by grace, and charged to demonstrate that inheritance through the good works that their faith demands and their mercy empowers.
