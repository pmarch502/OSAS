1 Corinthians 5 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 3-4): After establishing in chapters 1-2 that God's wisdom operates through the cross rather than through human rhetorical skill, and that the Spirit provides the only adequate faculty for spiritual discernment, Paul drew the devastating application in chapter 3: the Corinthians are not behaving as pneumatikoi (spiritual people) but as sarkinoi (fleshly people), as nepioi (infants) in Christ (3:1). The proof is their factionalism: "when one says, 'I am of Paul,' and another, 'I am of Apollos,' are you not merely human?" (3:4). Paul reframed the relationship between teachers and community: Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth (3:6). Neither planter nor waterer is anything; God is the one who matters (3:7). The Corinthians are "God's field, God's building" (3:9) — they belong to God, not to any teacher.

Paul then introduced the building metaphor. He laid a foundation — Jesus Christ — and others build on it, but each builder's work will be tested by fire on "the day" (3:13). The quality of the materials matters: gold, silver, and precious stones survive; wood, hay, and straw burn. A builder whose work survives receives a reward; a builder whose work burns "will suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire" (3:15). Then came a solemn warning of a different order: "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you? If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple" (3:16-17). The word naos ("temple, shrine, inner sanctuary") identifies the community as the dwelling place of God's Spirit. The warning is symmetrical and severe: destroying God's temple results in God's destroying the destroyer. The holiness of the temple is not negotiable.

Chapter 3 concluded by returning to the boasting theme: "So let no one boast in human beings. For all things are yours, whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas or the world or life or death or the present or the future — all are yours, and you are Christ's, and Christ is God's" (3:21-23). Everything belongs to the community because they belong to Christ, who belongs to God. The hierarchy is clear and the direction of belonging is upward, not lateral.

Chapter 4 developed the role of apostles as "servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries" (4:1). The key obligation of stewards is faithfulness (4:2). Paul declared his indifference to the Corinthians' judgment: "It is the Lord who judges me" (4:4). He warned them not to judge "before the time, before the Lord comes, who will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart" (4:5). He then drew a sharp contrast between the Corinthians' self-satisfaction and the apostles' suffering. The Corinthians are "already satisfied," "already rich," "reigning as kings" (4:8) — dripping with irony. Meanwhile the apostles are "last of all, like men sentenced to death" (4:9), "fools for Christ's sake" while the Corinthians are "wise in Christ," "weak" while they are "strong," "held in disrepute" while they are "held in honor" (4:10). The apostles are hungry, thirsty, poorly dressed, beaten, homeless, reviled, persecuted, slandered (4:11-13). Paul does not say this to shame them but to "admonish" them as "my beloved children" (4:14). He is their father in Christ Jesus through the gospel (4:15) and urges them to imitate him (4:16).

The chapter closes with a direct warning. Paul is sending Timothy, but he himself will come soon, "if the Lord wills" (4:19). "And I will find out not the talk of these arrogant people but their power" (4:19). The word phusioo ("to puff up, to inflate, to make arrogant") appears here and has appeared throughout chapter 4 (4:6, 4:18, 4:19). Paul's final question: "What do you wish? Shall I come to you with a rod, or with love and a spirit of gentleness?" (4:21). The "rod" (rhabdos) is the instrument of discipline. Paul leaves the choice to them.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 6): Chapter 6 begins with another case of community failure: "When one of you has a grievance against another, does he dare go to law before the unrighteous instead of the saints?" (6:1). The community members are taking disputes before pagan courts instead of resolving them internally. Paul asks: "Do you not know that the saints will judge the world?" (6:2). If they are destined to judge the world and even angels (6:3), they are certainly competent to judge "trivial cases" (6:2). He tells them to "appoint as judges even those of no standing in the assembly" (6:4) — even the least among them would be better than pagan judges for internal matters. "I say this to your shame. Can it be that there is no one among you wise enough to settle a dispute between the brothers?" (6:5). The connection to chapter 5 is direct: in chapter 5 the community failed to exercise internal discipline when it should have; in 6:1-8 the community goes to external courts when it should handle matters internally. Both failures reveal the same disease — the community does not understand the boundary between inside and outside, between the assembly and the world.

Chapter 6 then shifts to sexual immorality (6:12-20), connecting directly back to the porneia of chapter 5. Paul addresses the slogan "all things are lawful for me" (6:12) — apparently a phrase the Corinthians used to justify their behavior — and counters: "but not all things are beneficial" and "I will not be dominated by anything" (6:12). He argues that the body is not for porneia but "for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (6:13). The body will be raised (6:14). The believers' bodies are "members of Christ" (6:15) — to unite Christ's members with a prostitute is to make Christ one flesh with her (6:15-16). "The one who joins himself to the Lord becomes one spirit with him" (6:17). "Flee from sexual immorality" (6:18). "Your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God" (6:19). "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (6:20). The individual body as temple (6:19) extends the communal body as temple (3:16-17). The porneia case of chapter 5 is the specific instance; chapter 6:12-20 provides the theological reasoning for why porneia is incompatible with community identity.

How chapter 5 connects to what precedes and follows: Chapter 5 is not a change of subject. It is the first concrete case demonstrating the disease Paul has been diagnosing since chapter 1. The factionalism, the arrogance, the worldly wisdom, the self-congratulation — all of these produced a community so inflated with its own spiritual status that it tolerated within itself a sexual arrangement that even pagans found unacceptable. The word phusioo ("puffed up"), which appeared four times in chapter 4 (4:6, 4:18, 4:19) to describe the Corinthians' arrogant self-regard, reappears in 5:2 as the precise diagnosis of their failure in this case: "And you are puffed up!" Their arrogance is not an abstract theological error; it has a concrete consequence — they have failed to act when action was required.

The chapter also transitions the letter from the factionalism problem (chapters 1-4) to a series of specific behavioral failures (chapters 5-6). But the transition is organic, not abrupt. The specific cases are symptoms of the same root disease. A community that evaluates by worldly wisdom rather than by the Spirit's discernment, that boasts in human leaders rather than in the Lord, that considers itself spiritually rich and reigning as kings (4:8) — such a community will also fail to recognize when its own holiness has been violated. The arrogance that produces factions also produces tolerance of sin.

Chapter 5 also sets up chapter 6 by establishing the principle of internal community discipline. The assembly is responsible for judging those inside (5:12). This principle, stated at the end of chapter 5, immediately generates the problem of chapter 6: the community that should judge its own members (chapter 5) is instead outsourcing its disputes to pagan courts (6:1-8). Both chapters address the community's failure to exercise the judgment that its identity requires.

Verses 1-2: The Report and the Community's Failure

"It is actually reported that there is sexual immorality among you, and of a kind that is not tolerated even among pagans, for a man has his father's wife" (5:1).

The word holos ("actually, altogether, in general") intensifies the report. This is not rumor or speculation; the matter is widely known. The word porneia ("sexual immorality, fornication") is the broad term for illicit sexual conduct. In the LXX, porneia and its cognates translate the Hebrew zanah and its derivatives, covering a wide range of sexual transgressions that violate God's ordering of human relationships. The word will appear again in 6:13, 6:18, and 7:2, each time describing conduct incompatible with belonging to the Lord.

Paul's description is precise: "a man has his father's wife" (gynaika tina tou patros echein). The present infinitive echein ("to have") indicates an ongoing arrangement, not a single act. The phrase "his father's wife" (tou patros gynaika) rather than "his mother" (meter) strongly implies a stepmother — the father's wife by a marriage other than the one that produced this man. The father may be dead or divorced; the text does not specify. What matters is the relationship: a man is maintaining a sexual relationship with a woman who is or was his father's wife.

The Old Testament background is unmistakable and would have been immediately recognizable to any Jew or God-fearer in the Corinthian assembly. Leviticus 18:8 (LXX): "You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's wife; it is your father's nakedness" (aschemosynen gynaikos patros sou ouk apokalypseis; aschemosynen patros sou estin). The phrase "uncover the nakedness" (apokalypsai aschemosynen) is the Levitical idiom for sexual intercourse with a forbidden partner. Leviticus 18:8 places this prohibition within the comprehensive list of forbidden sexual relationships in Leviticus 18, introduced by the warning: "You shall not do as they do in the land of Egypt, where you lived, and you shall not do as they do in the land of Canaan, to which I am bringing you" (18:3). The punishment prescribed is severe: "every person who does any of these abominations — the persons who do them shall be cut off from among their people" (Leviticus 18:29, LXX: exolethreuthesontai hai psychai hai poiousai — "the souls that do them shall be utterly destroyed/cut off").

Deuteronomy 22:30 (23:1 in Hebrew) states it with stark brevity: "A man shall not take his father's wife, and he shall not uncover his father's covering" (ouk lepsetai anthropos gynaika tou patros autou kai ouk apokalypsei synkalymma tou patros autou). The metaphor of the "covering" (synkalymma, translating the Hebrew kanaph, "wing, skirt") represents the father's sexual claim and the relationship it constitutes. To take the father's wife is to violate the father's own boundary.

Deuteronomy 27:20 includes this sin in the covenant curses pronounced at Mount Ebal: "Cursed be anyone who lies with his father's wife, because he has uncovered his father's nakedness" (epikataratos ho koimomenos meta gynaikos tou patros autou, hoti apokalypsai synkalymma tou patros autou). The assembly responds "Amen" — the entire community ratifies the curse.

The Old Testament also records a narrative precedent. In Genesis 35:22, Reuben "went and lay with Bilhah his father's concubine." Jacob's response, recorded in Genesis 49:3-4 at his deathbed, was to strip Reuben of the firstborn's preeminence: "Unstable as water, you shall not have preeminence, because you went up to your father's bed; then you defiled it" (LXX: exebrasas hosper hydor, me ekzeseis; anebas gar epi ten koiten tou patros sou; tote emianas ten stronen hou anebas). Reuben lost his position as firstborn because of this act. The precedent demonstrates that taking a father's wife was considered a fundamental violation of familial order, with lasting consequences.

Paul adds a comparison that sharpens the indictment: "of a kind that is not tolerated even among pagans" (hetis oude en tois ethnesin). The word ethnesin ("nations, Gentiles, pagans") refers to those outside the covenant community. Roman law prohibited marriage between a man and his stepmother. Cicero, in Pro Cluentio 5.14, prosecuted a case involving a woman (Sassia) who married her former son-in-law, calling it "an incredible act, unheard of in all experience save for this single instance" (incredibile et praeter hanc unam in omni vita inauditum). Gaius, in the Institutes (1.63), classified marriage with a stepmother among unions forbidden by Roman law. The point is devastating: the Corinthian community, which has been enriched with every spiritual gift (1:7), which possesses the mind of Christ (2:16), which is God's temple indwelt by the Spirit (3:16), is tolerating conduct that even the pagan world surrounding them finds unacceptable. The community that should exceed the moral standards of the world has fallen below them.

"And you are arrogant! Ought you not rather to mourn? Let him who has done this be removed from among you" (5:2).

"And you are arrogant" (kai hymeis pephysiomenoi este) — the verb phusioo ("to puff up, to inflate, to make arrogant") appears here in the perfect passive participle: "you have been puffed up and remain puffed up." The perfect tense indicates an established condition, not a momentary lapse. This is the same word that dominated chapter 4: "some are arrogant" (ephusothesan tines, 4:18), "the talk of these arrogant people" (ton pephusiomenon, 4:19). The disease Paul diagnosed abstractly in chapter 4 now has a concrete manifestation. The community's self-inflation has produced moral blindness. They are so convinced of their spiritual superiority that they cannot recognize — or cannot bring themselves to act upon — a case of flagrant sexual transgression in their midst.

What should the community's response have been? Paul states it: "Ought you not rather to mourn?" (ouchi mallon epenthesate). The verb pentheo ("to mourn, to grieve, to lament") describes the response appropriate to a death or a catastrophe. In the LXX, the word describes mourning for the dead (Genesis 50:3, 2 Samuel 11:27), lamentation over disaster (Ezra 10:6, Nehemiah 1:4), and grief over sin. In Ezra 10:6, Ezra "did not eat bread or drink water, for he was mourning over the faithlessness of the exiles" (epenthei epi te asyntheteia tes apoikesias). The appropriate response to sin within the community is grief, not indifference, and certainly not arrogance.

The purpose of mourning is connected to action: "Let him who has done this be removed from among you" (hina arthe ek mesou hymon ho to ergon touto praxas). The verb airo ("to remove, to take away, to lift up") with ek mesou hymon ("from your midst") echoes Deuteronomy's formula for purging evil from the community. The aorist subjunctive arthe indicates decisive action: the one who has done this thing (ho to ergon touto praxas — "the one having practiced this deed") is to be expelled. Paul uses the word ergon ("work, deed") rather than hamartia ("sin") or porneia, which may emphasize the deliberate, ongoing nature of the arrangement — this is something being practiced, not something that happened once.

Verses 3-5: Paul's Judgment and the Assembly's Action

"For though absent in body, I am present in spirit; and as if present, I have already pronounced judgment on the one who did such a thing" (5:3).

Paul's language is precise about his relationship to the situation. He is agon to somati ("absent in body") — physically in another location. But he is paron de to pneumati ("present in spirit"). The word pneuma here most likely refers to Paul's own spirit — his personal presence in intentionality and authority — rather than to the Holy Spirit, though the distinction is not absolute since Paul understands his own spiritual activity as connected to the Spirit of God. The phrase communicates that physical distance does not diminish Paul's pastoral authority or his capacity to act.

"I have already pronounced judgment" (ede kekrika) — the perfect tense of krino ("to judge, to decide, to determine, to render a verdict") indicates a completed verdict with present consequences. Paul has not waited for further information or deliberation. He has already judged. The man's case is decided. The word kekrika will be echoed in the chapter's closing verses (5:12-13), where Paul distinguishes between judging those inside and those outside the community.

"In the name of our Lord Jesus, when you are assembled, and my spirit is with you, with the power of our Lord Jesus, you are to deliver this man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (5:4-5).

These verses describe an action to be taken by the assembled community. The syntax is complex, but the elements are identifiable:

The setting: "when you are assembled" (synachthenton hymon) — the action takes place when the community gathers for assembly. This is not a private act by individuals but a corporate decision of the ekklesia gathered together. The word synago ("to gather together, to assemble") is the verb from which synagoge derives; it describes the formal gathering of the community.

The authorization: "in the name of our Lord Jesus" (en to onomati tou kyriou hemon Iesou) — the action is performed under the authority and in the sphere of the Lord Jesus. The "name" carries the full weight of the person's authority, as it did in 1:2, 1:10, and 1:13. This is not mob action or personal vendetta; it is authorized by the Lord whose community this is.

The attendant presence: "and my spirit is with you" (kai tou emou pneumatos) — Paul's apostolic presence and authority are part of the proceeding even though he is physically absent.

The power: "with the power of our Lord Jesus" (syn te dynamei tou kyriou hemon Iesou) — the action is accomplished by the Lord's own power. The word dynamis ("power") has been a key term since 1:18 (the word of the cross is "the power of God") and 1:24 (Christ is "the power of God and the wisdom of God") and 2:4-5 (Paul came "in demonstration of the Spirit and of power" so that their faith would rest on "the power of God"). The same power that constitutes the community's existence also authorizes its discipline.

The action: "you are to deliver this man to Satan" (paradounai ton toiouton to Satana) — the verb paradidomi ("to hand over, to deliver, to entrust, to turn over") is the word used for Judas's betrayal of Jesus (Matthew 26:15, Mark 14:10, Luke 22:4) and for the legal handing over of a prisoner to authorities (Matthew 27:2, Acts 8:3). The person is being transferred from one sphere of authority to another. "Satan" (ho Satanas) is the adversary — the accuser (the Hebrew satan means "adversary, opponent") who in Job 1-2 operates with God's permission as the agent who tests and afflicts. In the LXX of Job, Satan presents himself before God and receives permission to afflict Job's possessions and then his body, but with limits: "only do not stretch out your hand against him" (Job 1:12), and "he is in your hand; only spare his life" (Job 2:6). The paradigm is significant: Satan operates under divine permission and within divine limits. The "handing over to Satan" is not abandonment to a power outside God's control; it is a transfer to the realm outside the community's protective boundaries, where the adversary operates.

The purpose of the handing over has two parts, and they are stated as a single sequence:

"For the destruction of the flesh" (eis olethron tes sarkos) — the word olethros ("destruction, ruin, loss") appears in the LXX in contexts of divine judgment: 1 Samuel 5:9, 11 (the destruction brought by the ark upon the Philistines); Jeremiah 48:3 (LXX 31:3, "a voice of crying and great destruction"). The word sarx ("flesh") has been a significant term in this letter. In 1:26, "not many wise according to the flesh" (kata sarka) described evaluation by worldly, human standards. In 3:1, Paul could not address them "as spiritual people but as fleshly people" (sarkinois). The "flesh" in Paul's usage in this letter represents the sphere of merely human existence oriented away from the Spirit — the natural, worldly mode of living that the Corinthians have been operating from despite having received the Spirit. The "destruction of the flesh" is not necessarily physical death (though it may include physical suffering); it is the breaking of the person's fleshly, self-oriented mode of existence. Removed from the community's protection and subjected to Satan's domain, the person would experience the consequences of life outside God's assembly — consequences that would, ideally, break the pattern of sinful behavior.

"So that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (hina to pneuma sothe en te hemera tou kyriou) — the conjunction hina ("so that, in order that") marks this as a purpose clause. The ultimate goal of the entire action — the assembly, the judgment, the handing over, the destruction of the flesh — is salvation, not punishment for its own sake. The "spirit" (pneuma) of the man is to be "saved" (sothe, aorist passive subjunctive of sozo) "in the day of the Lord" (en te hemera tou kyriou). The "day of the Lord" was introduced in 1:8 as "the day of our Lord Jesus Christ," transposing the prophetic yom YHWH onto Christ. The same day that Paul's thanksgiving in 1:8 described as the occasion when believers would stand "guiltless" (anenkletous) is now the standard against which the discipline of this man is measured. The discipline is designed to bring the man to the point where he will be saved on that day.

The logic is restorative, not eliminative. The community does not expel the man because he is worthless or beyond hope. The community expels the man precisely because he is worth saving, and the current arrangement — tolerated within the community — is leading him toward destruction on the day of the Lord. The expulsion is a drastic intervention intended to break the pattern and redirect the trajectory. The precedent in Israel's history is instructive: when God sent Israel into exile, it was not the end of the relationship but a severe measure within the covenant designed to produce repentance and return (Deuteronomy 30:1-6; Jeremiah 29:10-14; Hosea 2:14-23). The pattern of severe action with restorative intent is deeply embedded in the scriptural tradition.

Verses 6-8: The Leaven and the Passover

"Your boasting is not good. Do you not know that a little leaven leavens the whole lump?" (5:6).

The word kauchema ("boasting, ground of boasting, that in which one boasts") connects directly to the boasting theme that has run through the letter since 1:29 ("so that no flesh may boast before God"), 1:31 ("let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord"), 3:21 ("so let no one boast in human beings"), and 4:7 ("why do you boast as if you did not receive it?"). The Corinthians' boasting has been consistently identified as misplaced — directed at human teachers, human wisdom, and human status rather than at the Lord. Here the boasting is not good (ou kalon) because it coexists with unchecked sin. A community cannot simultaneously boast in its spiritual status and tolerate a violation that pagans would not tolerate. The boasting and the tolerance are related: the arrogance that inflates the community's self-regard also blinds it to its own corruption.

"Do you not know" (ouk oidate) — this phrase appears ten times in 1 Corinthians (3:16, 5:6, 6:2, 6:3, 6:9, 6:15, 6:16, 6:19, 9:13, 9:24). Each occurrence introduces something the Corinthians should already know but are acting as though they do not. The repeated formula suggests a community that has received extensive teaching but is failing to apply it.

"A little leaven leavens the whole lump" (mikra zyme holon to phyrama zymoi) — this is a proverbial saying about the pervasive effect of a small agent on a large mass. Leaven (zyme) is a piece of fermented dough that, when mixed into a new batch, causes the entire mass (phyrama, "lump of dough") to rise. The action is invisible, gradual, and total: once introduced, the leaven cannot be contained. It works through the entire lump.

In the LXX and in Jewish practice, leaven carried a dual significance. In everyday life, leaven was essential for bread-making and morally neutral. But in the context of Passover, leaven represented what must be eliminated. Exodus 12:15 (LXX): "Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread. On the first day you shall remove leaven from your houses" (exareite zymen ek ton oikion hymon). Exodus 12:19: "For seven days no leaven shall be found in your houses. If anyone eats what is leavened, that person shall be cut off from the assembly of Israel" (exolethreuthsetai he psyche ekeine ek synagoges Israel). Exodus 13:7: "No leavened bread shall be seen with you, and no leaven shall be seen with you in all your territory." The Passover purging of leaven was a comprehensive, house-by-house removal of every trace. Nothing leavened could remain. The connection to the man in chapter 5 is direct: as Israel purged every trace of leaven before Passover, so the community must purge the sinful element from its midst.

Paul is not making a general observation about impurity. He is applying a specific Exodus requirement to a specific community situation. The "little leaven" is the tolerated sin; the "whole lump" is the community. If the sin remains, it will work through the entire body, corrupting what should be holy.

"Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are unleavened" (5:7a).

"Cleanse out" (ekkatharate) — the aorist imperative of ekkathairo ("to cleanse thoroughly, to purge completely"). The prefix ek- intensifies the cleansing: this is thorough removal, not partial reduction. The Exodus instructions required Israel to remove all leaven, not most of it. The word "old" (palaian) identifies the leaven with the former state — the old existence that should have been left behind. Paul commands them to become in practice what they already are in identity: "as you really are unleavened" (kathos este azymoi). The word azymoi ("unleavened") describes their true identity. They are unleavened — their identity in Christ is pure, set apart, consecrated (1:2, hegiasmenois). The imperative to cleanse out the leaven is therefore not a command to achieve a new status but to align their practice with a status they already possess. The indicative grounds the imperative: because you are unleavened, become unleavened. Because you are God's holy people, act like God's holy people. This is the same logic that operates throughout the letter: the Corinthians have been sanctified (1:2) but must live in accordance with their sanctification.

"For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (5:7b).

"For" (kai gar) introduces the ground of the command. The statement to pascha hemon etythe Christos is compact and rich. The word pascha ("Passover") in the LXX translates the Hebrew pesach, the lamb slaughtered on the fourteenth of Nisan as described in Exodus 12:3-13. The Passover lamb was to be "without blemish" (probaton teleion, Exodus 12:5 LXX), slaughtered "between the evenings" (12:6), its blood applied to the doorposts and lintel so that the destroyer would pass over the house (12:7, 12:13, 12:23). The Passover was not merely a memorial; it was the foundational act of Israel's deliverance from Egypt. The annual observance reenacted and participated in the original event.

Paul identifies Christ as "our Passover" (to pascha hemon) — the community's Passover lamb. The verb etythe (aorist passive of thyo, "to sacrifice, to slaughter, to offer") places the action in the past: Christ has already been sacrificed. The Passover sacrifice has occurred. This is stated as a fact from which consequences follow. If the Passover lamb has been slaughtered, then the festival has begun. And if the festival has begun, the leaven must already have been removed.

The logic is temporal and sequential, drawn from the Passover calendar. In Jewish practice, the search for and removal of leaven (bedikat chametz) occurred before the lamb was slaughtered. By the time the Passover meal was eaten, the house was to be entirely free of leaven. Paul is telling the Corinthians: the lamb has already been slaughtered. You should already have removed the leaven. The fact that leaven remains in your midst is a violation of the Passover order itself. You are celebrating the festival with leaven still in the house.

The identification of Christ with the Passover lamb connects the community's life to the Exodus narrative. Israel's entire national existence began with the Passover: the blood of the lamb, the meal eaten in haste, the departure from slavery, the passage through the sea. The community in Corinth exists because of an analogous event: the sacrifice of Christ, the new Passover. Their communal life is a perpetual festival (as the next verse will make explicit), and the conditions of that festival include the removal of leaven. The Exodus is not merely a typological backdrop; it defines the community's identity and the expectations that follow from that identity.

"Let us therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of malice and evil, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (5:8).

"Let us celebrate the festival" (hoste heortazomen) — the word heortazo ("to keep festival, to celebrate a feast") appears only here in the New Testament. In the LXX, the verb describes the observance of Israel's festivals: Exodus 5:1, "let my people go, that they may celebrate a festival to me in the wilderness" (heortasatosin moi en te eremo). The present subjunctive (heortazomen) indicates ongoing action: the community's entire life is the festival. This is not a reference to a specific annual observance but to the permanent condition of the community whose Passover lamb has been sacrificed. Every day is Passover. Every gathering is the festival. And therefore every day requires the absence of leaven.

Paul now interprets the leaven metaphor, translating it from the agricultural to the moral sphere. The "old leaven" (palaia zyme) is identified as "the leaven of malice and evil" (zyme kakias kai ponerias). The word kakia ("malice, wickedness, ill-will, depravity") in the LXX describes the general condition of moral corruption. The word poneria ("evil, wickedness, baseness") is a near-synonym that emphasizes active malice. Together they characterize the old existence — the life before and apart from Christ, the unreformed condition that should have been left behind.

The alternative is "the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (azymois eilikrinias kai aletheias). The word eilikrinia ("sincerity, purity, genuineness") comes from a root that may mean "tested by sunlight" (eilikrineia, from heile, "sunlight," and krino, "to judge") — that which proves genuine when held up to the light. In 2 Corinthians 1:12 and 2:17, Paul uses the word for the sincerity of his own ministry. The word aletheia ("truth") in Paul's usage denotes reality as opposed to pretense, the genuine state of affairs as opposed to appearances. The unleavened bread of the community's festival life consists of genuine integrity and truthfulness — the opposite of the pretense involved in boasting about spiritual status while harboring unaddressed sin.

Verses 9-13: The Boundary Between Inside and Outside

"I wrote to you in my letter not to associate with sexually immoral people" (5:9).

Paul refers to a previous letter — not 1 Corinthians itself but an earlier communication that has not survived. The word epistole ("letter") and the aorist egrapsa ("I wrote") point to a prior correspondence. This confirms that 1 Corinthians is not Paul's first communication with the community; at least one earlier letter existed. Its content included an instruction about association with sexually immoral people (pornoi). The Corinthians apparently misunderstood — or chose to misunderstand — this instruction, prompting Paul's clarification.

"Not at all meaning the sexually immoral of this world, or the greedy and swindlers, or idolaters, since then you would need to go out of the world" (5:10).

"Not at all" (ou pantos) — Paul emphatically clarifies. His earlier instruction was not about avoiding all contact with sinful people in the surrounding society. Such avoidance would be impossible: opheilete ara ek tou kosmou exelthein — "you would then need to depart from the world." The community lives in Corinth, a commercial city. Its members work, trade, and interact with pagans daily. Paul lists the types of people they cannot avoid: pornoi ("sexually immoral"), pleonektai ("greedy, covetous"), harpages ("swindlers, robbers, extortioners"), and eidololatrai ("idolaters"). These four categories represent the moral landscape of pagan urban life. The community cannot withdraw from the world; it must live in it.

This clarification is important for understanding Paul's view of the community's relationship to the surrounding culture. The community is not a sect that retreats from society. It is "the assembly of God that is in Corinth" (1:2) — located in the city, engaged with the city, but not defined by the city's moral standards. The boundary is not geographical but relational and covenantal.

"But now I am writing to you not to associate with anyone who bears the name of brother if he is guilty of sexual immorality or greed, or is an idolater, reviler, drunkard, or swindler — not even to eat with such a one" (5:11).

"Now" (nyn de) marks the new, clarified instruction. The key phrase is ean tis adelphos onomazomenos — "if anyone named a brother" or "anyone who bears the name of brother." The word onomazomenos (present passive participle of onomazo, "to name, to call, to designate") describes someone who carries the title adelphos ("brother") — someone who is identified as a member of the community, who claims the name, who is recognized by others as belonging. The instruction is not about pagans but about insiders. The community's responsibility is not to reform the world but to maintain its own integrity.

The list of offenses expands beyond porneia. Six categories are named: pornos ("sexually immoral person"), pleonektes ("greedy person"), eidololatres ("idolater"), loidoros ("reviler, abuser, one who insults"), methysos ("drunkard"), and harpax ("swindler, robber"). The list combines sexual, economic, religious, verbal, and social offenses. Porneia is not uniquely disqualifying; greed, idolatry, abusive speech, drunkenness, and extortion are equally grounds for the community's refusal of fellowship. The issue is not one particular sin but a pattern of life incompatible with the community's identity as God's holy assembly.

"Not even to eat with such a one" (to toiouto mede synesthiein) — the prohibition of shared meals is the most severe form of social exclusion available to the community. In the ancient world, shared meals constituted and expressed social bonds. To eat with someone was to declare solidarity, partnership, and mutual acceptance. In the community's life, the common meal — which Paul will address at length in 11:17-34 — was the central act of fellowship (koinonia, 1:9; 10:16-17). To exclude someone from the table was to declare that the bond of fellowship was severed. The person retains the name "brother" in the text's description (onomazomenos adelphos), but the community's action declares that the name does not match the reality. The exclusion from the meal is the practical expression of the expulsion commanded in verse 2.

"For what have I to do with judging outsiders? Is it not those inside whom you judge? God judges those outside. 'Purge the evil person from among you'" (5:12-13).

Paul draws a sharp boundary between two spheres of responsibility: inside (esothen/eso) and outside (exothen/exo). The question ti gar moi tous exo krinein ("for what business is it of mine to judge those outside?") is rhetorical. Paul disclaims jurisdiction over those outside the community. His responsibility, and the community's responsibility, extends only to tous eso ("those inside"). The question ouchi tous eso hymeis krinete ("is it not those inside whom you judge?") expects the answer "yes." The community already exercises judgment over its own members — the question implies that internal judgment is a recognized function of the assembly. The problem is not that they lack the authority to judge but that they have failed to exercise it in this case.

"God judges those outside" (tous de exo ho theos krinei) — the outsiders are not beyond all judgment; they are simply beyond the community's judgment. God himself judges them. The community's jurisdiction is limited; God's is not. This division of judicial responsibility liberates the community from the impossible task of reforming pagan society while making them fully responsible for their own internal life.

The chapter closes with a direct quotation from Deuteronomy: "Purge the evil person from among you" (exarate ton poneron ex hymon auton). The LXX formula appears repeatedly throughout Deuteronomy with minor variations:

  • Deuteronomy 17:7 (LXX): kai exareis ton poneron ex hymon auton — "and you shall purge the evil from among yourselves." Context: the execution of anyone who serves other gods, after investigation and testimony of two or three witnesses.
  • Deuteronomy 17:12: "and you shall purge the evil from Israel." Context: the person who presumes to disobey the priest or judge.
  • Deuteronomy 19:19: "and you shall purge the evil from your midst." Context: the false witness.
  • Deuteronomy 21:21: "and you shall purge the evil from your midst." Context: the rebellious son.
  • Deuteronomy 22:21: "and you shall purge the evil from your midst." Context: the woman found not to be a virgin.
  • Deuteronomy 22:24: "and you shall purge the evil from your midst." Context: the case of adultery.
  • Deuteronomy 24:7: "and you shall purge the evil from your midst." Context: the kidnapper.

The formula is a refrain of communal purification in Deuteronomy's legal code. Every time Israel identifies a fundamental violation within the community, the response is the same: purge the evil. The formula's repetition in Deuteronomy establishes a principle: the community's holiness requires the removal of that which corrupts it. Israel could not maintain its identity as God's covenant people while harboring within itself conduct that violated the covenant. Paul applies the identical logic to the Corinthian assembly. The community is God's temple (3:16-17), God's field and building (3:9), the sanctified people of God (1:2). Its holiness requires the same vigilance that Israel's holiness required. The Deuteronomic formula is not arbitrarily chosen; it identifies the Corinthian assembly with Israel and applies Israel's own communal discipline to the new situation.

The final word of the chapter is therefore a scriptural command, not Paul's personal opinion. By closing with the Deuteronomic formula, Paul places his instruction within the continuous tradition of God's people maintaining their communal purity. The community is not innovating; it is doing what God's people have always been required to do.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 5 is built on a single controlling logic: the community's identity determines its responsibilities, and its failure to act on those responsibilities constitutes a betrayal of that identity.

The identity has been established in chapters 1-4: the community is "the assembly of God" (1:2), sanctified (1:2), called (1:2, 9, 24, 26), enriched with gifts (1:5-7), sustained by God's faithfulness (1:8-9), and constituted as God's temple where the Spirit dwells (3:16). This identity carries obligations. A community that is God's holy temple cannot harbor conduct that defiles it. A community that has been sanctified must maintain its sanctification. A community whose Passover lamb has been sacrificed must remove the leaven.

The Corinthians' failure is double. First, they have tolerated the sin itself — a man maintaining a sexual arrangement with his father's wife, a violation of explicit Levitical and Deuteronomic law, a practice that even pagans would not countenance. Second, and in Paul's presentation even more striking, they are "puffed up" about it — their arrogance has rendered them incapable of recognizing the danger.

Paul's remedy operates on two levels. At the practical level, the man must be expelled from the community's fellowship — removed from the table, handed over to the sphere outside the community's protective boundary. At the theological level, the community must understand why this expulsion is necessary. The leaven metaphor (vv. 6-8) explains: tolerated sin is not contained sin. A little leaven works through the entire lump. The Passover metaphor provides the ground: Christ, the Passover lamb, has been sacrificed, and therefore the community's entire existence is the Passover festival, which requires the thorough removal of all leaven.

The chapter also establishes a principle that governs the rest of the letter's argument about community behavior. The distinction between inside and outside (vv. 9-13) defines the community's sphere of responsibility. The assembly judges those within; God judges those without. This principle will be applied immediately in chapter 6, where Paul rebukes the community for taking internal disputes to external courts (6:1-8) — the mirror image of the problem in chapter 5. In chapter 5, the community fails to judge when it should. In chapter 6:1-8, the community seeks outside judgment when it should judge for itself. Both failures stem from the same confusion about the community's boundaries and responsibilities.

The argument's most remarkable feature is the restorative purpose that governs the severity of the discipline. The harshest action Paul commands — handing the man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh — is explicitly directed toward salvation: "so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (5:5). The severity serves the man's ultimate good. The community expels not to destroy but to reclaim. This pattern reflects the scriptural precedent of divine discipline as a means of restoration: God's judgments on Israel throughout the prophetic tradition were aimed not at termination but at return. The exile was not the end of the covenant but a severe measure within it. The "rod" that Paul mentioned in 4:21 is the same kind of instrument: painful, but wielded by a father toward his children (4:14-15), not by an executioner toward the condemned.

The closing Deuteronomic quotation — "Purge the evil person from among you" — identifies the Corinthian community with Israel and holds it to Israel's standard. The community that inherited Israel's Passover, Israel's temple language, and Israel's identity as God's holy people also inherits Israel's obligation to maintain communal holiness through communal discipline. The chapter does not introduce a new principle; it applies an old one to a new context. The people of God have always been responsible for the integrity of their own assembly. The Corinthians, for all their gifts and knowledge and spiritual endowment, have forgotten this.
