Revelation 22 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Revelation 21): Chapter 21 narrated the appearance of the new creation and the descent of the new Jerusalem. The first heaven and first earth had passed away, the sea was no more, and the holy city came down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband (21:1-2). A voice from the throne declared the theological center of the entire new order: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is among men, and he will dwell among them, and they shall be his people, and God himself will be among them" (21:3). Death, mourning, crying, and pain were abolished (21:4). God declared "I am making all things new" and authenticated the words as "faithful and true" (21:5). He identified himself as the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the end, and offered the water of life freely to the one who thirsts (21:6). The overcomer was promised inheritance and divine sonship; the cowardly, faithless, abominable, and their companions were assigned to the second death (21:7-8).

One of the seven bowl-angels — in deliberate structural mirror to 17:1, where the same category of angel showed the harlot — invited John to see the bride, the wife of the Lamb (21:9). John was carried in the Spirit to a great and high mountain (contrasting the wilderness of 17:3) and shown the holy city in elaborate detail. The city had God's glory, radiant like crystal-clear jasper (21:11). Its wall had twelve gates inscribed with the names of the twelve tribes of Israel and twelve foundation stones inscribed with the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb (21:12-14). The city was measured as a perfect cube — twelve thousand stadia per side — the shape of the Holy of Holies in Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:20), declaring that the entire city is what that innermost sanctum was: the place of God's immediate, unmediated presence (21:15-17). Its materials — jasper, transparent gold, twelve precious foundation stones, pearl gates — drew on the high priest's breastpiece (Exodus 28:17-20), Isaiah's jeweled Jerusalem (Isaiah 54:11-12), and the Eden tradition (Ezekiel 28:13). The entire city is simultaneously temple, garden, and bride (21:18-21).

Two defining absences characterized the new Jerusalem: no temple and no sun or moon. God and the Lamb are the temple; God's glory and the Lamb as its lamp provide all light (21:22-23). The nations walk by this light, the kings of the earth bring their glory into the city, the gates are never shut, nothing unclean enters, and only those written in the Lamb's book of life belong there (21:24-27).

Chapter 21 ended with the vision of the new Jerusalem described architecturally but not yet explored internally. The city has been seen from outside — its shape, dimensions, materials, gates, foundations, and illumination. What remains is the interior: the street, the river, the tree, the throne. Chapter 22 provides this.

How chapter 22 continues the argument: Chapter 22 divides into two distinct sections. The first (22:1-5) completes the new Jerusalem vision that began in 21:9, taking the reader inside the city to show the river of the water of life, the tree of life, the abolition of the curse, and the eternal reign of God's servants. The second (22:6-21) constitutes the closing frame of the entire document — the epilogue that authenticates the prophecy, issues final warnings and invitations, and brings the document to its conclusion with the cry "Come, Lord Jesus" and a grace benediction.

The connection to chapter 21 in the first section is seamless. There is no new visionary marker, no kai eidon ("and I saw"), no meta tauta ("after these things"). The angel who showed the city in 21:9 continues showing in 22:1 (kai edeixen moi, "and he showed me"). The street (plateia) mentioned in 21:21 reappears in 22:2. The throne of God and of the Lamb, present in the city (21:22-23 by implication), is now explicitly located (22:1, 3). The vision flows from the external architecture of chapter 21 to the internal landscape of 22:1-5 without interruption.

The second section (22:6-21) shifts from vision to direct speech, from the new Jerusalem to the present situation of the assemblies receiving the document. This shift corresponds to the document's triple genre identified in chapter 1: it is simultaneously apocalypse (the visions), prophecy (the call to respond), and letter (addressed to specific communities). Chapters 4-22:5 have been predominantly apocalyptic vision. The epilogue returns to the prophetic and epistolary registers, re-engaging the assemblies directly, reinforcing the document's authority, and issuing the final call to faithfulness.

Verses 1-2: The River and the Tree of Life

"Then he showed me a river of the water of life, clear as crystal, coming from the throne of God and of the Lamb, in the middle of its street. On either side of the river was the tree of life, bearing twelve kinds of fruit, yielding its fruit every month; and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations."

"Then he showed me a river of the water of life, clear as crystal" (kai edeixen moi potamon hydatos zoes lampron hos krystallon). The verb edeixen ("he showed") identifies the angel of 21:9-10 as the continuing guide. The river is described with three elements: it contains the "water of life" (hydor zoes), it is "clear as crystal" (lampron hos krystallon), and it flows from the throne.

The "water of life" (hydor zoes) has been anticipated throughout the document. In 7:17, the Lamb "will guide them to springs of the water of life" (epi zoes pegas hydaton). In 21:6, the one on the throne promised: "I will give to the one who thirsts from the spring of the water of life without cost" (ek tes peges tou hydatos tes zoes dorean). The river of 22:1 is the fulfillment of both promises: the springs to which the Lamb guides and the gift the one on the throne offers are now a river flowing through the city. What was promised to the great multitude in the interlude of chapter 7 and to the thirsty one in chapter 21 is now part of the permanent landscape of the new creation.

The adjective lampron ("bright, radiant, clear") was used for the bride's fine linen in 19:8 (byssinon lampron katharon, "fine linen, bright and clean") and will be used for Christ's self-identification in 22:16 (ho aster ho lampros ho proinos, "the bright morning star"). The river is not merely transparent but radiant — it shines with the quality of the divine presence that permeates the city. The comparison hos krystallon ("like crystal") recalls the "sea of glass like crystal" (thalassa hyaline homoia krystallo) before the throne in 4:6. In chapter 4, the crystalline sea was a barrier between the throne and those before it. In the new Jerusalem, the crystalline substance is not a barrier but a flowing river — the same quality of glassy brilliance, but now in motion, accessible, life-giving. What separated in chapter 4 now nourishes in chapter 22.

The prophetic sources converge from multiple directions.

Ezekiel 47:1-12 is the primary source. In Ezekiel's vision, the prophet was brought to the door of the temple, and "behold, water was flowing from under the threshold of the temple toward the east" (Ezekiel 47:1). The water flowed from the right side of the temple, past the altar, and out the south side of the gate. An angelic guide led Ezekiel through the water as it deepened: ankle-deep, knee-deep, waist-deep, and finally "a river that I could not ford, for the water had risen, enough water to swim in, a river that could not be forded" (47:5). On both banks of the river grew "very many trees" (47:7), and the guide explained: "These waters go out toward the eastern region... and the water becomes fresh... every living creature which swarms in every place where the river goes, will live" (47:8-9). "By the river on its bank, on one side and on the other, will grow all kinds of trees for food. Their leaves will not wither and their fruit will not fail. They will bear every month because their water flows from the sanctuary, and their fruit will be for food and their leaves for healing" (47:12). The verbal and conceptual parallels with Revelation 22:1-2 are extensive: the water flowing from the sanctuary/throne, the trees on both sides of the river, the monthly fruit, the healing leaves.

But the author of Revelation makes critical modifications to Ezekiel's vision. In Ezekiel, the water flows from the temple; in Revelation, there is no temple (21:22), so the water flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb. The author has already established that God and the Lamb have replaced the temple (21:22). The river's source follows this replacement: the function the temple served — as the origin of life-giving water — is now served directly by the throne. In Ezekiel, the river flowed outward from the city toward the Dead Sea; in Revelation, the river flows through the city, in the middle of its street. The life-giving water is not exported to the periphery but fills the center of communal life. In Ezekiel, the trees were "all kinds of trees for food"; in Revelation, the trees become "the tree of life" (to xylon tes zoes) — a specific identification that connects the vision not only to Ezekiel but to Genesis.

Zechariah 14:8 provides a parallel source: "In that day living waters (hydata zonta) will flow out of Jerusalem, half of them toward the eastern sea and the other half toward the western sea; it will be in summer as well as in winter." The "living waters" of Zechariah are perpetual — flowing in all seasons. The river in Revelation 22 is likewise perpetual: the tree yields fruit "every month" (kata mena hekaston), indicating unbroken, continuous supply.

Joel 3:18 (LXX 4:18): "A spring will go out from the house of the LORD and will water the valley of Shittim." Psalm 46:4 (LXX 45:5): "There is a river whose streams make glad the city of God, the holy dwelling places of the Most High." The psalm's "river whose streams make glad the city of God" is realized in Revelation 22:1 — the river flows through the city, proceeding from the divine presence.

Genesis 2:10: "Now a river flowed out of Eden to water the garden; and from there it divided and became four rivers." The river in Eden that watered the garden and then divided into the Tigris, Euphrates, Pishon, and Gihon is the prototype. In the new creation, the river again flows from the place of God's presence, but it does not divide — it remains one river, flowing through one city, from one throne. The unity reflects the completeness and wholeness of the new creation: there is no scattering, no division, no branching into remote regions.

"Coming from the throne of God and of the Lamb" (ekporeuomenon ek tou thronou tou theou kai tou arniou). The river's source is the throne, and the throne belongs jointly to God and to the Lamb. The phrase tou theou kai tou arniou ("of God and of the Lamb") presents God and the Lamb as sharing a single throne — not two thrones but one. This shared throne has been anticipated: in 3:21, the overcomer was promised a seat on Christ's throne "as I also overcame and sat down with my Father on his throne"; in 5:6, the Lamb was "in the center of the throne"; in 7:17, "the Lamb in the center of the throne will be their shepherd." The joint occupancy of the throne — God and the Lamb sharing a single seat of sovereignty — is one of the document's most distinctive declarations. The water of life flows from this shared authority: the river proceeds from the unified rule of God and the slain-yet-standing Lamb.

"In the middle of its street" (en meso tes plateias autes). The plateia ("broad street, main thoroughfare") was introduced in 21:21, where it was described as "pure gold, like transparent glass." The river flows down the center of this golden, transparent street. The public, communal space of the city is the location of life-giving water. Access is not restricted, not hidden in an inner chamber, not controlled by priests. The river is in the middle of the street — the most accessible, visible, central location possible.

"On either side of the river was the tree of life" (enteuthen kai ekeithen tou potamou xylon zoes). The phrase enteuthen kai ekeithen ("on this side and on that side," i.e., "on either side") is drawn directly from Ezekiel 47:12 (LXX): "on the river, on its bank, on this side and on that side" (epi tou potamou... enteuthen kai enteuthen). In Ezekiel, the trees on both banks were a plurality — "all kinds of trees." In Revelation, the plural becomes singular with collective force: "the tree of life" (xylon zoes). The singular xylon ("tree, wood") with the genitive zoes ("of life") is the same phrase from Genesis 2:9 (LXX): to xylon tes zoes ("the tree of life"). The shift from Ezekiel's many trees to Genesis's tree of life is the author's most significant interpretive move in these verses. The many trees become the one tree — the specific tree from which humanity was barred after the expulsion from Eden.

The tree of life was promised to the overcomer at Ephesus: "To the one who conquers I will grant to eat of the tree of life, which is in the paradise of God" (2:7). The promise made to the first assembly in the first message is now fulfilled in the last chapter. The tree is accessible, bearing fruit, on both sides of the river — not guarded by cherubim with a flaming sword (Genesis 3:24) but standing openly in the city of God.

"Bearing twelve kinds of fruit, yielding its fruit every month" (poioun karpous dodeka, kata mena hekaston apodidoun ton karpon autou). The twelve kinds of fruit (karpous dodeka) continue the document's structuring number. Twelve gates, twelve angels, twelve tribes, twelve foundations, twelve apostles, twelve thousand stadia, twelve pearls, and now twelve kinds of fruit. The number twelve encodes covenant completeness — the fullness of God's people and God's provision. The tree bears fruit kata mena hekaston ("each month, every month"), indicating perpetual, uninterrupted supply. There is no season of barrenness, no winter, no dormancy. The fruit is always available. Ezekiel 47:12 provides the source: "They will bear every month (kata mena kainon, 'month by month new') because their water flows from the sanctuary."

"And the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" (kai ta phylla tou xylou eis therapeian ton ethnon). The word therapeia ("healing, service, care") denotes the restoration of health, the mending of what is damaged. The source is again Ezekiel 47:12 (LXX): "their leaves for healing" (ta phylla autou eis hygiеian, "their leaves for health/healing"). In Ezekiel, the healing leaves suggested the curative properties of the trees watered by the temple stream. In Revelation, the healing is specifically for "the nations" (ton ethnon).

The mention of nations requiring healing in the new creation raises a question: if death, mourning, crying, and pain have been abolished (21:4), what healing remains? The word therapeia does not necessarily denote the curing of disease in a narrow clinical sense. In the LXX, the semantic range includes restoration, service, and care. The leaves serve the nations' therapeia — their ongoing restoration, their continuous renewal. The prophets anticipated the healing of the nations as part of the eschatological vision: Malachi 4:2 (LXX 3:20): "The sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings" (iasis en tais pterxyin autou). Isaiah 19:22: "The LORD will strike Egypt, striking and healing" (pataxei kyrios tous Aigyptious plege megale, kai iasetai autous iasei). The nations that walked in darkness (Isaiah 60:3), that were deceived by Babylon's sorcery (18:23), that were made drunk with the harlot's wine (17:2), now receive therapeia — the ongoing healing and restoration that comes from the tree watered by the river from the throne.

The tree of life with its healing leaves for the nations also serves as the final answer to the curse of Genesis 3. The tree that was forbidden is now accessible. The nations that were scattered at Babel (Genesis 11) and deceived by the dragon (12:9) are now healed. The river that watered Eden now flows from the throne. The entire Genesis narrative of creation, fall, curse, and exile is reversed.

Verses 3-5: No More Curse — The Servants' Eternal Reign

"There will no longer be any curse. The throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it, and his bond-servants will serve him; they will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads. And there will no longer be any night; and they will not have need of the light of a lamp nor the light of the sun, because the Lord God will illumine them; and they will reign forever and ever."

"There will no longer be any curse" (kai pan katathema ouk estai eti). The word katathema ("curse, accursed thing") is related to anathema, the LXX term for what is devoted to destruction — the ban (Hebrew cherem) that required the total destruction of cursed objects or persons. The declaration that "there will no longer be any curse" reverses the foundational curse of Genesis 3:14-19. In Genesis 3:14, God cursed the serpent. In 3:17, God cursed the ground "because of you" (dia se). In 3:19, the consequence is death: "you are dust, and to dust you shall return." The curse brought enmity, pain in childbirth, thorns and thistles, toilsome labor, and death. The entire created order was subjected to the effects of the curse.

Zechariah 14:11 (LXX) provides the direct prophetic source: "There will no longer be any curse (anathema)" (kai ouk estai anathema eti), using the same construction. Zechariah's oracle envisions the eschatological Jerusalem as a place where the curse — the ban of destruction — is permanently lifted. The author of Revelation follows Zechariah's language precisely.

The abolition of the curse in 22:3 completes a chain of reversals that runs through 21:1-22:5. The sea is no more (21:1) — the source of chaos eliminated. Death, mourning, crying, and pain are no more (21:4) — the consequences of the curse eliminated. There is no temple (21:22) — the mediating structure eliminated. There is no sun or moon needed (21:23) — the mediating luminaries eliminated. There is no night (22:5) — the domain of darkness eliminated. And now there is no curse (22:3) — the root cause of all the others eliminated. The series of negations (ouk... eti, "no longer") traces the dismantling of the fallen order back to its origin: the curse itself is removed, and with it, every consequence that flowed from it.

"The throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it" (kai ho thronos tou theou kai tou arniou en aute estai). The throne — the seat of divine sovereignty — is within the city. The shared throne of God and the Lamb (tou theou kai tou arniou) was established in 22:1 as the source of the river. Now its location is confirmed: it is in the city, among the inhabitants. The throne that appeared in heaven in 4:2, surrounded by living creatures and elders, is now in the new Jerusalem on earth. The descent of the city from heaven (21:2, 10) has brought the throne from heaven to earth. What was above is now among. The vision of chapters 4-5 — God on the throne, the Lamb in the center of the throne — is now the permanent, accessible reality of the new creation.

"And his bond-servants will serve him" (kai hoi douloi autou latreusousin auto). The verb latreuō ("to serve, to worship, to render sacred service") in the LXX is the technical term for priestly, liturgical service. Exodus 3:12: "You shall serve (latreusete) God on this mountain." Deuteronomy 6:13: "You shall fear the LORD your God and serve (latreuseis) him." Deuteronomy 10:12: "What does the LORD your God require of you, but to fear the LORD your God, to walk in all his ways, to love him, and to serve (latreuein) the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul." The word combines worship and service; it is the activity of the priest before God and the faithful Israelite in daily devotion. The bond-servants (douloi) are those who have been identified throughout the document as God's faithful people: 1:1 ("to show to his bond-servants"), 7:3 ("the bond-servants of our God"), 19:2 ("the blood of his bond-servants"), 19:5 ("his bond-servants, you who fear him"). Their latreuo — their sacred service-worship — is now performed in the direct presence of God and the Lamb, without temple, without mediating priest, without barrier.

"They will see his face" (kai opsontai to prosopon autou). This is one of the most extraordinary declarations in the entire document. Throughout Israel's scriptures, seeing God's face was understood as impossible for mortal humanity. Exodus 33:20: "You cannot see my face (to prosopon mou), for no man can see me and live (ou dynatai anthropos idein to prosopon mou kai zesai)." When Moses asked to see God's glory, God placed him in the cleft of a rock and covered him with his hand, allowing Moses to see only God's back: "You shall see my back (ta opiso mou), but my face (to prosopon mou) shall not be seen" (Exodus 33:23). Even Moses — the greatest prophet, who spoke with God "face to face" (Exodus 33:11; Deuteronomy 34:10) — was denied the full vision of God's face. The phrase "face to face" (prosopon pros prosopon) in Exodus 33:11 described the intimacy of the encounter, not the literal sight of God's face, as the same chapter makes explicit in 33:20.

The promise that God's servants "will see his face" reverses this prohibition entirely. What was lethal is now life-giving. What was hidden is now revealed. What was granted partially to one prophet is now given fully to all the bond-servants. The change is possible because the conditions have changed: in the old order, sinful humanity could not survive direct contact with the holy God. In the new creation, the curse is abolished (22:3), nothing unclean enters (21:27), and the bond-servants have been purified through the Lamb's blood (7:14) and clothed in righteous deeds (19:8). The barrier between God's holiness and human frailty has been removed.

The priestly blessing of Numbers 6:25-26 (LXX) anticipated this: "The LORD make his face (to prosopon autou) shine upon you (epiphanai... epi se) and be gracious to you; the LORD lift up his countenance (to prosopon autou) upon you and give you peace." The priestly blessing pronounced God's face-turned-toward-his-people as the source of grace and peace. In the new Jerusalem, the priestly blessing is no longer a spoken prayer but a lived reality: the servants see the face that was invoked but never seen.

Psalm 17:15 (LXX 16:15): "As for me, I shall behold your face (opsomai to prosopon sou) in righteousness; I will be satisfied when I awake with your likeness." Psalm 11:7 (LXX 10:7): "The upright will behold his face (opsontai to prosopon autou)." These psalmic hopes — the longing to see God's face, expressed as the highest possible aspiration of the righteous — are now declared fulfilled. The psalmist hoped; the bond-servants see.

"And his name will be on their foreheads" (kai to onoma autou epi ton metopon auton). The name on the forehead has been a defining image throughout the document. In 7:3, the bond-servants of God were sealed "on their foreheads" (epi ton metopon) before the trumpet judgments. In 9:4, the locust-plague was restricted from harming those "who have the seal of God on their foreheads." In 14:1, the 144,000 on Mount Zion had "his name and the name of his Father written on their foreheads" (to onoma autou kai to onoma tou patros autou gegrammenon epi ton metopon auton). The mark of the beast was placed on the right hand or the forehead (13:16); the seal of God was placed on the forehead alone. The contrast has been sustained throughout: two marks, two allegiances, two identities.

In 22:4, the name on the foreheads is definitive and permanent. The seal that protected in chapter 7, the name that identified in chapter 14, is now inscribed as the permanent mark of belonging in the new creation. The forehead is the most visible part of the person — the name cannot be hidden. The bond-servants belong to God openly, completely, and permanently.

The background in Israel's priestly tradition is Exodus 28:36-38. The high priest wore a golden plate on his forehead inscribed "Holy to the LORD" (hagion to kyrio, LXX). The plate was the outward sign that the priest was consecrated, set apart for divine service. In the new Jerusalem, every bond-servant bears what only the high priest once bore. The entire community is priestly — which is precisely what the document has declared from the beginning: the Lamb "has made us to be a kingdom, priests to his God and Father" (1:6); "you have made them to be a kingdom and priests to our God; and they will reign upon the earth" (5:10). The name on the foreheads is the visible enactment of this universal priesthood.

"And there will no longer be any night" (kai nyx ouk estai eti). The absence of night was stated in 21:25 as the reason the gates need never be shut. Here in 22:5 it is restated as a feature of life in the city. The elimination of night removes the domain of darkness, the time of danger, the period of diminished sight. In the document's symbolic geography, darkness has been associated with the beast's kingdom: the fifth bowl was poured out on the beast's throne, and "his kingdom became darkened" (eskotomene, 16:10). The absence of night in the new Jerusalem corresponds to the absence of everything the darkness represented — the domain of evil, the cover for deception, the time of fear.

"And they will not have need of the light of a lamp nor the light of the sun" (kai ouk echousin chreian photos lychnou kai photos heliou). This restates 21:23 with a slight expansion. The two sources of light in the old order — artificial (lychnou, "lamp") and natural (heliou, "sun") — are both superseded. The contrast with Babylon is pointed: in 18:23, one of the marks of Babylon's destruction was that "the light of a lamp (phos lychnou) will not shine in you any longer." In Babylon, the lamp went out as a sign of judgment. In the new Jerusalem, the lamp is not needed because a greater light has come. What Babylon lost through judgment, the new Jerusalem transcends through glory.

"Because the Lord God will illumine them" (hoti kyrios ho theos photisei ep' autous). The source is Isaiah 60:19 (LXX): "The LORD will be to you an everlasting light (phos aionion), and God will be your glory (doxan)." Also Psalm 36:9 (LXX 35:10): "In your light we see light" (en to photi sou opsometha phos). God himself is the source of illumination. The verb photizō ("to illumine, to give light to, to enlighten") describes a direct, unmediated act: God shines upon (ep' autous, "upon them") his servants. The created intermediaries — sun, moon, lamp — are replaced by the Creator himself as the direct source of light, just as the temple was replaced by God and the Lamb as the direct location of his presence.

"And they will reign forever and ever" (kai basileusousin eis tous aionas ton aionon). The verb basileuo ("to reign, to exercise royal authority") reaches its final expression here. The promise of reign has run through the entire document:

1:6 — "he has made us to be a kingdom, priests to his God and Father" (epoiesen hemas basileian, hiereis to theo kai patri autou) 5:10 — "you have made them to be a kingdom and priests to our God; and they will reign upon the earth" (basileusousin epi tes ges) 20:4 — the faithful "came to life and reigned with Christ for a thousand years" (ebasileusan meta tou Christou chilia ete) 20:6 — "they will be priests of God and of Christ and will reign with him for a thousand years" (basileusousin met' autou ta chilia ete)

The progression is significant. In 1:6 and 5:10, the faithful are declared to be a kingdom and priests who will reign. In 20:4, 6, they reign with Christ for a bounded period — a thousand years. In 22:5, the reign becomes eis tous aionas ton aionon — "unto the ages of the ages," the strongest expression of unending duration available in Greek. This is the same phrase used for God's own eternal existence (4:9-10; 10:6; 15:7) and for the torment of God's enemies (14:11; 19:3; 20:10). The reign of the bond-servants is elevated from the temporal to the eternal, from the millennium to the perpetual, from "with Christ for a thousand years" to "forever and ever." The document's promise to the assemblies reaches its ultimate fulfillment: those who conquer will not merely rule for a bounded period but will reign eternally.

Verses 6-7: Authentication and the Sixth Beatitude

"And he said to me, 'These words are faithful and true'; and the Lord, the God of the spirits of the prophets, sent his angel to show to his bond-servants the things which must soon take place. 'And behold, I am coming quickly. Blessed is he who heeds the words of the prophecy of this book.'"

"These words are faithful and true" (houtoi hoi logoi pistoi kai alethinoi). The authentication formula repeats 21:5, where God himself spoke from the throne: "Write, for these words are faithful and true." The same formula appeared in 19:9: "These are true words of God." The repetition at this juncture marks the transition from the visionary content to the epilogue. The angel authenticates the entire vision — not merely the immediately preceding verses but the document's full content. The adjectives pistoi ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") and alethinoi ("true, genuine, real") have been applied to Christ himself (3:14, "the faithful and true witness"; 19:11, "Faithful and True") and to God's judgments (16:7; 19:2). The words partake of the character of the one who speaks them.

"The Lord, the God of the spirits of the prophets, sent his angel to show to his bond-servants the things which must soon take place" (ho kyrios, ho theos ton pneumaton ton propheton, apesteilen ton angelon autou deixai tois doulois autou ha dei genesthai en tachei). This sentence deliberately echoes the document's opening verse: "The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his bond-servants the things which must soon take place" (1:1). The verbal parallels are precise: deixai tois doulois autou ("to show to his bond-servants") and ha dei genesthai en tachei ("the things which must soon take place") appear in both verses. The epilogue returns to the prologue, forming an inclusio — a literary frame that brackets the entire document. What was announced in 1:1 is now confirmed in 22:6: the same chain of revelation (God — angel — servants) remains operative, and the same urgency (en tachei, "soon, quickly") governs the message.

The title "the God of the spirits of the prophets" (ho theos ton pneumaton ton propheton) identifies God as the one who inspires and governs prophetic speech. The "spirits of the prophets" (ta pneumata ton propheton) refers to the prophetic impulse or gift. In 1 Corinthians 14:32, Paul uses the same phrase: "the spirits of prophets are subject to prophets" (pneumata propheton prophetais hypotassetai). The spirits that animate prophetic speech belong to God — he is their source and their sovereign. The author of Revelation thus claims for his document the same authority as the prophetic tradition of Israel: the God who spoke through Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel has spoken through this vision.

"And behold, I am coming quickly" (kai idou erchomai tachy). The speaker shifts abruptly from the angel to Jesus. The words erchomai tachy ("I am coming quickly") are spoken three times in this chapter (22:7, 12, 20), forming a triple declaration that dominates the epilogue. The same verb erchomenos ("the coming one") was used for God's own self-identification in 1:4, 8 ("who is and who was and who is to come"). The word tachy ("quickly, soon, without delay") reinforces the temporal urgency established in 1:1 (en tachei) and 1:3 ("the time is near"). The first-century assemblies are being told that the interval between their present suffering and the consummation they have been shown is short. The imminent return is not a separate topic from the vision; it is the application of the vision to the hearers' present situation.

"Blessed is he who heeds the words of the prophecy of this book" (makarios ho teron tous logous tes propheteias tou bibliou toutou). This is the sixth of seven beatitudes in the document, and it directly echoes the first: "Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear and keep what is written in it" (1:3). The verb tereo ("to keep, to guard, to heed, to observe") is the same in both verses. The inclusio extends from the blessing to the vocabulary: the document's first and sixth beatitudes use the same words for the same action — keeping the prophecy. The frame declares that the appropriate response to the document is not merely hearing or understanding but keeping — active, sustained obedience to what has been communicated.

The word propheteia ("prophecy") classifies the entire document as prophetic literature, not merely predictive writing. As noted in the analysis of 1:3, prophecy in the biblical tradition demands response and obedience; the document is not information to be filed but instruction to be acted upon.

Verses 8-9: The Second Worship-Rebuke

"I, John, am the one who heard and saw these things. And when I heard and saw, I fell down to worship at the feet of the angel who showed me these things. But he said to me, 'Do not do that. I am a fellow servant of yours and of your brethren the prophets and of those who heed the words of this book. Worship God.'"

"I, John, am the one who heard and saw these things" (Kago Ioannes ho akouon kai blepon tauta). John reasserts his identity as the witness, using the first person singular with his proper name, just as he did in 1:9 ("I, John, your brother and fellow partaker in the tribulation and kingdom and perseverance which are in Jesus"). The repetition of the self-identification near the end of the document confirms the named author and closes the literary frame: the same John who introduced himself in chapter 1 now speaks again in chapter 22. The verbs akouon ("hearing") and blepon ("seeing") in the present participle describe his ongoing experience: he is the one who hears and sees. The sensory language reinforces the nature of the document as eyewitness testimony — John reports what he has directly perceived through divinely enabled senses.

"I fell down to worship at the feet of the angel" (epesa proskynесai emprosthen ton podon tou angelou tou deiknuontos moi tauta). This is the second occurrence of John prostrating before an angel. The first was in 19:10, where John "fell at his feet to worship him" (epesa emprosthen ton podon autou proskynесai auto) and was rebuked. The verbal structure is nearly identical. The repetition is not an editorial mistake or an accidental doublet. It is a deliberate narrative device that reinforces the document's central concern: worship.

The fact that John falls a second time after having been corrected once communicates the persistence and subtlety of the temptation to misdirect worship. If even the seer — the one who has received the entire revelation, who has seen the beast and the harlot judged, who has heard the Hallelujah chorus — can again fall into the error of worshiping a creature, the danger is real and ongoing for every hearer. The assemblies in Asia, surrounded by imperial cult worship, by the beast's demand for proskynesis (13:4, 8, 12, 15), by the pervasive religious culture of Greco-Roman polytheism, needed to hear this correction twice.

"I am a fellow servant of yours and of your brethren the prophets and of those who heed the words of this book" (syndoulos sou eimi kai ton adelphon sou ton propheton kai ton terounton tous logous tou bibliou toutou). The angel's self-identification as a syndoulos ("fellow servant") repeats 19:10 but expands the fellowship. In 19:10, the angel said he was a fellow servant of John and "your brethren who hold the testimony of Jesus" (ton adelphon sou ton echonton ten martyrian Iesou). Here in 22:9, the fellowship includes "your brethren the prophets" (ton adelphon sou ton propheton) and "those who heed the words of this book" (ton terounton tous logous tou bibliou toutou). The expansion is significant. The first rebuke identified the community by their content (the testimony of Jesus). The second rebuke identifies the community by their function (the prophets) and their practice (those who keep the words). The angel, the seer, the prophets, and the obedient hearers all share the same status: syndouloi — fellow servants of the same master. None is worthy of worship. Only God is.

"Worship God" (to theo proskyneson). The imperative is identical to 19:10. The repetition drives home the point: the proper response to the glory of the vision is not to worship the messenger but to worship God. The command encapsulates the document's entire theological program. From 14:7 ("worship him who made the heaven and the earth") through the contrast between beast-worship and God-worship that structures the entire middle section of the document (chapters 13-14), to the Hallelujah chorus that celebrates God's sole worthiness to reign (19:1-6), the document has been arguing for the exclusive worship of God. The final worship-rebuke seals this argument.

Verses 10-11: Do Not Seal the Book

"And he said to me, 'Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near. Let the one who does wrong, still do wrong; and the one who is filthy, still be filthy; and let the one who is righteous, still practice righteousness; and the one who is holy, still keep himself holy.'"

"Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book" (Me sphragises tous logous tes propheteias tou bibliou toutou). The instruction is a deliberate contrast with Daniel 12:4, 9. In Daniel 12:4 (LXX), the angel told Daniel: "Shut up the words and seal the book (sphragison to biblion), until the time of the end." In Daniel 12:9: "Go, Daniel, for the words are concealed and sealed (esphragismena) until the time of the end." Daniel's vision was for a distant future; it was to be sealed because the time of fulfillment was not yet. The instruction to John is the opposite: do not seal (me sphragises), because "the time is near" (ho kairos engys estin). The reversal signals that what Daniel anticipated as remote has now arrived. The time of the end that required sealing has given way to the time of fulfillment that demands openness. The prophecy must be heard, not stored away.

The word kairos ("season, appointed time, decisive moment"), as in 1:3, designates not mere chronological passage (chronos) but an epoch of significance. The kairos is engys ("near, at hand") — the decisive moment is pressing upon the present. The assemblies are living in the time to which the prophecy speaks; the words must therefore be accessible and active, not sealed and dormant.

"Let the one who does wrong, still do wrong; and the one who is filthy, still be filthy; and let the one who is righteous, still practice righteousness; and the one who is holy, still keep himself holy" (ho adikon adikesato eti, kai ho rhyparos rhypantheto eti, kai ho dikaios dikaiosynen poiesato eti, kai ho hagios hagiastheto eti). These four statements form a chiastic structure: two negative categories (the one who does wrong, the one who is filthy) and two positive categories (the righteous, the holy). The aorist imperatives (adikesato, "let him do wrong"; rhypantheto, "let him be filthy"; poiesato, "let him practice"; hagiastheto, "let him be holy") are startling in the first two cases. They do not express divine approval of wrongdoing but declare the finality of the present moment's decisions. Because the time is near, because the kairos is engys, the postures people have adopted will define their destiny. There is no neutral ground. The wrongdoer and the filthy person will remain in their condition; the righteous and the holy person will remain in theirs.

The prophetic background is Daniel 12:10 (LXX): "Many will be purged, purified, and refined, but the wicked will act wickedly; and none of the wicked will understand, but those who have insight will understand." Daniel described a time when the trajectories of the wicked and the wise would diverge permanently. The author of Revelation applies the same principle to the present moment of the assemblies: the time for decision is now, and the consequences of that decision are being fixed.

The statements also echo Isaiah 6:9-10, where the prophet's commission included the paradoxical instruction to make the people's hearts dull, their ears heavy, and their eyes dim, "lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their hearts, and return and be healed." The hardening dynamic — where continued rejection results in confirmed rejection — is part of the prophetic tradition's understanding of how divine revelation functions. For those who receive it, it illuminates and transforms; for those who reject it, it confirms the rejection. The four statements of 22:11 express this principle as a declaration over the present moment: the revelation has been given, and the response defines the responder.

Verses 12-13: Coming Soon with Reward — The Alpha and Omega

"'Behold, I am coming quickly, and my reward is with me, to render to every man according to what he has done. I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end.'"

"Behold, I am coming quickly, and my reward is with me" (idou erchomai tachy, kai ho misthos mou met' emou). The second declaration of "I am coming quickly" (repeated from 22:7). The speaker is the risen Christ. The new element is the phrase "my reward is with me" (ho misthos mou met' emou). The word misthos ("reward, wages, recompense") appears in Isaiah 40:10 (LXX): "Behold, the Lord comes with might (meta ischyos), and his arm with authority; behold, his reward is with him (idou ho misthos autou met' autou), and his work (to ergon) before him." Also Isaiah 62:11 (LXX): "Say to the daughter of Zion, 'Behold, your Savior comes; behold, his reward is with him (idou ho misthos autou met' autou), and his work (to ergon autou) before him.'" Both Isaiah passages describe God coming with his reward already in hand — the recompense is not something he will discover upon arrival but something he brings. The author of Revelation applies these divine-coming passages to the risen Christ, consistent with the document's sustained identification of Christ's coming with YHWH's eschatological arrival.

"To render to every man according to what he has done" (apodounai hekasto hos to ergon estin autou). The verb apodounai ("to render, to give back, to repay") governs the distribution of the reward. The criterion is "his work" (to ergon autou) — what each person has done. This principle of judgment according to deeds pervades the scriptural tradition. Psalm 62:12 (LXX 61:13): "You repay (apodoseis) each person according to his works (kata ta erga autou)." Proverbs 24:12: "Will he not render to man according to his work?" Jeremiah 17:10: "I, the LORD, search the heart, I test the mind, to give to each one according to his ways (kata tas hodous autou), according to the fruit of his deeds." In this document, the great white throne judgment operated on the same principle: "the dead were judged according to their deeds" (kata ta erga auton, 20:12-13). The criterion of deeds has been consistent throughout: the messages to the seven assemblies each opened with "I know your works" (oida ta erga sou, 2:2, 19; 3:1, 8, 15); the dead at the final judgment were judged by their deeds; and now the returning Christ brings recompense according to the same standard.

The reward is not uniform. Every person (hekasto, "each one") receives according to his own work (to ergon autou). The overcomer inherits the new creation (21:7); the cowardly and faithless receive the second death (21:8). The two outcomes presented throughout the document are the two sides of the reward Christ carries with him.

"I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" (ego to Alpha kai to Omega, ho protos kai ho eschatos, he arche kai to telos). Three pairs of titles, each expressing the same reality from a different angle: comprehensive sovereignty from beginning to end.

"The Alpha and the Omega" (to Alpha kai to Omega) was first spoken by God in 1:8: "I am the Alpha and the Omega, says the Lord God, who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty." In 21:6, the one seated on the throne repeated it and added "the beginning and the end." Here in 22:13, the risen Christ claims all three pairs.

"The first and the last" (ho protos kai ho eschatos) was the risen Christ's self-identification in 1:17 ("I am the first and the last") and 2:8 (the message to Smyrna: "the first and the last, who was dead and has come to life"). This title originates in Isaiah's divine self-declarations: Isaiah 44:6 (LXX): "I am first (ego protos), and I am after these things (ego meta tauta); besides me there is no God." Isaiah 48:12 (LXX): "I am first (ego eimi protos), and I am forever (ego eimi eis ton aiona)."

"The beginning and the end" (he arche kai to telos) was spoken by God in 21:6.

The combination of all three titles in 22:13, spoken by the risen Christ, gathers the divine self-designations that have been distributed between God and the Lamb throughout the document into a single declaration. The titles that belong to God (1:8; 21:6) are claimed by Christ (22:13). This is consistent with the document's sustained pattern of identifying Christ with the attributes and prerogatives of Israel's God: they share the throne (22:1, 3), share the temple function (21:22), share the illuminating glory (21:23), and now share the self-designations. The document does not argue this identification abstractly but demonstrates it narratively and liturgically: the Lamb receives worship alongside God (5:13), and the Lamb bears the titles of God.

Verses 14-15: The Seventh Beatitude and the Final Exclusion

"Blessed are those who wash their robes, so that they may have the right to the tree of life, and may enter by the gates into the city. Outside are the dogs and the sorcerers and the immoral persons and the murderers and the idolaters, and everyone who loves and practices lying."

"Blessed are those who wash their robes" (makarioi hoi plynontes tas stolas auton). This is the seventh and final beatitude of the document, completing the series that began in 1:3. The seven beatitudes are:

1:3 — "Blessed is the one who reads and those who hear... and keep" 14:13 — "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on" 16:15 — "Blessed is the one who stays awake and keeps his clothes" 19:9 — "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" 20:6 — "Blessed and holy is the one who has a part in the first resurrection" 22:7 — "Blessed is he who heeds the words of the prophecy of this book" 22:14 — "Blessed are those who wash their robes"

The seven beatitudes form a complete series — the document's own commentary on what constitutes blessedness. The seventh beatitude serves as the climactic statement: the one who washes his robes.

The washing of robes connects to 7:14, where the great multitude's identity was explained: "These are the ones who come out of the great tribulation, and they have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb" (eplynan tas stolas auton kai eleukanan autas en to haimati tou arniou). The verb plynō ("to wash") is the same in both verses. The robes that were washed in 7:14 by those who endured the tribulation are the same robes that are washed in 22:14 by those who receive the blessing. The present participle plynontes ("those who are washing") indicates ongoing action — not a single past event but a continuous practice. The washing is not something done once and forgotten but something that characterizes the person's ongoing life.

The connection to 3:4-5 is also significant. In Sardis, "you have a few names... who have not soiled their garments" (ouk emolynon ta himatia auton), and the overcomer would be "clothed in white garments" (peribaleitai en himatiois leukois). The soiling of garments represents moral and spiritual compromise; the washing of garments represents purification and faithfulness. The seventh beatitude declares blessed those who maintain their garments in the washed condition — who persist in the purification that the Lamb's blood provides.

"So that they may have the right to the tree of life" (hina estai he exousia auton epi to xylon tes zoes). The word exousia ("right, authority, permission, power") indicates that access to the tree of life is not automatic but is a right that belongs to those who have washed their robes. The tree of life promised to the overcomer at Ephesus (2:7) is now available to those who meet the condition of robe-washing. The right (exousia) to the tree recalls 2:26, where the overcomer was given "authority (exousian) over the nations." The overcomer's exousia in 2:26 was authority to rule; the exousia in 22:14 is authority to eat from the tree — to have perpetual life in the new creation.

"And may enter by the gates into the city" (kai tois pylosin eiselthosin eis ten polin). Entry into the new Jerusalem is through the gates — the twelve gates inscribed with the names of the twelve tribes of Israel (21:12). Entry is conditional: it belongs to those who wash their robes. The city's gates are never shut (21:25), but not everyone may enter; nothing unclean, no one who practices abomination and lying, shall come in (21:27). The blessing defines the qualified entrants: the robe-washers, those purified by the Lamb.

"Outside are the dogs and the sorcerers and the immoral persons and the murderers and the idolaters, and everyone who loves and practices lying" (exo hoi kynes kai hoi pharmakoi kai hoi pornoi kai hoi phoneis kai hoi eidololatrai kai pas philon kai poion pseudos). The word exo ("outside") designates the realm beyond the city's walls. The list of those outside overlaps substantially with the list in 21:8 (the cowardly, unbelieving, abominable, murderers, immoral, sorcerers, idolaters, liars). The addition of "dogs" (kynes) is new. In Israel's scriptures, "dogs" is used as a term of contempt for those who are morally or ritually impure. Deuteronomy 23:18 (LXX): "You shall not bring the hire of a harlot or the wages of a dog (kynos) into the house of the LORD your God." The "dog" (keleb in Hebrew, kyon in Greek) in this verse refers to a male cult prostitute. Psalm 22:16 (LXX 21:17): "For dogs (kynes) have surrounded me." Philippians 3:2: "Beware of the dogs (tous kynas)." In the first-century Jewish context, "dogs" designated those who are outside the covenant, those whose behavior places them beyond the pale of the community. In the Didache 9:5, "Do not give what is holy to dogs," echoing Matthew 7:6.

The remaining categories are the same as those identified throughout the document. Sorcerers (pharmakoi) — practitioners of the deceptive enchantment that characterized Babylon (18:23: "all the nations were deceived by your sorcery [pharmakeia]"). Immoral persons (pornoi) — participants in the sexual and idolatrous system of Babylon. Murderers (phoneis) — those who shed the blood of the saints and prophets (18:24). Idolaters (eidololatrai) — those who worshiped the beast's image (13:15). Those who love and practice lying (pas philon kai poion pseudos) — those aligned with the dragon's deceptive program (12:9, "the one who deceives the whole world") and the false prophet's deceptions (13:14). The 144,000, by contrast, were those "in whose mouth no lie (pseudos) was found" (14:5).

The exclusion is comprehensive. The six categories represent the full range of participation in the beast's system: cultic prostitution (dogs), deceptive manipulation (sorcerers), sexual-idolatrous compromise (the immoral), violence against the faithful (murderers), worship of false gods (idolaters), and alignment with the dragon's deception (those who love and practice lying). The new Jerusalem is defined as much by what it excludes as by what it includes.

Verse 16: The Self-Identification of Jesus

"'I, Jesus, have sent my angel to testify to you these things for the assemblies. I am the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star.'"

"I, Jesus, have sent my angel to testify to you these things for the assemblies" (ego Iesous epempsa ton angelon mou martyresai hymin tauta epi tais ekklesiais). This is one of only two times in the entire document that the name "Jesus" appears without the title "Christ" or "Lord" — a simple, personal self-identification. The statement closes the communication chain established in 1:1: God gave the revelation to Jesus Christ; Jesus Christ sent his angel; the angel showed his servants. Here in 22:16, Jesus confirms his role in the chain: "I have sent my angel." The angel is his (ton angelon mou, "my angel"), and the purpose is testimony (martyresai, from martyreo, "to bear witness, to testify").

The phrase epi tais ekklesiais ("for the assemblies") brings the document full circle to its addressees. The document was sent "to the seven assemblies that are in Asia" (1:4); the messages of chapters 2-3 were addressed to specific assemblies; and now the epilogue confirms that the entire vision has been for the assemblies (epi tais ekklesiais). The assemblies are not incidental recipients but the intended audience — the people for whose sake the vision was given, the communities whose present faithfulness or failure is addressed by the vision's cosmic perspective.

"I am the root and the descendant of David" (ego eimi he rhiza kai to genos Daueid). Two images are combined. The "root" (rhiza) draws on Isaiah 11:1, 10 (LXX). Isaiah 11:1: "A shoot will spring from the stem of Jesse, and a branch from his roots (ek tes rhizes autou) will bear fruit." Isaiah 11:10: "Then in that day the root of Jesse (he rhiza tou Iessai) who stands as a signal for the peoples — to him the nations will seek, and his resting place will be glorious." In 5:5, one of the elders told John: "the Lion that is from the tribe of Judah, the Root of David (he rhiza Daueid), has overcome." The "root of David" identifies Jesus as both the source and the descendant of the Davidic line — the one who precedes David (as root) and proceeds from David (as descendant). The word genos ("offspring, descendant, family") specifies the human lineage: Jesus comes from David's line. The double identification — root and descendant — asserts both preexistence (he is David's source) and incarnation (he is David's heir).

"The bright morning star" (ho aster ho lampros ho proinos). The morning star (aster proinos) appeared in the promise to the overcomer at Thyatira: "I will give him the morning star" (dosoо auto ton astera ton proinon, 2:28). In 22:16, the morning star is not a gift given but the identity of the giver: Christ himself is the morning star. The overcomer receives the morning star because the overcomer receives Christ.

The morning star is the planet Venus, visible before dawn, the herald of the coming day. In Numbers 24:17 (LXX), Balaam's oracle declared: "A star (astron) shall come forth from Jacob, and a scepter shall rise from Israel." The star-from-Jacob prophecy was widely understood in the first century as messianic. The Targum on Numbers 24:17 reads: "A king shall arise out of Jacob, and be anointed the Messiah out of Israel." By identifying himself as the morning star, the risen Christ claims to be the fulfillment of Balaam's star prophecy and the herald of the eschatological dawn. The adjective lampros ("bright, radiant, shining") is the same word used for the river (22:1) and for the fine linen of the bride (19:8). The brightness characterizes everything that belongs to the new creation.

Verse 17: The Spirit and the Bride Say "Come"

"The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come.' And let the one who hears say, 'Come.' And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who wishes take the water of life without cost."

"The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come'" (to pneuma kai he nymphe legousin, Erchou). Two voices issue the same invitation. The Spirit (to pneuma) has been present throughout the document as the one who speaks through the messages: "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies" (2:7, 11, 17, 29; 3:6, 13, 22). The Spirit has been the voice of the risen Christ to the assemblies, the animating power of prophetic speech (22:6: "the God of the spirits of the prophets"), and the one who declares the blessedness of those who die in the Lord (14:13). The bride (he nymphe) has been identified as the community of the faithful, the wife of the Lamb (19:7; 21:2, 9). Together, the Spirit and the bride issue the invitation: Erchou — "Come."

The word erchou ("come") is an imperative. The question is: to whom is it directed? The context suggests a dual direction. First, the invitation is directed to Christ: "Come, Lord Jesus" will be the explicit form of this cry in 22:20, and the repeated "I am coming quickly" (22:7, 12, 20) establishes the theme of Christ's return. The Spirit and the bride call for Christ's arrival. Second, the invitation extends to the thirsty, as the next clause makes explicit.

"And let the one who hears say, 'Come'" (kai ho akouon eipato, Erchou). The one who hears the document read aloud (ho akouon, the present participle, corresponding to "those who hear" in 1:3) is invited to join the cry. The invitation to call "Come" is extended beyond the Spirit and the bride to every hearer. The cry for Christ's return is not a private prayer of the community's leadership but an open invitation for any hearer to join.

"And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who wishes take the water of life without cost" (kai ho dipson ercheistho, ho thelon labeto hydor zoes dorean). The second direction of the invitation becomes explicit: those who thirst are invited to come, and those who wish may take the water of life freely. The language draws directly on Isaiah 55:1 (LXX): "Ho! Every one who thirsts (hoi dipsontes), come to the waters (poreuesthе eph' hydor); and you who have no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price (aneu argyriou kai times)." Isaiah's invitation is to the restored covenant community: come and receive God's provision without payment. The author of Revelation echoes Isaiah's language and applies it to the water of life — the same water that flows as a river from the throne (22:1) and was promised freely by the one on the throne (21:6).

The word dorean ("freely, without cost, as a gift") is the same word used in 21:6. The water of life is a gift, not a purchase. The contrast with the beast's economic system is pointed: in 13:17, no one could buy or sell without the beast's mark. The beast's system required allegiance for access to commerce. The new creation's system operates on gift: life itself is given freely. No mark is required. No allegiance is coerced. The only condition is thirst (ho dipson, "the one who thirsts") and willingness (ho thelon, "the one who wishes").

This verse serves as the document's final evangelistic invitation. After twenty-two chapters of vision, warning, judgment, and promise, the document pauses to extend an open-ended invitation to anyone who hears it. The door is not closed. The water is available. The only barrier is the hearer's own refusal. The invitation stands alongside the warnings of 22:15 (the excluded) and the blessings of 22:14 (the robe-washers) as the document's final statement on the availability of salvation: it is free, it is offered, and the one who desires it may take it.

Verses 18-19: The Warning Against Alteration

"I testify to everyone who hears the words of the prophecy of this book: if anyone adds to them, God will add to him the plagues which are written in this book; and if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his part from the tree of life and from the holy city, which are written in this book."

"I testify to everyone who hears the words of the prophecy of this book" (Martyro ego panti to akouonti tous logous tes propheteias tou bibliou toutou). The speaker is most likely the risen Christ, continuing from 22:16 ("I, Jesus"), though the transition is not explicitly marked. The verb martyro ("I testify, I bear witness, I solemnly declare") carries legal weight: this is a formal declaration with binding force. The audience is comprehensive: panti to akouonti ("to everyone who hears") — every person who encounters this document, without exception. The phrase tous logous tes propheteias tou bibliou toutou ("the words of the prophecy of this book") repeats the language of 22:7 and 1:3, reinforcing the document's self-classification as prophecy.

"If anyone adds to them, God will add to him the plagues which are written in this book" (ean tis epithe ep' auta, epithеsei ho theos ep' auton tas plegas tas gegrammenas en to biblio touto). The warning against adding to the words has a specific precedent in Deuteronomy 4:2 (LXX): "You shall not add (ou prosthesete) to the word which I am commanding you, nor take away from it (ouk aphelеite ap' autou), that you may keep the commandments of the LORD your God which I command you." Also Deuteronomy 12:32 (LXX 13:1): "Whatever I command you, you shall be careful to do; you shall not add to it (ou prostheseis ep' auto) nor take away from it (ouk apheleis ap' autou)." The Deuteronomic injunction protected the Torah from human alteration; the author of Revelation applies the same injunction to his prophecy. The claim is significant: the author places his document under the same protective formula as the Torah. This does not necessarily mean he claims equality with Moses, but it does assert that his prophecy carries divine authority and must not be tampered with.

The penalty for adding is that God will add the plagues (tas plegas) described in the document — the judgments of the seals, trumpets, and bowls. The one who adds unauthorized content to the prophecy will receive the judgments it describes. The penalty is tailored to the offense: adding brings addition — of plagues.

"And if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his part from the tree of life and from the holy city" (kai ean tis aphele apo ton logon tou bibliou tes propheteias tautes, aphelei ho theos to meros autou apo tou xylou tes zoes kai ek tes poleos tes hagias ton gegrамmenon en to biblio touto). The penalty for subtraction is subtraction: God will take away (aphelei) the person's part (meros, "share, portion") from the tree of life and from the holy city. The tree of life (to xylon tes zoes) is the tree of 22:2 and the promise of 2:7. The holy city (he polis he hagia) is the new Jerusalem of 21:2, 10. To have one's share removed from the tree of life and the city is to be excluded from the new creation — to lose what the overcomers inherit, to forfeit the blessing of 22:14.

The word meros ("part, share, portion") appeared in 21:8, where the cowardly and faithless had their "part" (to meros auton) in the lake of fire. In 22:19, the meros is in the tree of life and the city — the positive inheritance. God will take away this positive share from the one who subtracts from the prophecy. The implication is that the person who diminishes the prophecy forfeits membership in the community it addresses. The integrity of the message and the integrity of the community's hope are bound together: to tamper with the words is to endanger one's participation in the reality the words describe.

The warning functions on multiple levels. Practically, it guards against scribal alteration in the process of copying and transmitting the document. Theologically, it asserts the completeness and sufficiency of the revelation: nothing needs to be added and nothing may be removed. The prophecy as delivered is the full message, and the assemblies are to receive it as such. The warning also addresses any who might attempt to soften the document's demands, remove its harder sayings, or add qualifications that dilute its urgency. The prophecy must be received whole.

Verses 20-21: "Come, Lord Jesus" — The Final Exchange and Benediction

"He who testifies to these things says, 'Yes, I am coming quickly.' Amen. Come, Lord Jesus. The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all. Amen."

"He who testifies to these things says, 'Yes, I am coming quickly'" (legei ho martyron tauta, Nai, erchomai tachy). The third and final declaration of "I am coming quickly" (erchomai tachy). The speaker is identified as ho martyron tauta ("the one who testifies to these things") — a title that identifies him with "the faithful witness" (ho martys ho pistos) of 1:5 and 3:14. The word nai ("yes, indeed, certainly") is an emphatic affirmation. The triple declaration of imminent coming (22:7, 12, 20) frames the entire epilogue as a conversation about Christ's return. The assemblies have heard what the future holds; now they hear the assurance that it is coming soon.

"Amen. Come, Lord Jesus" (Amen, erchou, kyrie Iesou). John's response — or the community's liturgical response — to Christ's declaration. The word Amen ratifies Christ's statement: "Yes, it is so — you are indeed coming." The imperative erchou ("Come!") then turns ratification into petition: not only is Christ's coming affirmed as true, but it is actively desired and requested. The combination of Amen (affirmation) and erchou (petition) moves from belief to longing.

The phrase erchou kyrie Iesou ("Come, Lord Jesus") is the Greek equivalent of the Aramaic Maranatha (maran atha, "Our Lord, come!" or marana tha, "Come, our Lord!") found in 1 Corinthians 16:22 and the Didache 10:6. The preservation of this liturgical cry in both Aramaic and Greek traditions indicates that it was among the earliest prayer-forms of the Jesus-following communities, predating the composition of the New Testament documents. Paul assumed his Greek-speaking Corinthian audience would recognize the Aramaic form without translation. The Didache placed it in the eucharistic liturgy. The author of Revelation closes his document with the same prayer, now in Greek: Come, Lord Jesus.

The prayer gathers the entire document into a single petition. Everything the assemblies have been shown — the fall of Babylon, the defeat of the beast, the binding of the dragon, the new heaven and earth, the new Jerusalem, the river and the tree of life, the eternal reign — all of it hinges on one event: the coming of the Lord. The prayer "Come, Lord Jesus" is the document's final word from the community to its Lord: we have heard, we believe, and we ask you to fulfill what you have promised.

"The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all. Amen" (he charis tou kyriou Iesou meta panton. Amen). The document closes with an epistolary benediction — the same form used to conclude the Pauline letters. Romans 16:20: "The grace of our Lord Jesus be with you." 1 Corinthians 16:23: "The grace of the Lord Jesus be with you." 2 Corinthians 13:14: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ... be with you all." The closing benediction confirms the document's genre as a letter addressed to real communities. The apocalyptic visions and prophetic warnings are enclosed within an epistolary frame: greetings in 1:4-5, benediction in 22:21. The communities that receive this letter receive it as they receive any letter — read aloud in the assembly, heard corporately, responded to communally.

The word charis ("grace, favor, gift") is the gift that comes from God through Christ. In 1:4, the document opened with "grace to you and peace" (charis hymin kai eirene). The closing grace matches the opening grace, completing the epistolary frame. The document that began with grace ends with grace. The entire content — the visions of judgment, the calls to repentance, the promises to overcomers, the destruction of Babylon, the descent of the new Jerusalem — is enclosed within a benediction of grace. The assemblies' faithfulness is not self-powered; it operates within the sphere of the Lord Jesus's grace.

The word panton ("all") — "with all" (meta panton) — extends the benediction universally. Not merely the seven assemblies, not merely the prophets, not merely the overcomers, but all who hear. The grace is as broadly available as the water of life (22:17: "let the one who wishes take the water of life without cost"). The document that has drawn sharp lines between the faithful and the unfaithful, between the Lamb's community and the beast's worshipers, ends with a benediction extended to all.

The final Amen seals the document. As in 1:6-7, where the doxology concluded with Amen and the first prophetic oracle was punctuated with "Even so, Amen" (nai, amen), the document closes with the same word of ratification. It is so. The prophecy is complete. The revelation is delivered. The assemblies have heard. The prayer has been spoken. The grace has been extended. The document is finished.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Revelation 22 performs two distinct functions within the document, corresponding to its two sections.

The first section (22:1-5) completes the new Jerusalem vision that began in 21:9. Where chapter 21 described the city's external architecture — walls, gates, foundations, dimensions, materials, illumination — chapter 22:1-5 describes its internal landscape: the river of the water of life flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb, the tree of life bearing twelve kinds of fruit with leaves for the healing of the nations, the abolition of the curse, the servants' unmediated access to God's face, and the eternal reign. Every element reverses a feature of the fallen creation: the river reverses the lost Eden (Genesis 2:10); the accessible tree reverses the barred tree (Genesis 3:24); the abolished curse reverses the curse of Genesis 3:14-19; seeing God's face reverses the prohibition of Exodus 33:20; the name on the foreheads reverses the beast's mark (13:16) and fulfills the protective seal (7:3); the absence of night reverses Babylon's darkness; and the eternal reign fulfills the promise of 1:6, 5:10, and 20:6. The first five verses of chapter 22 are the culmination of the entire biblical narrative as understood through the prophetic and apocalyptic tradition: the creation is restored, the curse is reversed, God dwells with his people, and his servants reign.

The second section (22:6-21) constitutes the document's epilogue — the closing frame that returns from visionary content to direct address. Its functions are multiple and overlapping:

Authentication (22:6): the words are declared "faithful and true," echoing 21:5 and 19:9, and the communication chain of 1:1 is reaffirmed.

Urgency (22:7, 12, 20): the triple declaration "I am coming quickly" impresses the imminence of Christ's return on the assemblies, reinforcing the temporal framework of 1:1 ("the things which must soon take place") and 1:3 ("the time is near").

Beatitude (22:7, 14): the sixth and seventh blessings — keeping the prophecy, washing robes — reiterate the document's demand for active faithfulness and ongoing purification.

Worship correction (22:8-9): the second angel-worship rebuke reinforces the document's central theme: worship belongs to God alone.

Openness (22:10): unlike Daniel's sealed book (Daniel 12:4, 9), this prophecy is not to be sealed, because the time is near. The message is for the present, not a distant future.

Finality (22:11): the four declarations about the wrongdoer, the filthy, the righteous, and the holy signal the decisiveness of the present moment. Choices are being fixed.

Judgment (22:12-13): Christ comes with his reward, rendering to each according to his deeds. The Alpha-Omega titles claim comprehensive divine sovereignty.

Invitation (22:17): the Spirit and the bride jointly cry "Come" — both to Christ (calling for his return) and to the thirsty (offering the water of life freely). The document's final evangelistic appeal stands open.

Warning (22:18-19): the Deuteronomic formula protects the prophecy from addition or subtraction, asserting its completeness and divine authority.

Closing prayer and benediction (22:20-21): "Come, Lord Jesus" — the earliest liturgical cry of the faith communities, the Maranatha of 1 Corinthians 16:22 — is the document's last petition. The grace benediction closes the epistolary frame.

For the first-century assemblies hearing this chapter read aloud, the message is both completion and summons. The vision of the new Jerusalem is complete: they have seen the city's glory, its river, its tree, its light, its eternal reign. They have heard the authentication of the words as faithful and true. They have been told that the time is near, that Christ is coming quickly, and that the water of life is freely available. They have been warned against tampering with the prophecy and against misdirecting worship. And they have been invited to join the cry: Come.

The chapter — and the document — ends not with a conclusion but with an expectation. The last substantive word from the community is erchou, "Come." The last word over the community is charis, "grace." The assemblies are left between the cry and the grace, between the petition for Christ's coming and the gift that sustains them until he does. The document does not resolve their present suffering. It reframes it. The assemblies now know what lies behind the beast's power (the dragon), what lies ahead of the dragon's defeat (the new creation), and what lies within their reach in the present moment (the water of life, the washed robes, the keeping of the prophecy). The document has given them the full vision. The rest is faithfulness.
