1 Corinthians 6 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 5): Paul confronted the community with a specific case of sexual immorality — a man maintaining a sexual arrangement with his father's wife, a violation of Leviticus 18:8 and Deuteronomy 22:30, conduct so extreme that even pagans would not tolerate it (5:1). The community's response was not grief but arrogance: "you are puffed up" (pephysiomenoi este, 5:2), using the same word (phusioo) that diagnosed the Corinthian disease throughout chapter 4. Paul commanded the community to assemble and deliver the man to Satan "for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (5:5). The discipline was restorative in purpose — severe intervention aimed at salvation, not elimination. Paul then developed the Passover metaphor: Christ, the Passover lamb, has been sacrificed (5:7), and therefore the community must purge the old leaven from its midst, celebrating the ongoing festival with "the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (5:8). The chapter concluded by establishing a sharp boundary between inside and outside. Paul disclaimed jurisdiction over outsiders: "what have I to do with judging those outside?" (5:12). But the community is responsible for judging those inside: "is it not those inside whom you judge?" (5:12). The closing command was the Deuteronomic formula: "purge the evil person from among you" (5:13, quoting Deuteronomy 17:7; 19:19; 21:21; 22:21; 24:7). The community failed to exercise internal judgment when it was required; it tolerated what should have been expelled.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 7): Chapter 7 opens with a new formula: "Now concerning the matters about which you wrote" (peri de hon egrapsate, 7:1). This signals a shift in the letter's structure. From this point forward, Paul is responding to specific questions the Corinthians raised in a letter to him. The first question concerns marriage and sexual relations. Paul's response addresses the unmarried and married, widows and virgins, divorce and separation. The governing principle is stated in 7:17: "let each person lead the life that the Lord has assigned to him, and to which God has called him" — a principle Paul applies to circumcision (7:18-19), slavery (7:21-24), and marriage (7:25-40). The chapter's tone is pastoral and advisory: "I say this by way of concession, not of command" (7:6); "I have no command of the Lord, but I give my judgment" (7:25). The urgency underlying the advice is eschatological: "the appointed time has grown very short" (7:29); "the present form of this world is passing away" (7:31).

How chapter 6 connects to what precedes and follows: Chapter 6 is not a new topic but the continuation and completion of the argument Paul began in chapter 5. The two chapters address the same underlying failure from opposite directions. In chapter 5, the community failed to exercise internal judgment when it should have — it tolerated flagrant sin within its midst. In 6:1-8, the community goes to external courts when it should judge for itself — it outsources internal disputes to pagan judges. Both failures expose the same confusion about the community's boundaries and identity. A community that does not understand the difference between inside and outside will simultaneously fail to judge insiders (chapter 5) and appeal to outsiders for judgment of insiders (6:1-8). The principle stated at the end of chapter 5 — "is it not those inside whom you judge?" (5:12) — generates both problems.

The second half of chapter 6 (vv. 12-20) circles back to the porneia of chapter 5, providing the theological foundation for why sexual immorality is incompatible with membership in the community. The man in chapter 5 was a specific case; 6:12-20 supplies the general principle. The body belongs to the Lord (6:13), the body's members are members of Christ (6:15), the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (6:19), and the community was bought with a price (6:20). These claims explain why the case in chapter 5 required such drastic action.

Chapter 6 also transitions the letter from Paul's initiative (chapters 1-6, where Paul addresses problems he has learned about from reports) to the Corinthians' initiative (chapters 7-16, where Paul responds to their written questions). The transition is thematic as well: the porneia discussion in 6:12-20 leads directly into the marriage and sexuality questions of chapter 7. The slogan "all things are lawful for me" (6:12), which Paul quotes and qualifies, represents the Corinthian attitude that generates both the sexual permissiveness of chapters 5-6 and the questions about marriage in chapter 7.

Verses 1-6: Internal Disputes Before External Courts

"When one of you has a grievance against another, does he dare go to law before the unrighteous instead of the saints?" (6:1).

The word tolma ("to dare, to have the audacity, to presume") carries a note of astonishment. Paul is not merely reporting a problem; he is expressing disbelief that it occurs. The phrase pragma echon pros ton heteron ("having a matter/case against another") uses pragma ("thing, matter, business, legal case") in its juridical sense — a dispute that requires adjudication. The dispute is between community members: one believer against another.

The contrast is between two forums. The first: epi ton adikon ("before the unrighteous"). The word adikos ("unrighteous, unjust") describes the pagan judges to whom the Corinthians are bringing their disputes. The word is loaded. Paul does not call them "judges" or "magistrates" or even "outsiders" (though he used exo, "outside," in 5:12-13). He calls them "the unrighteous" — people who, by definition, stand outside the community of those who have been made righteous. The word will reappear in verse 9 as the first category of those who "will not inherit the kingdom of God." The second forum: epi ton hagion ("before the saints"). The word hagioi ("saints, holy ones, those set apart") is Paul's standard designation for community members (1:2, "called saints"). The community consists of hagioi — people consecrated to God, set apart from the world. The fact that they are bringing their disputes before adikoi rather than hagioi is an inversion of the proper order.

The connection to chapter 5 is immediate. In 5:12, Paul established the principle: the community judges those inside; God judges those outside. In 6:1, the community is violating this principle in reverse: instead of judging its own members, it is asking outsiders to judge them. The community that failed to exercise internal discipline in the case of the incestuous man (chapter 5) now runs to external courts for its internal disputes. Both failures reveal the same inability to understand and maintain the boundary between the community and the world.

"Do you not know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is to be judged by you, are you incompetent to try trivial cases?" (6:2).

The formula ouk oidate ("do you not know?") appears here for the third time in the letter (after 3:16 and 5:6) and will appear four more times in this chapter alone (6:3, 6:9, 6:15, 6:16, 6:19). Its repeated use signals that the Corinthians possess knowledge they are not applying. They know these things; they are not acting on what they know.

The claim hoti hoi hagioi ton kosmon krinousin ("that the saints will judge the world") draws on a tradition rooted in Israel's scriptures and developed in Second Temple Jewish literature. Daniel 7:22 (LXX): "judgment was given to the saints of the Most High" (krima edoke hagiois hypsistou). Daniel 7:27: "the kingdom and the dominion and the greatness of the kingdoms under the whole heaven shall be given to the people of the saints of the Most High." In Daniel's vision, the "one like a son of man" receives dominion (7:13-14), and this dominion is shared with "the saints of the Most High" (7:18, 22, 27). The Wisdom of Solomon 3:8 describes the righteous: "they will judge nations and rule over peoples, and the Lord will reign over them forever." In the Enochic tradition (1 Enoch 1:9; 95:3; 96:1; 98:12), the righteous participate in judgment upon the wicked. Jesus himself promised his disciples: "you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28; cf. Luke 22:30).

Paul's argument is from the greater to the lesser (a qal wahomer, "light and heavy" argument, a standard rabbinic form). If the saints will judge the entire cosmos — the world in its totality — then certainly they are competent to adjudicate kriteria elachista ("the most trivial cases," literally "the smallest courts/judgments"). The word elachistos is the superlative of mikros ("small") — these are the very smallest matters, petty disputes. If you are destined for cosmic judgment, how can you be unable to settle a property disagreement?

"Do you not know that we will judge angels? How much more, then, matters pertaining to this life!" (6:3).

The escalation continues. Not only the world but angelous krinoumon ("we will judge angels"). The background is complex. In the Enochic literature, the fallen Watchers — the angels of Genesis 6:1-4 who left their proper station — are subject to judgment (1 Enoch 10:4-6, 12-13; 16:1; Jude 6: "the angels who did not stay within their own position of authority... he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day"). The participation of the righteous in this angelic judgment is part of the eschatological expectation. If the community will participate in judging celestial beings, then biotika ("matters pertaining to this life," from bios, "life, livelihood, daily existence") — mundane disputes about property, contracts, and money — should be well within their competence. The word biotika is deliberately dismissive. These earthly, material disputes are beneath the dignity of a people destined for cosmic judicial authority.

"So if you have ordinary cases, do you appoint as judges those who have no standing in the assembly?" (6:4).

The verse is syntactically difficult, and its tone may be either ironic or directive. The word exouthemenous (perfect passive participle of exoutheneo, "to despise, to treat as nothing, to regard as of no account") describes people who are "despised" or "of no standing." If the sentence is ironic: Paul is asking, "When you have everyday disputes, do you really seat as judges people who count for nothing in the assembly?" — meaning the pagan judges, who have no standing within the community. The irony would be biting: you seat as judges over your disputes the very people who are nothing in the community's estimation. If the sentence is directive: Paul is saying, "Appoint even those of lowest standing in the assembly" — even the least qualified community member would be better as a judge of internal disputes than a pagan outsider. Either reading serves the argument: the community possesses sufficient resources to handle its own disputes without recourse to external courts.

"I say this to your shame. Is it so, that there is not among you one wise person who will be able to decide between his brothers?" (6:5).

The phrase pros entropen hymin lego ("I say this to your shame") uses the word entrope ("shame, embarrassment, turning inward") — the very thing Paul said he was not doing in 4:14 (ouk entrepon hymas grapho, "I do not write to shame you"). There he withheld shame because he was admonishing his children. Here, addressing this particular failure, he deliberately invokes shame. The situation is shameful.

The irony cuts deep: outos ouk eni en hymin oudeis sophos ("is there not among you even one wise person?"). The word sophos ("wise") drips with irony given the Corinthians' self-assessment. This is the community that prided itself on wisdom (1:17-2:16), that boasted in its teachers' rhetorical brilliance, that considered itself rich and reigning (4:8). Yet among all these supposedly wise people, not one can be found competent to adjudicate a dispute between brothers? The word diakrinai ("to judge, to decide, to distinguish") echoes the vocabulary of judgment that runs through the letter. The phrase ana meson tou adelphou autou ("between his brother") uses the singular adelphos, emphasizing the familial relationship that makes the recourse to outside courts so incongruous. These are brothers going to court against brothers.

"But brother goes to law against brother, and that before unbelievers!" (6:6).

Paul restates the offense with maximum bluntness. Adelphos meta adelphou krinetai ("brother goes to law against brother") — the repetition of adelphos hammers the relational betrayal. The word apistos ("unbeliever, unfaithful, one without trust/faith") replaces adikos ("unrighteous") from verse 1. The two terms are not synonymous but complementary: the pagan judges are both adikoi (outside the sphere of God's righteousness) and apistoi (outside the sphere of faith/trust in God). The community is airing its internal conflicts before people who share neither its righteousness nor its faith.

Verses 7-8: The More Fundamental Failure

"To have lawsuits at all with one another is already a defeat for you. Why not rather suffer wrong? Why not rather be defrauded?" (6:7).

Paul now deepens the argument beyond the question of venue. The problem is not merely where the disputes are adjudicated but that they exist at all. The word hettema ("defeat, loss, shortcoming, diminishment") describes a failure, a falling short. The phrase holos hettema hymin estin ("it is already altogether a defeat for you") uses holos ("altogether, at all, completely") — the same intensifier from 5:1 ("it is actually reported"). Even before the question of pagan courts arises, the very existence of lawsuits among community members represents a defeat — a failure of the community to embody the ethic it professes.

Paul poses two rhetorical questions that invoke a radically different standard of behavior. Dia ti ouchi mallon adikeisthe ("why not rather be wronged?") — the verb adikeo ("to wrong, to treat unjustly, to injure") in the passive means "to suffer injustice." Dia ti ouchi mallon apostereisthe ("why not rather be defrauded?") — the verb apostereo ("to deprive, to defraud, to rob, to withhold what is owed") in the passive means "to suffer deprivation, to be cheated."

The ethic Paul invokes has clear roots in Jesus' teaching. Matthew 5:39-40: "If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also. And if anyone would sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well." Luke 6:29-30: "From one who takes away your cloak, do not withhold your tunic either. Give to everyone who begs from you, and from one who takes away your goods do not demand them back." The willingness to absorb injustice rather than retaliate or litigate is not passivity but a deliberate choice grounded in the cross. Paul has already argued that the apostolic pattern involves suffering wrong without retaliation: "when reviled, we bless; when persecuted, we endure; when slandered, we respond graciously" (4:12-13). The community should embody the same pattern. A community formed by the cross — the paradigmatic case of the righteous one suffering unjustly — should be willing to absorb wrong rather than fight in pagan courts over property and money.

"But you yourselves wrong and defraud — even your own brothers!" (6:8).

The reality is the opposite of the ideal. Alla hymeis adikeite kai apostereite ("but you yourselves wrong and defraud") — the same two verbs from verse 7 now appear in the active voice. Not only are the Corinthians unwilling to suffer wrong; they are the ones inflicting it. They are not merely failing to absorb injustice; they are perpetrating it. The phrase kai touto adelphous ("and this against brothers!") intensifies the indictment. The kai touto ("and this!") expresses outrage. They wrong and defraud, and their victims are their own brothers — members of the same community, children of the same Father, members of the same body.

The progression of the argument is devastating: (1) You should not take disputes to pagan courts (vv. 1-6). (2) You should not have disputes at all; you should absorb the wrong (v. 7). (3) In fact, you are the ones doing the wronging (v. 8). The Corinthians are not merely victims seeking a forum for redress; they are perpetrators using pagan courts as instruments of injustice against fellow believers. Each layer of the argument peels away another layer of pretense.

Verses 9-11: Those Who Will Not Inherit the Kingdom, and What the Community Was

"Or do you not know that the unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived: neither the sexually immoral, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor men who practice homosexuality, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor swindlers will inherit the kingdom of God" (6:9-10).

The formula e ouk oidate ("or do you not know?") introduces what Paul considers established teaching. The conjunction e ("or") links this to the preceding argument: "or" have you forgotten this fundamental truth that should be governing your behavior? The verb planasthe ("do not be deceived, do not wander, do not be led astray") is the present passive imperative of planao ("to lead astray, to deceive, to cause to wander"). In the LXX, the word describes Israel's straying from God's path: Isaiah 53:6 (LXX), "all we like sheep have gone astray" (pantes hos probata eplanenthemen). The warning me planasthe ("do not be deceived") signals that the Corinthians may be in danger of self-deception on this point — perhaps because the slogan "all things are lawful for me" (which Paul will address in v. 12) has led some to believe that behavioral categories no longer apply to those who have been spiritually endowed.

The claim: adikoi theou basileian ou kleronomesousin ("the unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom of God"). The word adikoi ("unrighteous") is the same word Paul used in verse 1 for the pagan judges. Its reappearance here creates a deliberate resonance: the community is taking its disputes before the adikoi (v. 1), while those who are adikoi will not inherit the kingdom (v. 9). The implication is uncomfortable: community members who practice injustice against their brothers (v. 8) are behaving like the adikoi who have no share in the kingdom.

The word kleronomeo ("to inherit") is deeply embedded in Israel's scriptural vocabulary. In the LXX, the verb and its cognates describe Israel's inheritance of the promised land — the central promise of the covenant. Genesis 15:7 (LXX): "I am the LORD who brought you out from Ur of the Chaldeans to give you this land to inherit it" (kleronomesai auten). Deuteronomy 1:8: "Go in and take possession of the land that the LORD swore to your fathers... to give to them and to their offspring after them." Psalm 37:9 (LXX 36:9): "those who wait for the LORD shall inherit the land" (kleronomesousin gen); 37:11 (LXX 36:11): "the meek shall inherit the land." The inheritance is not something earned but something received as the fulfillment of God's promise. However, possession of the inheritance was always conditional on faithfulness within the covenant. Israel inherited the land but could be — and was — expelled from it for covenantal unfaithfulness. The inheritance was a gift, but remaining in the inheritance required maintaining the covenant relationship.

The phrase basileian theou ("kingdom of God") appeared in 4:20: "the kingdom of God does not consist in talk but in power." It will appear again in 15:50: "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God." The kingdom of God is the sphere of God's sovereign reign — the reality that the community already participates in partially and will fully enter at the consummation. To "not inherit" the kingdom is to be excluded from this final, complete participation in God's reign.

Paul then provides a catalogue of ten types of persons who will not inherit the kingdom:

1. Pornoi ("sexually immoral") — the same word from 5:9, 10, 11. Those who engage in porneia, the broad term for sexual conduct that violates God's ordering of human relationships.

2. Eidololatrai ("idolaters") — those who worship idols. Listed in 5:10, 11 as well. Paul will address idolatry at length in chapters 8-10.

3. Moichoi ("adulterers") — those who violate the marriage covenant through sexual union with another's spouse. The word is distinct from pornos: moicheia is specifically the violation of an existing marriage bond.

4-5. Malakoi ("soft ones") and arsenokoitai ("men who bed males"). The word malakos literally means "soft" and in moral discourse described a person of weak character, one who yielded to pleasures or who played the passive role in male sexual relations. The word arsenokoites is a compound of arsen ("male") and koite ("bed," with sexual connotation, from which the English "coitus" derives). The compound appears to be drawn directly from Leviticus 18:22 (LXX): meta arsenos ou koimethese koiten gynaikos ("with a male you shall not lie the bed of a woman") and Leviticus 20:13 (LXX): hos an koimethe meta arsenos koiten gynaikos ("whoever lies with a male the bed of a woman"). The term identifies a specific category of sexual conduct prohibited in the Levitical code.

6. Kleptai ("thieves") — those who take what belongs to another by stealth.

7. Pleonektai ("greedy, covetous") — those who grasp for more than their share, who are driven by the desire to possess at others' expense. The word appeared in 5:10, 11.

8. Methysoi ("drunkards") — those characterized by habitual drunkenness. Listed in 5:11.

9. Loidoroi ("revilers, verbal abusers") — those who use speech to wound and demean. Listed in 5:11.

10. Harpages ("swindlers, robbers, extortioners") — those who seize what belongs to others by violence or fraud. The word appeared in 5:10, 11.

The list overlaps substantially with the list in 5:11 (pornos, pleonektes, eidololatres, loidoros, methysos, harpax) but expands it with four additional categories (moichoi, malakoi, arsenokoitai, kleptai). The overlap is not accidental. In 5:11, Paul listed those with whom the community should not even eat — those who bear the name of brother but whose behavior contradicts the name. Here in 6:9-10, Paul states the eschatological consequence: such people will not inherit the kingdom of God. The communal discipline of chapter 5 (expulsion from the fellowship) reflects the eschatological reality of chapter 6 (exclusion from the kingdom). The community's present judgment anticipates God's future judgment.

The structure of the list merits attention. It moves from sexual offenses (pornoi, eidololatrai, moichoi, malakoi, arsenokoitai) through property offenses (kleptai, pleonektai) to social offenses (methysoi, loidoroi, harpages). The range is comprehensive. Paul is not singling out one category of sin as uniquely disqualifying. Sexual immorality, greed, theft, drunkenness, verbal abuse, and extortion all stand on the same ground: all are incompatible with inheriting the kingdom.

The verb kleronomesousin is future tense: "will not inherit." The exclusion is eschatological — it pertains to the final inheritance, the full entry into God's kingdom. But the present-tense warning me planasthe ("do not be deceived") indicates that present behavior determines future inheritance. The kingdom is not inherited regardless of conduct. The one who practices these things — who is characterized by this pattern of life — will not receive the inheritance. This is consistent with the pattern throughout Israel's scriptures: the inheritance was given by grace, but maintaining it required faithfulness. Israel received the land as a gift; Israel lost the land through disobedience.

"And such were some of you. But you were washed, but you were sanctified, but you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (6:11).

This verse is the hinge of the entire section. The first clause — kai tauta tines ete ("and such things some of you were") — uses the imperfect ete ("you were"), pointing to a past state that has ended. The word tauta ("such things, these things") is neuter plural, referring to the entire list of behaviors and identities just catalogued. Some of the Corinthians were pornoi, eidololatrai, moichoi, malakoi, arsenokoitai, kleptai, pleonektai, methysoi, loidoroi, harpages. The community was not drawn from the morally respectable; it was drawn from the very categories Paul has just identified as excluded from the kingdom. The word tines ("some") indicates that not all were characterized by every vice, but the community collectively included people from every category on the list.

Then three verbs, each introduced by the strong adversative alla ("but"), each in the aorist middle or passive, each marking a decisive break between the past state and the present one:

Apeloususthe ("you were washed") — the aorist middle of apolouo ("to wash off, to wash away, to cleanse"). The word describes a thorough cleansing. In Acts 22:16, Ananias tells Paul: "Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins" (apolousai tas hamartias sou). The connection to baptism is natural: the physical act of immersion corresponds to the spiritual reality of cleansing from the past. The old identity — the pornos, the thief, the drunkard — was washed away. The middle voice (apeloususthe rather than the passive apelutheete) may indicate that the Corinthians participated in the washing: they submitted to it, accepted it, presented themselves for it. The washing was not imposed on them against their will but entered into voluntarily.

Hegiasthete ("you were sanctified") — the aorist passive of hagiazo ("to sanctify, to make holy, to set apart, to consecrate"). The passive voice indicates that God is the agent: the Corinthians were sanctified by God's action. This connects directly to 1:2, where Paul addressed them as hegiasmenois en Christo Iesou ("those who have been sanctified in Christ Jesus"). The sanctification is the same: they have been set apart from the world, consecrated to God, transferred from the realm of the adikoi to the realm of the hagioi. The word carries its full LXX weight: in Exodus 19:10, God commands Moses to "sanctify" (hagiason) the people before the giving of the Torah at Sinai; in Leviticus 20:7-8, "sanctify yourselves and be holy, for I am the LORD your God... I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (ego kyrios ho hagizon hymas). Sanctification separates a people from common use and dedicates them to God's purposes.

Edikaioothete ("you were justified") — the aorist passive of dikaioo ("to justify, to declare righteous, to vindicate, to acquit"). The Corinthians were declared righteous — their status before God was transformed from adikoi to dikaioi. The word connects to 4:4, where Paul said "I am not justified by this" (ouk en touto dedikaiomei), reserving the verdict of justification for the Lord's judgment. Here the justification has occurred: God has already declared the Corinthians righteous. The past tense is significant. Justification is presented as a completed event, not an ongoing process.

The instrumentality is specified: en to onomati tou kyriou Iesou Christou kai en to pneumati tou theou hemon ("in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in/by the Spirit of our God"). The "name" (onoma) of the Lord Jesus Christ carries the full weight of his person and authority, as it did in 1:2, 1:10, 5:4. The Spirit of God is the agent through whom the transformation occurred — the same Spirit who indwells the community (3:16), who reveals God's wisdom (2:10-12), and who constitutes the community as God's temple.

The three verbs together describe a comprehensive transformation. The washing cleansed the old life away. The sanctification set them apart for God. The justification declared them righteous in God's sight. The order is notable: washed, then sanctified, then justified. This sequence does not match any systematic theological order but follows an experiential logic: first the cleansing from the old (washing), then the dedication to the new (sanctification), then the declaration of standing (justification). All three are aorist — completed actions, decisive breaks with the past.

The force of this verse within the argument is twofold. First, it establishes the magnitude of the transformation the Corinthians have undergone. They were the very people on the list of the excluded. They are no longer those people. The change is real and radical. Second, it intensifies the warning. If they have been washed, sanctified, and justified — removed from the categories that exclude from the kingdom — then to return to those behaviors is to un-become what they have become. Paul does not state this consequence explicitly here, but the logic is inescapable: if the adikoi will not inherit the kingdom, and you were adikoi but have been transformed, then to revert to adikia is to place yourself back among those who will not inherit. The transformation was a rescue from a defined condition. Returning to that condition is returning to its consequences.

Verses 12-14: The "All Things Are Lawful" Slogan and the Body's Purpose

"All things are lawful for me, but not all things are beneficial. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be dominated by anything" (6:12).

The phrase panta moi exestin ("all things are lawful for me" or "all things are permissible for me") is almost certainly a Corinthian slogan that Paul is quoting back to them. The word exestin ("it is permitted, it is lawful, it is within one's rights") expresses permission or liberty. The slogan likely arose from a misapplication of Paul's own teaching about freedom from the law. If believers are no longer under Torah as a legal code, the Corinthians reasoned, then all behavioral restrictions have been removed. Everything is permissible. Paul taught them freedom; they heard license.

Paul responds to the slogan not by denying it outright but by qualifying it with two counters.

First: all' ou panta sympherei ("but not all things are beneficial/advantageous"). The verb symphero ("to bring together, to be profitable, to be advantageous, to be beneficial") introduces a criterion beyond mere permissibility. Even if an action is permitted, the question remains: does it benefit? Does it build up? Does it serve the good of the community and the individual? The word symphero will appear again in 10:23 and 12:7 ("the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common benefit"). Freedom is real, but freedom without regard for consequences is not wisdom — it is the kind of folly Paul has been diagnosing since chapter 1.

Second: all' ouk ego exousiasthesomai hypo tinos ("but I will not be mastered/dominated by anything"). The verb exousiazo ("to have authority over, to exercise power over, to dominate") in the passive means "to be brought under the power of, to be mastered by, to be enslaved to." The wordplay is deliberate: exestin (it is "permissible," from exousia, "authority/right") becomes exousiasthesomai ("I will be placed under the authority of"). The Corinthians claim authority over all things (panta moi exestin — "I have authority/permission over all things"); Paul counters that the exercise of unrestrained license actually produces the opposite of authority — it produces slavery. The person who does whatever is permissible without regard for consequences does not demonstrate freedom but demonstrates bondage to appetite. True freedom is the power to say no, not merely the right to say yes.

"Food is meant for the stomach and the stomach for food — and God will destroy both one and the other" (6:13a).

This appears to be another Corinthian slogan, or at least the beginning of one. The phrase ta bromata te koilia kai he koilia tois bromasin ("foods for the stomach and the stomach for foods") expresses a natural correspondence: food and stomach are made for each other. The apparent logic: just as the stomach is designed for food and food for the stomach, so the body has natural appetites that should be satisfied. If hunger justifies eating, then sexual desire justifies sexual fulfillment. The body wants what it wants; give it what it was made for.

Paul's insertion — ho de theos kai tauten kai tauta katargesei ("but God will destroy both the one and the others") — disrupts the analogy. The verb katargeo ("to render inoperative, to abolish, to destroy, to nullify") indicates that the stomach-food correspondence is temporary. God will abolish both. The digestive system and its objects belong to the present age, which is passing away (7:31). The body's appetites for food are not a permanent feature of existence; they belong to the mortal, perishable order that God will bring to an end.

"But the body is not meant for sexual immorality, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (6:13b).

Paul breaks the Corinthian analogy. The Corinthians wanted to extend the stomach-food logic to the body-sex relationship: as the stomach is for food, so the body is for sex. Paul denies the parallel. The body (soma) is not for porneia ("sexual immorality") but for the Lord (to kyrio). The correspondence is not body-sex but body-Lord. The body belongs to the Lord, and the Lord belongs to the body. The phrase ho kyrios to somati ("the Lord for the body") is remarkable: it asserts a reciprocal relationship. The body is given to the Lord for his service; the Lord is given to the body for its redemption and glorification. The Lord does not discard the body; he claims it.

The word soma ("body") in Paul's usage is not merely the physical frame. It is the whole person as a concrete, embodied being — the person in their physicality, their capacity for relationship, their public, tangible existence. What Paul is asserting is that the entire embodied person belongs to the Lord. The body is not a disposable container for an immaterial soul; it is the person as God made them, and it has a Lord who claims it.

"And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (6:14).

The conjunction de ("and, but") connects the body's belonging to the Lord with the evidence for that belonging: the resurrection. Ho theos kai ton kyrion egeiren ("God raised the Lord") — the aorist tense marks the resurrection as a completed historical event. Kai hemas exegerei dia tes dynameos autou ("and he will raise us through his power") — the future tense marks the believers' resurrection as a future event, accomplished by God's dynamis ("power"). The same word dynamis that described the gospel's power (1:18, 24) and the Spirit's power (2:4-5) and the kingdom's power (4:20) now describes the power by which God will raise the dead.

The argument against the Corinthian slogan is now complete. The stomach-food relationship is temporary; God will destroy both. But the body-Lord relationship is permanent. God raised the Lord's body from the dead, and God will raise the believers' bodies from the dead. The body is not disposable; it is destined for resurrection. Therefore the body cannot be treated as morally neutral territory where any appetite may be indulged. The body that God will raise is the body that must be used for the Lord's purposes now. What you do with your body matters precisely because your body has an eternal future.

Verses 15-17: Members of Christ and Union with a Prostitute

"Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? Shall I then take the members of Christ and make them members of a prostitute? Never!" (6:15).

Ouk oidate ("do you not know?") appears again. The claim: ta somata hymon mele Christou estin ("your bodies are members of Christ"). The word mele ("members, limbs, body parts") describes the organic connection between the individual believer's body and Christ. The image is not metaphorical in the sense of being merely illustrative; Paul means that the believers' bodies are genuinely incorporated into Christ. They are limbs of Christ's body. The word mele will be developed extensively in chapter 12, where Paul will elaborate the body of Christ metaphor: "you are the body of Christ and individually members of it" (12:27). But the foundation is laid here: the physical bodies of believers are Christ's members.

The question that follows is constructed to provoke horror: aras oun ta mele tou Christou poieso pornes mele ("shall I then take the members of Christ and make them members of a prostitute?"). The verb airo ("to take, to take up, to lift, to remove") followed by poieo ("to make, to create, to produce") describes the act of detaching Christ's members and re-attaching them to a prostitute (porne). The word porne is the feminine form of pornos and designates a woman who sells sexual access to her body. In Corinth, a major commercial city with a significant transient population, prostitution was widely available and socially tolerated among pagans.

The response: me genoito ("may it never be!" or "let it not happen!" — literally "may it not come to be"). This is Paul's strongest formula of rejection, used throughout his letters to deny conclusions that would follow from a faulty premise (Romans 3:4, 6, 31; 6:2, 15; 7:7, 13; 9:14; 11:1, 11; Galatians 2:17; 3:21). The phrase expresses not merely disagreement but revulsion at the very idea.

"Or do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body with her? For, as it is written, 'The two will become one flesh'" (6:16).

Paul grounds the horror in Scripture. The verb kollao ("to join, to glue, to cement, to unite, to cling to") in the passive (kollomenos) means "the one who is joined/united/glued to." In the LXX, the word describes close attachment: Deuteronomy 10:20, "you shall fear the LORD your God; you shall serve him and hold fast to him" (auto kollethese); Ruth 1:14, Ruth "clung to" (ekolleethe) Naomi. The verb carries a sense of permanent adhesion, not casual contact. The one who is joined to a prostitute hen soma estin ("is one body") — the sexual act creates a union, a one-body reality.

The scriptural authority is Genesis 2:24 (LXX): esontai hoi dyo eis sarka mian ("the two shall become one flesh"). Paul introduces it with gephsin gar ("for it says" or "for [Scripture] says"). Genesis 2:24 in its original context describes the marriage bond: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." The "one flesh" (sarx mia) language describes the organic union that sexual intercourse creates. Paul's application is direct: if the marriage union makes two people one flesh, then sexual union with a prostitute likewise makes two people one flesh. The Genesis principle operates regardless of the relational context. The one-flesh union is not an optional metaphysical upgrade available only within marriage; it is an inherent consequence of sexual intercourse itself. To unite with a prostitute is therefore to create a one-flesh bond between Christ's member and that prostitute — to drag Christ's own body into a union with porneia.

"But he who is joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him" (6:17).

The parallel structure is deliberate: ho kollomenos to kyrio hen pneuma estin ("the one who is joined/united to the Lord is one spirit"). The contrast between verse 16 and verse 17 is between hen soma ("one body") and hen pneuma ("one spirit"). Union with a prostitute creates a body-union; union with the Lord creates a spirit-union. The two unions are incompatible. One cannot simultaneously be one spirit with the Lord and one body with a prostitute. The claim is not that the body is irrelevant but precisely the opposite: because the body is a member of Christ (v. 15), what the body does matters for the spirit-union with the Lord. The body and the spirit are not separable compartments; they are aspects of a single person. To defile the body is to assault the spirit-union.

The verb kollao is the same in both verses. The one who is "joined" to the Lord is joined with the same kind of adhesion as the one who is "joined" to a prostitute. Both unions are real. Both are consequential. They cannot coexist.

Verses 18-20: Flight from Porneia and the Body as Temple

"Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body" (6:18).

The imperative pheugete ten porneian ("flee from sexual immorality") is urgent and unqualified. The verb pheugo ("to flee, to run away from, to escape") describes not resistance but flight — not standing to fight but running in the opposite direction. The same verb describes Joseph's response to Potiphar's wife in Genesis 39:12 (LXX): ephygen kai exelthen exo ("he fled and went outside"). The command recognizes that porneia is not a temptation to be negotiated with but a danger to be escaped. The present imperative (pheugete) indicates that this is to be an ongoing pattern: keep fleeing, make it your habit.

The next statement — pan hamartema ho ean poiese anthropos ektos tou somatos estin, ho de porneoun eis to idion soma hamartanei ("every sin that a person commits is outside the body, but the one who commits sexual immorality sins against his own body") — is debated. Some scholars read the first half as another Corinthian slogan: "every sin a person commits is outside the body" would be the Corinthians' argument that bodily actions do not affect one's spiritual standing. Paul would then counter: "but the one who commits sexual immorality sins against his own body." Whether or not the first half is a slogan, Paul's own point is clear: porneia is a sin with a unique relationship to the body. Other sins may use the body as an instrument, but porneia turns the body itself — the member of Christ, the temple of the Spirit — into the site of the violation. The body is not merely the tool by which the sin is committed; the body is the victim of the sin. The sexually immoral person sins eis to idion soma ("against his own body") — the preposition eis ("into, against, toward") indicates that the sin's damage is directed inward, against the very body that belongs to Christ and will be raised by God's power.

"Or do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God?" (6:19a).

This is the sixth ouk oidate in the chapter. The claim extends the temple metaphor from 3:16-17. There, the community as a whole was identified as naos theou ("God's temple") in which God's Spirit dwells. Here, the individual believer's body (to soma hymon, "your body" — though the plural hymon includes the whole community) is naos tou hagiou pneumatos ("a temple of the Holy Spirit"). The word naos is the same: the inner sanctuary, the dwelling place of the divine presence. The Holy Spirit (to hagion pneuma) is specified as tou en hymin ("who is in you") — the Spirit resides within the believer's body, as God's presence once resided in the temple's inner sanctuary.

The phrase hou echete apo theou ("whom you have from God") identifies the Spirit as a gift received from God. The verb echete ("you have") is present tense — the Spirit is currently possessed, an ongoing reality. The preposition apo ("from") identifies the source: the Spirit comes from God. The Spirit is not the community's own possession by right; it is a gift from God that establishes the body as God's dwelling place.

The implications of the temple identification are far-reaching. In 3:16-17, Paul warned that anyone who destroys God's temple will be destroyed by God. Now the individual body is also identified as God's temple. The logic of 3:17 applies: to defile or destroy what is holy — what God has made his dwelling place — is to invite God's corresponding action. The holiness of the temple is not negotiable, whether the temple is the community (3:16) or the individual body (6:19).

"You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (6:19b-20).

Ouk este heautoon ("you are not your own") — the negative claim of self-ownership. The Corinthian slogan "all things are lawful for me" (v. 12) implied autonomous self-determination: I have the right to do as I please with my own body. Paul denies the premise. You do not own yourself. The language of purchase — egorasthete gar times ("for you were bought with a price") — draws from the marketplace. The verb agorazo ("to buy, to purchase, to acquire in the marketplace") and the noun time ("price, value, cost") describe a commercial transaction. Something of value was paid, and as a result, ownership transferred. The Corinthians were acquired; they now belong to the one who purchased them.

The word agorazo appears in the LXX in various commercial contexts, but its most theologically significant usage connects to redemption. In Leviticus 25:47-55, the laws of redemption provide for a kinsman to "buy back" (a form of redemption language) a family member who has sold himself into slavery. The redeemed person's freedom was purchased; the redeemer paid the price. The concept of God as Israel's redeemer (go'el) — the one who buys back what has been lost or enslaved — runs throughout the prophets: Isaiah 43:1, "I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine"; Isaiah 44:22-23, "I have redeemed you... for the LORD has redeemed Jacob"; Isaiah 52:3, "you were sold for nothing, and you shall be redeemed without money." Paul does not specify what the "price" (time) was in this passage, but the context of the letter — "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (5:7) — points to Christ's death as the payment. The community was purchased by the death of Christ. Their bodies belong to the one who bought them.

The consequence: doxasate de ton theon en to somati hymon ("therefore glorify God in your body"). The verb doxazo ("to glorify, to honor, to magnify") is the aorist imperative — a command for decisive, concrete action. The preposition en ("in, with, by means of") followed by to somati hymon ("your body") indicates that the body is the instrument and location of glorification. Glorifying God is not a purely internal, spiritual activity; it takes place in and through the physical body. The body that is Christ's member, the temple of the Holy Spirit, the possession purchased with a price, must be used for its owner's glory. The command closes the argument by uniting every thread: the body belongs to the Lord (v. 13), the body is a member of Christ (v. 15), the body is united to the Lord in spirit (v. 17), the body is the Spirit's temple (v. 19), the body was purchased (v. 20). Therefore, use the body for the purpose for which it was purchased: the glory of God.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 6 carries forward and completes the argument about community identity and boundaries that Paul began in chapter 5. The chapter has two major sections, but they are not unrelated subjects. They are two manifestations of the same disease: the community does not understand what it is, what it was purchased for, and how its members must conduct themselves in light of that identity.

The first section (vv. 1-11) addresses the judicial failure. The community that should judge its own members (5:12) is instead submitting its disputes to pagan courts (6:1-6). Paul's argument escalates through three levels: (1) You should handle these disputes internally, because you are destined to judge the world and angels (vv. 1-6). (2) You should not have lawsuits at all, because you should be willing to absorb wrong rather than litigate against brothers (v. 7). (3) In fact, you are the ones committing the wrong, and wronging your own brothers at that (v. 8). The escalation exposes not merely a procedural error (wrong venue) but a moral failure (the community is perpetrating injustice). The vice catalogue of verses 9-10 widens the lens: the behaviors that exclude from God's kingdom include the very things some Corinthians are doing (wronging and defrauding brothers, v. 8). The reminder in verse 11 — "such were some of you, but you were washed, sanctified, justified" — establishes the stakes. The transformation was real. To revert is to undo what God did.

The second section (vv. 12-20) addresses the sexual failure. The Corinthian slogan "all things are lawful" (v. 12) expresses a theology of freedom without accountability — a distortion of Paul's own teaching about freedom from the law. Paul counters the slogan with three qualifications: not all things are beneficial (v. 12a), freedom exercised without restraint becomes slavery (v. 12b), and the body is destined for resurrection, not for disposal (vv. 13-14). The body is a member of Christ (v. 15); sexual union with a prostitute creates a one-flesh bond that drags Christ's member into porneia (vv. 15-16). The body is the Spirit's temple (v. 19); defiling it is defiling God's dwelling place. The body was purchased at a price (v. 20); using it for porneia is misusing what belongs to another.

The two sections converge on a single point: the Corinthians' bodies and communal life are not their own to dispose of as they see fit. They belong to God, who purchased them through Christ and indwells them through the Spirit. Their behavior — judicial, sexual, social — must reflect this ownership. The chapter thus extends the holiness argument of 3:16-17 (the community is God's temple and must not be destroyed) and 5:6-8 (the Passover community must purge the leaven) into two specific areas of Corinthian failure: their use of pagan courts and their tolerance of sexual immorality.

The phrase ouk oidate ("do you not know?") appears six times in this chapter (vv. 2, 3, 9, 15, 16, 19), more than in any other chapter of the letter. The repetition is significant. Paul is not introducing new doctrine. He is calling the Corinthians back to what they already know — truths they have received, teachings they have been given, realities about their own identity that they are systematically failing to live out. The Corinthian problem is not ignorance but inconsistency. They know what they are; they do not act on what they know. They know they are saints destined to judge the world (v. 2), yet they cannot settle petty disputes among themselves. They know they are washed, sanctified, and justified (v. 11), yet they practice the behaviors of the unwashed. They know their bodies are Christ's members and the Spirit's temple (vv. 15, 19), yet they join those bodies to prostitutes.

The transition to chapter 7 is prepared by the final verses of chapter 6. The body's belonging to God, the significance of sexual conduct, the proper use of the body — these themes generate the questions about marriage and celibacy that the Corinthians raised in their letter and that Paul will address beginning in 7:1. The porneia that Paul has been addressing in chapters 5-6 becomes the background against which Paul's counsel about marriage — "because of the temptation to sexual immorality, each man should have his own wife and each woman her own husband" (7:2) — should be read. Marriage is not merely a social arrangement; it is part of the framework by which the community maintains the holiness of the body that belongs to God.

The argument across chapters 5-6 can be summarized as a single sustained claim: the community's identity as God's holy people, purchased by Christ, indwelt by the Spirit, and destined for resurrection and cosmic judgment, requires the community both to exercise internal discipline (chapter 5, handling the incestuous man; 6:1-8, handling disputes internally) and to maintain the holiness of its members' bodies (5:1-5, expelling the man whose sexual conduct defiles the community; 6:12-20, refusing to join Christ's members to prostitutes). The community cannot be God's temple and simultaneously harbor what defiles God's temple. The community cannot be the body of Christ and simultaneously unite Christ's members to porneia. The community cannot be the assembly of the saints and simultaneously behave as the adikoi who will not inherit the kingdom. What the community is determines what the community must do — and what it must not tolerate.
