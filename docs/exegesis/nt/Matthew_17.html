Matthew 17

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 16): The Pharisees and Sadducees — united for the first time in the document — demanded "a sign from heaven" (16:1). Jesus refused, calling them a "wicked and adulterous generation" and pointing to "the sign of Jonah" (16:4). He then warned the disciples to beware the "leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:6), which the disciples initially misunderstood as being about bread. Jesus rebuked their failure to remember the two wilderness feedings (the five loaves for the five thousand with twelve baskets remaining, the seven loaves for the four thousand with seven baskets remaining), and the disciples finally grasped that "leaven" referred to the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees (16:12). At Caesarea Philippi, Peter confessed Jesus as "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16), and Jesus called this a revelation from "my Father who is in heaven" (16:17). Jesus then spoke of building his ekklesia on "this rock," gave Peter the keys of the kingdom, and for the first time revealed his coming death and resurrection in Jerusalem (16:21). When Peter rebuked him for speaking of suffering, Jesus responded with devastating force: "Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance (skandalon) to me. For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man" (16:23). He then taught that discipleship requires self-denial, cross-bearing, and willingness to lose one's life for his sake (16:24-26). He concluded with two statements that bear directly on chapter 17: "For the Son of Man is going to come with his angels in the glory of his Father, and then he will repay each person according to what he has done" (16:27), and "Truly, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" (16:28).

What comes after (Matthew 18): The disciples approach Jesus with the question, "Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" (18:1). Jesus calls a child into their midst and declares that unless they turn and become like children, they will not enter the kingdom (18:3). He warns severely against causing "one of these little ones who believe in me" to stumble (skandalisthe, 18:6) — better to have a millstone tied around one's neck. He extends the warning: if your hand or foot or eye causes you to stumble, cut it off, "for it is better for you to enter life" maimed than to be cast whole into the fire (18:8-9). The parable of the lost sheep follows (18:10-14), then the process for addressing sin within the community (18:15-20), then Peter's question about how many times to forgive (18:21-22), and finally the parable of the unforgiving servant (18:23-35), which ends with the master delivering the unforgiving servant to the torturers "until he should pay all his debt" and Jesus's warning: "So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart" (18:35). Chapter 18 is the fourth major discourse in the document, focused entirely on life within the community of disciples — humility, care for the vulnerable, accountability, and the non-negotiable demand of forgiveness.

How chapter 17 connects them: Chapter 16 ended with Peter's confession, the first passion prediction, and the promise that some standing there would see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom. Chapter 17 opens six days later with exactly that: three disciples see Jesus transfigured in glory on a mountain, with Moses and Elijah beside him and the Father's voice confirming his identity. The transfiguration is the fulfillment of 16:28, but the chapter immediately descends from the mountaintop into the valley of failed discipleship, demonic affliction, and a second passion prediction. The movement is from glory to suffering to the practical questions of communal life (chapter 18). The pattern mirrors Jesus's own trajectory: he is the glorious Son whom the Father affirms, and he is the one who must suffer and die. The disciples must hold both realities together. Their failure to heal the demon-possessed boy (17:14-20) exposes how far they are from doing so. Their grief at the second passion prediction (17:23) shows they still cannot integrate glory and suffering. The temple tax episode (17:24-27) then introduces the question of the community's relationship to Israel's institutions — a question that chapter 18 will develop into a full discourse on what the kingdom community looks like from the inside. Chapter 17 is the hinge between the revelation of who Jesus is (chapters 14-16) and the instruction on how the community formed around him must live (chapter 18).

The Transfiguration: Glory on the Mountain (vv. 1-8)

"And after six days (meth' hemeras hex) Jesus took with him Peter and James and John his brother, and led them up a high mountain by themselves (kat' idian)."

The temporal marker "after six days" is unusual in this document. The author rarely specifies exact intervals. The phrase connects directly to 16:28, the promise that some standing there would see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom. Six days have passed; now the promise is fulfilled. But the phrase also carries a deeper resonance in Israel's scripture. In Exodus 24:15-16, when Moses went up Mount Sinai, "the cloud covered the mountain. The glory of the LORD dwelt on Mount Sinai, and the cloud covered it six days. And on the seventh day he called to Moses out of the midst of the cloud." The parallel is precise: six days, a high mountain, a cloud, a divine voice. The author is presenting the transfiguration as a Sinai event — a theophany in which God's glory is revealed on a mountain after a six-day interval.

Jesus takes three disciples: Peter, James, and John. These three form an inner circle within the twelve, present at moments of particular revelation or intensity. The phrase "by themselves" (kat' idian) marks this as a private disclosure, not a public sign. The pattern is consistent with the parables discourse (chapter 13), where deeper understanding was given to the disciples privately while the crowds received only parables. The transfiguration is not for the multitudes; it is for those already committed to following Jesus.

"And he was transfigured before them (metemorphothe emprosthen auton), and his face shone like the sun (elampsen to prosopon autou hos ho helios), and his clothes became white as light (ta de himatia autou egeneto leuka hos to phos)."

The verb metamorphoo (to transform, to change form) describes a change that is visible and external but originates from within. The shining face echoes Moses's experience on Sinai: when Moses came down from the mountain after speaking with God, "the skin of his face shone" (Exodus 34:29-35, LXX: dedoxastai he opsis tou prosopou autou — "the appearance of his face was glorified"). Moses's face reflected borrowed glory; it shone because he had been in God's presence. Jesus's face shines with its own radiance — "like the sun." The simile recalls the author's language in 13:43, where "the righteous will shine like the sun (eklampousin hos ho helios) in the kingdom of their Father." What the righteous will be at the consummation, Jesus is now. The transfiguration is a proleptic disclosure: the glory that belongs to the end of the age breaks through into the present moment.

The whiteness of the garments — "white as light" (leuka hos to phos) — draws on the imagery of divine and angelic appearance throughout Israel's scriptures. In Daniel 7:9, the Ancient of Days is described: "his clothing was white as snow." The angel at the empty tomb in this document will wear garments whose "appearance was like lightning, and his clothing white as snow" (28:3). White garments signify the heavenly realm, the sphere of God's own glory. Jesus's transformation does not add something foreign to him; it reveals what is already true of his nature but has been hidden by his ordinary human appearance.

"And behold, there appeared to them Moses and Elijah (Mouses kai Elias), talking with him (syllalountes met' autou)."

The appearance of Moses and Elijah together is laden with significance for a first-century Jewish audience. Both figures have specific connections to the mountain, to divine revelation, and to eschatological expectation.

Moses received the Torah on Sinai. He is the mediator of the covenant, the one through whom God gave Israel its law and identity. His death is unique in Israel's story: God himself buried Moses, and "no one knows the place of his burial to this day" (Deuteronomy 34:5-6). The mystery surrounding his burial generated speculation about his continued existence and future role. More importantly for this text, Moses is the one who prophesied: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you, from your brothers — it is to him you shall listen" (Deuteronomy 18:15). The divine voice in the transfiguration will echo this text directly.

Elijah is the great prophet of the northern kingdom who confronted Baal worship, experienced his own theophany at Horeb (1 Kings 19:8-18 — the same mountain as Sinai), and departed this life not by death but by ascension in a chariot of fire (2 Kings 2:11). Malachi 4:5 (Hebrew 3:23) promised: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes." Elijah's return was a standard Jewish expectation for the end of days, and the disciples will raise this very issue in verses 10-13.

Moses and Elijah represent the Torah and the Prophets — the entirety of Israel's scriptures. Their presence with Jesus on the mountain is a statement about fulfillment: the one to whom the Law and the Prophets pointed is here, and they are "talking with him" (syllalountes) — the conversation is between equals, not between a disciple and his teachers. Jesus is not receiving instruction from Moses and Elijah; he is in dialogue with them as the one they anticipated.

"And Peter said to Jesus, 'Lord, it is good that we are here (kalon estin hemas hode einai). If you wish, I will make three tents here (treis skenas), one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah.'"

Peter's response is inadequate, though not absurd. The word "tents" (skenai) resonates with the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot), during which Israel dwelt in booths to commemorate the wilderness wandering and God's sheltering presence (Leviticus 23:42-43). The feast also carried eschatological overtones: Zechariah 14:16-19 envisions the nations going up to Jerusalem to celebrate Sukkot in the messianic age. Peter may be attempting to prolong the moment of glory, to build structures that will house the divine presence permanently. But his suggestion reveals a misunderstanding. He places Jesus alongside Moses and Elijah as one of three figures of comparable status — "one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah." This leveling is precisely what the divine voice will correct.

Peter addresses Jesus as "Lord" (kyrie) — the same title the Canaanite woman used (15:22, 25, 27), the same the disciples used when the boat was sinking (8:25, 14:28, 30). But his suggestion shows that even with the right title, he does not yet grasp the full reality. His impulse is to contain the glory, to build something, to manage the theophany. The mountain is not a place to build; it is a place to hear.

"He was still speaking when, behold, a bright cloud (nephele photeina) overshadowed them (epeskiasen autous), and a voice from the cloud said, 'This is my beloved Son (houtos estin ho huios mou ho agapetos), with whom I am well pleased (en ho eudokesa); listen to him (akouete autou).'"

The cloud (nephele) is the cloud of God's presence — the same cloud that covered Sinai (Exodus 24:15-18), that filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34-38), that guided Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21-22), and that filled Solomon's temple at its dedication (1 Kings 8:10-11). The adjective "bright" (photeina) distinguishes this from a natural cloud; it is luminous, a cloud of light. The verb "overshadowed" (epeskiasen) echoes the LXX of Exodus 40:35, where the cloud "overshadowed" (epeskiazen) the tent of meeting and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle. The same verb appeared at the annunciation in Luke 1:35 ("the power of the Most High will overshadow you"), and its usage here marks the mountain as a place where God's presence has descended in the fullest sense.

The voice from the cloud speaks three declarations.

First: "This is my beloved Son" (houtos estin ho huios mou ho agapetos). These are the same words spoken at Jesus's baptism (3:17), with one difference: the baptism voice said "this is" (houtos estin) in the third person, addressing the audience rather than Jesus directly. The declaration is a citation of Psalm 2:7, the royal enthronement psalm: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you." But the addition of "beloved" (agapetos) evokes Genesis 22:2, where God told Abraham, "Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love (hon egapesas in the LXX)" — the beloved son who was to be offered as a sacrifice. The echo is ominous: Jesus has just predicted his death (16:21), and the Father's identification of him as "the beloved Son" carries the resonance of Isaac on Moriah.

Second: "with whom I am well pleased" (en ho eudokesa). This echoes Isaiah 42:1: "Behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights." The Servant of Isaiah 42 is the one who will bring justice to the nations, who will not break a bruised reed or quench a smoldering wick — the same text the author cited at length at 12:17-21 after Jesus withdrew from the Pharisees' murder plot. The Father's pleasure in the Son is not merely affection but the affirmation that Jesus is the Isaianic Servant whose mission includes both glory and suffering.

Third: "listen to him" (akouete autou). This is new — it was not part of the baptism declaration. It comes from Deuteronomy 18:15: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers — it is to him you shall listen (autou akousesthe in the LXX)." Moses prophesied a successor — a prophet like himself who would speak God's words and whom the people must obey. The Father's command on the mountain identifies Jesus as that prophet. The command also corrects Peter's suggestion: Peter wanted to build tents for three figures of equal standing; the Father says there is one to whom they must listen. Moses and Elijah are witnesses, but Jesus is the one the Law and the Prophets were pointing toward. The imperative "listen to him" has particular force given that Jesus has just spoken of his coming death and Peter has refused to listen (16:22-23). The Father's command is: listen to him — even when he speaks of suffering.

"When the disciples heard this, they fell on their faces (epesan epi prosopon auton) and were terrified (ephobethesan sphodra). But Jesus came and touched them (hepsato auton), saying, 'Rise, and have no fear (egerthete kai me phobeisthe).' And when they lifted up their eyes, they saw no one but Jesus only (oudsena eidon ei me auton Iesoun monon)."

The prostration and terror are the standard human response to theophany in Israel's scripture. When Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD, "I fell on my face" (Ezekiel 1:28). When Daniel encountered the angelic figure, "I fell on my face in deep sleep with my face to the ground" (Daniel 10:9). The disciples' response confirms that what they have experienced is a genuine encounter with divine glory, not a vision or dream.

Jesus's response — "Rise and have no fear" — echoes the reassurance given in theophanic encounters (Daniel 10:12, "Fear not, Daniel"; compare also the angel's words at 28:5, "Do not be afraid"). The verb "touched" (hepsato) is physical, embodied; it is the same verb used when Jesus touched lepers (8:3), the eyes of the blind (9:29, 20:34), and the bier of the dead man's son (Luke 7:14 parallel). The touch brings them from prostration back to their feet, from terror to composure.

When they look up, they see "Jesus only" (Iesoun monon). Moses and Elijah have departed. The bright cloud is gone. The voice has spoken. What remains is Jesus — alone, undivided, without peers. The visual reduction from three figures to one enacts what the Father's voice declared: Jesus is the one to whom they must attend. The Law and the Prophets have done their work; they have pointed to him and now they withdraw, leaving "Jesus only."

The Question About Elijah (vv. 9-13)

"And as they were coming down the mountain, Jesus commanded them, 'Tell no one the vision (to horama), until the Son of Man is raised from the dead (heos hou ho huios tou anthropou ek nekron egerthe).'"

Jesus calls the experience a "vision" (horama), a term used for divinely granted sight (Acts 9:10, 10:3, 16:9 — though those references are from a different author). The command to secrecy is consistent with the author's pattern: at 16:20, Jesus charged the disciples to tell no one that he was the Christ. The secrecy is temporary, bounded by the resurrection — "until the Son of Man is raised from the dead." The transfiguration will make sense only after the resurrection; before then, it would be misunderstood as a confirmation of the kind of messianic glory that does not pass through death. The disciples have just heard Jesus predict his death (16:21) and have been rebuked for resisting that prediction (16:22-23). The glory they have witnessed must not be separated from the suffering that lies ahead. Only the resurrection will integrate the two.

"And the disciples asked him, 'Then why do the scribes say that first Elijah must come (Elian dei elthein proton)?'"

The question arises naturally from what they have just seen. Elijah appeared on the mountain. The scribal teaching, based on Malachi 4:5-6 (Hebrew 3:23-24), held that Elijah would return before the great day of the LORD — "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes. And he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers, lest I come and strike the land with a decree of utter destruction." If the end-time events are in motion (and the transfiguration suggests they are), where does Elijah fit? Has his appearance on the mountain fulfilled the expectation? Or is something else going on?

"He answered, 'Elijah does come (Elias men erchetai), and he will restore all things (apokatastesai panta). But I tell you that Elijah has already come (Elias ede elthen), and they did not recognize him (ouk epegnosan auton), but did to him whatever they pleased (epoiesan en auto hosa ethelesean). So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands (houtos kai ho huios tou anthropou mellei paschein hyp' auton).'"

Jesus's answer operates on two levels. First, he affirms the scribal expectation: Elijah does come, and his role is to "restore all things" (apokatastesai panta — the verb apokathistemi carries the sense of returning something to its original condition, repairing what has been broken). The restoration Malachi envisioned — turning hearts, repairing the rupture between generations — is a real expectation.

Second, he declares that the expectation has already been fulfilled, but in a form the authorities did not recognize: "Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him." The failure of recognition is the same failure the author has been documenting since the beginning: the Pharisees did not recognize Jesus's authority (9:3, 34; 12:24), the people of Nazareth did not recognize the prophet among them (13:57), Herod misidentified Jesus as the risen Baptist (14:2). Now the scribes have missed Elijah as well. The pattern of non-recognition extends even to the forerunner.

"They did to him whatever they pleased" refers to John the Baptist's imprisonment and execution, narrated at 14:3-12. Herod arrested John, and Herodias arranged his beheading at a banquet. The phrase "whatever they pleased" (hosa ethelesean) underscores the arbitrary violence: John's death was a matter of the powerful doing what they wanted with God's messenger. The verb thelein (to will, to wish) carries the weight of human will exercised against God's purposes.

The statement "So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands" draws a direct parallel between the fate of the forerunner and the fate of the one he preceded. If Elijah (John) was not recognized and was killed, the Son of Man will likewise not be recognized and will likewise suffer. The fates are linked because the rejection is the same rejection. The people who did to Elijah "whatever they pleased" are the same people who will do the same to the Son of Man. The logic of the author's narrative is relentless: rejection of the messenger entails rejection of the one the messenger announces.

"Then the disciples understood (tote synekan hoi mathetai) that he was speaking to them of John the Baptist."

The verb syniemi (to understand, to put together) has been a key term in the document. The disciples' understanding here is partial but real — they grasp that John is the Elijah figure. What they have not yet grasped is the full implication: if John's fate prefigures Jesus's, then the suffering Jesus predicted at 16:21 is not optional but inevitable. The forerunner suffered; the one he announced will suffer likewise. The transfiguration's glory and the coming suffering are not alternatives but two aspects of the same identity.

The Disciples' Failure and the Healing of the Boy (vv. 14-21)

"And when they came to the crowd, a man came up to him and, kneeling before him (gonypeton auton), said, 'Lord, have mercy on my son (kyrie, eleeson mou ton huion), for he has seizures (selenizetai) and he suffers terribly. For often he falls into the fire, and often into the water. And I brought him to your disciples, and they could not heal him (ouk edynethesan auton therapeusai).'"

The descent from the mountain into the crowd presents a stark contrast. On the mountain: glory, the divine voice, Moses and Elijah, shining garments. At the foot of the mountain: a father's anguish, a boy seized by demonic forces, and the disciples' impotence. The juxtaposition is the author's way of insisting that the transfiguration's glory does not exempt the community from the realities of suffering and spiritual struggle. The mountain experience must be carried into the valley.

The man kneels (gonypeteo — the same verb used by the leper at 8:2 in some manuscripts, and by the Canaanite woman's posture of worship at 15:25). His address — "Lord, have mercy on my son" (kyrie, eleeson) — uses the same language the Canaanite woman used (15:22). The verb eleeo (to have mercy) again connects to the Hosea 6:6 principle that governs this document's understanding of what God desires.

The boy's condition is described as seleniazetai — literally, "moonstruck," from selene (moon). The term was used for epileptic-like seizures, which popular belief associated with the phases of the moon. The author does not editorialize on the medical nature of the condition; the description of falling into fire and water indicates violent, life-threatening episodes. The affliction is attributed in verse 18 to a demon, consistent with the document's framework in which physical affliction and spiritual oppression overlap.

The critical detail is the final statement: "I brought him to your disciples, and they could not heal him." Jesus had given the twelve authority over unclean spirits and diseases at 10:1 — "he gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal every disease and every affliction." They had been commissioned and equipped. Yet they could not (ouk edynethesan) heal this boy. The failure is not a failure of commissioning but a failure in the exercise of what they had been given.

"And Jesus answered, 'O faithless and twisted generation (o genea apistos kai diestrammen), how long am I to be with you? How long am I to bear with you (heos pote anecsomai hymon)? Bring him here to me.'"

Jesus's exclamation is addressed broadly — not only to the disciples but to the "generation" (genea) as a whole. The words "faithless and twisted" (apistos kai diestrammen) are drawn directly from the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy 32. In Deuteronomy 32:5, Moses says of Israel: "They have dealt corruptly with him; they are no longer his children because they are blemished; they are a crooked and twisted generation" (genea skolia kai diestrammen in the LXX). In Deuteronomy 32:20, God says: "I will hide my face from them; I will see what their end will be, for they are a perverse generation (genea exestrammen), children in whom is no faithfulness (pistis)." The Song of Moses is the great indictment of Israel's unfaithfulness despite God's provision — God fed them in the wilderness, bore them on eagles' wings, gave them the land, and they turned to other gods.

By echoing this text, Jesus places the present generation's faithlessness within the long pattern of Israel's history. The disciples' inability to heal the boy is not an isolated failure; it is symptomatic of the same faithlessness that has characterized Israel's response to God across centuries. The exclamation "how long am I to be with you" carries a note of exasperation that reflects not merely human frustration but the divine patience straining under the weight of persistent unbelief — the same note sounded in the wilderness narratives (Numbers 14:11, "How long will this people despise me? And how long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs that I have done among them?").

"And Jesus rebuked him (epetimesen auto), and the demon came out of him, and the boy was healed instantly (apo tes horas ekeines)."

The verb epitimao (to rebuke, to command with authority) is the same verb used when Jesus stilled the storm (8:26) and when he rebuked demons elsewhere (Mark 1:25, 9:25). The phrase "from that hour" (apo tes horas ekeines) marks the healing as immediate and complete — the same temporal marker used for the centurion's servant (8:13) and the Canaanite woman's daughter (15:28). Where the disciples failed, Jesus succeeds without difficulty. The contrast is the point.

"Then the disciples came to Jesus privately (kat' idian) and said, 'Why could we not cast it out (dia ti hemeis ouk edynethemen ekbalein auto)?'"

The disciples approach privately — the same pattern from the parables discourse (13:10, 36) and the defilement discussion (15:12, 15). Their question is direct: why did we fail? They had been given authority (10:1). They presumably attempted to exercise it. Something was missing.

"He said to them, 'Because of your little faith (dia ten oligopistian hymon). For truly, I say to you, if you have faith like a grain of mustard seed (hos kokkon sinapeoos), you will say to this mountain, "Move from here to there," and it will move, and nothing will be impossible for you (ouden adynatesei hymin).'"

The word oligopistia (little faith) is the noun form of the adjective oligopistos (of little faith) that Jesus has used repeatedly in the document. At 6:30, he addressed those anxious about food and clothing: "O you of little faith." At 8:26, he rebuked the disciples in the storm: "Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?" At 14:31, he caught the sinking Peter: "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" At 16:8, he addressed the disciples who worried about bread: "O you of little faith." The term is not a designation of no faith but of insufficient faith — faith that exists but does not extend far enough, trust that begins but falters. The disciples are not accused of unbelief (apistia) but of littleness (oligo-) in their belief. They are not on the side of the Pharisees (who actively reject) but they are not yet where they need to be.

The mustard seed image appeared in the parable at 13:31-32, where it described the kingdom's growth from smallest beginnings to shelter for the birds. Here it is applied to faith. The mustard seed was proverbially the smallest seed known in Palestinian agriculture; Jesus says faith even that small would be sufficient to move a mountain. The statement is hyperbolic — "moving mountains" was a common Jewish metaphor for accomplishing what seems impossible (compare the rabbinic expression "uprooter of mountains" for a great teacher who resolved seemingly intractable problems). The point is not that the disciples need massive faith; they need real faith, genuine trust, even in small quantity. The problem is not the size of their faith but the quality. A tiny amount of authentic, undivided trust in God's power is sufficient for anything; a large amount of divided, anxious, doubting pseudo-faith accomplishes nothing.

The promise "nothing will be impossible for you" (ouden adynatesei hymin) echoes the angel's words to Mary in Luke 1:37 ("nothing will be impossible with God," though this is a different author) and more directly echoes God's own statement in Genesis 18:14: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" The impossible becomes possible not because of human capacity but because faith connects the person to the God for whom nothing is impossible. The disciples' failure was not a failure of technique or authority but a failure to trust the God whose authority they had been given to exercise.

The Second Passion Prediction (vv. 22-23)

"As they were gathering in Galilee (systrephomenon de auton en te Galilaia), Jesus said to them, 'The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men (ho huios tou anthropou mellei paradidosthai eis cheiras anthropon), and they will kill him (kai apoktenousin auton), and he will be raised on the third day (kai te trite hemera egerthesetai).' And they were greatly distressed (elypethesan sphodra)."

This is the second of three passion predictions in the document (the first at 16:21, the third at 20:17-19). Each prediction is more specific than the last. The first named the destination (Jerusalem), the agents (elders, chief priests, scribes), and the outcome (suffering, death, resurrection). This second prediction is shorter and more concentrated, focusing on three elements: betrayal, death, and resurrection.

The verb "delivered" (paradidosthai) is significant. It is a present passive infinitive — the Son of Man "is about to be handed over." The passive voice suggests that the delivering is done by someone, but Jesus does not specify who. In the broader context of the document, the verb paradidomi carries multiple layers: Judas will "hand over" (paradidomi) Jesus to the authorities (26:15-16, 21, 23-25, 45-46), but God also "hands over" the Son in a deeper sense (compare Romans 8:32, though this is a different author's vocabulary). The word "hands" (cheiras) creates a wordplay: the Son of Man is handed over into the hands of men. The Son of Man (huios tou anthropou) is delivered to men (anthropon) — the title contains the very word for those who will kill him. The one who bears the designation "son of man" is given over to "men" who will destroy him.

"They will kill him" (apoktenousin auton) — the verb is in the active voice, future tense. The killing is a deliberate human act that will happen.

"He will be raised on the third day" (te trite hemera egerthesetai) — the passive "will be raised" (egerthesetai) identifies God as the one who raises. Human hands kill; God's hand raises. The third day carries resonance from Hosea 6:2: "After two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up, that we may live before him." In its original context, Hosea 6:2 referred to Israel's restoration after judgment. The author's application to Jesus's resurrection suggests that Jesus's death and raising are understood as Israel's own death and restoration enacted in a single representative figure.

The disciples' response — "they were greatly distressed" (elypethesan sphodra) — is different from their response to the first prediction, where Peter rebuked Jesus outright (16:22). They have learned enough not to rebuke, but they have not yet learned to accept. The word lypeo (to grieve, to distress) appears elsewhere in the document at 19:22 (the rich young man went away grieving) and 26:22 (the disciples were "very sorrowful" when Jesus announced one of them would betray him). Grief is the response of those who hear unwelcome truth and cannot yet integrate it. They do not dispute the prediction this time, but they cannot receive it with understanding. The grief is genuine — these are people who love their teacher and are being told he will die. But the grief also reveals that they have focused on the death and not heard the resurrection.

The Temple Tax: Sons and Strangers (vv. 24-27)

"When they came to Capernaum, the collectors of the two-drachma tax (hoi ta didrachma lambanontes) came up to Peter and said, 'Does your teacher not pay the two-drachma tax (to didrachmon)?' He said, 'Yes.'"

The didrachma (two-drachma coin) was the Greek equivalent of the half-shekel, the annual tax every Jewish male over twenty was required to pay for the maintenance of the Temple. The biblical basis is Exodus 30:11-16: "Each one who is numbered in the census shall give this: half a shekel according to the shekel of the sanctuary... the rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less." The tax was understood as atonement money (kopher, a ransom for the soul) and supported the daily sacrifices and Temple upkeep. By the first century, it was collected annually, typically around the month of Adar (roughly February-March), through local collectors before being sent to Jerusalem. The tax was a marker of Jewish identity and covenant membership — paying it signified belonging to Israel and supporting its central institution.

The collectors' question — "Does your teacher not pay the tax?" — is phrased to expect a positive answer (the Greek ou construction anticipates "yes"). The question itself may carry suspicion: is this teacher loyal to the Temple? Does he participate in Israel's institutional life? Given the conflicts with the Pharisees and scribes throughout the document, the question has an edge. Peter's answer — "Yes" — is immediate and unreflective. He has not consulted Jesus.

"And when he came into the house, Jesus spoke to him first (proephthasen auton ho Iesous), saying, 'What do you think, Simon? From whom do kings of the earth take toll or tax (tele e kensos)? From their sons (apo ton huion auton) or from others (apo ton allotrion)?'"

Jesus initiates the conversation — the verb prophthanein means to anticipate, to speak before the other has a chance. Jesus knows what has happened without being told, a detail consistent with the author's presentation of Jesus's knowledge throughout the document (9:4, 12:25, 22:18). He poses a question from political reality. Kings impose taxes on their subjects, not on their own children. The royal household is exempt. The sons of the king are free (eleutheroi).

"And when he said, 'From others,' Jesus said to him, 'Then the sons are free (ara ge eleutheroi eisin hoi huioi).'"

The logic is simple but its implications are profound. The Temple is the house of God — Jesus has called it "my Father's house" (implied in the document, explicit in John 2:16, and consistent with 12:6 where Jesus declared himself "greater than the Temple"). If God is the king, and Jesus is the Son (as the voice from the cloud just declared), then Jesus is free from the Temple tax. He is not a subject paying tribute to a foreign sovereign; he is the Son in his Father's house. The tax is for others (allotrioi — strangers, those who are not family members), not for sons.

The plural "sons" (huioi) is significant. Jesus does not say "the Son is free" but "the sons are free." The plural extends the exemption beyond himself. If the disciples are also sons of the Father (and the document has established this: 5:9, 45; 6:9; 7:11; 13:38), then they too are, in principle, free from the obligation. The passage makes a claim about the identity of Jesus's community: they are the royal household, children of the king, not subject-taxpayers in a foreign realm.

"However, not to give offense to them (hina de me skandalisomen autous), go to the sea and cast a hook and take the first fish that comes up, and when you open its mouth you will find a shekel (statera). Take that and give it to them for me and for you."

The word "offense" (skandalizo) has been a central term in the document. At 5:29-30, it described what causes a person to sin. At 13:21, it described falling away under pressure. At 16:23, Jesus called Peter a skandalon. At 18:6-9 (immediately after this episode), Jesus will warn severely against causing little ones to skandalizesthai. Here, Jesus voluntarily limits his own freedom to avoid skandalizing — causing others to stumble. The sons are free, but freedom is exercised with regard for others. This is the same logic that governs the author's broader ethic: the kingdom's values include self-limitation for the sake of those who might be harmed by the exercise of legitimate rights.

The instruction to find a coin in a fish's mouth is unique in the document. A stater (shekel) was worth four drachmas — exactly two didrachmas, sufficient for two persons ("for me and for you"). The provision is miraculous but understated. The author does not narrate the outcome — whether Peter went, whether the fish appeared, whether the coin was found. The instruction simply stands as given. The provision comes from the sea, from God's creation, through an act that requires Peter's obedience (casting the hook) but ultimately depends on divine arrangement. The pattern is consistent with the feeding miracles: human action (casting a net, distributing loaves) combined with divine abundance.

The episode's placement after the second passion prediction is theologically loaded. Jesus has just spoken of being "delivered into the hands of men" — of surrendering himself to human power. Now he voluntarily submits to a human institution (the Temple tax) despite being free from it. The Son who will be handed over to death is the same Son who voluntarily pays a tax he does not owe. Both acts involve the willing self-limitation of one who has the right not to submit. Both enact the principle stated at 16:24-25: the one who would save his life must lose it.

The provision for Peter — "for me and for you" — also establishes a pattern. Jesus does not pay only for himself; he covers Peter as well. The one coin pays for both. This small detail anticipates the larger reality that Jesus's death will provide for others what they cannot provide for themselves. The atoning function of the Temple tax (Exodus 30:15-16, "ransom for your lives") is now met by a provision that comes through Jesus.

The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. It fulfills the promise of 16:28 while redefining its meaning. "Some standing here will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" is fulfilled six days later on the mountain, where Peter, James, and John see Jesus transfigured in glory. But the glory is temporary, bounded by a command to secrecy, and immediately followed by suffering and failure. The kingdom's coming is not a single event of unambiguous triumph; it is a reality that breaks through in moments of revelation but must be lived out through the valley of suffering, demonic opposition, and the disciples' own inadequacy. The author prevents the reader from separating glory from suffering.

2. It establishes Jesus's identity through the Father's direct declaration. The voice from the cloud speaks over Jesus for the second time in the document (the first at 3:17). The declaration combines Psalm 2 (royal sonship), Isaiah 42 (the Servant in whom God delights), and Deuteronomy 18 (the prophet like Moses whom the people must hear). The addition of "listen to him" is a direct rebuke to Peter's attempt to place Jesus alongside Moses and Elijah as one among equals and to Peter's earlier refusal to listen when Jesus spoke of suffering (16:22). The Father's command demands that the community receive all of Jesus's teaching — including the predictions of death and resurrection that the disciples resist.

3. It links the forerunner's fate to the Messiah's fate. The Elijah discussion (vv. 9-13) establishes a pattern: the forerunner was not recognized and was killed; the Messiah will likewise not be recognized and will suffer. The non-recognition is not an accident but part of the pattern of Israel's history — the same pattern narrated in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32), the same failure that led Israel to reject the prophets generation after generation. What "they" did to John, "they" will do to Jesus. The fates are parallel because the rejection is the same rejection.

4. It exposes the disciples' persistent failure of faith. The contrast between the mountaintop glory and the valley-floor failure is devastating. The same Peter who witnessed the transfiguration belongs to the community that could not heal a demon-possessed boy. Jesus's diagnosis is "little faith" (oligopistia) — not unbelief but insufficient trust. The problem is not the absence of authority (they were given it at 10:1) but the failure to exercise it with genuine reliance on God. The mustard seed saying insists that even tiny genuine faith is sufficient; the disciples' large but divided faith is not. This characterization continues the pattern from chapters 14-16: the disciples are learning but have not yet arrived.

5. It grounds Jesus's teaching about self-denial in his own practice. The temple tax episode shows Jesus voluntarily submitting to an obligation he does not owe, for the sake of not causing offense. He is the Son, free from the Father's tax, yet he pays it. This enacts the principle of 16:24-25 — losing one's life (surrendering one's rights, one's freedom, one's prerogatives) for the sake of others. The connection to the coming passion is implicit but clear: the Son who freely pays a tax he does not owe will freely give a life he does not owe. Both are acts of voluntary self-limitation by the one who has the greatest right to claim exemption.

6. It presents Moses and Elijah as witnesses who defer to Jesus. The Law (Moses) and the Prophets (Elijah) appear alongside Jesus not as equals but as conversation partners who confirm his identity and then withdraw, leaving "Jesus only." The author's hermeneutical principle — the old scriptures point to and are fulfilled in Jesus (5:17, "I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them") — is enacted visually on the mountain. The scribe trained for the kingdom brings out old and new (13:52), but the new has priority: "listen to him."

7. It uses the Sinai typology to position Jesus as greater than Moses. The six-day interval, the high mountain, the bright cloud, the divine voice, the shining face — every element of the transfiguration echoes Moses's experience on Sinai. But the differences are as important as the parallels. Moses's face reflected God's glory; Jesus's face radiates its own glory. Moses received the law from God; Jesus is the one God commands the people to hear. Moses is a servant in God's house (as later stated in Hebrews 3:5, though by a different author); Jesus is the Son over the house. The Sinai typology positions Jesus not as another Moses but as the one Moses anticipated — the prophet like Moses who is greater than Moses.

8. It transitions from the question of Jesus's identity to the question of community life. Chapters 14-16 established who Jesus is: the provider who feeds multitudes, the one who walks on water, the Christ and Son of the living God. The transfiguration is the climax of this identity revelation. But the chapter then turns immediately to questions that arise from that identity: how does the community exercise the authority it has been given (vv. 14-21)? How does it relate to the suffering its Lord has predicted (vv. 22-23)? How does it relate to the institutions of Israel (vv. 24-27)? These questions prepare for chapter 18, where the fourth discourse will address the internal life of the community directly — its humility, its care for the vulnerable, its discipline, and its forgiveness.
