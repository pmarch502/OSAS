Romans 4 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Romans 3): Paul's argument in chapter 3 moved through four stages. First (3:1-8), he addressed the objection that his leveling of Jew and Gentile in chapter 2 had rendered Jewish identity meaningless. He answered that the advantage of the Jew is real — they were entrusted with the oracles of God — but that this advantage does not grant immunity from judgment. God's faithfulness persists despite Israel's unfaithfulness. Second (3:9-20), Paul assembled a catena of scriptural quotations from the Psalms and Isaiah to demonstrate that Israel's own scriptures testify to universal sinfulness: "None is righteous, no, not one" (3:10). The catena concluded with the principle that "by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight, since through the law comes knowledge of sin" (3:20). Third (3:21-26), Paul revealed the solution: the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, through the faithfulness of Jesus Christ, available to all who believe without distinction, enacted through Christ's death as the hilasterion — the mercy seat where God's justice and mercy converge. Fourth (3:27-31), Paul drew the implications: boasting is excluded because no one earned their justification; the one God justifies both circumcised and uncircumcised through the same pistis; and the law is not overthrown but established.

The final two verses of chapter 3 are the immediate launching pad for chapter 4. Paul asserted that "God is one — who will justify the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith" (3:30). This is a claim about the unity of God's method: one God, one mechanism of justification for all. Then Paul confronted the objection this claim provokes: "Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (3:31). This claim — that justification by faith does not destroy the Torah but establishes it — demands a demonstration. Paul cannot simply assert it; he must show it from the Torah itself. The Torah must testify that justification by faith was always God's method. Romans 4 is that demonstration.

What the author argues immediately after (Romans 5): Paul opens chapter 5 with "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:1). The word "therefore" (oun) signals that what follows is a consequence of what has been established. The justification by faith demonstrated in chapter 4 through Abraham now yields its fruit: peace with God, access to grace, hope in the glory of God. Paul then develops the present experience of the justified community — rejoicing even in suffering, because suffering produces endurance, endurance produces tested character, tested character produces hope, "and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (5:5). The argument then moves to the ground of this confidence: "while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly" (5:6). The justified are those who were previously ungodly (the same word, asebes, used in 4:5 for those whom God justifies). From 5:12 onward, Paul shifts to the universal scope, comparing Adam and Christ: through one man's trespass, condemnation spread to all; through one man's act of righteousness, justification and life spread to all. The law's role in this drama is specified: "the law came in to increase the trespass" (5:20) — confirming the diagnostic function Paul identified in 3:20.

How chapter 4 connects these: Romans 3 concluded with two claims that demand support. First, the claim that the one God justifies both circumcised and uncircumcised by the same means (3:30). Second, the claim that faith upholds the law rather than overthrowing it (3:31). Romans 5 begins from the accomplished fact that "we have been justified by faith" (5:1) and builds upon it. Chapter 4 is the bridge: it provides the scriptural proof that both claims in 3:30-31 are true by examining the Torah's own testimony about Abraham. Abraham was reckoned righteous by faith before he was circumcised (proving that the one God justifies the uncircumcised by faith) and while he was uncircumcised he received circumcision as a seal of the righteousness he already had by faith (proving that circumcision is established, not overthrown, when faith is primary). David confirms the same principle from a different angle: blessedness consists in having sins not counted, apart from works (Psalm 32). The promise to Abraham that he would be heir of the world came through the righteousness of faith, not through the law (which would not exist for four more centuries). And the faith Abraham exercised — trusting God to bring life from death — corresponds to the faith of those who trust the God who raised Jesus from the dead (4:24). Chapter 4 thus demonstrates that the principle announced in chapter 3 was always God's method, and it prepares the ground for chapter 5's declaration that the justified have peace with God and stand in grace.

The chapter's argument is continuous and single-threaded: it opens with a question about Abraham (4:1), answers it from Genesis 15:6 (4:3), reinforces it from Psalm 32 (4:6-8), establishes the timing (4:9-12), identifies the scope of the promise (4:13-17), describes the content of Abraham's faith (4:18-22), and applies the whole to present believers (4:23-25). There is no change of subject. Every verse advances the same argument: that the Torah itself testifies to justification by faith apart from works.

Verses 1-3: The Question and the Torah's Answer

"What then shall we say was gained by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh?" (4:1).

The question ti oun eroumen is a diatribe formula Paul uses throughout Romans to introduce the next stage of an argument (cf. 6:1, 7:7, 8:31, 9:14, 9:30). The question is not idle; it arises directly from 3:27-31. Paul has just excluded boasting (3:27), declared that justification is by faith apart from works of the law (3:28), affirmed that the one God justifies both circumcised and uncircumcised by faith (3:30), and claimed that faith upholds the law (3:31). The natural test case is Abraham. If Paul's principle is true, it must be visible in Abraham, because Abraham is the foundation of the entire covenant. If Abraham was justified by works, then Paul's teaching is an innovation that contradicts the Torah. If Abraham was justified by faith, then Paul's teaching is the Torah's own testimony.

The phrase ton propatora hemon kata sarka ("our forefather according to the flesh") identifies Abraham as the physical ancestor of the Jewish people. The pronoun hemon ("our") includes Paul himself among Abraham's descendants. The qualifier kata sarka ("according to the flesh") parallels Paul's usage in 1:3, where Christ was "of the seed of David according to the flesh." The phrase limits the claim to physical descent: Abraham is "our" forefather in the sphere of flesh — biological lineage. The limitation is deliberate; Paul is about to argue that Abraham is father of more than just his physical descendants.

The word heurenai (the aorist infinitive of heurisko, "to find, to gain, to obtain, to discover") asks what Abraham "found" or "gained" — what was the result, the outcome, the acquisition of Abraham's experience? The question assumes that something happened to Abraham in his relationship with God, and asks what it was and how it was obtained.

"For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God" (4:2).

Paul introduces a conditional: ei gar Abraam ex ergon edikaiothe, echei kauchema — "for if Abraham was justified from works, he has a boast." The verb edikaiothe is the aorist passive of dikaioo ("to justify, to declare righteous") — the same verb that has dominated the argument since 2:13. The phrase ex ergon ("from works") specifies the hypothetical basis: works, deeds, performances. If this were how Abraham was justified, he would have kauchema — "a boast, ground for boasting, something to boast about." This word is cognate with kauchesis ("boasting"), which Paul excluded in 3:27. The logic is: if Abraham earned his righteous standing through his deeds, then he has grounds for self-congratulation.

But Paul immediately qualifies: all' ou pros theon — "but not before God." The phrase pros theon ("toward God, in relation to God, in God's presence") limits the boast. Even if Abraham could boast before human beings on the basis of his works, he could not boast before God. The reason is not stated here explicitly but is implied by what follows: before God, Abraham's standing was not based on works but on faith.

"For what does the Scripture say? 'Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness'" (4:3).

The appeal is to scripture: ti gar he graphe legei — "for what does the scripture say?" The word graphe ("scripture, writing") in Paul's usage carries the authority of God's own speech. What scripture says, God says. Paul's answer to the question of 4:1 is not his own reasoning but a direct quotation from the Torah itself.

The quotation is Genesis 15:6 (LXX): episteusen de Abraam to theo, kai elogisthe auto eis dikaiosynen — "And Abram believed God, and it was reckoned to him for righteousness." This verse will govern the entire chapter. Every subsequent argument Paul makes is an exposition of this single sentence.

The verb episteusen (aorist of pisteuo, "to believe, to trust, to rely upon, to be faithful") describes Abraham's response to God's promise. The context in Genesis 15 is specific: God had promised Abraham that his own son, not Eliezer of Damascus, would be his heir (Genesis 15:4). God brought Abraham outside and said, "Look toward heaven, and number the stars, if you are able to number them... So shall your offspring be" (Genesis 15:5). Abraham was childless. Sarah was barren. The promise was physically impossible. And Abraham "believed God" — he trusted that what God said would come to pass.

The verb elogisthe (aorist passive of logizomai, "to reckon, to count, to calculate, to credit") is the word that will dominate Romans 4. It appears eleven times in this chapter (4:3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23, 24). The verb logizomai is a bookkeeping term: it means to enter something into an account, to credit a ledger, to calculate a sum. In the LXX, it is used for calculation and reckoning in various senses: numbering (Genesis 31:15, "he has reckoned us as strangers"), planning (Psalm 40:17 LXX, "the Lord will reckon concerning me"), accounting (Leviticus 7:18, "it shall not be reckoned to the one who offers it"). The passive voice (elogisthe) indicates that someone other than Abraham performed the reckoning. The implied agent is God: God reckoned, God credited, God entered into the account.

What was reckoned is stated with the preposition eis: eis dikaiosynen — "for righteousness," "as righteousness," "unto righteousness." The preposition eis indicates purpose, result, or equivalence: the faith was credited as if it were righteousness, or credited resulting in a status of righteousness. Abraham's act of trusting God's promise was the item God entered into the ledger, and the value God assigned to it was "righteousness" — right standing, covenant faithfulness, the status of being dikaios.

The critical observation for Paul's argument is what Genesis 15:6 does not say. It does not say Abraham obeyed God and it was counted to him as righteousness. It does not say Abraham was circumcised and it was counted to him as righteousness. It does not say Abraham offered sacrifices and it was counted to him as righteousness. What was reckoned as righteousness was believing — trusting God's word when the visible evidence contradicted it.

Verses 4-5: The Logic of Wages versus Gift

"Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due" (4:4).

Paul introduces an analogy from ordinary economic life to illuminate the logic of logizomai ("reckoning"). The analogy is between two different ways something can be credited to a person's account.

The first scenario: to de ergazomeno ho misthos ou logizetai kata charin alla kata opheilema — "to the one who works, the wage is not reckoned according to grace but according to debt." The word ergazomeno (present participle of ergazomai, "to work, to labor, to perform") describes the worker. The word misthos ("wage, pay, reward, recompense") is what the worker receives. The verb logizetai ("is reckoned, is credited") is the same verb from Genesis 15:6. The wage is "reckoned" — entered into the account. But it is reckoned kata opheilema ("according to debt, according to what is owed") rather than kata charin ("according to grace, according to gift, according to favor"). The distinction is between obligation and generosity. When someone works, the employer owes the wage. The payment is not a gift but a debt. The worker has a claim; the employer has an obligation. The reckoning is a matter of justice, not generosity.

The analogy targets the concept of "works" in the justification question. If Abraham was justified ex ergon ("from works"), then his righteousness was a wage owed to him by God — something Abraham earned and God was obligated to credit. This would mean God was in Abraham's debt, which is theologically impossible. If God owes anyone anything, then God is not sovereign, and human beings have a claim on God's justice. Paul's analogy shows that the "works" model turns the God-human relationship into an employer-employee transaction where God is the debtor.

"And to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness" (4:5).

The second scenario is the one that matches Abraham: to de me ergazomeno, pisteuonti de epi ton dikaiounta ton asebe, logizetai he pistis autou eis dikaiosynen — "to the one who does not work but trusts the one who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned for righteousness."

The phrase me ergazomeno ("not working") does not mean laziness or moral indifference. In context, it means not attempting to earn right standing through performance. The contrast is with the "worker" of verse 4: the person who does not work is the person who does not try to put God in his debt through deeds.

The phrase pisteuonti epi ton dikaiounta ton asebe ("believing on the one who justifies the ungodly") is remarkable. The participle pisteuonti ("believing, trusting") describes the person's posture. The object of trust is not an abstract principle but a specific actor: ton dikaiounta ton asebe — "the one who justifies the ungodly." The word asebe (accusative singular of asebes, "ungodly, impious, irreverent") is a strong word. In the LXX, the asebes is the opposite of the dikaios: the wicked person, the one who has no regard for God, the one who stands opposed to the righteous. Proverbs 17:15 (LXX) warns: "He who justifies the wicked (ton asebe) and he who condemns the righteous — both of them are an abomination to the LORD." This verse from Proverbs declares that justifying the ungodly is an abomination. Paul is asserting that God does exactly what Proverbs calls an abomination when done by human judges. The difference is that God can do what human judges cannot: God can justify the ungodly because God has addressed the sin through the hilasterion (3:25). A human judge who acquits the guilty is corrupt; God who acquits the guilty is righteous, because God has dealt with the guilt through Christ's death. The hilasterion of 3:25 is the necessary precondition for the justification of the ungodly in 4:5.

The phrase logizetai he pistis autou eis dikaiosynen ("his faith is reckoned for righteousness") repeats the language of Genesis 15:6 almost verbatim. This is the second scenario: instead of a wage owed, a gift given. The faith is reckoned as righteousness not because faith is a meritorious work that earns the credit, but because God freely assigns the value "righteousness" to the act of trusting. The reckoning is kata charin ("according to grace"), not kata opheilema ("according to debt").

The connection to Paul's broader argument is precise. In 3:24, the justified are described as dikaioumenoi dorean te autou chariti — "being justified freely by his grace." The dorean ("freely, as a gift") of 3:24 corresponds to the kata charin ("according to grace") of 4:4. The gift-character of justification, stated in chapter 3 as a theological principle, is now demonstrated in chapter 4 from the Torah's own account of Abraham.

Verses 6-8: David's Confirmation from Psalm 32

"Just as David also speaks of the blessing of the one to whom God counts righteousness apart from works" (4:6).

The word kathaper ("just as, even as, in the same way that") introduces a second scriptural witness. Paul has just established from Genesis 15:6 that Abraham was reckoned righteous through faith, not through works. Now he summons David to confirm the same principle from a different angle.

The phrase ton makarismon tou anthropou ho ho theos logizetai dikaiosynen choris ergon ("the blessing of the person to whom God reckons righteousness apart from works") describes what David is about to say. The word makarismos ("blessing, declaration of blessedness, pronouncement of happiness") is derived from makarios ("blessed, happy, fortunate") — the same word that opens the Beatitudes in Matthew 5:3-12 and that characterizes Psalm 1:1 (LXX: makarios aner, "blessed is the man"). David is about to pronounce someone blessed. The person pronounced blessed is the one to whom God logizetai dikaiosynen choris ergon — "reckons righteousness apart from works." The verb logizetai is again the Genesis 15:6 word. The phrase choris ergon ("apart from works, without works") reprises the choris nomou ("apart from the law") of 3:21 and the choris ergon nomou ("apart from works of the law") of 3:28. Paul's terminology is consistent: righteousness comes apart from works, apart from law, through faith.

The choice of David as second witness is not arbitrary. David is the king, the archetypal ruler of Israel, the man "after God's own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14, LXX: kata ten kardian autou). If both Abraham (the father of the covenant) and David (the king of the covenant) testify to the same principle, the principle is established on the testimony of Israel's two foundational figures.

"'Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin'" (4:7-8).

Paul quotes Psalm 32:1-2 (LXX 31:1-2): makarioi hon aphethasan hai anomiai kai hon epekalyphthesan hai hamartiai. makarios aner hou ou me logisetai kyrios hamartian — "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds were forgiven and whose sins were covered. Blessed is the man whose sin the Lord will certainly not reckon."

The psalm is attributed to David. Its context in the Psalter is significant. Psalm 32 is a psalm of confession and thanksgiving for forgiveness. The psalmist describes the misery of unconfessed sin (32:3-4: "When I kept silent, my bones wasted away... my strength was dried up") and the relief of confession (32:5: "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin"). The psalm's blessedness is specifically the blessedness of the forgiven sinner — not the blessedness of the sinless person, but the blessedness of the one whose sins have been addressed by God.

Three words describe what God does with the sinner's offenses:

First, aphethasan (aorist passive of aphiemi, "to release, to send away, to forgive"): the lawless deeds (anomiai, "lawlessnesses" — acts that violate the nomos, the law) are "forgiven" — released, sent away, dismissed. The image is of a debt cancelled or a prisoner freed. The forgiveness is passive: God is the implied agent who does the releasing.

Second, epekalyphthesan (aorist passive of epikalypto, "to cover over, to conceal, to hide"): the sins (hamartiai, "missings of the mark, sins") are "covered" — hidden from sight, no longer visible. In the Day of Atonement ritual, the blood sprinkled on the hilasterion "covered" the sins of the people, rendering them no longer visible to the divine gaze. The word choice connects to the hilasterion imagery of 3:25: the mercy seat was the place where sins were covered.

Third, ou me logisetai (aorist subjunctive of logizomai with the double negative ou me, the strongest possible negation): the Lord will "certainly not reckon" the sin. The verb is the same logizomai of Genesis 15:6 and the rest of Romans 4, but now in the negative. In Genesis 15:6, God reckoned faith as righteousness — a positive entry in the ledger. In Psalm 32:2, God refuses to reckon sin — a refusal to make a negative entry. The two actions are complementary: God credits righteousness where there was none, and God refuses to debit sin where there was much. The bookkeeping metaphor is complete: the ledger shows a credit of righteousness (Genesis 15:6) and an absence of debit for sin (Psalm 32:2).

Paul's use of Psalm 32 is strategic. Abraham's case in Genesis 15:6 demonstrated the positive side: faith reckoned as righteousness. David's psalm demonstrates the negative side: sin not reckoned. Together they establish that "righteousness apart from works" has two dimensions: the crediting of righteousness to those who trust, and the non-crediting of sin to those who are forgiven. Both come from Torah and both describe the same principle Paul has announced in 3:21-26.

David's own history makes the psalm's testimony more powerful. David was not a man without sin. His adultery with Bathsheba, his murder of Uriah (2 Samuel 11), and his subsequent repentance (2 Samuel 12, Psalm 51) are among the most prominent narratives in Israel's scriptures. If David — the sinning, repenting, forgiven king — declares blessed the one whose sin is not reckoned, he speaks from personal experience. His blessedness was not earned through sinlessness but received through forgiveness. This is Paul's point: David's blessedness, like Abraham's righteousness, was not the result of performance but of divine grace.

Verses 9-12: The Timing of Abraham's Circumcision

"Is this blessing then only for the circumcised, or also for the uncircumcised? For we say that faith was counted to Abraham as righteousness" (4:9).

The question ho makarismos oun houtos epi ten peritomen e kai epi ten akrobystian ("is this blessing upon the circumcision or also upon the uncircumcision?") returns to the central question of 3:29-30: is God the God of Jews only, or also of Gentiles? The "blessing" (makarismos) is the one David pronounced in Psalm 32 — the blessing of having sins not reckoned, of being credited with righteousness apart from works. Paul asks: is this blessing restricted to the circumcised (Jews), or does it extend to the uncircumcised (Gentiles)?

Paul sets up the question by restating the foundation: legomen gar, elogisthe to Abraam he pistis eis dikaiosynen — "for we say that faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness." The verb legomen ("we say") appeals to the shared ground established in verse 3: Paul and his interlocutor agree on what Genesis 15:6 says. The question is about the implications of that shared text.

"How then was it counted to him? Was it before or after he had been circumcised? It was not after, but before he was circumcised" (4:10).

The question pos oun elogisthe ("how then was it reckoned?") is not about the mechanism but about the timing. The word pos ("how") here means "under what circumstances" or "in what condition." The specific circumstance Paul asks about is circumcision: en peritome onti e en akrobystia ("while being in circumcision or in uncircumcision?"). Was Abraham circumcised or uncircumcised at the time God reckoned his faith as righteousness?

Paul's answer is emphatic: ouk en peritome all' en akrobystia — "not in circumcision but in uncircumcision." This is a chronological fact verifiable from the Torah's own narrative. Genesis 15:6, where faith was reckoned as righteousness, occurs in the narrative before Genesis 17, where circumcision was instituted. The narrative sequence matters. Abraham was reckoned righteous in Genesis 15. Circumcision was commanded in Genesis 17. Between these two events, Genesis 16 records the birth of Ishmael, which itself took time (Abraham was 86 when Ishmael was born, Genesis 16:16, and 99 when circumcision was instituted, Genesis 17:1). Abraham was uncircumcised for at least thirteen years after being reckoned righteous. The reckoning of righteousness was not contingent on circumcision, because circumcision did not yet exist when the reckoning occurred.

This chronological argument is not Paul's invention. It is a straightforward reading of the Torah's own sequence. Paul is not reading against the text; he is reading the text in its own order. Any first-century reader who knew the sequence of Genesis would recognize the point: Genesis 15 comes before Genesis 17.

"He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised. The purpose was to make him the father of all who believe without being circumcised, so that righteousness would be counted to them as well" (4:11).

Paul now defines what circumcision was for Abraham: semeion elaben peritomes, sphragida tes dikaiosynes tes pisteos tes en te akrobystia — "he received a sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith that was in the uncircumcision." Two words define circumcision's function.

First, semeion ("sign, mark, indicator"). A sign points to something beyond itself. The sign is not the reality it signifies; it indicates, marks, or identifies the reality. In the LXX, the rainbow is called a semeion of God's covenant with Noah (Genesis 9:12-13, 17). The blood on the doorposts at Passover was a semeion (Exodus 12:13). Circumcision itself is described in Genesis 17:11 (LXX) as a semeion of the covenant: kai estai eis semeion diathekes — "and it shall be for a sign of the covenant." Paul is using the Torah's own language: circumcision is a sign.

Second, sphragis ("seal, stamp, mark of authenticity, mark of ownership"). A seal authenticates, confirms, and secures. In the ancient world, a seal pressed into wax or clay authenticated a document or marked ownership of property. Sealing indicates that the content is genuine and the authority behind it is real. The seal does not create the content; it confirms what already exists. Paul's language is precise: circumcision sealed the righteousness Abraham already possessed. It authenticated what was already true. The righteousness came through faith while Abraham was uncircumcised; circumcision came afterward as God's confirmation of that pre-existing righteousness.

The purpose clause (eis to einai auton patera panton ton pisteuonton di' akrobystias) explains God's design: "so that he might be the father of all who believe through uncircumcision." The phrase patera panton ("father of all") extends Abraham's paternity beyond the physical descendants kata sarka mentioned in 4:1. Abraham is father — not by biological descent but by spiritual precedent — of everyone who believes while uncircumcised. They follow the same pattern Abraham established: trusting God without the sign of circumcision, and being reckoned righteous on that basis. The purpose is eis to logisthenai kai autois ten dikaiosynen — "so that righteousness would be reckoned to them also." The reckoning that happened to Abraham in Genesis 15:6 is the paradigm for the reckoning that happens to uncircumcised believers. They receive the same credit Abraham received, in the same condition Abraham was in when he received it.

"And to make him the father of the circumcised who are not merely circumcised but who also walk in the footsteps of the faith that our father Abraham had before he was circumcised" (4:12).

Abraham is also father of the circumcised — but not of the circumcised merely because they are circumcised. The phrase tois ouk ek peritomes monon ("not from circumcision only") introduces a crucial qualifier. Being circumcised is not sufficient to claim Abraham as father. The circumcised who are truly Abraham's children are those alla kai tois stoichousin tois ichnesin tes en akrobystia pisteos tou patros hemon Abraam — "who also walk in the footsteps of the faith of our father Abraham that was in uncircumcision."

The verb stoicheo ("to walk in line, to march in step, to follow in order") carries the image of soldiers marching in formation, each placing his feet in the tracks of the one ahead. The word ichnesin (dative plural of ichnos, "footprint, track, trace") makes the image concrete: the circumcised who are truly Abraham's children are those who step into the footprints of Abraham's faith. And Paul specifies which faith: tes en akrobystia pisteos — "the faith that was in uncircumcision." Abraham's paradigmatic faith was the faith he exercised while uncircumcised. The circumcised Jew who claims Abraham as father must walk in the footsteps of uncircumcised Abraham's faith. Circumcision alone does not make one Abraham's child; walking in Abraham's faith does.

The structure of verses 11-12 creates a careful architecture:

  • Abraham is father of the uncircumcised who believe (v. 11) — so that righteousness is reckoned to them.
  • Abraham is father of the circumcised who also walk in his pre-circumcision faith (v. 12) — not merely by circumcision but by faith.

The effect is to subordinate circumcision to faith in both cases. The uncircumcised who believe are Abraham's children through faith. The circumcised who also believe are Abraham's children through faith. In both cases, the operative element is faith, not circumcision. Circumcision is a sign and seal that authenticates the faith that preceded it; it is not the basis of the relationship.

This argument directly supports 3:29-30: "Is God the God of Jews only? Is he not the God of Gentiles also? Yes, of Gentiles also, since God is one — who will justify the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith." The one God justifies both groups by the same means, and Abraham is the proof: he was justified by faith before circumcision, making him father of both uncircumcised believers and circumcised believers.

Verses 13-15: The Promise, the Law, and Their Incompatibility

"For the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith" (4:13).

Paul now shifts from the relationship between faith and circumcision to the relationship between faith and the law. The word gar ("for") connects this to the preceding argument: the reason Abraham is father of all who believe (vv. 11-12) is that the promise itself was grounded in faith, not in law.

The phrase he epangelia to Abraam e to spermati autou ("the promise to Abraham or to his seed") identifies the recipient of the promise: Abraham and his seed (sperma, "seed, offspring, descendant"). The content of the promise is specified: to kleronomon auton einai kosmou — "that he should be heir of the world." The word kleronomos ("heir, one who inherits, one who receives an allotment") in the LXX is connected to the concept of kleros ("lot, portion, inheritance"), which was used for Israel's inheritance of the land of Canaan (Deuteronomy 4:21, 12:9, 15:4). The word kosmos ("world, ordered creation, inhabited earth") extends the scope of the promise beyond the land of Canaan to the entire world.

This raises a question: where does the Torah promise Abraham that he would be "heir of the world"? The specific phrase does not appear in Genesis. The promises to Abraham in the Torah include: "I will make of you a great nation" (Genesis 12:2); "all the families of the earth shall be blessed in you" (Genesis 12:3); "to your offspring I give this land" (Genesis 15:18); "I have made you the father of a multitude of nations" (Genesis 17:5); "in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed" (Genesis 22:18). Paul appears to be reading the cumulative force of these promises: the land, the nations, the blessing of all families — and drawing the conclusion that Abraham's inheritance, when all these promises are read together, amounts to the world. This is not a novel reading; Sirach 44:21 (second century BCE) says of Abraham: "Therefore the Lord assured him with an oath that the nations would be blessed through his offspring, that he would multiply him like the dust of the earth and exalt his offspring like the stars, and give them an inheritance from sea to sea and from the Euphrates to the ends of the earth." The expansion of the land promise to encompass the earth is already attested in pre-Pauline Jewish interpretation. Jubilees 22:14 similarly speaks of Abraham's descendants inheriting "from sea to sea."

The critical claim is the mechanism: ou dia nomou... alla dia dikaiosynes pisteos — "not through the law... but through the righteousness of faith." The promise did not come to Abraham through the law because the law did not yet exist. The Torah was given at Sinai through Moses, approximately four centuries after Abraham. Paul states this chronological fact in Galatians 3:17: "The law, which came 430 years afterward, does not annul a covenant previously ratified by God." The promise was given and received in the sphere of dikaiosyne pisteos — "righteousness of faith," the same righteousness described in Genesis 15:6. The promise and the faith-righteousness belong to the same moment; the law belongs to a much later moment.

"For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be the heirs, faith is null and the promise is void" (4:14).

Paul states the consequence of the alternative: ei gar hoi ek nomou kleronomoi, kekenootai he pistis kai katergeitai he epangelia — "for if those from the law are heirs, the faith has been emptied and the promise has been rendered inoperative." The phrase hoi ek nomou ("those from the law, those whose identity is defined by the law") describes people who claim the inheritance on the basis of Torah-observance.

If this claim is valid, two things follow. First, kekenootai he pistis — "faith has been emptied." The verb kenoo (perfect passive, kekenootai) means "to empty, to make void, to render without content." If the inheritance comes through law, then faith is irrelevant, drained of its content and purpose. Why trust God's promise if the inheritance depends on legal performance? Second, katergeitai he epangelia — "the promise has been rendered inoperative." The verb katargeo (present passive, katergeitai) is the same word Paul used in 3:3 for the potential nullification of God's faithfulness. If the inheritance comes through law-observance rather than through promise, then the promise itself is cancelled. God's word to Abraham — the promise made before the law existed — would be retroactively voided by a condition imposed four centuries later.

Paul's logic is: the promise and the law cannot both be the basis of the inheritance, because they operate on different principles. The promise operates by faith and grace (4:16 will make this explicit). The law operates by performance and obligation. If the law determines who inherits, then the promise is redundant. But the promise came first and was the basis on which Abraham was reckoned righteous. Therefore the law cannot be the basis of inheritance without destroying the prior arrangement.

"For the law brings wrath, but where there is no law there is no transgression" (4:15).

Paul states the reason the law cannot serve as the basis of the promise: ho gar nomos orgen katergazetai — "for the law works wrath." The verb katergazomai ("to produce, to accomplish, to bring about, to work out") describes what the law actually does in practice. The law does not produce righteousness; it produces orge — "wrath, anger, divine indignation." This echoes 3:20: "through the law comes knowledge of sin." The law reveals sin, and sin under the law becomes transgression (parabasis, "stepping across, violation of a known boundary"), and transgression provokes wrath. The law turns sin into a conscious violation of a known standard, which makes it more culpable, not less.

The second clause explains: hou de ouk estin nomos oude parabasis — "where there is no law, neither is there transgression." The word parabasis ("transgression, violation, overstepping") is distinct from hamartia ("sin, missing the mark"). Sin exists apart from the law (as Paul will argue in 5:13: "sin indeed was in the world before the law was given"), but transgression — the conscious violation of a known commandment — requires a known commandment to violate. Where there is no law, there is no boundary to step across, and therefore no transgression in the technical sense. The law creates the category of transgression by establishing explicit commands that can be explicitly violated.

The argument is a reductio ad absurdum. If the inheritance depended on the law, then: the law brings wrath (because it reveals and multiplies transgression), and therefore the inheritance would come through wrath rather than through promise. But the very concept of "inheritance by promise" presupposes grace, not wrath. Wrath and promise work in opposite directions. The law cannot be the vehicle of the promise because the law produces the opposite of what the promise offers.

Verses 16-17: The Promise Rests on Grace, Abraham as Father of Many Nations

"That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring — not only to the adherent of the law but also to the one who shares the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all" (4:16).

The phrase dia touto ek pisteos ("therefore from faith") draws the conclusion from verses 13-15: the inheritance comes from faith. The reason is hina kata charin — "in order that it might be according to grace." The purpose clause (hina) reveals God's design: the inheritance was structured around faith rather than law so that it would operate by grace rather than by obligation. Grace and faith belong together, just as law and works belong together (4:4-5). If the inheritance came through law, it would be a wage owed (4:4). Because it comes through faith, it is a gift freely given (4:5).

The result of being grounded in grace: eis to einai bebaian ten epangelian panti to spermati — "so that the promise might be certain to all the seed." The word bebaia ("firm, secure, reliable, guaranteed") is a legal term used for the guarantee or confirmation of a contract. A bebaios agreement is one that cannot be broken, challenged, or revoked. The promise is guaranteed — made unshakeable — precisely because it rests on grace rather than on human performance. If it depended on human law-keeping, its certainty would be contingent on human obedience, which Paul has already shown to be universally deficient (3:9-20). Because it depends on God's grace, its certainty rests on God's faithfulness, which Paul has already shown to be unshakeable (3:3-4).

The scope of "all the seed" is then specified: ou to ek tou nomou monon alla kai to ek pisteos Abraam — "not to the one from the law only but also to the one from the faith of Abraham." Two groups constitute Abraham's seed: those "from the law" (Jewish believers who possess the Torah) and those "from the faith of Abraham" (Gentile believers who share Abraham's faith without possessing the Torah). Both groups inherit because the inheritance is from faith, not from law. If it were from law, only the first group could inherit. Because it is from faith, both groups inherit.

The verse concludes: hos estin pater panton hemon — "who is father of us all." The pronoun hemon ("of us") is inclusive: Paul, the Jewish believers, the Gentile believers in Rome — all of them together claim Abraham as father. This is not fatherhood kata sarka (4:1) but fatherhood through shared faith.

"As it is written, 'I have made you the father of many nations' — in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17).

The scriptural citation is Genesis 17:5 (LXX): hoti patera pollon ethnon tetheika se — "because I have set you as father of many nations." The word ethnon ("of nations") is the same word translated "Gentiles" throughout Paul's argument. When God told Abraham "I have made you father of many nations (ethnon)," God was — in Paul's reading — declaring that Abraham's fatherhood would extend to the ethnoi, the Gentiles, the nations beyond Israel. The promise was never restricted to one nation; it encompassed many nations from the beginning. Paul reads the "many nations" of Genesis 17:5 not merely as a prediction of biological descent (the Ishmaelites, the Edomites, and other peoples descended from Abraham) but as a declaration of spiritual fatherhood over all who share Abraham's faith.

Paul then describes the God in whom Abraham believed with two participial phrases that characterize God's distinctive power:

First, tou zoopoiountos tous nekrous — "the one who gives life to the dead." The verb zoopoieo ("to make alive, to give life, to vivify") appears in the LXX for God's power to give life: "the LORD kills and brings to life (zoopoieo); he brings down to Sheol and raises up" (1 Samuel 2:6, LXX). The phrase tous nekrous ("the dead ones") identifies the objects of this life-giving power. God gives life to the dead. In Abraham's immediate context, the "dead" are his own body ("as good as dead," 4:19) and Sarah's dead womb. The God Abraham trusted was the God who could bring life from biological death. For Paul's audience, the phrase also points to the resurrection of Jesus, which Paul will invoke explicitly in 4:24.

Second, kalountos ta me onta hos onta — "the one who calls the things that are not as though they are." The verb kaleo ("to call, to name, to summon into being") in the LXX is God's creative act: "God called (ekalesen) the light Day, and the darkness he called Night" (Genesis 1:5). God's calling is not merely naming but constituting — when God calls something, it becomes what God calls it. The phrase ta me onta ("the things that are not, the non-existent things") describes what does not yet exist. The phrase hos onta ("as being, as though they exist") describes how God treats them: God speaks of non-existent things as though they already exist, and by so speaking brings them into existence. This is the creative power of God's word: God's speech creates reality. When God told Abraham his offspring would be like the stars, those offspring did not yet exist. God spoke of them as existing. And they came into existence through God's promise and God's power.

These two descriptions of God — the one who gives life to the dead and the one who calls non-existent things into existence — define the kind of faith Abraham exercised. Abraham did not trust in a visible, verifiable reality. He trusted in a God whose power operates precisely where human capacity is exhausted: in death and in non-existence. Faith, as Paul describes it through Abraham, is trust in the God who creates something from nothing and life from death.

Verses 18-22: The Content of Abraham's Faith

"In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations, as he had been told, 'So shall your offspring be'" (4:18).

The phrase par' elpida ep' elpidi episteusen ("against hope, upon hope, he believed") is a compressed paradox. The first phrase par' elpida ("against hope, beyond hope, contrary to hope") acknowledges that from a human standpoint, there was no reason to hope. Abraham was old. Sarah was barren. The visible evidence pointed to impossibility. The second phrase ep' elpidi ("upon hope, in hope, resting on hope") describes the basis on which Abraham nevertheless believed: the hope grounded not in visible evidence but in God's word. Abraham believed against the evidence and upon the promise.

The content of his faith is specified: eis to genesthai auton patera pollon ethnon — "so that he might become the father of many nations." The clause references Genesis 17:5 again. Abraham believed that he would become what God said he would become: father of many nations. The quotation from Genesis 15:5 — houtos estai to sperma sou, "so shall your offspring be" — points back to the moment when God showed Abraham the stars and promised offspring as numerous. Abraham believed this when he had no child at all.

"He did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was as good as dead (since he was about a hundred years old), or when he considered the barrenness of Sarah's womb" (4:19).

The verb katanoeo ("to consider, to observe, to contemplate, to take careful note of") indicates that Abraham did not ignore the evidence. He considered (katenoesen) his own body (to heautou soma), which was ede nenekromenon ("already having been deadened, as good as dead"). The verb nekroo ("to put to death, to make dead, to render lifeless") in the perfect passive participle indicates a completed state: Abraham's body was in a condition of deadness, past the point of natural procreative capacity. He was hekatontaetes pou hyparchon — "being about a hundred years old." The number is from Genesis 17:17, where Abraham himself laughs and says, "Shall a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old?"

He also considered ten nekrosin tes metros Sarras — "the deadness of Sarah's womb." The word nekrosis ("deadness, dying, state of death") is cognate with nekroo and nekros. Sarah's womb is described with the same death-language as Abraham's body. Both participants in the promise — the father and the mother — were in a condition of biological death with respect to procreation.

Paul's point is that Abraham's faith was not naive or uninformed. He did not believe because he was unaware of the impossibility. He took careful note of the impossibility — his dead body, her dead womb — and believed anyway. His faith was not the absence of knowledge but trust in the face of knowledge. This connects directly to the description of God in verse 17: God "gives life to the dead." Abraham's body and Sarah's womb were dead, and Abraham trusted the God who gives life to the dead to give life to them.

"No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God, but he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God" (4:20).

The phrase eis de ten epangelian tou theou ou diekrithe te apistia — "toward the promise of God he did not waver in unbelief." The verb diakrino ("to judge between, to discriminate, to waver, to hesitate, to doubt") in the passive can mean "to be divided within oneself, to waver, to be in two minds." Abraham was not divided: he did not oscillate between trust and doubt. The word apistia ("unbelief, unfaithfulness, lack of trust") is the opposite of pistis. In 3:3, Paul used apistia for Israel's unfaithfulness and asked whether it would nullify God's faithfulness. Here, Abraham exemplifies the opposite of Israel's apistia: he exhibited pistis.

Instead of wavering, alla enedunamothe te pistei — "he was empowered by faith" or "he grew strong in faith." The verb endunamoo ("to strengthen, to empower, to make strong") is passive: Abraham was strengthened. The passive may imply God as the agent: God strengthened Abraham's faith. Faith is not merely a human decision; it is a capacity in which one can be strengthened by God.

The phrase dous doxan to theo — "giving glory to God" — describes the accompanying posture. The verb didomi doxan ("to give glory") is the fundamental act of worship: acknowledging God's greatness, power, and faithfulness. In Romans 1:21, the failure to give God glory was the beginning of the descent into idolatry: "they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him." Abraham's faith reverses this primordial failure. Where humanity in 1:21 refused to glorify God, Abraham glorified God. Where humanity exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images (1:23), Abraham looked at his dead body and Sarah's dead womb and gave glory to the living God. Faith as Paul describes it through Abraham is the antithesis of the idolatry described in Romans 1.

"Fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised" (4:21).

The word plerophoretheis (aorist passive participle of plerophoreo, "to fill completely, to fulfill, to be fully convinced, to be assured") describes the completeness of Abraham's conviction. The word carries the sense of being "filled full" — there was no gap in Abraham's conviction, no space left for doubt. The content of his conviction was hoti ho epengeltai dynatos estin kai poiesai — "that what he had promised, he was able also to do." Abraham's faith was not general optimism or abstract confidence; it was specific trust that God possessed the power (dynatos, "able, powerful, capable") to accomplish what he had specifically promised (epengeltai, from epangellomai, "to promise, to pledge"). Faith, in Paul's description, is confidence in God's ability to keep God's word.

"That is why his faith was 'counted to him as righteousness'" (4:22).

The word dio ("therefore, for this reason") draws the conclusion: because Abraham believed in this specific way — against hope, upon hope, considering the dead body and the dead womb, not wavering, fully convinced of God's power — his faith was reckoned (elogisthe) as righteousness (eis dikaiosynen). The quotation of Genesis 15:6 returns for the third time in the chapter (after 4:3 and 4:9). The repetition creates a frame: the chapter began with Genesis 15:6 (4:3), passed through the exposition, and returns to Genesis 15:6 (4:22) with the accumulated weight of everything argued between. The reader now understands what "Abraham believed God" entailed: not casual assent but costly trust in the face of impossibility, grounded in the character of the God who gives life to the dead.

Verses 23-25: The Application to Present Believers

"But the words 'it was counted to him' were not written for his sake alone" (4:23).

The phrase ouk egrathe de di' auton monon hoti elogisthe auto — "it was not written on account of him alone that it was reckoned to him." The verb egrathe (aorist passive of grapho, "to write") refers to the writing of Genesis 15:6 as scripture. The passive points to the divine authorship behind the human writing: the text was written (by God through the human author) not merely as a historical record of what happened to Abraham but as a paradigm for all who would come after.

The phrase di' auton monon ("on his account alone") excludes the possibility that Genesis 15:6 is merely biographical. It is not just Abraham's story. The text was written with a wider audience in view. This hermeneutical principle — that scripture's significance extends beyond its immediate historical subject — is fundamental to Paul's reading of the Torah. The Torah is not merely a record of the past but a pattern for the present and the future.

"But for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord" (4:24).

The phrase alla kai di' hemas — "but also on our account." The pronoun hemas ("us") includes Paul and his audience — the community of believers in Rome, both Jewish and Gentile. Genesis 15:6 was written for them. The reckoning described in that verse — faith counted as righteousness — is the paradigm for what happens to them.

The condition is specified: tois pisteuousin epi ton egeiranta Iesoun ton kyrion hemon ek nekron — "to those who believe on the one who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead." The structure mirrors Abraham's faith precisely:

Abraham believed in the God who gives life to the dead (4:17) — present believers believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead (4:24).

Abraham believed that God could bring life from his dead body and Sarah's dead womb — present believers believe that God brought life from the dead body of Jesus. The object of faith has the same structure: trust in God's power to overcome death. What was implicit in Abraham's faith (trust in the God who gives life to the dead) has become explicit in the faith of believers (trust in the God who raised Jesus). Abraham trusted a promise that pointed forward; believers trust an event that has already occurred. But both trust the same God exercising the same power: the power to bring life from death.

The verb egeiranta (aorist active participle of egeiro, "to raise, to awaken, to lift up") describes God's action: God raised Jesus. The aorist tense points to a specific past event: the resurrection. The title ton kyrion hemon ("our Lord") identifies Jesus with the kyrios — the word used in the LXX to translate the divine name YHWH. The possessive hemon ("our") claims Jesus as the Lord of the believing community.

"Who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:25).

The final verse of the chapter states the meaning of Jesus's death and resurrection in compressed, parallel clauses:

hos paredothe dia ta paraptomata hemon — "who was handed over on account of our trespasses." kai egerthe dia ten dikaiosin hemon — "and was raised on account of our justification."

The verb paredothe (aorist passive of paradidomi, "to hand over, to deliver up, to surrender") is the same verb used for God's "handing over" of humanity to the consequences of their sin in 1:24, 26, 28 (paredoken autous, "he gave them over"). In chapter 1, God handed humanity over to the consequences of their idolatry. In 4:25, God handed Jesus over to the consequences of humanity's sin. The same verb connects the two acts: the handing-over of 1:24-28 was judgment; the handing-over of 4:25 is redemption. God's response to human sin is not only wrath (the handing-over of chapter 1) but also grace (the handing-over of his Son).

The word paraptomata ("trespasses, false steps, transgressions") describes the offenses: dia ta paraptomata hemon ("on account of our trespasses"). The preposition dia with the accusative indicates cause or reason: Jesus was handed over because of our trespasses. His death addressed the sins that needed addressing.

The verb egerthe (aorist passive of egeiro, "to raise") describes the resurrection. The preposition dia with the accusative again indicates cause or reason: dia ten dikaiosin hemon ("on account of our justification"). The word dikaiosis ("justification, the act of declaring righteous, the enactment of right standing") appears only here and in Romans 5:18 in the entire New Testament. It is the noun form of the verb dikaioo that has dominated the argument. Jesus was raised on account of our justification — the resurrection is the event that accomplishes or certifies justification. If Christ was handed over for our sins (addressing the problem), he was raised for our justification (enacting the solution). The death deals with the sin; the resurrection establishes the new standing.

The parallelism is not a simple equation of death = forgiveness and resurrection = justification, as though these are separable events with separable effects. Rather, both clauses describe two aspects of the single saving event. The death and resurrection together constitute the ground of justification. The hilasterion of 3:25 (Christ's death as the mercy seat) and the resurrection of 4:25 are the two faces of the same act by which God justifies the ungodly (4:5).

This verse also creates the bridge to Romans 5. The word dikaiosis ("justification") appears next in 5:18: "one act of righteousness leads to justification (dikaiosin) and life for all." The theme of 4:25 — raised for our justification — flows directly into 5:1: "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God." The justification accomplished through Christ's death and resurrection (4:25) is the justification received by faith (5:1) that produces peace with God.

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Romans 4 is a sustained demonstration from the Torah that the principle announced in 3:21-31 — justification by faith apart from works of the law — was always God's method. The chapter is not a digression, an illustration, or a supplementary example. It is the proof. Paul claimed in 3:31 that "we uphold the law" by teaching justification by faith. Chapter 4 demonstrates this by showing that the law itself — the Torah's own narrative about Abraham and David — teaches justification by faith.

The chapter divides into five movements.

The first movement (4:1-5) poses the question and establishes the principle. What did Abraham gain? Paul quotes Genesis 15:6 — Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness — and draws out the logic: the reckoning was not a wage for work performed but a gift freely given. God justifies the ungodly, and faith — not works — is reckoned as righteousness.

The second movement (4:6-8) summons a second witness. David in Psalm 32 pronounces blessed the person whose sins are forgiven, covered, and not reckoned. This is the negative complement of Abraham's case: Abraham shows righteousness positively credited; David shows sin negatively not debited. Together they establish the full picture of "righteousness apart from works."

The third movement (4:9-12) addresses the question of circumcision. By attending to the Torah's own chronology, Paul demonstrates that Abraham was reckoned righteous before he was circumcised. Circumcision was a sign and seal of a pre-existing righteousness, not the cause of that righteousness. Abraham is therefore father of both uncircumcised believers (who share his pre-circumcision faith) and circumcised believers (who walk in the footsteps of his pre-circumcision faith). The wall between circumcised and uncircumcised is bypassed not by abolishing circumcision but by showing that faith preceded it and was the actual basis of righteousness.

The fourth movement (4:13-17) addresses the relationship between promise and law. The promise to Abraham that he would be heir of the world came through the righteousness of faith, not through the law (which would not exist for four centuries). If the inheritance depended on the law, the promise would be void and faith would be empty, because the law produces wrath (by revealing and multiplying transgression), not inheritance. The inheritance therefore depends on faith, so that it may rest on grace, so that it may be guaranteed to all Abraham's seed — both those from the law and those who share Abraham's faith. Abraham is father of many nations, as Genesis 17:5 declares.

The fifth movement (4:18-25) describes the content of Abraham's faith and applies it to the present. Abraham believed against hope, considered his dead body and Sarah's dead womb, and was fully convinced that God could do what God had promised. This faith was reckoned as righteousness. And the text recording this reckoning was written not for Abraham alone but for all who believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. Jesus was handed over for our trespasses and raised for our justification. The paradigm of Abraham's faith — trusting the God who gives life to the dead — finds its ultimate expression in faith in the God who raised Jesus.

The pronoun tracking across the chapter reveals Paul's rhetorical strategy. In 4:1, "our forefather" (hemon) identifies Abraham as the ancestor of Paul and his Jewish interlocutor. In 4:11-12, the scope expands: Abraham is father of "all who believe without being circumcised" and also of the circumcised who walk in his faith. In 4:16, Abraham is "father of us all" (pater panton hemon) — the "us" now includes the entire believing community, Jew and Gentile together. In 4:24-25, "us" (hemas, hemon) refers to the community who believes in the God who raised Jesus. The pronouns trace a progressive widening from Jewish ancestry to universal spiritual fatherhood. Abraham begins as "our forefather according to the flesh" (4:1) and ends as "father of us all" (4:16), where "us" is every believer regardless of circumcision.

The verb logizomai dominates the chapter as its organizing word, appearing eleven times (4:3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23, 24). This is the verb of Genesis 15:6 — "it was reckoned to him" — and Paul builds his entire argument around it. God reckons faith as righteousness (positive). God does not reckon sin (negative). The reckoning is a gift, not a wage. The reckoning happened before circumcision. The reckoning is for all who believe. The scriptural record of the reckoning was written for later generations. The same reckoning applies to those who believe in the God who raised Jesus. The chapter is, in essence, a meditation on what it means for God to "reckon" — to credit, to account, to assign a status — and it demonstrates that this reckoning was always a matter of faith and grace, never of works and obligation.

One critical observation concerns the relationship between 4:5 and the broader argument of chapters 1-3. Paul says God "justifies the ungodly" (ton asebe). The word asebes ("ungodly") describes the very people condemned in 1:18: "the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness (asebeian) and unrighteousness of men." The people under wrath in chapter 1 are the asebeis. The people God justifies in chapter 4 are the asebeis. Paul's argument has come full circle: the wrath revealed against the ungodly (1:18) is addressed by the God who justifies the ungodly (4:5) through the hilasterion of Christ's blood (3:25). The same group — the ungodly — is both the object of wrath and the object of justification. This is the resolution of the tension Paul identified in 3:26: God is both "just" (he does not ignore ungodliness) and "the justifier" (he declares the ungodly righteous). The justice was satisfied at the hilasterion; the justification is enacted through faith.

Finally, the chapter's argument about Abraham functions as Paul's demonstration that the Torah was never about what later generations assumed it was about. The Torah's own narrative shows that Abraham was reckoned righteous through faith, not through circumcision or Torah-observance. Circumcision was a sign and seal, not a cause. The law was not the vehicle of the promise. The promise was given and received in the sphere of faith. Paul is not reading against the Torah; he is reading the Torah's own story in its own sequence and drawing out what the story itself says. The claim of 3:31 — "we uphold the law" — is vindicated: Paul's teaching about justification by faith does not overthrow the law but reveals what the law was testifying to all along.
