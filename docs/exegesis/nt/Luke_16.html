Luke 16 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 15): Luke 15 opens with a narrative frame that controls everything that follows: "Now all the tax collectors and sinners were coming near Jesus to listen to Him. And both the Pharisees and the scribes began to complain, saying, 'This Man receives sinners and eats with them'" (15:1-2). The Pharisees' complaint — that Jesus associates with and shares meals with sinners — provokes three parables, each about something lost and found: a lost sheep (one out of a hundred), a lost coin (one out of ten), and a lost son (one out of two). Each parable ends with celebration over what was recovered. The first two parables end with an explicit application about heaven's joy over "one sinner who repents" (15:7, 10). The third and longest parable — the two sons — depicts a younger son who squanders (diaskorpizō) his father's wealth in a distant country, comes to ruin, repents, and returns to his father, who celebrates his return extravagantly. The older son, who has remained home and served faithfully, is enraged by the celebration and refuses to enter the feast. The father goes out to plead with him, saying "this brother of yours was dead and has begun to live, and was lost and has been found" (15:32). The older son represents the Pharisees and scribes of the opening frame — those who resent God's lavish reception of the lost. The chapter's argument is that God actively seeks and joyfully recovers what is lost, and that the real danger lies not in being lost but in being the one who refuses to celebrate the recovery. The parable leaves the older brother's response unresolved: we never learn whether he enters the feast. That open ending becomes the platform for chapter 16.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 17:1-10): Luke 17 opens with Jesus speaking to the disciples about stumbling blocks: "It is inevitable that stumbling blocks come, but woe to one through whom they come!" (17:1). It is better to have a millstone hung around one's neck than to cause "one of these little ones" to sin (17:2). The teaching then turns to forgiveness within the community: if a brother sins, rebuke him; if he repents, forgive him — even seven times in a day (17:3-4). The apostles ask for increased faith, and Jesus responds that faith the size of a mustard seed can uproot a mulberry tree (17:5-6). He follows with the parable of the unworthy slave: when a slave has done everything commanded, he does not expect thanks but says "we are unworthy slaves; we have done only that which we ought to have done" (17:10). The theme of this section is the conduct expected of those who follow Jesus: vigilance against causing others to stumble, radical forgiveness, faith without self-congratulation, and service without the expectation of special reward. The section thus addresses the inner life of the community of followers — how they treat one another and how they regard their own obedience.

How chapter 16 connects these: Luke 15 ended with a parable about two sons — one who squandered everything and returned, and one who served faithfully but refused to celebrate his brother's restoration. The Pharisees were the older brother, resentful of grace extended to sinners. Luke 17 will turn to the internal conduct of the community: stumbling blocks, forgiveness, faith, humble service. Chapter 16 is the bridge. It opens with Jesus speaking "to the disciples" (16:1) about a manager who squanders (diaskorpizō — the same verb used for the prodigal son in 15:13) his master's possessions and must reckon with the consequences. The teaching then moves through a series of sayings about faithfulness with wealth, the impossibility of serving both God and mammon, and the Pharisees' love of money. The chapter culminates in a narrative about a rich man who feasted lavishly every day while a poor man starved at his gate — and the irreversible reversal that followed death. The final words of the chapter — "If they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead" (16:31) — bring the argument to a sharp point about the adequacy and authority of the scriptures the audience already possesses. The movement from chapter 15 through 16 into 17 thus traces an arc from God's pursuit of the lost, through the right and wrong uses of the resources entrusted to God's people, to the responsibilities of those within the community of followers. Chapter 16 is where the implications of the older brother's attitude are given their sharpest economic and eschatological expression.

Verses 1-8a: The Parable of the Shrewd Manager

"Now He was also saying to the disciples, 'There was a rich man who had a manager, and this manager was reported to him as squandering his possessions. And he summoned him and said to him, "What is this I hear about you? Give an accounting of your management, for you can no longer be manager." And the manager said to himself, "What am I to do, since my master is taking the management away from me? I am not strong enough to dig; I am ashamed to beg. I know what I will do, so that when I am removed from the management people will welcome me into their homes." And he summoned each one of his master's debtors, and he began saying to the first, "How much do you owe my master?" And he said, "A hundred jugs of oil." And he said to him, "Take your bill, and sit down quickly and write fifty." Then he said to another, "And how much do you owe?" And he said, "A hundred kors of wheat." He said to him, "Take your bill, and write eighty." And his master complimented the unrighteous manager because he had acted shrewdly.'"

The opening phrase "He was also saying to the disciples" (elegen de kai pros tous mathētas) signals both continuity and a shift in audience. The word kai ("also") connects this teaching to what preceded it — the three parables of chapter 15, which were addressed to the Pharisees and scribes in response to their complaint. Now Jesus turns to the disciples, but as verse 14 will make clear, the Pharisees are still listening. The instruction is directed at the disciples, but it is spoken within earshot of those who love money.

The parable begins with a rich man (anthrōpos tis ēn plousios) and a manager (oikonomos). The oikonomos in a first-century Greco-Roman household was a steward — often a slave or freedman — who managed the master's estate, business transactions, and financial affairs. He acted with the master's authority in the master's absence, collecting debts, making loans, and managing property. The position was one of delegated trust: the manager handled resources that belonged to someone else.

This manager is "reported" (dieblēthē, from diaballō, literally "to throw across," and the root of diabolos, "slanderer/accuser") as "squandering" (diaskorpizōn) his master's possessions. The verb diaskorpizō is the same word used of the prodigal son in 15:13, who "squandered his estate in wild living." The verbal echo is unmistakable and deliberate. The prodigal squandered his own inheritance; this manager squanders someone else's property. Both face the consequences of waste, but the manager's situation is more precarious because the resources were never his to begin with.

The master's response is swift: "Give an accounting of your management, for you can no longer be manager" (apodos ton logon tēs oikonomias sou; ou gar dynē eti oikonomein). The phrase apodos ton logon means "render the account" — produce the books, show the records. The termination is not a threat but a decision already made. The manager's tenure is over; only the accounting remains.

The manager's internal deliberation (eipen en heautō, "he said within himself") reveals his predicament. He has three options, two of which he rejects: digging (manual labor — "I am not strong enough," ouk ischuō) and begging ("I am ashamed," aischynomai). The third option is the one he seizes: "I know what I will do, so that when I am removed from the management people will welcome me into their homes" (dexōntai me eis tous oikous autōn). The verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome") is significant — it is the same verb used in 15:2 where the Pharisees complain that Jesus "receives" (prosdechetai) sinners. The manager's plan is to create a network of reciprocal obligation so that when he loses his position, others will welcome him because he has done something for them.

He then calls in the master's debtors. The first owes "a hundred jugs of oil" (hekaton batous elaiou). A batos (Hebrew bat) was a liquid measure of approximately 8-9 gallons. A hundred batous would be roughly 800-900 gallons of olive oil — a substantial quantity representing the yield of a significant olive grove. The manager reduces this to fifty — a fifty percent reduction. The second debtor owes "a hundred kors of wheat" (hekaton korous sitou). A koros (Hebrew kor) was a dry measure of approximately 10-12 bushels. A hundred kors would be roughly 1,000-1,200 bushels — the yield of a large wheat farm. The manager reduces this to eighty — a twenty percent reduction.

The different percentages are notable. The reduction on oil (50%) is much larger than on wheat (20%). Some scholars have suggested that the manager may be removing his own commission or the interest that had been added to the debt. Charging interest on loans to fellow Israelites was prohibited by the Torah (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:36-37; Deuteronomy 23:19-20). In first-century practice, however, interest was frequently embedded in the stated amount of the debt — the contract would record a higher principal to disguise the interest. If the "extra" amount represented either the manager's commission or disguised interest, then by removing it the manager would be bringing the debts into compliance with the Torah's prohibition while simultaneously benefiting himself through the goodwill he creates. Under this reading, the master cannot object without admitting that the original debts contained illegal interest. The differing reduction percentages (50% for oil, 20% for wheat) correspond roughly to known interest rates in the ancient Near East, where oil, as a commodity with greater price volatility, typically carried higher interest rates than grain.

Whether or not this interest-removal theory is correct, the narrative's emphasis falls on the manager's shrewdness, not his ethics. The text calls him "the unrighteous manager" (ton oikonomon tēs adikias, literally "the manager of unrighteousness"), which is not a compliment to his character. What is commended is his practical wisdom in the face of crisis.

"And his master complimented the unrighteous manager because he had acted shrewdly" (epēnesen ho kyrios ton oikonomon tēs adikias hoti phronimōs epoiēsen). The word kyrios here most naturally refers to the master within the parable, not to Jesus. The master recognizes that the manager, though dishonest, has acted with intelligence and foresight. The adverb phronimōs ("shrewdly, prudently") comes from phronimos ("wise, prudent, sensible"), the same word used positively by Jesus elsewhere in Luke — the "faithful and sensible steward" (pistos oikonomos ho phronimos) in 12:42 whom the master puts in charge of his household. The difference between the two stewards is not in their shrewdness but in their faithfulness: the steward in chapter 12 was both faithful and shrewd; the manager in chapter 16 is shrewd but unrighteous. The master commends the one quality that merits commendation without endorsing everything else.

Verse 8b-9: The Application — Shrewdness and Eternal Dwellings

"'For the sons of this age are more shrewd in relation to their own kind than the sons of light. And I say to you, make friends for yourselves by means of the wealth of unrighteousness, so that when it is all gone, they will receive you into the eternal dwellings.'"

This is where the parable transitions from narrative to instruction, and the speaker shifts from the master within the story to Jesus himself. The phrase "and I say to you" (kai egō hymin legō) marks Jesus's direct application.

The observation that "the sons of this age" (hoi huioi tou aiōnos toutou) are "more shrewd in relation to their own kind" (phronimōteroi hyper tous huious tou phōtos) than "the sons of light" (hoi huioi tou phōtos) draws a contrast between two groups. "Sons of this age" are those whose horizon is limited to the present world order — people whose primary concern is navigating earthly affairs. "Sons of light" is a designation found in the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QS 1:9; 3:13; the War Scroll, 1QM 1:1-3) for the covenant community — those who belong to God's realm. The contrast is not between evil people and good people but between those who apply their intelligence to worldly affairs and those who should be applying equal or greater intelligence to the affairs of God's kingdom but are not. The sons of this age show more practical wisdom in their sphere than the sons of light show in theirs. It is a rebuke dressed as an observation.

The instruction that follows is startling: "make friends for yourselves by means of the wealth of unrighteousness" (poiēsate heautois philous ek tou mamōna tēs adikias). The word mamōnas (from Aramaic mamona) means "wealth, property, money." The genitive tēs adikias ("of unrighteousness") qualifies it — this is not righteous wealth or heavenly treasure but earthly wealth, which belongs to the present age and is inherently entangled with the world's systems of injustice. Jesus does not instruct his disciples to accumulate this wealth but to use it strategically — to "make friends" (philous) with it.

The purpose: "so that when it is all gone" (hotan eklipē) — the verb ekleipō means "to fail, to run out, to come to an end." Wealth will inevitably fail. It is not a permanent resource. When it does, "they will receive you into the eternal dwellings" (dexōntai hymas eis tas aiōnious skēnas). The verb dechomai ("receive, welcome") echoes both the manager's hope that people would "welcome" him (v. 4) and the Pharisees' complaint that Jesus "receives" sinners (15:2). The "eternal dwellings" (aiōnious skēnas, literally "eternal tents/tabernacles") contrasts sharply with the temporary homes the manager hoped to be welcomed into. The manager used his master's resources to secure a temporary earthly refuge; the disciples are to use worldly wealth to secure an eternal one.

The word skēnas ("tents, tabernacles") carries scriptural weight. The skēnē is the tabernacle — the dwelling place of God among Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 25:8-9; 26:1). The "eternal tents" suggest the permanent dwelling of God's people in God's presence, reversing the transience that the wilderness tabernacle represented. The logic of the instruction is: wealth is temporary and belongs to a world that is passing away; use it now, while you have it, in ways that create lasting relationships and eternal welcome — specifically, by using it generously for the benefit of others, particularly the poor and dispossessed. This is exactly what the rich man in verses 19-31 will fail to do.

Who are the "they" (dexōntai) who will receive the disciples into eternal dwellings? The subject is unspecified. It could be the poor and needy who were served with the wealth — that is, those whose friendship was earned by generosity will testify on behalf of the generous at the judgment. It could be angelic hosts or God himself, using an impersonal plural as a circumlocution for divine action (a common Semitic construction to avoid directly naming God as the subject). Either way, the point is that wise use of earthly wealth produces eternal results, while hoarding or self-indulgent use of it produces nothing that survives the wealth's failure.

Verses 10-13: Faithfulness with What Is Entrusted

"'The one who is faithful in a very little thing is also faithful in much; and the one who is unrighteous in a very little thing is also unrighteous in much. Therefore if you have not been faithful in the use of unrighteous wealth, who will entrust the true wealth to you? And if you have not been faithful in the use of that which is another's, who will give you that which is your own? No servant can serve two masters; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and wealth.'"

These four sayings form a unified argument about the relationship between earthly wealth and the things of God. Each saying builds on the previous one.

The first saying (v. 10) establishes a general principle: character is consistent across scale. How a person handles a small responsibility reveals how they will handle a large one. Faithfulness in small things is not a stepping stone to faithfulness in large things — it is the same faithfulness, expressed at different scales. Likewise, unrighteousness in small things is not a minor failing that will be corrected when the stakes are higher; it is the same character of unfaithfulness, which will only be amplified by greater responsibility.

The second saying (v. 11) applies this principle to wealth. "Unrighteous wealth" (adikos mamōnas) is the "very little thing" (elachiston). This designation is striking — worldly wealth, which the world treats as supremely important, Jesus calls "a very little thing." If the disciples have not been faithful in handling this small, temporary, tainted thing (money), who will entrust them with "the true wealth" (to alēthinon, "the genuine, the real")? The true wealth is contrasted with mammon: it is the genuine treasure of the kingdom, the eternal substance of which earthly wealth is merely a shadow. The logic assumes that earthly wealth is a test — a trial run in which the person's trustworthiness is evaluated before the genuine riches are assigned.

The third saying (v. 12) adds another contrast: "that which is another's" (to allotrion) versus "that which is your own" (to hymeteron). Worldly wealth is "another's" — it does not belong to the person who holds it. This echoes the entire framework of the parable: the manager was handling his master's possessions, not his own. All earthly wealth, in this framework, is entrusted property. The person who proves faithful with what belongs to someone else will be given "that which is your own" — the inheritance, the portion that actually belongs to the disciple in the age to come. The contrast underscores the stewardship theology that pervades this chapter: humans are managers, not owners. The resources they hold are on loan from the true owner.

The fourth saying (v. 13) draws the absolute conclusion: "No servant can serve two masters" (oudeis oiketēs dynatai dysi kyriois douleuein). The word oiketēs ("household servant") is more specific than doulos ("slave") — it denotes a servant who belongs to a household, who lives under the master's roof and is integrated into the domestic economy. Such a servant has undivided loyalty by definition; the household can only function if the servant's allegiance is clear. "Either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to one and despise the other" — the pairings are not emotional descriptions but descriptions of practical loyalty. In a Semitic idiom, "hate" can mean "to set aside, to give lesser priority to" (as in Genesis 29:31, where Leah is "hated" — meaning not preferred — compared to Rachel). The servant who is devoted to one master will inevitably neglect the other. The conclusion is absolute: "You cannot serve God and wealth" (ou dynasthe theō douleuein kai mamōna). God and mammon are presented as competing masters, each demanding total allegiance. The word douleuein ("to serve as a slave") implies complete subjection. This is not about avoiding wealth but about the impossibility of giving ultimate allegiance to both.

This series of sayings connects directly to the parable. The shrewd manager used wealth skillfully to prepare for his future; the disciples must use wealth faithfully to prepare for theirs. The manager was commended for shrewdness; the disciples are called to something higher — faithfulness. The manager treated his master's property as a tool for self-preservation; the disciples must treat the wealth entrusted to them as a test of character whose results determine whether they will receive the genuine riches of the kingdom.

Verses 14-15: The Pharisees' Response

"Now the Pharisees, who were lovers of money, were listening to all these things and were ridiculing Him. And He said to them, 'You are the ones who justify yourselves in the sight of people, but God knows your hearts; because that which is highly esteemed among people is detestable in the sight of God.'"

The narrator interrupts to provide both audience information and characterization: the Pharisees were philargyroi — "lovers of money" (from philos, "loving," and argyros, "silver"). This is the narrator's editorial comment, not Jesus's accusation, and it explains why the teaching about mammon provokes their reaction. They were "listening to all these things" (ēkouon de tauta panta) — they had been hearing the entire discourse, including the parable and the sayings about faithfulness and the impossibility of serving two masters. Their response is to "ridicule" him (exemyktērizon auton). The verb ekmyktērizō means literally "to turn up the nose at, to sneer at, to mock." It appears only here and in 23:35, where the rulers sneer at Jesus on the cross. The parallel is significant: the Pharisees who sneer at Jesus's teaching about money in chapter 16 are linked verbally to the rulers who will sneer at him on the cross.

Jesus's response addresses the core of their religious posture: "You are the ones who justify yourselves in the sight of people" (hymeis este hoi dikaiountes heautous enōpion tōn anthrōpōn). The verb dikaioō ("to justify, to declare righteous") is used reflexively — they declare themselves righteous before the watching public. Their righteousness is performed for a human audience. "But God knows your hearts" (ho de theos ginōskei tas kardias hymōn). The contrast between human sight (enōpion tōn anthrōpōn) and divine knowledge (ho theos ginōskei) demolishes the Pharisees' self-justification. What matters is not what people esteem but what God sees.

The concluding statement inverts human and divine valuation: "that which is highly esteemed among people is detestable in the sight of God" (to en anthrōpois hypsēlon bdelygma enōpion tou theou). The word bdelygma ("abomination, detestable thing") is one of the strongest words in the Greek scriptures for something God rejects. In the LXX, bdelygma is the standard translation of the Hebrew to'evah, used for idolatry (Deuteronomy 7:25-26), for the practices of the nations that Israel must not imitate (Deuteronomy 18:12), and for the "abomination of desolation" in Daniel (Daniel 11:31; 12:11). The word hypsēlon ("exalted, lofty, highly esteemed") describes what human society values most — wealth, status, public honor, the appearance of righteousness. Jesus declares that these exalted things are, in God's sight, on the same level as idols and abominations. This is not a correction but a total inversion of the value system the Pharisees operate within.

This directly continues the argument from chapter 15. The Pharisees complained that Jesus received sinners. Jesus told three parables showing that God actively seeks the lost and rejoices over their return. The older brother — who represents the Pharisees — resented the celebration. Now the Pharisees, who are characterized as lovers of money, ridicule Jesus's teaching about the proper use of wealth. Their love of money and their self-justification before people are presented as two aspects of the same spiritual problem: they have chosen mammon over God, and they use the appearance of righteousness to disguise that choice.

Verses 16-17: The Law, the Prophets, and the Kingdom

"'The Law and the Prophets were proclaimed until John came; since that time the gospel of the kingdom of God has been preached, and everyone is forcing his way into it. But it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one stroke of a letter of the Law to fail.'"

These two verses, often read as isolated pronouncements, are tightly connected to both the preceding rebuke and the following narrative. Jesus has just told the Pharisees that what they esteem is detestable to God. Now he addresses the scriptures they claim to uphold.

"The Law and the Prophets were proclaimed until John" (ho nomos kai hoi prophētai mechri Iōannou). This marks a temporal boundary: the era of "the Law and the Prophets" as the primary mode of God's address to Israel extended until John the Baptist. "Since that time the gospel of the kingdom of God has been preached" (apo tote hē basileia tou theou euangelizetai) — from John onward, a new proclamation has been underway: the good news of God's kingdom. This does not mean the Law and Prophets have been abolished; verse 17 will immediately insist on their permanence. Rather, the mode has shifted: the kingdom of God is now being actively proclaimed as present reality, not merely promised for the future.

"And everyone is forcing his way into it" (kai pas eis autēn biazetai). The verb biazomai can be read as middle voice ("everyone forces his way in") or passive ("everyone is being forced/urged into it"). In the middle voice, it suggests violent effort — people are pressing, pushing, straining to enter the kingdom. This connects to the "narrow door" of 13:24, where Jesus told his audience to "strive" (agōnizesthe) to enter. The kingdom is not passively received but requires urgent, forceful action to enter. The word pas ("everyone") is sweeping — the kingdom's proclamation has created a universal pressing, including by the tax collectors and sinners whom the Pharisees despise (cf. 15:1). The very people the Pharisees rejected are forcing their way into the kingdom, while the Pharisees themselves stand outside sneering.

Verse 17 then prevents any misunderstanding: "It is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one stroke of a letter of the Law to fail" (eukopōteron de estin ton ouranon kai tēn gēn parelthein ē tou nomou mian keraian pesein). A keraia ("stroke, serif") is the smallest decorative mark on a Hebrew letter — the tiny hook or projection that distinguishes, for example, a bet from a kaf. Jesus's claim is hyperbolic in scale: the entire physical cosmos will sooner collapse than the smallest detail of the Torah will prove void. The Law remains fully in force. The kingdom does not replace the Law; it fulfills and vindicates it.

This pair of sayings is directed at the Pharisees, and the logic runs against them in a specific way. The Pharisees claim to be the guardians of the Law. Jesus tells them that the Law remains fully valid — but that the kingdom of God is now the lens through which the Law must be read. The problem is not that the Pharisees are too devoted to the Law but that their devotion is selective and self-serving. They love money (v. 14), they justify themselves before people (v. 15), and they sneer at the teaching that exposes these failures. The Law and the Prophets, which they claim to uphold, actually condemn the very behavior they practice. This sets up the climax of the chapter: the rich man in verses 19-31 will be told that if he and his brothers had listened to "Moses and the Prophets," they would not need any further warning.

Verse 18: Divorce and the Law's Continuing Authority

"'Everyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery, and he who marries one who is divorced from a husband commits adultery.'"

This verse often strikes readers as disconnected from its context — a sudden intrusion of a divorce saying into a discussion about wealth and the kingdom. But within the flow of the argument, it serves a specific function: it provides a concrete example of what it means for "not one stroke of the Law" to fail.

The saying addresses the practice of divorce as regulated in Deuteronomy 24:1-4, which permitted a man to write a certificate of divorce and send his wife away. The grounds ("he has found some indecency in her") were debated between the schools of Hillel and Shammai in the first century: Shammai limited the grounds to sexual immorality, while Hillel permitted divorce for virtually any cause. Jesus's statement is more restrictive than either school: divorce and remarriage constitutes adultery, full stop.

The connection to the surrounding argument is not primarily about sexual ethics but about the Law's continuing authority and its proper interpretation. The Pharisees have been presented as those who "justify themselves" before people and love money — they use the Law selectively, applying it where it suits them and bending it where it does not. Divorce in the first century had significant economic dimensions: a man divorcing his wife could reclaim the dowry, remarry advantageously, and consolidate wealth. The practice was entangled with the economic self-interest that Jesus has been critiquing throughout the chapter. By stating that divorce-and-remarriage constitutes adultery despite Deuteronomy 24's permission, Jesus demonstrates that the Law's deepest intent (the permanence of the marriage covenant) stands even when the Law's provisions had been used to circumvent that intent. The Pharisees use the Law's letter to serve their interests; Jesus insists the Law's keraia — every stroke — points to God's original purpose.

This also connects to Malachi 2:13-16, where God declares hatred for divorce in the context of Israel's unfaithfulness, using marriage as a metaphor for the covenant relationship. The unfaithful husband who divorces his wife to pursue another parallels Israel's unfaithfulness to God — a parallel that is directly relevant to Jesus's charge that the Pharisees cannot serve both God and mammon. The divorce saying is thus not a digression but a pointed illustration: the Law the Pharisees claim to uphold, when read to its full depth, indicts the very practices they justify.

Verses 19-31: The Rich Man and Lazarus

"'Now there was a rich man, and he habitually dressed in purple and fine linen, enjoying himself in splendor every day. And a poor man named Lazarus was laid at his gate, covered with sores, and longing to be fed from the scraps which fell from the rich man's table; not only that, the dogs also were coming and licking his sores. Now it happened that the poor man died and was carried away by the angels to Abraham's arms; and the rich man also died and was buried. And in Hades he raised his eyes, being in torment, and saw Abraham far away and Lazarus in his arms. And he cried out and said, "Father Abraham, have mercy on me and send Lazarus, so that he may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool off my tongue, for I am in agony in this flame." But Abraham said, "Child, remember that during your life you received your good things, and likewise Lazarus bad things; but now he is being comforted here, and you are in agony. And besides all this, between us and you a great chasm has been set, so that those who want to go over from here to you will not be able, nor will any people cross over from there to us." And he said, "Then I request of you, father, that you send him to my father's house — for I have five brothers — in order that he may warn them, so that they will not come to this place of torment as well." But Abraham said, "They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them." But he said, "No, father Abraham, but if someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent!" But he said to him, "If they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead."'"

This narrative — whether parable or illustrative story — is the culmination of the chapter's entire argument. Every theme that has been developed since verse 1 converges here.

The rich man (vv. 19-21). The rich man is unnamed, which is unusual in a narrative culture where names carry significance. He is described entirely by his consumption: he "habitually dressed in purple and fine linen" (enedidysketo porphyran kai bysson). Purple dye (porphyra) was extracted from murex sea snails and was extraordinarily expensive — a garment dyed in Tyrian purple was a mark of extreme wealth and often of royalty or high rank (cf. Judges 8:26; Esther 8:15; Daniel 5:7, 16, 29). Fine linen (byssos) was an imported luxury fabric, the same material used for the high priest's garments (Exodus 28:39 LXX, where byssos translates the Hebrew shesh) and for the curtains of the tabernacle (Exodus 26:1). The rich man dresses daily in what the high priest wears for sacred service — clothing that evokes priestly and royal status — not for worship but for "enjoying himself in splendor every day" (euphrainomenos kath' hēmeran lamprōs). The verb euphrainō ("to celebrate, to make merry, to feast") is the same verb used in the prodigal son parable for the celebration at the younger son's return (15:23, 24, 29, 32) and for the older brother's complaint that "you never gave me a young goat, so that I might celebrate with my friends" (15:29). The rich man feasts every day — his entire life is the celebration that the older brother coveted.

Lazarus (Lazaros, the Greek form of the Hebrew Eleazar, meaning "God helps") is the only named character in any of Jesus's parables in the Synoptic Gospels. The name itself makes a theological claim: "God helps" — but in his earthly life, no help is evident. He is "laid at his gate" (ebeblēto pros ton pylōna autou) — the verb ballō in the pluperfect passive suggests he was placed there by others, unable to position himself. He is covered with sores (heilkōmenos, from helkos, "ulcer, wound"). He longs "to be fed from the scraps which fell from the rich man's table" (epithymōn chortasthēnai apo tōn piptontōn apo tēs trapezēs tou plousiou). The verb epithymeō ("to desire, to long for") and chortasthēnai ("to be filled, to be satisfied") echo the prodigal son, who "longed to have his fill" (epethymei chortasthēnai) of the pig pods (15:16). The prodigal, a Jewish son, found himself in the condition of a Gentile outcast; Lazarus, at the gate of a Jewish rich man, is in the condition of a starving beggar among dogs. The dogs (kynes) that lick his sores are unclean animals in Jewish law — their presence intensifies his degradation. He is in a state of complete destitution and ritual contamination.

The rich man's sin is not any specific act of cruelty. The text does not say he beat Lazarus, drove him away, or explicitly denied him food. His sin is what he did not do: Lazarus was at his gate, visible, daily, and the rich man — who feasted lavishly every single day — did nothing. This is the failure described in verses 1-13: the failure to use worldly wealth to "make friends" who will welcome you into eternal dwellings. Lazarus was at the gate. The rich man had the resources. The opportunity was constant and unmissable. He simply did not act. He served mammon — his purple, his linen, his daily feasting — and left Lazarus to the dogs.

The reversal after death (vv. 22-26). Both men die. The poor man is "carried away by the angels to Abraham's arms" (apenechthenai hypo tōn angelōn eis ton kolpon Abraam). The word kolpos means "bosom, chest, lap" — the place of intimate closeness, as a child rests against a parent's chest. "Abraham's bosom" was a Jewish expression for the place of the righteous dead — rest in the company of the patriarchs. The rich man "was buried" (etaphē) — presumably with the elaborate funeral his wealth would have afforded, though the narrative mentions only the burial and nothing of any angelic escort or reception.

In Hades (en tō hadē), the rich man looks up from torment (en basanois) and sees Abraham "far away" (apo makrothen) with Lazarus in his kolpos. The spatial imagery is deliberate: in life, the rich man was inside and Lazarus was outside at his gate; in death, the positions are reversed. Lazarus is in the place of intimacy and comfort; the rich man is at a distance, looking in.

The rich man's request is revealing: "Father Abraham, have mercy on me and send Lazarus, so that he may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool off my tongue" (pempson Lazaron hina bapsē to akron tou daktylou autou hydatos kai katapsyxē tēn glōssan mou). Even in Hades, the rich man treats Lazarus as a servant — someone to be "sent" on an errand. He addresses Abraham as "father" (pater), asserting his covenant identity, his Abrahamic lineage. He knows Lazarus by name — this is not a stranger. He knew who Lazarus was all along. He saw him at his gate, knew his name, and did nothing.

Abraham's response begins with tenderness — "child" (teknon) — acknowledging the relationship. But the content is devastating: "Remember that during your life you received your good things, and likewise Lazarus bad things; but now he is being comforted here, and you are in agony" (nyn de hōde parakaleitai, sy de odynasai). The verb parakaleo ("to comfort, to console") echoes the Beatitudes in Luke 6:24: "But woe to you who are rich, for you are receiving your comfort in full" (apechete tēn paraklēsin hymōn). The rich have already received their consolation; there is none left. The reversal is total and mirrors the Magnificat's declaration: "He has filled the hungry with good things; and sent away the rich empty-handed" (1:53). What Mary sang, this narrative dramatizes.

The "great chasm" (chasma mega) that has been "set" (estēriktai, from stērizō, "to fix, to establish firmly") makes the separation permanent. Those who wish to cross from comfort to torment or from torment to comfort cannot. The verb estēriktai is in the perfect passive, indicating a settled, established state — the chasm was set and remains set. This finality contrasts with the time before death, when crossing between the two conditions was easy: the rich man had only to send food from his table to his gate. What was trivially easy in life is impossibly permanent in death.

The request concerning the brothers (vv. 27-31). The rich man's second request shifts from himself to his family: "send him to my father's house — for I have five brothers — in order that he may warn them" (diamartyretai autois, "that he may solemnly testify to them"). He still treats Lazarus as an errand boy, still says "send him." But the request reveals something important: the rich man now understands that his fate was the consequence of his life's choices, and he fears that his brothers, who presumably live as he did, will arrive at the same destination.

Abraham's answer is the theological center of the chapter: "They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them" (echousin Mōysea kai tous prophētas; akousatōsan autōn). The phrase "Moses and the Prophets" is a standard designation for the Torah and the Prophetic writings — the Hebrew scriptures. Abraham's claim is that the scriptures are sufficient. They contain everything the brothers need to know in order to avoid the rich man's fate. The implication is that Moses and the Prophets already teach what the rich man failed to practice: care for the poor, justice for the destitute, the proper use of wealth.

And indeed they do. Deuteronomy 15:7-11 commands: "If there is a poor person among you... you shall not harden your heart, nor close your hand from your poor brother; but you shall freely open your hand to him" (Deut. 15:7-8). Leviticus 19:9-10 requires leaving gleanings for the poor and the stranger. Isaiah 58:6-7 defines true fasting as loosing the bonds of wickedness, sharing bread with the hungry, bringing the homeless poor into one's house. Ezekiel 16:49 identifies the sin of Sodom — a city associated with fiery judgment — as precisely the rich man's sin: "Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had arrogance, abundant food, and careless ease, but she did not help the poor and needy." The rich man did not need a visitor from the dead to tell him what Moses and the Prophets already said. He had the scriptures. He did not listen to them.

The rich man protests: "No, father Abraham, but if someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent!" (ean tis apo nekrōn poreuthē pros autous metanoēsousin). He insists that the scriptures are insufficient — that something extraordinary, something miraculous, is needed. A visitor from the dead would surely convince them.

Abraham's final statement closes the chapter and the argument: "If they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead" (oud' ean tis ek nekrōn anastē peisthēsontai). The verb anistēmi ("to rise, to stand up") is the standard verb for resurrection in the New Testament. The statement operates on two levels simultaneously.

On the surface, within the narrative, Abraham is making a claim about the human heart: a person who has hardened himself against the clear teaching of scripture will not be moved even by a supernatural sign. The problem is not a lack of evidence but a refusal to hear what the evidence already says. Adding more evidence — even the most dramatic possible evidence, a resurrection — will not overcome a will that has already rejected the available testimony.

For Luke's audience, the statement carries an additional layer of irony. Someone did rise from the dead — Jesus himself. And the response of the religious leadership was precisely what Abraham predicted: they were not persuaded. The Pharisees and the scribes, who had Moses and the Prophets, who were "lovers of money" (v. 14), who justified themselves before people (v. 15), who sneered at Jesus's teaching about wealth (v. 14) — these same people will not be persuaded by the resurrection. The narrative anticipates the rejection that Luke will record in Acts: the resurrection of Jesus will be preached, and many will refuse to believe. Abraham's statement is thus both a general principle and a specific prophecy: the problem is not the insufficiency of revelation but the hardness of the hearts that receive it.

The Chapter's Unified Argument

Luke 16 is a sustained argument about the use and misuse of entrusted resources, directed first at the disciples and then at the Pharisees, culminating in a narrative that dramatizes the eternal consequences of the choices made during earthly life.

The shrewd manager and the sayings on faithfulness (vv. 1-13) establish the framework: all wealth is entrusted, never owned. Humans are managers, not masters. The shrewd manager, though unrighteous, demonstrated the practical intelligence that the disciples should apply to their own stewardship of worldly resources. The key insight is that wealth is temporary ("when it is all gone") and should be deployed to create lasting, eternal outcomes — specifically through generosity that "makes friends" among those in need. The sayings on faithfulness then raise the stakes: how one handles worldly wealth is a test that determines whether one will be entrusted with the "true wealth" of the kingdom. The absolute conclusion — "you cannot serve God and wealth" — draws the line.

The confrontation with the Pharisees (vv. 14-18) exposes those who have failed the test. The Pharisees are lovers of money who justify themselves before people but are detestable to God. They claim to uphold Moses and the Prophets, but their love of money reveals that they serve mammon, not God. The Law remains fully in force — not one stroke will fail — but the Pharisees' selective application of the Law, which serves their economic interests (including the manipulation of divorce law), demonstrates that they do not actually hear what Moses and the Prophets say.

The rich man and Lazarus (vv. 19-31) is the dramatic illustration of everything that precedes it. The rich man is what the Pharisees are becoming — someone who feasts in splendor while a man named "God helps" lies at his gate. The reversal after death enacts the principle of verses 10-12: the rich man was unfaithful with the worldly wealth entrusted to him, and so the "true wealth" — comfort in Abraham's presence — was never given to him. He served mammon (purple, linen, daily feasting), and the master he did not serve (God, represented by the need at his gate) rendered the final judgment. The chasm that separates him from Lazarus in death is the permanent crystallization of the gate he maintained between them in life.

The chapter's final exchange — about Moses and the Prophets — ties the entire argument into a knot. The Pharisees have Moses and the Prophets. The rich man's brothers have Moses and the Prophets. The scriptures are sufficient to reveal God's will about wealth, about care for the poor, about the consequences of self-serving indulgence. The problem is not that revelation is lacking but that the hearers refuse to hear. Even a resurrection — the most extraordinary sign imaginable — will not persuade those who have already stopped their ears against the scriptures they claim to revere. This connects backward to 15:1-2, where the Pharisees complained that Jesus received sinners: they saw the kingdom arriving before their eyes and sneered at it, just as their forebears had the prophets and rejected them. And it connects forward to 17:1-10, where Jesus turns to the disciples to address how those who have heard and accepted should conduct themselves: with vigilance against causing others to stumble, with radical forgiveness, and with the humility of slaves who have merely done their duty.

The chapter thus functions as a warning from two directions. To the disciples: use the wealth you have wisely, generously, and faithfully, because it is a test of your trustworthiness for greater things. To the Pharisees and all who resemble them: the scriptures you already possess are sufficient to condemn your love of money and your neglect of the poor; no additional sign will save you if you refuse to hear what Moses and the Prophets have already said.
