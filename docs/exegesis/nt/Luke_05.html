Luke 5 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 4): Luke 4 established Jesus's identity and mission through three interconnected movements. The wilderness testing (4:1-13) answered the question raised by the baptism and genealogy: What kind of Son of God is Jesus? He is the Son who, unlike Israel, trusts God's word, worships God alone, and refuses to manipulate God — all three responses drawn from Deuteronomy's account of Israel's wilderness failures. The Nazareth synagogue scene (4:14-30) defined the content and scope of his mission through Isaiah 61 — good news to the poor, release to captives, sight to the blind, freedom to the oppressed, the year of the Lord's favor — and through the Elijah-Elisha precedents demonstrated that when Israel rejects the prophet, God's saving work moves to others, including Gentiles. The hometown that initially marveled attempted to kill him when those implications became clear. The Capernaum ministry (4:31-44) then demonstrated in action what the Nazareth sermon declared in words: an exorcism releasing a captive of an unclean spirit, the healing of Simon's mother-in-law, evening healings attended to individually, and the declaration that Jesus "must" (dei) preach the kingdom of God to other cities because he was "sent for this purpose." The chapter ended with Jesus preaching in synagogues, his mission defined and underway but his community of followers not yet constituted.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 6): Jesus and his disciples walk through grainfields on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees challenge them. Jesus responds with the precedent of David eating the consecrated bread and declares that "the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath." On another Sabbath, he heals a man with a withered hand in the synagogue while the scribes and Pharisees watch to see if he will do so, and "they were filled with rage and discussed together what they might do to Jesus." The controversy that began in chapter 5 has now reached the point of active plotting. Jesus then withdraws to a mountain to pray, spends all night in prayer, and in the morning selects twelve from among his disciples whom he also names "apostles" — the formal constitution of a new community. He comes down and delivers a sermon (Luke's "Sermon on the Plain") that opens with blessings on the poor, the hungry, the weeping, and the hated, paired with woes against the rich, the full, the laughing, and the well-spoken-of. The chapter thus moves from escalating conflict with the religious authorities to the formal establishment of a new community and the ethical instruction that will govern it.

How chapter 5 connects these: Luke 4 established the mission; Luke 6 will formalize the community and deepen the conflict. Chapter 5 is the bridge between these, accomplishing two things simultaneously. First, it constitutes the initial band of followers — Jesus does not merely announce a program; he gathers people who will participate in it. Simon, James, John, and Levi are all called in this chapter. Second, it introduces the controversy with the Pharisees and scribes that will dominate the middle portion of the Gospel. In chapter 4, Jesus's conflict was with his hometown; in chapter 5, the opposition shifts to the religious leadership. The chapter weaves these two threads — gathering followers and generating opposition — around a series of demonstrations of Jesus's authority (over fish, over leprosy, over paralysis, over sin), each of which raises the stakes of the question: Who is this man, and what does his authority imply for the structures and assumptions of Israel's religious life?

Verses 1-3: The Crowd, the Lake, and the Boat

"Now it happened that while the crowd was pressing around Him and listening to the word of God, He was standing by the lake of Gennesaret; and He saw two boats lying at the edge of the lake; but the fishermen had gotten out of them and were washing their nets. And He got into one of the boats, which was Simon's, and asked him to put out a little way from the land. And He sat down and began teaching the people from the boat."

The scene opens with a detail that marks a transition in the scale of Jesus's ministry: the crowd is "pressing around Him" (ton ochlon epikesthai auto). The verb epikeimai means to press upon, to crowd against. The word of God (ton logon tou theou) has attracted a crowd so large that it physically constrains Jesus. This is the first time in Luke that "the word of God" (ho logos tou theou) appears as a description of Jesus's teaching. The phrase carries weight: in the LXX, "the word of God" or "the word of the LORD" is characteristically the prophetic formula — the content of divine revelation delivered through a prophet (1 Samuel 9:27, 1 Kings 12:22, Jeremiah 1:2). By calling Jesus's teaching "the word of God," the author places it in the category of prophetic speech — not human commentary on God's word, but God's word itself.

The "lake of Gennesaret" (limnen Gennesaret) is Luke's name for what Matthew and Mark call "the Sea of Galilee" (thalassa tes Galilaias). The use of limne (lake) rather than thalassa (sea) is geographically more precise — it is in fact a freshwater lake, not a sea — and may reflect Luke's wider Greco-Roman literary sensibility.

Jesus gets into Simon's boat. Simon has already appeared in the narrative (4:38, the healing of his mother-in-law) but has not yet been formally called. He is a fisherman, and his boat becomes an improvised teaching platform. Jesus "sat down" (kathisas) — the posture of a teacher delivering instruction, the same posture he assumed in the Nazareth synagogue (4:20). The boat creates a natural amphitheater: Jesus is separated from the crowd by a few meters of water, able to address them without being crushed. But the use of Simon's boat is not merely practical; it draws Simon into the orbit of Jesus's mission before the explicit call has been issued.

Verses 4-7: The Miraculous Catch of Fish

"When He had finished speaking, He said to Simon, 'Put out into the deep water and let down your nets for a catch.' Simon answered and said, 'Master, we worked hard all through the night and caught nothing, but I will do as You say and let down the nets.' When they had done this, they enclosed a great quantity of fish, and their nets began to break; so they signaled to their partners in the other boat to come and help them. And they came and filled both the boats, so that they began to sink."

This episode is unique to Luke. Neither Matthew nor Mark records a miraculous catch of fish in connection with the calling of the first disciples. Matthew 4:18-22 and Mark 1:16-20 narrate the calling as a simple command ("Follow me") followed by immediate obedience. Luke's account is far more elaborate, and the addition of the miraculous catch transforms the calling from a bare act of obedience into a response to a revelation of divine power.

Simon addresses Jesus as "Master" (epistata). This word is distinctive to Luke in the Gospels — he uses it six times (5:5, 8:24, 8:45, 9:33, 9:49, 17:13), and no other Gospel writer uses it. The word epistates means "one who stands over," a supervisor or overseer. It conveys respect and deference without the full weight of "Lord" (kyrios). Simon recognizes Jesus as an authority figure but not yet as anything more.

Simon's response contains a tension between professional expertise and deference. As an experienced fisherman, he knows that if they caught nothing during the night — the optimal time for fishing on the lake — the prospects in daylight are poor. "We worked hard all through the night" (di holes nyktos kopiasantes) emphasizes the effort and duration of their failed attempt. The word kopiao means to labor to the point of exhaustion. But Simon adds: "but at Your word" (epi de to rhemati sou) — literally "upon Your word." The phrase epi to rhemati is significant. The word rhema in the LXX frequently carries the sense of a divine utterance, a word that accomplishes what it declares (as in Isaiah 55:11: "So will My word be which goes forth from My mouth; it will not return to Me empty, without accomplishing what I desire"). Simon defers to Jesus's word against his own judgment and experience.

The result exceeds all proportion. The nets begin to break (dierrēsseto ta diktya auton). They signal their partners (metochoi, "sharers," "partners" — a commercial term indicating a business relationship) in the other boat. Both boats are filled to the point of sinking. The catch is not merely good; it is overwhelming, beyond the capacity of the equipment and vessels to contain. The superabundance is characteristic of divine action in Israel's scriptures — the oil that filled every vessel the widow could find (2 Kings 4:1-7), the manna that appeared in quantities far beyond what was gathered (Exodus 16:13-18), the provision that overflows the need.

Verses 8-10a: Simon Peter's Response

"But when Simon Peter saw that, he fell down at Jesus's feet, saying, 'Go away from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man!' For amazement had seized him and all his companions because of the catch of fish which they had taken; and so also were James and John, sons of Zebedee, who were partners with Simon."

Simon's reaction is not gratitude, not excitement, not commercial calculation. It is fear and self-abasement. He "fell down at Jesus's feet" (prosepesen tois gonasin Iesou) — literally "fell at the knees of Jesus," a posture of prostration. He has shifted from "Master" (epistata, v. 5) to "Lord" (kyrie, v. 8). The escalation in address reflects the escalation in his understanding of who he is dealing with.

"Go away from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man" (exelthe ap' emou hoti aner hamartolos eimi, kyrie). This response has deep roots in Israel's experience of encountering the divine. When Isaiah saw the LORD seated on His throne in the temple, his response was: "Woe is me, for I am ruined! Because I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:5). When Manoah realized he had seen the angel of the LORD, he said to his wife: "We will surely die, for we have seen God" (Judges 13:22). When Ezekiel saw the likeness of the glory of the LORD, "I fell on my face" (Ezekiel 1:28). The pattern is consistent: the encounter with divine power produces not comfort but terror, not self-congratulation but acute awareness of one's own unworthiness. Simon does not say "I am unworthy" in the abstract; he says "I am a sinful man" (aner hamartolos) — a specific moral self-assessment. The presence of the holy reveals the unholy, not as abstract doctrine but as experienced reality.

The reaction is described as "amazement" (thambos, a word indicating not mere surprise but something closer to awe or dread) that "had seized him" (perieschon). The verb periecho means to surround, to encompass — the amazement is not something Simon feels; it is something that grips him, an involuntary overwhelm. His companions share it, and the author here names them for the first time as a group: James and John, sons of Zebedee, Simon's partners.

Verses 10b-11: The Call

"And Jesus said to Simon, 'Do not fear, from now on you will be catching men.' When they had brought their boats to land, they left everything and followed Him."

Jesus's first words are "Do not fear" (me phobou). This is the standard divine response to the fear produced by theophany. The angel said it to Zechariah (1:13) and to Mary (1:30). God said it to Abraham (Genesis 15:1), to Isaac (Genesis 26:24), to Jacob (Genesis 46:3), to Moses (Numbers 21:34), to Joshua (Joshua 8:1), to Gideon (Judges 6:23). The formula "do not fear" consistently accompanies a commission: the one who encounters God's power and recoils in unworthiness is told not to fear and then given a task. The fear is not dismissed as irrational; it is acknowledged as appropriate but then superseded by the call.

"From now on you will be catching men" (apo tou nyn anthropous ese zogrōn). The verb zogreō means literally "to capture alive" — it is used of taking prisoners of war alive rather than killing them (in the LXX, it appears in this sense in 2 Samuel 2:26; in 2 Timothy 2:26, the only other NT use, it describes being captured by the devil). The word transforms the metaphor: Simon will no longer catch fish (which die when caught) but will catch human beings (who are brought to life when caught). The fishing imagery is not decorative; it arises directly from the situation. Jesus meets Simon in his professional context and redefines that context in the service of the kingdom.

"From now on" (apo tou nyn) marks a decisive break. This phrase appears in Luke at moments of irrevocable transition (1:48, "from now on all generations will call me blessed"; 12:52, "from now on five members in one household will be divided"; 22:69, "from now on the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God"). It signals that the catch of fish was not merely a miracle but a turning point — Simon's life divides into before and after this moment.

The response is total: "they left everything and followed Him" (aphentes panta ekolouthēsan auto). The word panta ("everything") is comprehensive — nets, boats, the miraculous catch itself, their livelihood, their families' economic security. The immediate context makes this more striking than in Matthew or Mark: in those Gospels, the disciples leave their nets and follow at a simple command. In Luke, they leave everything after witnessing an overwhelming demonstration of divine power and after Simon has confessed his sinfulness and received a commission. The obedience is not blind; it is the response of men who have seen something that recalibrates every other consideration.

Verses 12-14: The Cleansing of a Leper

"While He was in one of the cities, behold, there came a man covered with leprosy; and when he saw Jesus, he fell on his face and implored Him, saying, 'Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean.' And He stretched out His hand and touched him, saying, 'I am willing; be cleansed.' And immediately the leprosy left him. And He ordered him to tell no one, 'But go and show yourself to the priest and make an offering for your cleansing, just as Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.'"

The leper is described as "covered with leprosy" (pleres lepras) — the word pleres ("full of") indicates an advanced and severe case. Leprosy (lepra) in the first-century Jewish context was not merely a medical condition; it was a category of ritual impurity governed by detailed legislation in Leviticus 13-14. A person diagnosed with leprosy by a priest was declared "unclean" (tamē), required to live outside the camp or community, wear torn clothes, cover the upper lip, and cry out "Unclean! Unclean!" to warn others away (Leviticus 13:45-46). Leprosy rendered a person socially dead — cut off from worship, community, and family. Contact with a leper made the one who touched him ritually unclean.

The leper's approach is itself remarkable. He falls on his face (pesōn epi prosōpon) — the same posture of prostration that Simon assumed. His words are carefully constructed: "Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean" (kyrie ean thelēs dynasai me katharisai). He does not doubt Jesus's ability (dynasai, "you are able"); his uncertainty is about Jesus's willingness (ean thelēs, "if you are willing"). The distinction is important: the leper's faith is in Jesus's power; his question is whether that power will be exercised on his behalf — whether Jesus will choose to extend himself toward someone in his condition.

Jesus's response has two components, both extraordinary. First, "He stretched out His hand and touched him" (ekteinas tēn cheira hēpsato autou). The touch is the scandal. Under the purity regulations, touching a leper would make Jesus ritually unclean. The expected dynamic is that uncleanness is contagious — it flows from the unclean to the clean. But in Jesus's action, the dynamic is reversed: cleanness flows from Jesus to the leper. The touch does not defile Jesus; it purifies the leper. This reversal is programmatic for the entire Gospel: Jesus enters the space of impurity (touching lepers, eating with sinners, associating with tax collectors) not to become impure but to extend purity and restoration.

Second, Jesus's verbal response: "I am willing; be cleansed" (thelō katharisthēti). The verb katharizō ("to cleanse") in the imperative carries the force of a command addressed to the condition itself, similar to the commands addressed to the fever (4:39) and the demon (4:35). The result is immediate: "immediately the leprosy left him" (eutheōs hē lepra apēlthen ap' autou).

Jesus then gives the healed man two instructions. First, "tell no one" (mēdeni eipein). This command to silence is consistent with the pattern established in 4:35 and 4:41 — Jesus controls the disclosure of his identity and the publicity of his actions. Second, "show yourself to the priest and make an offering for your cleansing, just as Moses commanded" (deizon seauton tō hierei kai prosenenke peri tou katharismou sou kathōs prosetaxen Mōysēs). This refers to the procedure prescribed in Leviticus 14:1-32, where a person healed of leprosy must present himself to the priest, undergo a ritual involving two birds, cedarwood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop, wait seven days, and then offer sacrifices. Only after this procedure is the person officially declared clean and restored to the community.

Jesus's instruction to follow the Mosaic procedure is significant on multiple levels. It shows that Jesus does not operate in opposition to the Torah; he directs the healed man to fulfill its requirements. The phrase "as Moses commanded" (kathōs prosetaxen Mōysēs) is respectful of the Mosaic legislation. But the final phrase — "as a testimony to them" (eis martyrion autois) — introduces ambiguity. A "testimony" (martyrion) can be either a positive witness or a legal evidence-against. The word autois ("to them") most likely refers to the priests. The healed leper, presenting himself with a condition reversed that only God was believed able to reverse (2 Kings 5:7, where the king of Israel says, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man sends word to me to cure a man of his leprosy?"), would serve as evidence that something unprecedented is happening. The testimony could function either as an invitation to recognize God's work or as a standing witness against those who refuse to see it.

Verses 15-16: Withdrawal to Pray

"But the news about Him was spreading even farther, and large crowds were gathering to hear Him and to be healed of their sicknesses. But Jesus Himself would often slip away to the wilderness to pray."

These two verses function as a summary and transition. The word about Jesus (ho logos, "the word" — the same term used in verse 1 for "the word of God") spreads, and the crowds grow. The people come for two things: "to hear" (akouein) and "to be healed" (therapeuesthai). The ministry announced in Isaiah 61 and demonstrated in chapter 4 is producing its effect.

But the author immediately pairs the growing public demand with Jesus's private practice: "He would often slip away to the wilderness to pray" (autos de ēn hypochōrōn en tais erēmois kai proseuchomenos). The imperfect tense (ēn hypochōrōn... proseuchomenos) indicates ongoing, habitual action — this is not a one-time event but a pattern. The word hypochōreō ("to withdraw," "to retire") suggests deliberate retreat, and the location is the "wilderness" (erēmoi) — the same environment as the temptation. The wilderness in Luke is not merely empty space; it is the place of encounter with God, the place of testing and preparation.

Luke's emphasis on Jesus's prayer life is one of his most distinctive contributions. Luke records Jesus praying at his baptism (3:21), in the wilderness here (5:16), before choosing the twelve (6:12), before Peter's confession (9:18), at the transfiguration (9:28-29), before teaching the disciples to pray (11:1), in Gethsemane (22:41-44), and from the cross (23:34, 46). This pattern establishes prayer as the sustaining practice of Jesus's ministry — the source from which authority, discernment, and perseverance flow. The juxtaposition of growing public demand with private prayer implies that Jesus's power is not autonomous but depends on his relationship with the Father.

Verses 17-20: The Setting for the Paralytic's Healing

"One day He was teaching; and there were some Pharisees and teachers of the law sitting there, who had come from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem; and the power of the Lord was present for Him to perform healing. And some men were carrying on a bed a man who was paralyzed; and they were trying to bring him in and to set him before Him. But not finding any way to bring him in because of the crowd, they went up on the roof and let him down through the tiles with his stretcher, into the middle of the crowd, in front of Jesus."

The author introduces a new group into the narrative: "Pharisees and teachers of the law" (Pharisaioi kai nomodidaskaloi). This is their first appearance in Luke's Gospel. The word nomodidaskalos ("teacher of the law") is rare — it appears only here, in Acts 5:34, and in 1 Timothy 1:7 in the entire New Testament. These figures have come "from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem" (ek pasēs kōmēs tēs Galilaias kai Ioudaias kai Ierousalēm). The geographical spread is notable: they have come from the widest possible range, suggesting an organized effort to observe and evaluate Jesus's activity. This is no accidental gathering of local religious leaders; it is a deliberate assembly of observers.

The author inserts a narrative aside: "the power of the Lord was present for Him to perform healing" (dynamis kyriou ēn eis to iasthai auton). The "power of the Lord" (dynamis kyriou) is the divine energy that enables healing. The phrasing implies that this power is not Jesus's permanent possession in the way that a skill belongs to a craftsman; rather, it is described as being "present" (ēn, "was there") for the purpose of healing. This subtle note aligns with Luke's consistent presentation of Jesus as operating in dependence on the Spirit and the Father rather than autonomously.

The paralyzed man's arrival is the first example in Luke of the persistence of faith overcoming obstacles. The men carrying him cannot get through the crowd, so they go up on the roof. In the typical construction of a Palestinian house, the roof was flat, accessed by an external staircase, and made of wooden beams covered with packed earth and tiles. They "let him down through the tiles" (dia tōn keramōn kathēkan auton) — they remove roofing material to create an opening and lower the man on his stretcher into the room below. The effort required — carrying a paralyzed man up stairs, dismantling part of a roof, lowering him down — demonstrates extraordinary determination. These men are not passive observers; they are active agents of the paralytic's encounter with Jesus.

Verses 20-21: The Pronouncement of Forgiveness and the Controversy

"Seeing their faith, He said, 'Friend, your sins are forgiven you.' The scribes and the Pharisees began to reason, saying, 'Who is this man who speaks blasphemies? Who can forgive sins, but God alone?'"

Jesus responds to "their faith" (tēn pistin autōn) — the faith of the men who brought the paralytic, not (or not only) the faith of the paralytic himself. The word pistis here means active trust — the kind that dismantles roofs. Faith in Luke is not primarily a set of beliefs but a disposition that acts on its convictions despite obstacles.

But Jesus's response is startling. The men have brought a paralytic, expecting healing. Jesus says: "Friend, your sins are forgiven you" (anthrōpe, aphewntai soi hai hamartiai sou). The word anthrōpe ("man," "friend") is a direct address. The verb aphiēmi ("to forgive," literally "to release," "to send away") is in the perfect passive: "your sins have been forgiven" — the action has been completed, and its effects stand. The passive voice is what scholars call the "divine passive" (passivum divinum): the unstated agent of the action is God. Jesus does not say "I forgive your sins"; he says "your sins are forgiven you" — but the fact that he pronounces this on his own authority, without sacrifice, without priestly mediation, without any of the mechanisms established in the Torah for dealing with sin, is what provokes the reaction.

The scribes and Pharisees identify the issue immediately: "Who can forgive sins, but God alone?" (tis dynatai hamartias apheinai ei mē monos ho theos). This is not a misunderstanding on their part; it is a correct statement of Israel's theology. Forgiveness of sins belongs to God. Isaiah 43:25: "I, even I, am the one who wipes out your transgressions for My own sake, and I will not remember your sins." Psalm 51:4: "Against You, You only, I have sinned." Exodus 34:6-7: the LORD proclaims Himself as "forgiving iniquity, transgression and sin." The mechanisms for dealing with sin in the Torah — the sacrificial system, the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16), the sin and guilt offerings (Leviticus 4-5) — all involve priestly mediation, animal sacrifice, and the temple. Jesus bypasses all of these and pronounces forgiveness directly.

The charge is "blasphemy" (blasphēmias) — speech that dishonors God by claiming for a human what belongs to God alone. The question the religious leaders ask is the correct question: Who is this man? The entire scene is constructed to force this question to the surface.

Verses 22-26: The Healing as Demonstration

"But Jesus, aware of their reasonings, answered and said to them, 'Why are you reasoning in your hearts? Which is easier, to say, "Your sins are forgiven you," or to say, "Get up and walk"? But, so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins' — He said to the paralyzed man — 'I say to you, get up, and pick up your stretcher, and go home.' Immediately he got up before them, and picked up what he had been lying on, and went home glorifying God. They were all struck with astonishment and began glorifying God; and they were filled with fear, saying, 'We have seen remarkable things today.'"

Jesus perceives "their reasonings" (tous dialogismous autōn) — the word dialogismos means inner deliberation, mental calculation. The author presents Jesus as having access to the internal thoughts of the religious leaders. This capacity was attributed in Israel's scriptures to God alone: "the LORD searches all hearts, and understands every intent of the thoughts" (1 Chronicles 28:9); "You know when I sit down and when I rise up; You understand my thought from afar" (Psalm 139:2). The very ability that enables Jesus to perceive their reasoning is itself evidence relevant to the question they are asking.

Jesus poses a question: "Which is easier, to say, 'Your sins are forgiven you,' or to say, 'Get up and walk'?" (ti estin eukopōteron, eipein, aphewntai soi hai hamartiai sou, ē eipein, egeire kai peripatei). The logic is precise. In one sense, it is "easier" to say "your sins are forgiven" because no one can verify or falsify the claim — forgiveness is invisible. To say "get up and walk" is harder because the claim is immediately testable. A failed healing would be publicly undeniable. But in another sense, the power to forgive sins is greater than the power to heal paralysis, because forgiveness belongs to God's exclusive prerogative while healing has been granted to prophets (Elijah, Elisha). Jesus's argument is: I will do the verifiable thing (heal the paralysis) so that you may know I have authority for the unverifiable thing (forgive sins). The visible miracle authenticates the invisible declaration.

The title "Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthrōpou) appears here for the first time in Luke. The phrase originates in Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and is given "dominion, glory and a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations and men of every language might serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion which will not pass away." In its Danielic context, the Son of Man is a figure who receives authority from God. Jesus's use of the title here is carefully calibrated: the Son of Man has "authority on earth" (exousian... epi tēs gēs) to forgive sins. The phrase "on earth" is significant — it locates this authority not in the heavenly court but in the present, earthly ministry. The claim is not merely that sins will be forgiven eschatologically (at the final judgment) but that forgiveness is being enacted now, on earth, through the Son of Man's spoken word.

The healing is immediate and complete: "immediately he got up before them" (parachrēma anastas enōpion autōn). The man picks up his stretcher and goes home "glorifying God" (doxazōn ton theon). The crowd responds with astonishment, glorifying God, and being "filled with fear" (eplēsthēsan phobou). The word phobos ("fear") here is not merely emotional fright but the awe produced by the direct encounter with divine power. Their exclamation — "We have seen remarkable things today" (eidomen paradoxa sēmeron) — uses the word paradoxon, "contrary to expectation," "extraordinary." The word sēmeron ("today") echoes the "today" of Jesus's Nazareth declaration (4:21: "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled"). In Luke, "today" marks the moments when God's action breaks into the present.

Verses 27-28: The Calling of Levi

"After that He went out and noticed a tax collector named Levi sitting in the tax booth; and He said to him, 'Follow Me.' And he left everything behind, and got up and began to follow Him."

The call of Levi follows the same pattern as the call of Simon, James, and John but with a crucial difference in social category. Tax collectors (telōnai) in first-century Palestine were not merely revenue officers; they were agents of the Roman occupation, collecting customs duties on goods passing through their district. They typically worked under a system of tax farming where they contracted with the Roman authorities for the right to collect taxes and then extracted enough to cover the contract plus their own profit. This system was inherently exploitative, and the tax collectors were despised both as collaborators with the occupying power and as economic predators. In Jewish social taxonomy, "tax collectors" were regularly paired with "sinners" (hamartōloi) as a fixed phrase (5:30, 7:34, 15:1, Matthew 9:10-11, 11:19, 21:31-32).

Levi is "sitting in the tax booth" (kathēmenon epi to telōnion) — he is at work, engaged in his profession, when Jesus issues the call. The command is identical to what Jesus will give to others: "Follow Me" (akolouthei moi). There is no preliminary conversation, no negotiation, no condition. The call comes as a direct imperative.

Levi's response mirrors the fishermen's: "he left everything behind, and got up and began to follow Him" (katalipōn panta anastas ēkolouthei autō). The phrase "left everything" (katalipōn panta) is the same totality seen in verse 11. But what Levi leaves is different from what the fishermen left. The fishermen could, in principle, return to their nets (and Peter apparently did, John 21). A tax collector who abandons his post abandons his contract with Rome, his income, and his social position — such as it was. The decision is more irrevocable, the cost more final.

The calling of Levi also extends the social range of Jesus's community. Simon, James, and John were working-class fishermen — not outcasts but not elite. Levi is in a different category altogether: he is a social pariah, ritually suspect, economically tainted. By calling Levi, Jesus is not merely adding a follower; he is making a statement about who belongs in the community that the kingdom of God creates.

Verses 29-32: The Banquet and the Controversy

"And Levi gave a big reception for Him in his house; and there was a great crowd of tax collectors and other people who were reclining at the table with them. The Pharisees and their scribes began grumbling at His disciples, saying, 'Why do you eat and drink with the tax collectors and sinners?' And Jesus answered and said to them, 'It is not those who are well who need a physician, but those who are sick. I have not come to call the righteous but sinners to repentance.'"

Levi hosts a "big reception" (dochēn megalēn) — the word dochē means a banquet, a formal feast. The guests are "a great crowd of tax collectors and other people" (ochlos polys telōnōn kai allōn). Levi's social world is populated by other tax collectors and by people classified as "others" — those outside the circle of the observant. In the first-century Jewish context, table fellowship was not a casual social act; it was a statement of solidarity and acceptance. To eat with someone was to identify with them, to treat them as members of one's community. The purity-conscious would not eat with those whose observance of tithing, purity, and dietary laws was in question.

The Pharisees and their scribes (hoi Pharisaioi kai hoi grammateis autōn) direct their complaint not to Jesus directly but to "His disciples" (tous mathētas autou). This is the first use of the word mathētai ("disciples," "learners") in Luke. The complaint is specific: "Why do you eat and drink with the tax collectors and sinners?" (dia ti meta tōn telōnōn kai hamartōlōn esthiete kai pinete). The issue is not that Jesus teaches these people or heals them; it is that he eats with them. Eating together implies acceptance, equality at the table, a shared status. The Pharisees' objection is that Jesus's table fellowship communicates a acceptance of people whom the religious community has classified as outside the boundaries of covenant faithfulness.

Jesus answers with a proverb and a mission statement. The proverb: "It is not those who are well who need a physician, but those who are sick" (ou chreian echousin hoi hygiainontes iatrou alla hoi kakōs echontes). The proverb is self-evident and therefore unanswerable. A physician who avoids the sick has abandoned his purpose. The implication is that Jesus's mission is inherently directed toward those who are in a condition that needs remedying. If the Pharisees agree that tax collectors and sinners are "sick" (morally, spiritually), then they should expect the physician to be among them, not among the "healthy."

The mission statement: "I have not come to call the righteous but sinners to repentance" (ouk elēlytha kalesai dikaious alla hamartōlous eis metanoian). Several elements are important. "I have come" (elēlytha) — the perfect tense indicates a completed arrival with continuing purpose; it is a statement of mission, not merely itinerary. "To call" (kalesai) — the same verb used for calling Levi (v. 27) and used in the LXX for God's calling of Israel (Isaiah 41:9, 42:6, 43:1). "The righteous" (dikaious) and "sinners" (hamartōlous) — the question is whether Jesus uses "righteous" straightforwardly (those who are genuinely righteous do not need his call) or with irony (those who consider themselves righteous do not think they need his call, but they may be wrong). In context, either reading works: the point is that Jesus's table fellowship with sinners is not a violation of his mission but its direct expression. He came precisely for these people. The phrase "to repentance" (eis metanoian) — found in Luke but not in the Matthean parallel (Matthew 9:13) — specifies the purpose of the calling. Jesus does not accept sinners as they are in the sense of leaving them unchanged; he calls them to repentance (metanoia, a turning, a reorientation of life). But the repentance is offered in the context of acceptance, not as its precondition. Jesus eats with them first and calls them to repentance through the fellowship, not after they have demonstrated worthiness.

Verses 33-35: The Question about Fasting

"And they said to Him, 'The disciples of John often fast and offer prayers, and the disciples of the Pharisees also do the same, but Yours eat and drink.' And Jesus said to them, 'You cannot make the attendants of the bridegroom fast while the bridegroom is with them, can you? But the days will come; and when the bridegroom is taken away from them, then they will fast in those days.'"

A new challenge is raised, this time about fasting. The question compares Jesus's disciples unfavorably with two other groups: the disciples of John (the Baptist) and the disciples of the Pharisees. Both groups practice regular fasting and structured prayer. Jesus's disciples "eat and drink" (esthiousin kai pinousin) — the phrase, following the banquet with tax collectors, emphasizes their participation in festive meals rather than ascetic discipline. The accusation is not merely about dietary practice; it is about the entire tone and posture of Jesus's movement. It looks celebratory rather than penitential.

Jesus's response introduces a new metaphor: the bridegroom (nymphios). "You cannot make the attendants of the bridegroom fast while the bridegroom is with them, can you?" (mē dynasthe tous huious tou nymphōnos en hō ho nymphios met' autōn estin poiēsai nēsteusai). The "attendants of the bridegroom" (huioi tou nymphōnos, literally "sons of the wedding hall") are the wedding guests, the friends of the groom. In Jewish practice, the wedding celebration lasted seven days, during which fasting was not only unnecessary but inappropriate. The wedding feast was a time of joy, abundance, and celebration.

The bridegroom metaphor carries resonance from Israel's scriptures. The relationship between God and Israel was characteristically described as a marriage: "Your Maker is your husband — the LORD of hosts is His name" (Isaiah 54:5); "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19); the entire Song of Songs was read as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel. The prophets described Israel's unfaithfulness as adultery (Hosea 1-3, Ezekiel 16, Jeremiah 3). By identifying himself as the bridegroom, Jesus places himself in the role that the scriptures assign to God in relation to His people. The celebration at Levi's table is not irreverence; it is the appropriate response to the bridegroom's presence.

But Jesus adds a darker note: "the days will come, and when the bridegroom is taken away from them" (eleusontai de hēmerai, kai hotan aparthē ap' autōn ho nymphios). The verb apairō ("to take away," "to remove") in the passive suggests forcible removal — the bridegroom will not depart voluntarily but will be "taken away." This is the first hint in Luke of Jesus's eventual death. The language is veiled but unmistakable: there will come a time when the bridegroom is no longer present, and then fasting will resume. The present time is a time of celebration because the bridegroom is here; the future will bring his removal and the sorrow that attends it.

Verses 36-39: The Parables of the Patch and the Wine

"And He was also telling them a parable: 'No one tears a piece of cloth from a new garment and puts it on an old garment; otherwise he will both tear the new, and the piece from the new will not match the old. And no one puts new wine into old wineskins; otherwise the new wine will burst the skins and it will be spilled out, and the skins will be ruined. But new wine must be put into fresh wineskins. And no one, after drinking old wine wishes for new; for he says, "The old is good enough."'"

Jesus concludes the chapter with three short parables, all addressing the same fundamental issue: the relationship between what Jesus is bringing and the existing religious structures.

The patch parable (v. 36). Luke's version differs from Matthew's (Matthew 9:16) and Mark's (Mark 2:21). In Matthew and Mark, an unshrunk patch is put on an old garment, and the shrinkage of the new patch tears the old garment. In Luke, the issue is different: who would tear a piece from a new garment to patch an old one? The result is doubly destructive — the new garment is ruined (torn), and the patch does not match the old garment. Luke's version makes a sharper point: attempting to combine the new with the old does not merely fail; it damages both. The new cannot be dismembered to repair the old, and the old cannot accommodate the new.

The wineskin parable (vv. 37-38). New wine (oinos neos) is still fermenting, still producing gas. Old wineskins (askous palaious) have already been stretched to their limit by previous fermentation and have lost their elasticity. New wine in old skins will produce pressure the skins cannot accommodate; the skins will burst, the wine will be lost, and the skins will be destroyed. New wine requires fresh wineskins (askous kainous) — containers with the flexibility to accommodate the expansion. The parable is not a judgment against the old wineskins; they served their purpose with the old wine. But new wine demands new containers. What Jesus is bringing — the kingdom of God, the year of the Lord's favor, the calling of sinners, the table fellowship that disregards purity boundaries — cannot be contained within the existing structures of Pharisaic practice (fasting schedules, purity regulations, social boundaries). The attempt to force it into those structures will destroy both.

The old wine saying (v. 39). This verse is unique to Luke and is often considered puzzling. "No one, after drinking old wine wishes for new; for he says, 'The old is good enough'" (oudeis piōn palaion thelei neon; legei gar, ho palaios chrēstos estin). Some manuscripts read chrēstoteros ("better") rather than chrēstos ("good," "good enough"). The saying acknowledges a human reality: those accustomed to old wine (established religious practice, familiar structures, proven ways) do not naturally desire new wine. The old wine has mellowed; the new wine is sharp, unfinished, unpredictable. This is not a commendation of the old over the new; it is a diagnosis of resistance. It explains why the Pharisees object to what Jesus is doing. They have the old wine and are satisfied with it. The new wine Jesus offers — forgiveness pronounced without sacrifice, table fellowship with sinners, celebration instead of fasting, a community that includes tax collectors — does not taste good to those whose palate is formed by the old. The saying has a note of sadness or realism: the resistance Jesus encounters is not irrational from the perspective of those who hold it. They genuinely prefer what they have. But their preference does not change the fact that the new wine is here and requires new containers.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 5 advances the narrative from the announcement and demonstration of Jesus's mission (chapter 4) to two critical developments: the formation of a community and the emergence of religious opposition.

The gathering of followers (vv. 1-11, 27-28) answers the question that chapter 4 left open: If Jesus has a mission from God to proclaim good news, release captives, and heal the sick, who will participate in this work? The answer comes through two calling scenes. Simon, James, and John are called through a demonstration of overwhelming power that reveals Jesus's identity and produces a response of awe, confession of sinfulness, and total commitment. Levi is called through a direct summons that crosses the most significant social boundary in Jewish religious life. Together, these callings establish the community that will carry the mission forward, and they define its character: it is composed not of the religiously qualified but of sinful fishermen and social outcasts.

The demonstrations of authority (vv. 4-7, 12-14, 17-26) show Jesus exercising power over the natural world (the catch of fish), over ritual impurity (the cleansing of leprosy), and over both physical affliction and sin (the healing of the paralytic and the pronouncement of forgiveness). Each demonstration escalates the stakes. The catch of fish reveals power over creation. The touch of the leper reverses the direction of impurity. The forgiveness of sins claims a prerogative that belongs to God alone. The healing of the paralytic then authenticates the forgiveness claim, establishing that the Son of Man has authority on earth to do what only God can do.

The emerging controversy (vv. 21, 30, 33) introduces the conflict that will intensify through the rest of the Gospel. The Pharisees and scribes raise three objections: Jesus speaks blasphemy by forgiving sins, he eats with tax collectors and sinners, and his disciples do not fast. Each objection addresses a different dimension of the threat Jesus poses to the existing religious order: his authority (who can forgive?), his associations (with whom does he eat?), and his practice (why does his movement look different from the established patterns of piety?). Jesus answers each objection with an argument from his mission: the Son of Man has authority to forgive; the physician must go to the sick; the bridegroom's presence demands celebration, not mourning.

The concluding parables (vv. 36-39) interpret the entire chapter. What Jesus is bringing cannot be accommodated within the existing structures. The new garment cannot be cut up to patch the old one. The new wine cannot be put into old wineskins. And those who have grown accustomed to the old wine will not naturally desire the new. This is not a rejection of Israel's scriptures or God's prior work — Jesus himself cited Moses (v. 14) and operated within the synagogue system (4:16, 44). It is a declaration that the kingdom of God as Jesus embodies it represents something genuinely new that requires new forms of community, new patterns of practice, and a new understanding of who belongs to God's people. The old is not condemned, but it cannot contain what is coming. Those who insist on the old will find themselves unable to receive the new — not because the new wine is inferior, but because their palate has been shaped by what they already have.
