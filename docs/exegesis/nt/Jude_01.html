Jude

Preliminary Note on the Letter's Structure

Jude is a single, self-contained letter — the shortest in the collection apart from 2 and 3 John. It has no chapter divisions to navigate; the entire document is one continuous argument. Because there is no preceding or following chapter by the same author, the "surrounding argument" must be traced within the letter itself. The analysis below follows the letter's own movement from start to finish, treating each section as a development of the argument that precedes it.

The letter also presents an important question of genre. It is not a theological treatise. It is not a pastoral letter addressing a community's internal questions. It is an urgent warning — a piece of crisis correspondence. The author had intended to write about a different topic ("our common salvation," v. 3) but changed course because of a specific threat that demanded immediate attention. Everything in the letter serves this single rhetorical purpose: to identify the threat, demonstrate its seriousness through historical and prophetic precedent, and instruct the recipients on how to respond. The letter's argument is built on a technique deeply rooted in Jewish interpretive tradition: the appeal to known examples from Israel's past as patterns that illuminate present dangers.

The Salutation: Who Is Writing, and to Whom (vv. 1-2)

"Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James, to those who are called, beloved in God the Father and kept for Jesus Christ: May mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you."

The author identifies himself as "Jude" (Ioudas, the Greek form of Judah), describing himself with two relationships: he is a "servant" (doulos) of Jesus Christ and a "brother of James." He does not call himself an apostle. The self-designation as doulos places him in the tradition of Israel's great figures who bore the same title — Moses is called "servant of the LORD" (doulos kyriou) throughout the LXX (Deuteronomy 34:5, Joshua 1:1), as are David (2 Samuel 7:5), the prophets (Amos 3:7), and Israel collectively (Isaiah 41:8-9). To be a doulos of the LORD is not a mark of low status but of authorized service under the sovereign.

The identification as "brother of James" implies a James well-known enough to need no further identification. In the first-century communities, the most prominent James was the brother of Jesus who led the Jerusalem assembly (Acts 15:13, Galatians 2:9). If this is that James, then Jude is also a brother of Jesus — yet he does not say "brother of Jesus." He calls himself Jesus's doulos instead. This is worth noting: even a biological sibling defines himself by service, not by kinship, when it comes to Jesus. The relationship is reconfigured around authority, not family.

The recipients are described with three participles that function as titles: "called" (kletois), "beloved" (egapemenois) in God the Father, and "kept" (teteremenois) for Jesus Christ. Each of these terms carries significant weight.

"Called" (kletois): In the LXX and in broader first-century Jewish usage, the "called" are those whom God has summoned — Israel was "called" out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1), called by name (Isaiah 43:1), called to be God's people. The word does not describe an internal spiritual state; it describes an action God has taken toward a group.

"Beloved" (egapemenois) in God the Father: The perfect passive participle indicates a settled state — they have been loved and remain loved. The phrase "in God the Father" locates this love: its source and sphere is God. Israel is called God's "beloved" (agapetos) throughout the prophets — "Israel my beloved" appears in various forms in the LXX. This is covenant language.

"Kept" (teteremenois) for Jesus Christ: The verb tereo (to keep, guard, preserve) will reappear at critical moments in this letter — in verse 6, the angels who did not "keep" (tereo) their proper position are now "kept" (tereo) in chains for judgment; in verse 13, wandering stars for whom the gloom of darkness has been "kept" (tereo); in verse 21, the readers are told to "keep" (tereo) themselves in God's love. The author builds a deliberate pattern with this word, and its first appearance here in the salutation establishes the baseline: the recipients are people who are being kept — preserved, guarded — for Jesus Christ. The preposition "for" (with the dative Iesou Christo) indicates that they are being kept in view of, or for the sake of, Jesus Christ's purposes.

The greeting itself — "mercy, peace, and love" — differs from the more common "grace and peace" found in the Pauline letters. The inclusion of "mercy" (eleos) and "love" (agape) in place of or alongside "grace" may reflect the letter's overall emphasis: the recipients will need mercy, peace, and love in the face of the crisis described, and they will need to exercise all three in their response (as vv. 22-23 will make explicit).

The Occasion: An Intended Letter Displaced by Urgency (vv. 3-4)

"Beloved, although I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation, I found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints. For certain people have crept in unnoticed who long ago were designated for this condemnation, ungodly people, who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ."

The shift in purpose (v. 3). Jude had planned to write about "our common salvation" (tes koines hemon soterias) — a term that implies a salvation shared by both author and recipients, something they hold in common. But something intervened. The word "necessary" (anagken) conveys compulsion: the situation forced his hand. The letter the recipients are reading is not the letter Jude originally intended to send. This detail is not merely biographical; it signals the seriousness of the crisis. Whatever Jude wanted to discuss about their shared salvation had to wait because something was threatening that very salvation.

The phrase "contend for the faith" (epagonizesthai te ... pistei) uses a word from athletic competition — epagonizomai means to fight, to struggle, to contend as in a contest. The faith (te pistei) here has the definite article: it is not faith as a subjective attitude but "the faith" — the body of teaching, the content of what has been handed over. And it was "once for all delivered to the saints" (hapax paradotheise tois hagiois). The word hapax (once for all) emphasizes finality and completeness: this delivery happened once, decisively, and is not to be repeated, supplemented, or altered. The verb paradotheise (from paradidomi, to hand over, to entrust) is the standard term for the transmission of tradition — the same word used for the passing on of authoritative teaching (cf. 1 Corinthians 11:2, 23; 15:3). "The saints" (tois hagiois) are the holy ones, the set-apart community.

The picture is clear: there is a fixed body of teaching that was entrusted to the community, and it is now under assault. Jude is not inviting theological exploration; he is calling for defense.

The infiltrators described (v. 4). "Certain people have crept in unnoticed" (pareisedusan tines anthropoi). The verb pareisduo means to slip in secretly, to enter by stealth — it carries connotations of deception and concealment. These are not outsiders attacking the community from without; they are people who have entered the community and are operating within it. This is critical for the entire letter: the danger Jude addresses is internal, not external. The people he warns about are present among the recipients, participating in their communal life (as v. 12 will make explicit — "these are hidden reefs at your love feasts").

Three descriptors follow:

First, they were "long ago designated for this condemnation" (palai progegrammenoi eis touto to krima). The word progegrammenoi (from prographo, to write beforehand) has been understood in various ways, but within the letter's own argument, the most natural reading is that the judgment about to be described was written about long ago in the examples Jude is about to cite — the exodus generation (written about in Numbers), the angels (written about in traditions known to the audience), Sodom and Gomorrah (written about in Genesis), and the prophecy of Enoch (vv. 14-15). In other words, the fate of people like these infiltrators was already recorded in Israel's received tradition. The "writing beforehand" refers to the scriptural and prophetic precedents Jude is about to deploy. The condemnation (krima) is defined by those examples, not by an abstract decree.

Second, they are "ungodly" (asebeis). This word will become the letter's signature term. It appears in verse 4, then reappears with emphatic force in the Enoch prophecy (v. 15), where it occurs four times in a single sentence: "to execute judgment on all and to convict all the ungodly of all their deeds of ungodliness that they have committed in such an ungodly way, and of all the harsh things that ungodly sinners have spoken against him." The word asebeia and its cognates in the LXX describe people who act without reverence for God, who behave as though God's authority is irrelevant. It is not mere unbelief; it is active defiance of God's order.

Third, they "pervert the grace of our God into sensuality" (metatithentes ten tou theou hemon charita eis aselgeian). The verb metatithemi means to transpose, to transfer, to change one thing into another. The charge is specific: they are taking "the grace of our God" — the unmerited favor and generosity that the community has received — and converting it into a license for aselgeia (sensuality, licentiousness, lack of moral restraint). Grace, which was given as the ground of their common salvation, is being transformed into permission for indulgence. This is not a theoretical distortion; it is a lived practice within the community.

Fourth, they "deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ" (ton monon despoten kai kyrion hemon Iesoun Christon arnoumenoi). The word despotes (master, sovereign ruler) is stronger than kyrios (lord); it conveys absolute ownership and authority. The denial (arnoumenoi) may not be verbal — these people have "crept in" and presumably affirm Jesus in their speech. The denial is enacted through their conduct: by converting grace into license and living without reverence, they functionally deny the authority of the one they claim to follow. Their behavior contradicts their profession.

Three Historical Precedents of Judgment (vv. 5-7)

"Now I want to remind you, although you once fully knew it, that Jesus, who saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe. And the angels who did not keep their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling, he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day — just as Sodom and Gomorrah and the surrounding cities, which likewise indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire, serve as an example by undergoing a punishment of eternal fire."

The transitional phrase "I want to remind you" (hypomnēsai de hymas boulomai) signals that the audience already knows these examples. Jude is not teaching new material; he is recalling known history to draw a parallel. This is a standard Jewish homiletical technique — the appeal to scriptural exempla as patterns for the present.

First example: The exodus generation (v. 5). "Jesus [or 'the Lord'], who saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe." The textual tradition varies on whether the subject is "Jesus" (Iesous), "the Lord" (kyrios), or "God" (theos). The reading "Jesus" (attested in several important manuscripts) would be striking — it would mean that Jude identifies the agent who delivered Israel from Egypt with Jesus, implying a pre-existence christology consistent with some first-century Jewish readings of the Angel of the LORD who accompanied Israel. But regardless of which reading the author wrote, the point of the example is the same: the same agent who saved also destroyed. Rescue from Egypt did not guarantee survival in the wilderness.

This is the most foundational of the three examples, and Jude places it first for a reason. The exodus generation was God's chosen, delivered people. They saw the plagues, crossed the sea, received manna, heard the voice at Sinai. Yet "afterward" (to deuteron, literally "the second time," or "subsequently") those who "did not believe" (tous me pisteusantas) were destroyed. The word "destroyed" (apolesen) is unambiguous: they perished. Numbers 14 records the event most directly — after the faithless report of the spies, God declared that the entire generation (with the exceptions of Joshua and Caleb) would die in the wilderness and never enter the promised land (Numbers 14:29-35). Psalm 95:10-11 recalls it: "For forty years I loathed that generation... I swore in my wrath, 'They shall not enter my rest.'" 1 Corinthians 10:1-12 uses the identical example for an identical purpose: to warn a present community that past divine rescue does not exempt them from judgment if they fall into unfaithfulness.

The relevance to Jude's argument is precise: being among the saved people does not immunize a person against destruction. The infiltrators are within the community of the saved — they have "crept in" among those who are "called, beloved, and kept." But the exodus generation was also called, beloved, and delivered, and they were destroyed for unbelief. The pattern is established: salvation received does not equal salvation completed, and those within the delivered community can be destroyed.

Second example: The angels who abandoned their station (v. 6). "The angels who did not keep their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling, he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day."

This example draws on traditions well-known in Second Temple Judaism, most prominently the Enoch literature (1 Enoch 6-16) and its interpretation of Genesis 6:1-4, where "the sons of God" (bene ha'elohim) took human wives. In the Enoch tradition, these are angelic beings (Watchers) who abandoned their heavenly station, descended to earth, cohabited with human women, and taught forbidden knowledge. Their punishment was imprisonment in darkness until the final judgment.

The verb tereo (to keep) creates a devastating wordplay: the angels did not "keep" (tereo) their own position, so they are being "kept" (tereo) in chains. This is the same verb used of the recipients in verse 1 — they are "kept for Jesus Christ." The parallel is deliberate and cautionary. Being "kept" can go two ways: those who maintain their position are kept for Christ; those who abandon their position are kept for judgment. The word is structurally central to the letter's message.

The phrase "their own position of authority" (ten heauton archen) uses arche in the sense of domain, sphere of rule, or assigned station. "Their proper dwelling" (to idion oiketerion) reinforces this: they had a place assigned to them and they left it. The offense is not merely disobedience in a general sense but the abandonment of an assigned role and the crossing of a boundary established by God. The angels were given a domain; they transgressed it.

For Jude's argument, this example establishes a second principle: those who were given exalted positions by God and then abandoned them face severe consequences. The infiltrators are doing something analogous — they have received grace (v. 4) but are abandoning the boundaries it implies, crossing into territory (sensuality, denial of the Master) that grace does not authorize.

Third example: Sodom, Gomorrah, and the surrounding cities (v. 7). "Just as Sodom and Gomorrah and the surrounding cities, which likewise indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire, serve as an example by undergoing a punishment of eternal fire."

"Likewise" (ton homoion tropon toutois) links Sodom's sin to the angels' sin described in the previous verse. The connection is the crossing of boundaries — the angels crossed the boundary between heavenly and earthly; Sodom crossed the boundary of natural sexual conduct. The phrase "pursued unnatural desire" (apelthousai opiso sarkos heteras, literally "going after other/strange flesh") describes the pursuit of sexual congress outside the boundaries established by creation. In the Genesis 19 account, the men of Sodom demanded to have sexual access to the angelic visitors — flesh that was "other" in the most literal sense.

The destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah by fire from heaven (Genesis 19:24-25) was, in first-century Jewish and early Christian usage, the paradigmatic example of divine judgment. Isaiah 1:9-10 invokes it. Jeremiah 23:14 compares Jerusalem's prophets to Sodom. Ezekiel 16:49-50 recalls its sins (notably including pride, excess of food, prosperous ease, and failure to aid the poor and needy alongside the "abomination" of their conduct). Jesus himself used it as a benchmark of judgment (Matthew 10:15, 11:23-24). By the first century, "Sodom" was shorthand for a community that had given itself over to depravity and received catastrophic, visible divine punishment.

The phrase "serve as an example" (deigma) and "undergoing a punishment of eternal fire" (pyros aioniou diken hypechousai) present Sodom's destruction as an ongoing exhibit — the participle hypechousai (undergoing) is present tense, suggesting that their punishment continues to serve as a visible warning. The "eternal fire" (pyros aioniou) may refer to the finality and irreversibility of the destruction rather than to ongoing conscious torment — the cities were utterly and permanently destroyed. They were never rebuilt. The fire did its work and the result is permanent.

The three examples together form a complete pattern:

1. The exodus generation: God's own delivered people, destroyed for unbelief after rescue. 2. The angels: Exalted beings with assigned stations, imprisoned for abandoning their position. 3. Sodom: A human community, annihilated for crossing created boundaries into depravity.

Each example escalates. The first involves God's covenant people. The second involves heavenly beings. The third involves total civic destruction. Together they demonstrate that no status — not covenant membership, not exalted heavenly position, not civic existence — protects against judgment when boundaries are transgressed and faithfulness is abandoned. This is the framework within which Jude wants his audience to understand the infiltrators.

Application to the Infiltrators: Their Character Exposed (vv. 8-13)

The infiltrators compared to the three examples (v. 8). "Yet in like manner these people also, relying on their dreams, defile the flesh, reject authority, and blaspheme the glorious ones."

"In like manner" (homoios mentoi kai) ties the infiltrators directly to the three preceding examples. They do the same kinds of things. The phrase "relying on their dreams" (enypniazomenoi) suggests that these people claim visionary or revelatory authority — they are "dreamers" who base their behavior on supposed spiritual experiences. Three charges follow, each corresponding to one of the three examples:

  • "Defile the flesh" (sarka miainousin) — corresponds to Sodom's sexual transgression.
  • "Reject authority" (kyrioteta athetousin) — corresponds to the angels' abandonment of their station. The word kyriotes (lordship, dominion, authority) is related to kyrios (lord); rejecting kyrioteta is rejecting the authority structure established by the Master/Lord they claim to follow (v. 4).
  • "Blaspheme the glorious ones" (doxas blasphemousin) — "glorious ones" (doxas, literally "glories") refers to angelic or heavenly beings. The infiltrators speak contemptuously of spiritual authorities.

Michael and the devil: A contrast in speech (v. 9). "But when the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, was disputing about the body of Moses, he did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, 'The Lord rebuke you.'"

This episode draws on a tradition associated with the work known as the Assumption (or Testament) of Moses, known in the first century but not preserved in full. The point is not the narrative details but the contrast. Michael is an archangel — a being of the highest angelic rank. Even he, when facing the devil himself in a dispute, did not dare to pronounce a "blasphemous judgment" (krisin blasphemias). Instead, he deferred to a higher authority: "The Lord rebuke you" (epitimesai soi kyrios — an echo of Zechariah 3:2, where the LORD says to the Satan, "The LORD rebuke you"). If the most powerful angel will not speak presumptuously against even a fallen spiritual power, the audacity of mere humans blaspheming "the glorious ones" (v. 8) is exposed as reckless arrogance.

Verse 10 drives the contrast home: "But these people blaspheme all that they do not understand, and they are destroyed by all that they, like unreasoning animals, understand instinctively." The infiltrators combine two failures: they speak contemptuously about things beyond their comprehension (the spiritual realm, divine authority), and they are controlled by things that require no understanding at all — their instinctual, animal-like appetites. They blaspheme what is above them and are enslaved to what is beneath them.

Three more historical comparisons (v. 11). "Woe to them! For they walked in the way of Cain and abandoned themselves for the sake of gain to Balaam's error and perished in Korah's rebellion."

The "woe" (ouai) is a prophetic pronouncement of impending disaster — the same form used by the prophets (Isaiah 5:8-23, Habakkuk 2:6-19) and by Jesus in this study's earlier documents. Three new figures are invoked:

  • The way of Cain: In Genesis 4, Cain's fundamental failing was not simply murder but the prior rejection of God's warning. God told Cain that sin was "crouching at the door" and that he must "rule over it" (Genesis 4:7). Cain proceeded regardless. The "way of Cain" is the path of one who hears God's warning and acts in defiance of it. In first-century Jewish tradition (reflected in texts like the Targumim and early midrashim), Cain also came to represent those who denied divine retribution and moral accountability.
  • Balaam's error: Balaam (Numbers 22-25, 31:16) was a prophet who, for pay, sought to undermine God's people. He could not curse Israel directly because God prevented it, but according to Numbers 31:16, he counseled Balak to entice Israel into sexual immorality and idolatry at Baal-Peor — an incident that resulted in a plague killing 24,000 (Numbers 25:1-9). "Balaam's error" (te plane tou Balaam) is the corruption that comes from turning prophetic or spiritual authority into a means of personal gain, and specifically the willingness to lead God's people into sin for profit. The phrase "abandoned themselves for the sake of gain" (misthou exechythesan) uses the image of pouring oneself out — they have emptied themselves in their greed.
  • Korah's rebellion: Numbers 16 records Korah's challenge to the authority of Moses and Aaron: "You have gone too far! For all in the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the LORD is among them. Why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the LORD?" (Numbers 16:3). Korah claimed that the entire congregation was equally holy, making Moses's authority unnecessary. The ground opened and swallowed him and his followers. "Korah's rebellion" (te antilogia tou Kore) is the refusal to recognize divinely established authority — the same charge made in verse 8 ("reject authority"). The infiltrators, like Korah, deny that any authority stands above them.

These three figures are not random selections. They form a pattern: Cain defied God's direct warning (attitude), Balaam corrupted God's people for money (motive), Korah rejected divinely appointed leadership (structure). The infiltrators combine all three: they defy warning, act from self-interest, and reject authority.

Extended metaphors for the infiltrators (vv. 12-13). Jude now unleashes a cascade of images, each describing the infiltrators' true nature.

"These are hidden reefs at your love feasts" (houtoi eisin hoi en tais agapais hymon spilades). The word spilades can mean "hidden rocks" (reefs that wreck ships because they cannot be seen beneath the surface) or "spots/blemishes." Either meaning serves Jude's point: these people are present at the community's shared meals (the agapais, "love feasts" — communal meals that accompanied or included the Lord's Supper in first-century practice), and they are a hidden danger. They "feast with you without fear" (syneuochoumenoi aphobos) — the word aphobos (without fear, fearlessly) connects back to the characterization of them as people who have no reverence, no awe, no appropriate trembling before God or the community's sanctity. They eat alongside the faithful without any sense that their presence is incongruous with their conduct.

"Shepherds feeding themselves" (heautous poimainontes): Ezekiel 34 is the unmistakable background. God pronounces judgment against the shepherds of Israel who "feed themselves" instead of feeding the flock: "You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones, but you do not feed the sheep" (Ezekiel 34:3). The infiltrators are taking from the community rather than serving it. They are parasites in leadership clothing.

"Waterless clouds, swept along by winds" (nephelai anydroi hypo anemon parapheromenai): They promise rain but deliver nothing. They appear to offer refreshment and life but are empty. The image suggests people who make impressive appearances — perhaps through speech, teaching, or claimed revelations — but produce nothing of substance.

"Fruitless trees in late autumn, twice dead, uprooted" (dendra phthinoporina akarpa dis apothanonta ekrizothenta): Late autumn (phthinoporina) is harvest time — if a tree has no fruit at harvest, it has failed completely. "Twice dead" (dis apothanonta) is a striking phrase. A tree that is merely dead might be revived; one that is twice dead is beyond recovery. "Uprooted" (ekrizothenta) adds finality. This language echoes John's warning in Matthew 3:10: "Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire." The infiltrators are fruitless, dead, and removed from the soil that might have sustained them.

"Wild waves of the sea, casting up the foam of their own shame" (kymata agria thalasses epaphrizonta tas heauton aischynas): Isaiah 57:20 provides the background: "The wicked are like the tossing sea; for it cannot be quiet, and its waters toss up mire and dirt." The infiltrators' own conduct produces visible evidence of their shame — their deeds are on display for anyone willing to look.

"Wandering stars, for whom the gloom of utter darkness has been reserved forever" (asteres planetai hois ho zophos tou skotous eis aiona tetetretai): "Wandering stars" (asteres planetai) refers to celestial bodies that do not follow fixed courses — they appear bright but are unreliable for navigation. In 1 Enoch 18:13-16 and 21:1-6, wandering stars are explicitly identified as symbols for angels who transgressed their appointed courses, imprisoned in a place of darkness. This links directly back to the angels of verse 6. The darkness "reserved" for them uses tetetretai — the perfect passive of tereo, the same word from verses 1 and 6. The pattern completes itself: the recipients are "kept" (tereo) for Jesus Christ; the rebellious angels are "kept" (tereo) in chains; the gloom is "kept" (tereo, reserved) for the wandering stars. The verb tereo runs through the letter as its structural spine, and its direction — toward Christ or toward darkness — depends on whether one maintains or abandons one's assigned position.

The Prophecy of Enoch (vv. 14-16)

"It was also about these that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all and to convict all the ungodly of all their deeds of ungodliness that they have committed in such an ungodly way, and of all the harsh things that ungodly sinners have spoken against him.'"

Jude here cites 1 Enoch 1:9 almost verbatim. The description "the seventh from Adam" locates Enoch in the genealogy of Genesis 5 and assigns him the authority of extreme antiquity — he is among the earliest generations of humanity, predating the flood. In Second Temple Judaism, Enoch held a position of enormous authority as a visionary who was taken by God without dying (Genesis 5:24, "Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him"). The Enoch literature was widely read and authoritative in many first-century Jewish circles, and Jude treats this prophecy as genuine and applicable.

The prophecy itself is built around the word "ungodly" (asebes/asebeia). The fourfold repetition in a single sentence is rhetorical hammering — it is impossible to miss:

1. "all the ungodly" (pantas tous asebeis) — the scope is comprehensive 2. "all their deeds of ungodliness" (panton ton ergon asebeias auton) — their works are characterized by it 3. "committed in such an ungodly way" (hon esebesen) — even the manner of their deeds is ungodly 4. "ungodly sinners" (hamartolon aseben) — the word defines their identity

This fourfold repetition picks up and amplifies the word from verse 4, where the infiltrators were first called "ungodly people" (asebeis). What was introduced as a descriptor is now revealed as the defining characteristic that brings judgment. The Enoch prophecy declares that judgment will come not primarily for specific individual sins but for the comprehensive condition of ungodliness — a way of being that pervades everything: deeds, manner, speech, and identity.

"The Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones" (elthen kyrios en hagiais myriasin autou): The imagery draws on Deuteronomy 33:2 (LXX), where the LORD comes from Sinai with "ten thousands of holy ones" (myriades hagion). The coming is for judgment — "to execute judgment on all" (poiesai krisin kata panton). This is not selective; it is universal. "To convict all the ungodly" (elegxai pantas tous asebeis) uses elegcho, which means to expose, to bring to light, to prove guilty — it is not merely condemnation but demonstration of guilt.

Verse 16 applies the Enoch prophecy to the infiltrators: "These are grumblers, malcontents, following their own sinful desires, loud-mouthed boasters, showing favoritism to gain advantage."

"Grumblers" (goggystai): This word directly recalls the wilderness generation. The verb goggyzo is the LXX word for Israel's complaining against Moses and God in the desert (Exodus 16:2, 7-8; Numbers 14:2, 27, 29; 17:5). The people "grumbled" (egoggysan) against Moses, against God's provision, against the hardships of the journey. Paul uses the identical word and example in 1 Corinthians 10:10: "do not grumble, as some of them did and were destroyed by the Destroyer." By calling the infiltrators "grumblers," Jude explicitly identifies them with the exodus generation of verse 5 — the delivered people who were destroyed for unbelief.

"Malcontents" (mempsimoiroi, literally "fault-finders with their lot"): They are dissatisfied with what they have been given — their assigned station, their circumstances, the authority structure over them. This connects to the angels who abandoned their station (v. 6) and to Korah who challenged Moses's authority (v. 11).

"Following their own sinful desires" (kata tas epithymias heauton poreuomenoi): This echoes the description of Sodom (v. 7) and the charge that they "defile the flesh" (v. 8).

"Loud-mouthed boasters" (to stoma auton lalei hyperogka, literally "their mouth speaks swelling things"): Arrogant speech, reminiscent of Daniel 7:8, 20 (the horn that "spoke great things") and recalling the blaspheming of verse 8.

"Showing favoritism to gain advantage" (thaumazontes prosopa ophelias charin, literally "admiring faces for the sake of profit"): They flatter the powerful for personal benefit. This connects to Balaam's error (v. 11) — the corruption of spiritual authority for material gain.

Every descriptor in verse 16 maps back to the earlier examples. Jude is not piling up random insults; he is systematically demonstrating that the infiltrators recapitulate every pattern of rebellion and corruption already established as bringing judgment.

The Apostolic Warning: This Was Predicted (vv. 17-19)

"But you must remember, beloved, the predictions of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ. They said to you, 'In the last time there will be scoffers, following their own ungodly passions.' It is these who cause divisions, worldly people, devoid of the Spirit."

The shift to "but you, beloved" (hymeis de, agapetoi) marks a turn. Jude has been describing the infiltrators; now he addresses the recipients directly. The term "beloved" (agapetoi) echoes the salutation's "beloved in God the Father" (v. 1) and draws the recipients back into their identity as the loved, called, and kept.

"Remember the predictions of the apostles" (mnesthete ton rhematon ton proeiremenon hypo ton apostolon): Jude distinguishes himself from the apostles — he says "the apostles... they said to you" (autoi elegon hymin), placing himself outside that circle. He is not an apostle; he is a doulos (v. 1). The apostles had warned that "in the last time" (ep' eschatou tou chronou) scoffers would arise. Similar warnings appear in Acts 20:29-30 (Paul to the Ephesian elders: "fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock"), 2 Timothy 3:1-5 ("in the last days there will come times of difficulty"), and 2 Peter 3:3. The point is that the infiltrators' appearance is not a surprise; it was anticipated.

"Following their own ungodly passions" (kata tas heauton epithymias poreuomenoi ton asebeion): The phrase nearly replicates verse 16 ("following their own sinful desires"), now with the addition of "ungodly" (ton asebeion) — tying back again to the letter's signature word.

Verse 19 gives three final descriptors:

  • "These who cause divisions" (hoi apodiorizontes): They create factions within the community. The verb apodiorizō means to draw boundaries, to separate off. The infiltrators are fragmenting the fellowship.
  • "Worldly" (psychikoi): The word psychikos means "natural," "of the soul/psyche" as opposed to spiritual. It describes people operating on a merely human level, without the dimension of the Spirit. In 1 Corinthians 2:14, Paul uses the identical word: "the natural person (psychikos anthropos) does not accept the things of the Spirit of God."
  • "Devoid of the Spirit" (pneuma me echontes, literally "not having Spirit"): This is the most devastating assessment. Whatever their claims to spiritual authority (the "dreams" of v. 8), they do not actually possess the Spirit. Their spiritual pretensions are fraudulent.

Instructions to the Faithful: What the Recipients Must Do (vv. 20-23)

"But you, beloved, building yourselves up in your most holy faith and praying in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life. And have mercy on those who doubt; save others by snatching them out of the fire; to others show mercy with fear, hating even the garment stained by the flesh."

"But you, beloved" (hymeis de, agapetoi) again — the same turn as verse 17. The double use creates a structure: verses 17-19 told the recipients to remember the apostolic warnings; verses 20-23 tell them what to do in light of those warnings. The letter is now fully prescriptive.

Four commands to the community (vv. 20-21):

The main verb is "keep yourselves" (heautous teresate) — an imperative of tereo, the letter's key word. In verse 1, the recipients were described as "kept for Jesus Christ" (passive — God does the keeping). Here, they are told to "keep yourselves" (active — they must participate). This is not a contradiction but a two-sided reality: God keeps them (v. 1) and they must keep themselves (v. 21). Both are true simultaneously. The doxology in verses 24-25 will return to God's keeping power, completing the frame.

What they are to keep themselves in is "the love of God" (en agape theou) — they are to remain within the sphere of God's love. This is not an instruction to love God (though that is implied) but to stay positioned within the love God has for them. The parallel with verse 1 is exact: they were described as "beloved in God the Father"; they are now told to keep themselves in that state of being loved, not to wander out of it.

Three participial phrases describe how they keep themselves:

1. "Building yourselves up in your most holy faith" (epoikodomountes heautous te hagiotate hymon pistei): The faith is again "the faith" — the body of teaching from verse 3 — and it is called "most holy" (hagiotate, a superlative). They build on it as a foundation.

2. "Praying in the Holy Spirit" (en pneumati hagio proseuchomenoi): Their prayer life is to be Spirit-directed. This contrasts sharply with the infiltrators who are "devoid of the Spirit" (v. 19). The recipients have what the infiltrators lack.

3. "Waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life" (prosdechomenoi to eleos tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou eis zoen aionion): They are to live in active expectation. The mercy (eleos) they await is future — it "leads to eternal life" (eis zoen aionion). Eternal life here is not a present possession to be taken for granted but a destination toward which mercy carries them. The posture is forward-looking: they have been called and loved and kept (v. 1), but the consummation — eternal life — is still ahead.

Three responses to those at risk (vv. 22-23):

The textual tradition of these verses varies, with some manuscripts presenting two groups and others three. The most likely reading presents three groups:

1. "Have mercy on those who doubt" (hous men eleate diakrinomenous): Some in the community are wavering — they are in a state of doubt or dispute (diakrinomenous, from diakrino, to be divided in one's mind). The response is mercy (eleate). Not condemnation, not expulsion, but compassion. These are people on the edge, not yet committed to the infiltrators' path.

2. "Save others by snatching them out of the fire" (hous de sozete ek pyros harpazontes): Some are further along the path of destruction — they are already in the fire (the fire imagery recalls both Sodom's destruction in v. 7 and the eschatological judgment of vv. 14-15). The response is urgent rescue: snatch them out. The word harpazo (to seize, to grab, to snatch) conveys violence and urgency — this is not a gentle conversation but a forceful intervention.

3. "To others show mercy with fear, hating even the garment stained by the flesh" (hous de eleate en phobo misountes kai ton apo tes sarkos espilomenon chitona): A third group requires mercy combined with fear (phobos) — the fear of contamination. "Hating even the garment stained by the flesh" draws on the imagery of Zechariah 3:3-5, where the high priest Joshua stands before the LORD in filthy garments, which are removed and replaced with clean ones. The "garment stained by the flesh" represents the defilement that clings to those who have been involved in the infiltrators' conduct. Show mercy, yes, but with the awareness that their corruption can spread. Even the clothing that touched their flesh is contaminated. The metaphor warns: you can try to rescue the person, but do not be casual about the sin that has attached to them.

This three-tiered response is remarkably nuanced for a letter that has been so forceful in its denunciation. Jude does not simply write off everyone associated with the infiltrators. He distinguishes between the doubting (show mercy), the endangered (snatch them from the fire), and the deeply contaminated (show mercy with extreme caution). The community's responsibility is not merely to protect itself but to attempt recovery of those who have been drawn in — while being realistic about the dangers of proximity to corruption.

The Doxology: God's Power to Keep (vv. 24-25)

"Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy, to the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever. Amen."

The letter closes with a doxology that returns to the verb tereo one final time, though here it uses the related verb phylasso (to guard, to keep from): "to him who is able to keep you from stumbling" (to dynamento phylaxai hymas aptaistous). The word aptaistous (without stumbling, sure-footed) appears only here in the entire collection. It describes a traveler who does not lose footing — who does not trip and fall. After a letter filled with examples of catastrophic falling — the exodus generation, the angels, Sodom, Cain, Balaam, Korah — the assurance that God is able to keep the faithful from stumbling is profoundly reassuring.

"And to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy" (kai stesai katenopion tes doxes autou amomous en agalliasei): The verb stesai (to cause to stand, to present) envisions a moment of formal presentation — being made to stand before God's glorious presence. The word amomous (blameless, without blemish) is sacrificial language from the LXX — animals offered to God had to be without defect (amomos, Exodus 29:1, Leviticus 1:3, 10; Numbers 6:14). God will present the faithful as an offering without blemish. "With great joy" (en agalliasei) — this is not a fearful tribunal but a joyful presentation.

The closing ascription gives God four attributes: glory (doxa), majesty (megalosyne), dominion (kratos), and authority (exousia) — before all time (pro pantos tou aionos), now (kai nyn), and forever (kai eis pantas tous aionas). The letter that began with the infiltrators' denial of the Master's authority (v. 4) ends by affirming God's total, eternal sovereignty. The authority the infiltrators reject is the authority that spans all ages.

The Letter's Argument as a Whole

Jude's argument proceeds with tight logical coherence:

1. Identity of the recipients (vv. 1-2): Called, beloved, kept. These are established members of the community.

2. The crisis (vv. 3-4): People have infiltrated the community who pervert grace into license and deny the Master's authority. This necessitates contending for the faith.

3. Historical proof that such people are judged (vv. 5-7): Three precedents establish that being within the community of the saved (exodus), holding an exalted position (angels), or being a prosperous city (Sodom) does not protect against judgment when faithfulness is abandoned.

4. The infiltrators recapitulate these patterns (vv. 8-13): Their conduct maps precisely onto the historical examples. They defile flesh (Sodom), reject authority (angels), and blaspheme what they do not understand. Additional comparisons to Cain, Balaam, and Korah further demonstrate the type.

5. Prophecy confirms their judgment (vv. 14-16): Enoch's prophecy, from the most ancient times, describes exactly the judgment awaiting the ungodly, and the infiltrators match every detail.

6. The apostles anticipated this (vv. 17-19): The recipients should not be surprised; they were warned.

7. What the recipients must do (vv. 20-23): Keep themselves in God's love (the tereo imperative), build on the faith, pray in the Spirit, wait for mercy. And respond to those at risk with appropriate mercy at each level.

8. God is able (vv. 24-25): The one who keeps them (v. 1) is able to keep them from stumbling and to present them blameless.

The tereo (Keep) Structure

The verb tereo functions as the structural backbone of the entire letter:

  • v. 1: The recipients are "kept for Jesus Christ" (teteremenois Iesou Christo)
  • v. 6: The angels who did not "keep" their position are "kept" in chains (me terēsantas ... teterēken)
  • v. 13: Darkness is "reserved/kept" for the wandering stars (teterētai)
  • v. 21: The recipients are told to "keep yourselves" in God's love (heautous tērēsate)
  • vv. 24-25: God is able to "keep/guard" them from stumbling (phylaxai — a near synonym completing the pattern)

The message encoded in this structure is pointed: being "kept" is not a static guarantee but a dynamic relationship. God keeps (v. 1, vv. 24-25), but the recipients must keep themselves (v. 21). The angels who failed to keep their position demonstrate that those entrusted with a station can abandon it. The exodus generation, who were delivered but destroyed, demonstrates that initial rescue does not preclude subsequent judgment. The letter as a whole argues that the community's status as "called, beloved, and kept" is genuine and grounded in God's power, but it requires their active participation — building, praying, waiting, and keeping themselves in God's love — and it can be forfeited by those who, like the infiltrators, pervert grace and reject authority.

What the First-Century Hearer Would Understand

A first-century audience receiving this letter would recognize several things immediately:

1. The examples are authoritative. The exodus, the Watchers tradition, Sodom, Cain, Balaam, Korah, and the Enoch prophecy were not obscure references. They were the common currency of Jewish moral instruction, widely known and regularly cited in synagogue teaching, homilies, and Second Temple literature. Jude's audience is expected to know these stories and draw the intended parallels without extensive explanation.

2. The danger is internal. The infiltrators are not foreign persecutors; they are people inside the community. This makes the threat existential — it threatens the community's identity from within.

3. Grace has boundaries. The infiltrators' fundamental error is treating grace as permission for license. For a first-century audience shaped by covenant thinking, this would be immediately recognizable as a distortion. God's covenant with Israel was gracious — God chose them, delivered them, sustained them. But the covenant carried obligations, and violations had consequences. Grace and obligation were not opposites; they were bound together. To separate grace from obligation (as the infiltrators do) is to destroy the covenant structure itself.

4. Status does not guarantee outcome. The parade of examples — delivered Israel, exalted angels, favored cities — all make the same point: having received God's favor does not make one immune to judgment. The first-century hearer, shaped by Israel's own history of covenant blessing followed by exile due to unfaithfulness, would find this deeply familiar.

5. The response demanded is both communal and individual. The community must contend for the faith (v. 3), but individuals must also keep themselves (v. 21) and must engage in differentiated mercy toward those at various stages of being drawn away (vv. 22-23). Neither corporate solidarity nor individual vigilance alone is sufficient.
