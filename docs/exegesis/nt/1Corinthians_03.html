1 Corinthians 3 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 2): Paul used his own arrival in Corinth as evidence of the principle he established in chapter 1. He came deliberately without rhetorical brilliance, "in weakness and in fear and in much trembling" (2:3), so that the Corinthians' faith would rest on "the power of God" rather than "the wisdom of men" (2:5). He then introduced a critical distinction. There is a genuine wisdom, but it belongs to God, was hidden from the rulers of this age (whose ignorance led them to crucify "the Lord of glory," 2:8), and is inaccessible to any human faculty — eye, ear, or heart (2:9). This wisdom is revealed exclusively through the Spirit, who alone "searches everything, even the depths of God" (2:10). Paul drew a sharp line between two kinds of persons: the psychikos anthropos ("natural person"), who operates from unaided human faculties and cannot receive the things of the Spirit because they appear foolish to him (2:14); and the pneumatikos ("spiritual person"), who possesses the Spirit and therefore can "judge all things" (2:15). The chapter climaxed with a breathtaking claim drawn from Isaiah 40:13: "Who has known the mind of the Lord so as to instruct him? But we have the mind of Christ" (2:16). The community that has received the Spirit possesses the very mind of the Lord — the capacity to evaluate all things from Christ's perspective.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 4): Paul extends the argument about how the Corinthians should regard their teachers. "This is how one should regard us, as servants of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God" (4:1). The key qualification for stewards is faithfulness, not impressiveness: "it is required of stewards that they be found faithful" (4:2). Paul then says something remarkable about judgment: "I do not even judge myself... It is the Lord who judges me" (4:3-4). The judgment that matters belongs to the Lord, who "will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart" (4:5) — language that echoes both the "hidden wisdom" of 2:7 and the fire that tests builders' work in 3:13. Paul then reveals that his references to himself and Apollos have been representative: "I have applied all these things to myself and Apollos for your benefit, brothers, that you may learn by us not to go beyond what is written, that none of you may be puffed up in favor of one against another" (4:6). The phrase "puffed up" (physioumenoi) introduces the word that will become Paul's signature diagnosis of the Corinthian disease — it appears seven times in this letter, more than in all his other letters combined. Paul then drives the point home with the question that distills chapters 1-3 into a single sentence: "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?" (4:7). Everything is gift. Boasting is absurd.

How chapter 3 connects these: Chapter 2 built the theoretical framework: there are two kinds of persons (psychikos and pneumatikos), two kinds of wisdom (this age's and God's), and two kinds of discernment (natural and Spirit-given). Chapter 3 now turns that framework directly on the Corinthians and tells them which category they fall into. The answer is devastating: despite possessing the Spirit, they are behaving as sarkinoi ("fleshly") and nepioi ("infants"), not as pneumatikoi. The proof is their factionalism — the very thing reported in 1:11-12. Chapter 3 then reframes the relationship between the community and its teachers through two metaphors — agriculture and construction — that strip the teachers of the status the Corinthians have been attributing to them and place God as the only one who matters. The chapter climaxes with a warning about the community as God's temple and a final inversion: rather than the community belonging to its teachers, all things — including the teachers — belong to the community, and the community belongs to Christ, and Christ belongs to God. Chapter 4 will then complete the argument by defining the proper way to regard apostles (as servants and stewards) and exposing the Corinthians' inflated self-assessment. The movement from chapter 2 to chapter 4 through chapter 3 is seamless: you have been given the mind of Christ (2:16) — but you are not using it, as your factionalism proves (3:1-4) — so here is how teachers actually function (3:5-17) — and here is the proper posture toward them (3:18-23, continued in 4:1-7).

Verses 1-4: The Devastating Application — You Are Not Spiritual

"And I, brothers, could not address you as spiritual people but as people of the flesh, as infants in Christ" (3:1).

The word kago ("and I") connects directly back to Paul's personal testimony that opened chapter 2 ("and I, when I came to you," 2:1). The particle kai links what follows to everything Paul has just argued about the psychikos and the pneumatikos. Having described these two categories in 2:14-16, Paul now reveals where the Corinthians stand. The opening is emphatic: ouk edyneethen lalesai hymin hos pneumatikois — "I was not able to speak to you as to spiritual people." The verb edyneethen ("I was not able") echoes ou dynatai in 2:14 (the psychikos person "is not able" to understand the things of the Spirit). But the application is reversed: it was not the Corinthians who lacked the ability to receive; it was Paul who lacked the ability to deliver his message in its fullness because the audience was not ready.

Three terms define the Corinthians' condition:

Pneumatikois ("spiritual") — the same word from 2:13 and 2:15. This is what they should be. They have received the Spirit (2:12). They possess the mind of Christ (2:16). They lack nothing in any gift (1:7). By every theological measure, they should qualify as pneumatikoi. But Paul could not address them as such.

Sarkinois ("fleshly, made of flesh") — derived from sarx ("flesh"), with the suffix -inos indicating material composition. The word describes what something is made of: a sarkinos person is a person whose substance is flesh. In 2 Corinthians 3:3, Paul uses the same word for "tablets of human hearts" — "not on tablets of stone but on tablets of fleshly hearts" (en plaxin kardiais sarkinais). The word does not necessarily carry a moral judgment; it describes a condition of being constituted by flesh rather than being governed by the Spirit. Paul's use of sarkinois here describes the Corinthians as they were when he first arrived — immature, not yet developed in the Spirit, operating from their natural human constitution.

Nepiois en Christo ("infants in Christ") — the word nepios means an infant, a small child who cannot yet speak (the etymology is ne-epos, "not yet speaking"). In the LXX, it describes those who are simple, naive, immature — Psalm 19:7 (LXX 18:8): "the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple (ta nepia)"; Psalm 119:130 (LXX 118:130): "the unfolding of your words gives light; it gives understanding to the simple (nepiois)." The word does not describe wickedness but immaturity — the state of one who has not yet grown into the understanding available to them. The phrase en Christo ("in Christ") is crucial: the Corinthians are infants, but they are infants in Christ. They are genuinely within the sphere of Christ's lordship. Their problem is not that they are outside the faith but that they have not grown within it.

"I fed you with milk, not solid food, for you were not yet ready for it. And even now you are not yet ready" (3:2).

The milk/solid food distinction maps directly onto the wisdom categories of chapter 2. The "milk" (gala) corresponds to the basic proclamation: "Jesus Christ and him crucified" (2:2) — the foundational message Paul delivered when he first came to Corinth. The "solid food" (broma) corresponds to the "wisdom among the mature" (sophia en tois teleiois) that Paul described in 2:6 — the deeper understanding of God's hidden plan, the mystery decreed before the ages for the community's glory. The word teleioi ("mature") in 2:6 is the antonym of nepioi ("infants") here. The Corinthians should have progressed from nepioi to teleioi, from milk to solid food. They have not.

The phrase ou gar edynasthe ("for you were not yet able") uses the same verb of inability (dynamai) that appeared in 2:14 for the psychikos person. But the sting is in the next clause: all' oud' eti nyn dynasthe — "and even now you are not yet able." The word eti ("still") and nyn ("now") intensify the indictment. This is not merely a description of their past state; it is their present condition. Time has passed since Paul's initial visit. Apollos has come and gone (Acts 18:27-19:1). The community has been taught by multiple teachers. Yet they have not matured. They are stuck.

"For you are still of the flesh. For while there is jealousy and strife among you, are you not of the flesh and behaving only in a human way?" (3:3).

Paul shifts from sarkinois (v. 1, "made of flesh," describing material composition) to sarkikoi (v. 3, "pertaining to the flesh, characterized by flesh," describing operative behavior). The shift is subtle but significant. In verse 1, Paul described their condition when he first arrived — constitutionally immature, naturally fleshy. In verse 3, he describes their current behavior — actively operating according to the flesh, their conduct governed by fleshly patterns rather than the Spirit. The move from -inos to -ikos marks a transition from "what you were made of" to "how you are behaving." They should have outgrown their initial fleshliness; instead, their behavior continues to be characterized by it.

The evidence Paul cites is precise: zelos kai eris — "jealousy and strife." These are not random vices. They are the specific symptoms of competitive factionalism. The word zelos ("jealousy, envy, rivalry, zeal") in its negative sense describes the desire to have what another possesses or to surpass another's standing. The word eris ("strife, quarreling, contention") is the same word from 1:11, where Paul reported that "there are quarrels (erides) among you." Paul is circling back to the opening diagnosis. The evidence for fleshliness is not sexual immorality (that will come in chapter 5), not idolatry (chapters 8-10), not worship disorder (chapters 11-14). The evidence is the faction problem itself. Their competitive attachment to human teachers is the proof that they are operating kata anthropon ("according to a human standard") — literally "according to man."

The rhetorical question ouk anthropoi este ("are you not [merely] human?") is withering. The word anthropoi ("human beings") is used here almost as a term of rebuke. After everything Paul has argued in chapters 1-2 — that the community has received the Spirit who searches the depths of God (2:10), that they possess the mind of Christ (2:16), that they have been enriched in every way (1:5) — they are behaving as though none of this were true. They are acting like people who have not received the Spirit at all, evaluating everything by the criteria of the present age, competing for status through teacher-allegiance as though they were attending a sophist's lecture hall rather than participating in the assembly of God.

"For when one says, 'I follow Paul,' and another, 'I follow Apollos,' are you not being merely human?" (3:4).

Paul repeats the faction slogans from 1:12, but this time he names only two: "I am of Paul" and "I am of Apollos." The reduction from four slogans to two is strategic. Paul is about to address his own relationship with Apollos directly (vv. 5-9), using their collaboration as a model for how the Corinthians should understand the relationship between teachers. The Cephas and Christ factions drop away because the argument that follows will focus on the specific case Paul can illustrate from his own experience: he and Apollos, working in the same field, serving the same God.

The question ouk anthropoi este ("are you not [merely] human?") repeats the verdict of verse 3. The repetition drives the point home. The factionalism is the definitive evidence of spiritual immaturity. It is not one problem among many; it is the diagnostic symptom that reveals the underlying condition. A community that divides along the lines of human leaders is a community operating from human — not spiritual — criteria.

Verses 5-9: The Agricultural Metaphor — Servants in God's Field

"What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you believed, as the Lord assigned to each" (3:5).

The questions ti oun estin Apollos? ti de estin Paulos? are deliberately framed with the neuter interrogative ti ("what"), not the masculine tis ("who"). Paul does not ask "who is Apollos?" — which would invite a personal evaluation, a comparison of credentials and qualities. He asks "what is Apollos?" — which asks about function, role, category. The question strips the teachers of personal significance and reduces them to their functional role.

The answer: diakonoi di' hon episteusate — "servants through whom you believed." The word diakonoi means attendants, servers, those who carry out tasks assigned by another. In Greek usage, a diakonos was a table server, a messenger, one who performed practical service at another's direction. The word carries no connotation of authority or prestige; it denotes subordinate service. The preposition di' ("through") is critical. The teachers are the instruments through whom the Corinthians came to faith, not the source of that faith. They are channels, not fountains. The Corinthians' faith passed through Paul and Apollos on its way from God to them. Attaching oneself to the channel rather than to the source is a category error.

The qualifier hos ho kyrios edoken hekasto ("as the Lord assigned to each") introduces a further limitation. Each teacher's role was not self-chosen but assigned (edoken, "gave," from didomi) by the Lord. The word hekasto ("to each") individualizes the assignment: Paul received one task, Apollos another, and neither chose his own. The Lord distributed the roles. The Corinthians' preference for one teacher over another is therefore an implicit critique of the Lord's assignment.

"I planted, Apollos watered, but God was causing the growth" (3:6).

Paul introduces the agricultural metaphor with three verbs arranged in a deliberate sequence. Ego ephyteusa ("I planted") — the aorist tense marks the action as a completed past event. Paul's planting was his founding visit to Corinth, the eighteen months during which he established the community (Acts 18:1-11). Apollos epotisen ("Apollos watered") — also aorist, also a completed past event. Apollos's watering was his subsequent ministry in Corinth, during which he "greatly helped those who through grace had believed" (Acts 18:27). The two aorists mark two discrete, completed contributions.

But the third verb breaks the pattern: ho de theos euxanen ("but God was giving the growth"). The imperfect tense euxanen contrasts sharply with the two aorists. The aorists describe punctual actions completed and finished. The imperfect describes continuous, ongoing action. While Paul planted (once) and Apollos watered (once), God was continuously, persistently, without interruption, causing the growth. The growth is God's ongoing work, not a single event. Paul and Apollos performed time-bound tasks; God's activity sustains the field from beginning to end. The imperfect tense is not incidental; it is the argument in miniature. The human agents come and go; God's work is ceaseless.

The agricultural metaphor carries deep resonance in Israel's scriptures. Israel itself was God's planting. Isaiah 5:1-7: "My beloved had a vineyard on a very fertile hill. He dug it and cleared it of stones, and planted it with choice vines... the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah are his pleasant planting." Psalm 80:8-11 (LXX 79:9-12): "You brought a vine out of Egypt; you drove out the nations and planted it." Jeremiah 2:21: "I planted you a choice vine, wholly of pure seed." Isaiah 61:3: "they will be called oaks of righteousness, the planting of the LORD, that he may be glorified." In every case, the planter is God. The vine, the field, the planting belongs to him. Human workers may tend the field, but the field is God's. A first-century hearer steeped in Israel's scriptures would hear Paul claiming for the Corinthian community the same identity Israel bore: God's planting.

"So neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but only God who gives the growth" (3:7).

The word oudeis ("nothing") is absolute. The planter is nothing. The waterer is nothing. The word ti after oute ("not anything") strips the teachers of independent significance. This is not false modesty; it is a theological claim grounded in the agricultural metaphor. A field does not owe its existence to the farmhand who scattered seed or poured water. The field owes its existence to the one who owns it and who alone can cause life to emerge from dead seed buried in soil. No amount of planting or watering produces growth if God does not act. The teachers are necessary instruments — Paul does not deny this; he planted, and Apollos watered, and these were real contributions — but they are instruments of a process that belongs entirely to God.

The Corinthians' factionalism treats the instruments as though they were the source. It is as though a field quarreled over which farmhand it preferred, while ignoring the owner who gave it life.

"He who plants and he who waters are one, and each will receive his own wages according to his own labor" (3:8).

Two statements, each important. First: ho phyteuon kai ho potizon hen eisin — "the one planting and the one watering are one." The word hen ("one thing" — neuter, not masculine heis) indicates unity of purpose and function, not identity of person. Paul and Apollos are not the same person, but they are engaged in a single enterprise. The factions treat them as competitors; Paul declares them collaborators. The neuter hen is the same word Jesus uses in John 10:30 ("I and the Father are one" — hen esmen) and John 17:11 ("that they may be one" — hen osin). It describes an organic unity of purpose and work, not numerical identity.

Second: hekastos de ton idion misthon lepsetai kata ton idion kopon — "each will receive his own reward according to his own labor." The word misthon ("wages, reward, payment") introduces a concept Paul will develop in verses 10-15. Each worker will be evaluated and compensated individually, but the evaluation is based on kopon ("labor, toil, effort"), not on the number of followers each attracts. The word kopos in Paul's usage describes hard, often thankless work — in 1 Thessalonians 2:9, Paul recalls his "labor and toil" (kopon kai mochthon) among the Thessalonians, working night and day so as not to burden anyone. In 1 Corinthians 15:58, he will urge the Corinthians that their "labor in the Lord is not in vain" (ho kopos hymon ouk estin kenos en kyrio). The evaluation criteria is faithfulness in labor, not celebrity among disciples.

The juxtaposition of the two clauses is precise. The planter and waterer are hen ("one") in their corporate function — they serve the same field, the same God, the same purpose. Yet each will receive his idion ("own, individual") reward according to his idion ("own, individual") labor. Unity of mission does not erase individuality of accountability. Paul and Apollos are one in their service; they will be evaluated separately for the quality of that service.

"For we are God's fellow workers; you are God's field, God's building" (3:9).

This verse is the hinge between the agricultural metaphor (vv. 5-8) and the construction metaphor (vv. 10-17). Three possessive genitives, each beginning with theou ("of God"), hammer the same point:

Theou gar esmen synergoi — "for we are God's fellow workers." The word synergoi ("co-workers, collaborators") can be parsed two ways: (a) "workers together with God" — Paul and Apollos are God's co-laborers, working alongside God in his field; or (b) "fellow workers belonging to God" — Paul and Apollos are co-workers who belong to God, working together in his service. Either reading serves Paul's argument. The genitive theou establishes ownership: whether "co-workers with God" or "co-workers belonging to God," the workers belong to God, not to the Corinthians.

Theou georgion — "God's field." The word georgion (from ge, "earth/land," and ergon, "work") means a cultivated plot, a farm, a tilled field. The genitive theou makes God the owner. The Corinthians are God's cultivated land. The field does not belong to the farmer who plants it or the laborer who waters it; it belongs to the landowner. The entire factionalism is thereby exposed as a confusion of ownership. The Corinthians have been claiming to belong to their teachers ("I am of Paul," "I am of Apollos"); Paul says they belong to God.

Theou oikodome — "God's building." The word oikodome (from oikos, "house," and domo, "to build") means a construction, an edifice, a building in progress. The genitive theou again establishes ownership. The transition from georgion to oikodome shifts the metaphor from agriculture to construction, preparing for the extended building imagery of verses 10-17. The community is not only a field God tends but a structure God is constructing. Both metaphors serve the same purpose: the community belongs to God, and human workers are subordinate agents in God's project.

Verses 10-15: The Construction Metaphor — Foundation, Builders, and Fire

"According to the grace of God given to me, like a skilled master builder I laid a foundation, and someone else is building upon it. Let each one take care how he builds upon it" (3:10).

Paul now develops the oikodome metaphor. He describes his own role with precision: kata ten charin tou theou ten dotheisan moi — "according to the grace of God given to me." The grace (charis) was given (dotheisan, passive participle of didomi — a gift received, not a qualification earned). Paul's role as foundation-layer rests entirely on grace, not on personal merit or rhetorical skill. The phrase echoes 1:4, where Paul thanked God for "the grace of God that was given you" (te chariti tou theou te dotheise hymin). What Paul possesses, like what the Corinthians possess, is a gift.

The title sophos architekton ("skilled master builder") uses the word sophos ("wise, skilled") in its practical, artisanal sense — not the philosophical sophia he has been critiquing, but the craftsman's wisdom, the knowledge of how to build something properly. The architekton is the chief builder, the one who designs and oversees the construction, who ensures the foundation is correctly laid. In the LXX, the word appears in Isaiah 3:3, where among the skilled people God will remove from Jerusalem is the sophos architekton — the "skillful craftsman" or "expert builder." Paul claims this role for himself: he laid the foundation of the Corinthian community.

The word themelion ("foundation") is crucial. In the LXX, the word is used for the foundation of the temple: 1 Kings 5:17 (LXX 3 Kingdoms 5:17) describes Solomon laying the foundation (themelios) of the house of the LORD; Ezra 3:10-11 describes the laying of the temple foundation with priestly trumpets and Levitical cymbals, and the people shouting with a great shout when "the foundation of the house of the LORD was laid." Isaiah 28:16: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a foundation stone (lithon... eis ta themelia), a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation: whoever believes will not be put to shame." Paul's claim to have laid a themelion connects directly to the temple imagery he will invoke explicitly in verses 16-17. The Corinthian community is not just any building; it is the building that corresponds to the temple of God, and Paul laid its foundation.

The phrase allos de epoikodomei ("and another builds upon it") uses the present tense, indicating ongoing construction. Others are building now, on the foundation Paul laid. The word allos ("another") is indefinite and includes Apollos but is not limited to him. Multiple builders may contribute.

The warning blepeto... pos epoikodomei ("let each one take care how he builds") shifts the focus from the foundation (which is settled) to the superstructure (which is under construction). The verb blepeto ("let him watch, let him take care, let him be careful") is a command. The concern is not whether to build — building will happen — but how to build. The quality of the construction matters, and it is the builder's responsibility.

"For no one can lay a foundation other than the one that is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (3:11).

The foundation is identified with absolute clarity: themelion gar allon oudeis dynatai theinai para ton keimenon, hos estin Iesous Christos — "for no one is able to lay another foundation besides the one that is laid, which is Jesus Christ." The verb dynatai ("is able") with the negation oudeis ("no one") expresses impossibility. This is not a prohibition (you must not lay another foundation) but a statement of impossibility (no one can). The foundation has been laid, and it cannot be changed or replaced. The relative clause hos estin Iesous Christos ("which is Jesus Christ") names the foundation without ambiguity.

The present participle ton keimenon ("the one lying [there]") uses the present tense to describe the foundation as currently in place, already established, a fait accompli. This is the same Jesus Christ Paul preached: "Jesus Christ and him crucified" (2:2). The foundation is the proclamation of the crucified Messiah — not a philosophical system, not a rhetorical tradition, not a human teacher's wisdom. Any construction on the community must begin from this ground. Any builder who attempts to substitute a different foundation — a different basis for the community's identity — is attempting the impossible.

"Now if anyone builds on the foundation with gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, straw" (3:12).

Six materials, arranged in two groups of three. The first three — chrysion ("gold"), argyrion ("silver"), lithous timious ("precious stones") — are durable, fireproof, valuable. The second three — xyla ("wood"), chorton ("hay, grass"), kalamen ("straw, stubble") — are flammable, cheap, perishable. The contrast is not between good and evil but between enduring and perishable, between quality that survives testing and work that does not. The materials represent the quality of what is built on the foundation — the teaching, the leadership, the community formation that various builders contribute. Gold, silver, and precious stones are the materials of the temple itself: 1 Chronicles 29:2 lists David's provision for the temple as "gold... silver... bronze... iron... wood... onyx stones, stones for setting, antimony, stones of various colors, and all sorts of precious stones (lithous timious) and marble." 1 Kings 7:9-10 (LXX 3 Kingdoms 7:9-10) describes the temple's stones as "costly stones" (lithoi timioi). The durable materials evoke temple construction — appropriate, since Paul will identify the community as God's temple in verse 16.

Wood, hay, and straw, by contrast, are the materials of temporary structures and animal shelter. The image is vivid: some builders construct with temple-quality materials; others slap together something from field cuttings. Both build on the same foundation. The difference is in what they add to it.

The pronoun tis ("anyone") is indefinite and inclusive. This is not addressed exclusively to Paul and Apollos but to every teacher, leader, and contributor who builds on the community's foundation. The scope expands: anyone who exercises influence over the community's formation is a builder and will be held accountable for the quality of their work.

"Each one's work will become manifest, for the Day will disclose it, because it will be revealed by fire, and the fire itself will test what sort of work each one has done" (3:13).

The word hekastou ("of each one") individualizes the judgment. The assessment is personal and unavoidable. The phrase to ergon phaneron genesetai ("the work will become manifest, visible, apparent") uses the future tense: the quality of each builder's work, which may be unclear now, will become undeniably visible. The present obscurity of the quality is temporary.

The agent of disclosure is he hemera ("the Day"). The article he makes this a specific, recognized day — not just any day, but the Day. In Paul's usage, this connects directly to 1:8: "the day of our Lord Jesus Christ" (ten hemeran tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou). Behind this lies the prophetic concept of the yom YHWH — the day of the LORD — which in Amos 5:18-20, Joel 2:1-11, Zephaniah 1:14-18, Malachi 3:2, and Isaiah 2:12 is the day when God acts decisively in judgment and salvation. The Day will deloun ("disclose, make clear, reveal") the character of each builder's work.

The instrument of testing is pyr ("fire"). The phrase en pyri apokalyptetai ("is revealed by/in fire") uses the same root (apokalypto) as "the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" in 1:7. Fire in the prophetic tradition serves as God's instrument of purification and judgment. Malachi 3:2-3: "For he is like a refiner's fire and like fullers' soap. He will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver, and he will purify the sons of Levi and refine them like gold and silver." Isaiah 48:10: "Behold, I have refined you, but not as silver; I have tried you in the furnace of affliction." Zechariah 13:9: "I will put this third into the fire, and refine them as one refines silver, and test them as gold is tested." Fire in these texts does not annihilate indiscriminately; it separates what is durable from what is not. It consumes dross while purifying gold. The fire Paul describes functions identically: it tests (dokimasei, "will assay, will prove, will examine") the quality (hopoion, "what sort, what kind") of each builder's work.

The object of the fire's testing is the work, not the worker. The phrase to ergon hekastou hopoion estin ("what sort of work each one has done") focuses the judgment on the construction, not on the builder's person. This distinction is essential for understanding verses 14-15.

"If the work of anyone survives, he will receive a reward" (3:14).

The conditional ei tinos to ergon menei ("if anyone's work remains, survives, endures") uses the verb meno ("to remain, to abide, to survive"). The fire tests, and work built from gold, silver, and precious stones endures the test. The builder whose work survives misthon lepsetai ("will receive a reward"). The word misthon is the same as in verse 8 — "wages, payment, recompense for labor." The reward is not salvation itself (which is not described as a misthon anywhere in Paul's letters) but compensation for faithful and quality labor. The builder who constructed well will receive recognition and reward from the Lord who assigned the task (cf. 4:5: "then each one will receive his commendation from God").

"If anyone's work is burned up, he will suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire" (3:15).

The conditional ei tinos to ergon katakaesetai ("if anyone's work is burned up") describes the opposite outcome. The builder constructed with wood, hay, and straw; the fire consumes the entire superstructure. The builder zemioothesetai ("will suffer loss, will be penalized, will forfeit"). The verb zemioo means to incur damage, to be fined, to lose what one might have had. The loss is the misthon — the reward for quality work. The work is gone. The investment of labor produced nothing that endures.

But then the remarkable qualifier: autos de soothesetai ("he himself will be saved"). The builder whose work is destroyed is not destroyed with it. The pronoun autos ("he himself") is emphatic, distinguishing the builder from his work. The work perishes; the person survives. The verb soothesetai ("will be saved") is the future passive of sozo — the same word used throughout chapters 1-2 for salvation (1:18: "to us who are being saved"; 1:21: "to save those who believe"). The builder who built poorly is still saved.

The qualification houtos de hos dia pyros ("but in this manner — as through fire") describes the nature of the salvation. The image is vivid: a person escaping from a burning building, losing everything they built but getting out alive. The phrase hos ("as, like") makes this a comparison, not a literal description of the mechanism of salvation. The builder's salvation is real but bare — stripped of all the work they invested, saved with nothing to show for their labor, like someone who survives a fire but loses their house and everything in it.

This passage is addressed to builders — those who teach, lead, and form the community. The subject throughout has been the quality of ministry exercised by teachers within the community, not the spiritual condition of ordinary community members. The "work" that is tested is the work of building on the foundation — the teaching, formation, and leadership that contributes to the community's growth. Paul is warning teachers (including himself) that their ministry will be tested, and some will discover that their contribution, however extensive, was worthless — wood, hay, and straw rather than gold, silver, and precious stones. The warning applies directly to the faction problem: teaching that divides the community, that promotes personal followings, that operates by the criteria of worldly wisdom rather than the mind of Christ, may well be building with straw.

Verses 16-17: The Temple Warning — Destruction Brings Destruction

"Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?" (3:16).

The phrase ouk oidate ("do you not know?") is one of Paul's characteristic rhetorical devices in this letter. It appears ten times in 1 Corinthians (3:16, 5:6, 6:2, 6:3, 6:9, 6:15, 6:16, 6:19, 9:13, 9:24), always introducing something the Corinthians should already know but are failing to act upon. The formula conveys: this is not new information; this is a truth you already possess that should be governing your behavior but is not.

The claim: naos theou este — "you are God's temple." The word naos (not hieron) is specific. In Greek temple architecture, the hieron designated the entire temple complex — courts, porticos, surrounding structures. The naos designated the inner sanctuary, the dwelling place of the god, the holy of holies. In the LXX, naos is used for the innermost part of the temple where God's presence dwelled: 1 Kings 6:5 (LXX 3 Kingdoms 6:5) uses naos for the inner sanctuary of Solomon's temple; Psalm 18:6 (LXX 17:7): "from his temple (naou) he heard my voice"; Habakkuk 2:20: "the LORD is in his holy temple (en nao hagio autou); let all the earth keep silence before him." The naos is where God is present.

The pronoun hymeis ("you" — second person plural) is emphatic and corporate. Paul is not describing individual believers as individual temples; he is describing the community as a whole as God's temple. The "you" is the assembled community in Corinth. Together, collectively, as a body, they constitute the naos theou. This is confirmed by the next clause: to pneuma tou theou oikei en hymin — "the Spirit of God dwells in you [plural]." The verb oikei ("dwells, resides, inhabits") is the characteristic term for taking up residence. The Spirit has taken up residence in the community as God's presence once dwelled in the tabernacle and then the temple. The community is the location of God's presence on earth. The preposition en with the plural hymin means "among you" or "in you [as a group]" — the Spirit inhabits the community, not just individual members in isolation.

The temple identification carries extraordinary weight. The Jerusalem temple was the center of Israel's life — the place where heaven and earth intersected, where God's name dwelled (Deuteronomy 12:5: "the place that the LORD your God will choose... to put his name and make his habitation there"), where sacrifices were offered, where forgiveness was obtained, where pilgrims gathered. For Paul to say that a community of mostly Gentile believers in a pagan commercial city constitutes God's naos is a claim of staggering proportions. It also raises the stakes of the factionalism to the highest possible level: the quarreling is not merely disrupting a social club; it is tearing apart the dwelling place of God.

"If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple" (3:17).

The conditional ei tis ton naon tou theou phtheirei, phtherei touton ho theos — "if anyone destroys/corrupts God's temple, God will destroy/corrupt that person." The verb phtheiro has a range of meaning: "to corrupt, to ruin, to destroy, to spoil, to defile." Paul uses the same verb in both the protasis and apodosis: the action and the consequence mirror each other. Whoever corrupts God's temple will be corrupted by God. The lex talionis pattern — the punishment matching the crime — is deliberate. This is not an arbitrary punishment but an exact correspondence: what you do to God's dwelling, God does to you.

This warning must be distinguished from the scenario in verses 14-15. There, a builder whose work is poor (wood, hay, straw) suffers the loss of his work but is personally saved. Here, the person who destroys the temple is destroyed by God. The difference is between building poorly and destroying. One builder adds inadequate materials to the structure; the structure may be weakened, but the building still stands, and the builder, though losing his reward, survives. Another person actively corrupts or ruins the temple itself — not merely building poorly on it but tearing it apart, defiling it, destroying its integrity. That person faces not loss of reward but God's destruction.

In the immediate context, what would constitute destroying God's temple? Paul has been arguing since 1:10 that the community is being torn apart by factionalism. The schismata of 1:10 (the word for tearing cloth) are ripping the community — God's temple — into pieces. Anyone whose behavior or teaching divides the community, corrupts its unity, defiles the dwelling place of God's Spirit, is destroying God's temple. The warning is not abstract or eschatological only; it addresses the present situation directly. The faction-promoters, those who divide the community along the lines of teacher-allegiance, are engaging in temple-destruction. The consequence Paul announces is severe: phtherei touton ho theos — God will destroy that person.

The grounding for this severity: ho gar naos tou theou hagios estin — "for the temple of God is holy." The word hagios ("holy, sacred, set apart, consecrated") connects to the letter's opening: the Corinthians are "sanctified" (hegiasmenois) in Christ Jesus, "called saints" (kletois hagiois) (1:2). The holiness of the temple is the holiness of the community — both are set apart for God, consecrated by God's own action, belonging to God's sacred sphere. To violate what is holy is to invite the consequences that Israel's scriptures consistently attach to desecration. When Nadab and Abihu offered unauthorized fire before the LORD, fire came out from the LORD and consumed them (Leviticus 10:1-2). When Uzzah touched the ark, the LORD struck him down (2 Samuel 6:6-7). When Belshazzar used the vessels from God's temple for his banquet, the writing appeared on the wall and his kingdom was taken (Daniel 5:1-30). The principle is consistent: what is holy belongs to God, and violating it brings God's direct action.

The final clause hoitines este hymeis ("which [temple] you are") reiterates the corporate identification. The pronoun hymeis ("you" plural) is emphatic by position: you — the Corinthian community, the squabbling, faction-ridden, infant-like community — you are that holy temple. The weight falls on the incongruity: the temple is holy, and the people who are that temple are tearing it apart. The indictment could not be sharper.

Verses 18-23: The Final Inversion — All Things Are Yours

"Let no one deceive himself. If anyone among you thinks that he is wise in this age, let him become a fool that he may become wise" (3:18).

The phrase medeis heauton exapatato ("let no one deceive himself") uses the present imperative of exapatao ("to thoroughly deceive, to beguile"). The self-deception Paul targets is specific: ei tis dokei sophos einai en hymin en to aioni touto ("if anyone among you thinks he is wise in this age"). The verb dokei ("thinks, supposes, imagines") does not describe actual wisdom but the illusion of it. The qualifiers en hymin ("among you") and en to aioni touto ("in this age") locate the supposed wisdom within the community and within the present age's categories. Someone in the community fancies himself wise by the standards of this age — the same standards Paul has been demolishing since 1:18.

The prescription: moros genestho, hina genetai sophos — "let him become a fool, that he may become wise." The paradox is deliberate and matches the paradox of the cross throughout the letter. The path to genuine wisdom passes through what this age calls foolishness. The person who clings to this age's wisdom must release it — must accept the label "fool" from the world's perspective — in order to receive the wisdom that is from God. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is a reorientation of the epistemological framework. The mind of Christ (2:16) is not reached by escalating worldly wisdom to higher levels but by abandoning worldly wisdom's claims to sovereignty and receiving what God reveals through the Spirit.

"For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For it is written, 'He catches the wise in their craftiness'" (3:19).

Paul grounds the inversion in two scriptural quotations, both introduced as authoritative ("it is written").

The first is from Job 5:13 (LXX): ho drassomenos tous sophous en te panourgia auton — "he who seizes the wise in their craftiness." In the context of Job, these are the words of Eliphaz in his first speech. Eliphaz is describing God's power to overturn human plans: God "does great things and unsearchable, marvelous things without number" (Job 5:9); "he sets on high those who are lowly, and those who mourn are lifted to safety" (Job 5:11); "he catches the wise in their own craftiness, and the schemes of the wily are brought to a quick end" (Job 5:12-13). The word panourgia ("craftiness, cunning, shrewdness, clever scheming") describes intelligence deployed for self-serving ends — wisdom used as a tool for personal advantage. God does not merely outthink the cunning; he turns their very craftiness into the trap that catches them. Their own scheming becomes the instrument of their undoing.

This is precisely what happened at the cross. The rulers of this age, exercising their worldly wisdom and political power, crucified Jesus — and in doing so, unwittingly accomplished God's hidden plan (2:7-8). Their craftiness became the mechanism of their defeat. The Job quotation is not ornamental; it describes the actual pattern of the cross.

"And again, 'The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are futile'" (3:20).

The second quotation is from Psalm 94:11 (LXX 93:11), but Paul modifies it. The LXX reads: kyrios ginoskei tous dialogismous ton anthropon, hoti eisin mataioi — "the Lord knows the thoughts of human beings (anthropon), that they are futile." Paul substitutes ton sophon ("of the wise") for ton anthropon ("of human beings"). The modification is deliberate: Paul applies to the wise specifically what the psalm says of humanity generally. The Lord's knowledge is comprehensive and penetrating — he knows the dialogismous ("reasonings, deliberations, calculations") of the wise, and his verdict is that they are mataioi ("vain, empty, futile, purposeless"). The word mataios in the LXX describes what is insubstantial and accomplishes nothing. Ecclesiastes repeatedly uses the cognate mataiotes ("vanity, futility") for human endeavors that fail to achieve lasting significance. The Lord's evaluation of human wisdom — even at its most sophisticated — is that it produces nothing of enduring value. It is mataios.

Together, the two quotations complete the argument that began in 1:19 (Isaiah 29:14: "I will destroy the wisdom of the wise"). God catches the wise in their craftiness (Job 5:13). God knows the wise's thoughts are futile (Psalm 94:11). The wisdom of this age is not merely inferior to God's wisdom; it is actively self-defeating and ultimately empty. The Corinthians' attachment to human wisdom as the criterion for evaluating teachers is attachment to something God has declared futile.

"So let no one boast in men" (3:21a).

The conjunction hoste ("so, therefore, consequently") marks the conclusion drawn from everything in chapters 1-3. The prohibition medeis kauchastho en anthropois ("let no one boast in human beings") returns to the theme of 1:29 ("so that no flesh may boast before God") and 1:31 ("let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord"). The specific application is now clear: boasting "in men" (en anthropois) means boasting in human teachers — "I am of Paul," "I am of Apollos," "I am of Cephas." The faction slogans are a form of boasting in human beings. Paul declares it over.

"For all things are yours, whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas or the world or life or death or the present or the future — all are yours" (3:21b-22).

The reason no one should boast in particular teachers is not that the teachers are worthless but that all the teachers already belong to the community: panta gar hymon estin — "for all things are yours." The reversal is stunning. The Corinthians have been saying "I belong to Paul" or "I belong to Apollos" — treating themselves as possessions of their preferred teacher, defining their identity by attachment to a human leader. Paul inverts the ownership: Paul belongs to you. Apollos belongs to you. Cephas belongs to you. You do not belong to them; they belong to you. The faction slogans were exactly backward.

Paul lists the teachers first — eite Paulos eite Apollos eite Kephas — and then expands the scope of what belongs to the community to breathtaking proportions: eite kosmos eite zoe eite thanatos eite enestota eite mellonta — "whether the world or life or death or the present or the future." The world (kosmos) — the entire created order — belongs to the community. Life and death, the two ultimate realities of human existence, belong to the community. The present and the future — the totality of time — belong to the community. The scope is total. Nothing is excluded from the community's inheritance. This echoes Romans 8:38-39, where Paul declares that "neither death nor life... nor things present nor things to come" can separate the community from God's love.

The panta hymon estin ("all things are yours") rests on the community's identity as God's people, heirs of God's promises. In Genesis 1:28-30, God gave humanity dominion over all living things. In Psalm 8:6 (LXX 8:7): "You have given him dominion over the works of your hands; you have put all things under his feet." In Daniel 7:27: "the kingdom and the dominion and the greatness of the kingdoms under the whole heaven shall be given to the people of the saints of the Most High." The community of the Messiah inherits the dominion that belongs to God's people. Attaching oneself to one teacher as a faction-marker is not merely unnecessary; it is absurd — like an heir to the entire estate fighting over which servant he prefers, when all the servants already work for him.

"And you are Christ's, and Christ is God's" (3:23).

The chain of belonging reaches its climax. The Corinthians have been saying "I am of Paul" (ego Paulou). Paul has said "all things are yours" (panta hymon). Now: hymeis de Christou — "and you are Christ's." The Corinthians belong to Christ. Not to Paul, not to Apollos, not to Cephas. To Christ. The genitive Christou ("of Christ") is the genitive of belonging: you are Christ's possession, Christ's people, under Christ's lordship. This was already established in 1:2 (they are "sanctified in Christ Jesus") and 1:9 (they were "called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord"). The faction slogans misplaced the community's belonging; Paul restores it.

Christos de theou — "and Christ is God's." The chain terminates in God. All things belong to the community. The community belongs to Christ. Christ belongs to God. The entire hierarchy of belonging resolves into God as the ultimate possessor of all things. This is not a statement about Christ's subordination in nature or essence; it is a statement about the structure of the economy of salvation. In this letter, Paul will develop this theme further: in 11:3, "the head of Christ is God"; in 15:24-28, Christ will hand over the kingdom to God the Father, and "the Son himself will be subjected to the one who put all things in subjection under him, so that God may be all in all." The pattern is consistent: Christ's relationship to God is one of derivation and submission within the saving plan, just as the community's relationship to Christ is one of belonging and dependence. Everything points back to God, and everything proceeds from God. The faction slogans, which pointed to human teachers, are replaced by a chain that points to God through Christ.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 3 completes the argument begun in 1:10 and developed through chapters 1-2. The chapter performs five functions, each building on the last:

First (vv. 1-4), Paul applies the categories of chapter 2 to the Corinthians themselves. The pneumatikos/psychikos distinction was not abstract theory; it was a diagnostic tool. The diagnosis: the Corinthians are sarkinoi, nepioi, anthropoi — fleshly, infantile, merely human. The evidence is their factionalism. Every claim Paul made in chapter 2 about possessing the mind of Christ and discerning all things spiritually is now turned against the Corinthians: you should be exercising spiritual discernment, but your behavior proves you are not.

Second (vv. 5-9), Paul dismantles the basis of factionalism by redefining the role of teachers. They are not competing leaders to be ranked by followers; they are diakonoi (servants) performing tasks assigned by the Lord. The agricultural metaphor strips them of independent significance: the planter and the waterer are nothing; only God who gives the growth matters. The field belongs to God, not to the farmhands.

Third (vv. 10-15), Paul shifts to a construction metaphor and introduces accountability for builders. He laid the foundation (Jesus Christ), and others build on it. The quality of each builder's work will be tested by fire on the Day. Some work will survive; some will be consumed. The builder whose work burns loses his reward but is personally saved. This section addresses teachers directly: their ministry will be evaluated, and inferior work — including the kind that promotes factions and operates by worldly wisdom — will be exposed as worthless.

Fourth (vv. 16-17), Paul raises the stakes to their maximum height by identifying the community as God's temple — the naos, the inner sanctuary where God's Spirit dwells. Anyone who destroys this temple will be destroyed by God. The factionalism is not poor building technique; it is temple-destruction. It defiles and tears apart the dwelling place of God. The warning is the most severe in the letter so far.

Fifth (vv. 18-23), Paul issues the final prohibition against boasting in human beings and replaces the faction slogans with a chain of belonging that resolves in God. All things belong to the community — including the teachers they were fighting over. The community belongs to Christ. Christ belongs to God. The competitive attachment to human leaders is not only spiritually immature; it inverts the actual order of things. The teachers serve the community, not the reverse.

The three chapters together (1-3) form a single sustained argument: the Corinthian community was created by God's calling and grace (1:1-9); it has fractured along the lines of human teacher-allegiance (1:10-17); the theology of the cross exposes all human wisdom-boasting as incompatible with the gospel (1:18-31); Paul's own practice embodied this principle (2:1-5); God's wisdom is hidden from the world and revealed only through the Spirit (2:6-16); the Corinthians, despite possessing the Spirit, are behaving as though they do not (3:1-4); teachers are servants in God's field and builders on God's foundation (3:5-15); the community is God's holy temple, and destroying it brings God's judgment (3:16-17); therefore, stop boasting in human teachers, because all things already belong to you, you belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God (3:18-23).

The movement from 1:10 to 3:23 is a single arc: from the diagnosis of faction (1:10-12) through the theology that exposes its absurdity (1:13-2:16) to the direct application to the Corinthians (3:1-4) and the correct understanding of teachers (3:5-17) and finally to the proper ordering of all relationships under God (3:18-23). The argument is complete. Chapter 4 will add a personal appeal and a warning about Paul's forthcoming visit, but the theological case against factionalism is finished by 3:23.
