Mark 10 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Mark 9): The chapter opened with the transfiguration — a divine endorsement of Jesus as "My beloved Son" with the command "listen to Him" (9:7). The command to listen is pointed because the last thing Jesus taught was the first passion prediction (8:31-33): the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and rise. The chapter then narrated a sequence that exposed the disciples' persistent failure to grasp what Jesus was teaching. They could not cast out a spirit because "this kind cannot come out by anything except prayer" (9:29) — their power was inadequate to the task, and they had not sought God's. Jesus issued his second passion prediction: "The Son of Man is to be handed over to men, and they will kill Him; and when He has been killed, He will rise three days later" (9:31). The disciples "did not understand" and "were afraid to ask Him" (9:32). Their incomprehension immediately manifested in a dispute about which of them was the greatest (9:34). Jesus responded with a radical redefinition of greatness: "If anyone wants to be first, he shall be last of all and servant of all" (9:35). He took a child and placed the child among them as a living illustration: "Whoever receives one child like this in My name receives Me" (9:37). The chapter closed with warnings about causing "little ones who believe" to stumble (9:42), followed by the severe teaching on self-amputation — cutting off the hand, foot, or eye that causes one to sin rather than being "thrown into hell" (9:43-48). The final image was salt: "Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another" (9:50). The chapter thus ended with a cluster of themes — greatness redefined as service, children as models of reception, the radical cost of avoiding sin, and peace among disciples — that Mark 10 will develop in a sustained and structured way.

What the author argues immediately after (Mark 11): Jesus arrives in Jerusalem — the destination announced in 10:32-34. He enters riding a colt, and the crowds shout "Hosanna! Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord" and "Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David" (11:9-10), quoting Psalm 118:25-26. The crowd's expectation is royal and Davidic. Jesus enters the temple, looks around at everything, and leaves (11:11) — a deliberate act of inspection before judgment. The next day he curses a fig tree that has leaves but no fruit (11:12-14) and drives the merchants from the temple, declaring that the house of prayer for all nations has become a den of robbers (11:15-17). The chief priests and scribes begin seeking how to destroy him (11:18). When the disciples notice the withered fig tree, Jesus teaches about faith, prayer, and forgiveness (11:22-26). The religious leaders then challenge Jesus's authority (11:27-33), and he refuses to answer because they refused to answer honestly about John's baptism. The movement from Mark 10 to Mark 11 is the movement from the road to the destination, from teaching about the kingdom's values to enacting the kingdom's judgment on the institution that should have embodied those values.

How chapter 10 connects them: Mark 9 ended with the disciples arguing about greatness, Jesus placing a child among them, and warnings about the radical cost of sin. Mark 11 will bring Jesus to Jerusalem where the cross awaits and the religious establishment will be judged. Mark 10 is the road between these two points — geographically (Jesus moves from the Jordan region through Jericho toward Jerusalem), thematically (the chapter systematically defines what the kingdom of God requires and what it excludes), and dramatically (Jesus issues his third passion prediction and the disciples demonstrate, for the final time on the road, that they still do not understand what following him means). The chapter proceeds through five episodes that all address the same question from different angles: what does it take to enter the kingdom of God? The Pharisees test Jesus on divorce, and he appeals to God's original design over Mosaic concession. Children are brought forward, and Jesus declares that the kingdom belongs to those who receive it as children do. A wealthy man asks about eternal life and walks away because the cost is too high. The disciples are astonished that the wealthy cannot easily enter the kingdom. James and John request positions of honor and are told that greatness is servanthood and that the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom. Finally, a blind beggar cries out, is healed by faith, and follows Jesus on the road — providing a living contrast to every person in the chapter who could not or would not follow. The chapter is the author's final, comprehensive statement of what it means to enter God's kingdom before the story arrives at the place where the kingdom's cost will be paid in full.

Verses 1-12: Marriage, Divorce, and God's Original Design

"Setting out from there, Jesus went to the region of Judea and beyond the Jordan; crowds gathered to Him again, and, as He was accustomed, He once more began to teach them."

The geographic note marks a major transition. Jesus has been operating in Galilee and its surrounding regions since 1:14. Now he moves south, into Judea and the region beyond the Jordan (Perea). The destination is Jerusalem, as 10:32 will make explicit. The author's phrase "as He was accustomed" (hos eiothei) signals continuity of practice — Jesus's public teaching is a pattern, not an occasional event. The crowds gather "again" (palin), indicating that this is the same kind of public reception he has had throughout the Galilean ministry.

"And some Pharisees came up to Jesus, testing Him, and began questioning Him whether it was lawful for a man to divorce his wife."

The word "testing" (peirazontes) is the same verb used for Satan's testing of Jesus in the wilderness (1:13) and the Pharisees' demand for a sign (8:11). The author consistently marks hostile challenges with this word. The question itself — "Is it lawful (exestin) for a man to divorce his wife?" — was a live debate in first-century Judaism. The school of Shammai restricted divorce to cases of sexual immorality; the school of Hillel permitted it for virtually any cause, including a burned meal (Mishnah Gittin 9:10, though codified later, reflects positions that circulated in the first century). The question is designed to force Jesus into one camp or the other, or to produce a statement that could be used against him. The geographic setting "beyond the Jordan" adds political danger: this is Herod Antipas's territory, and Antipas had recently executed John the Baptist for criticizing his unlawful marriage to Herodias (6:17-29). A public statement about the lawfulness of divorce in Herod's jurisdiction is a politically charged act.

"And He answered and said to them, 'What did Moses command you?' They said, 'Moses permitted a man to write a certificate of divorce and send his wife away.'"

Jesus does not answer the question directly. Instead he responds with a counter-question that forces the Pharisees to state the textual basis for their position. They cite Deuteronomy 24:1-4, which describes the procedure for divorce: if a man finds "some indecency" in his wife, he writes her a certificate of divorce and sends her away. It is important to note what Deuteronomy 24 actually says. The passage does not command divorce; it regulates an existing practice. The commandment in Deuteronomy 24 is actually in verse 4: a divorced woman who has married another man cannot return to her first husband, "for that is an abomination before the Lord." The legislation assumes divorce happens and seeks to prevent a specific consequence (the woman being treated as property shuttled between men). The Pharisees, however, characterize this as Moses "permitting" (epetrepsen) divorce — they read the procedural regulation as authorization.

"But Jesus said to them, 'Because of your hardness of heart he wrote you this commandment.'"

Jesus reframes the Mosaic legislation. The divorce provision is not an expression of God's ideal but a concession to human failure. The phrase "hardness of heart" (sklerokardia) is a diagnosis that carries enormous weight in Israel's story. The hardened heart is the defining characteristic of Pharaoh, who saw God's mighty acts and refused to relent (Exodus 7:13, 22; 8:15, 19, 32; 9:7, 12, 34-35). It is the condition of the wilderness generation that tested God despite having witnessed his provision (Psalm 95:8, LXX 94:8: "Do not harden your hearts as at Meribah"). And within this very document, the author has used the same diagnosis for the disciples: after the first feeding of the multitude, "their heart was hardened" (6:52); after the second feeding, Jesus asked them, "Do you have a hardened heart?" (8:17). The hardness of heart is not merely stubbornness; it is the spiritual condition in which a person has been exposed to God's actions and purposes and still cannot perceive them. Moses wrote the divorce legislation because the people's hearts were already impervious to God's original design.

"But from the beginning of creation, God created them male and female. For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother, and the two shall become one flesh; so they are no longer two, but one flesh. Therefore, what God has joined together, no person is to separate."

Jesus appeals over Moses to creation itself. The move is significant: he does not dispute that Moses permitted divorce, but he relativizes that permission by placing it within a larger narrative. The concession came because of hardened hearts; God's actual purpose is stated in Genesis. Jesus quotes two texts: Genesis 1:27 ("God created them male and female") and Genesis 2:24 ("a man shall leave his father and mother, and the two shall become one flesh"). The first establishes that the male-female pairing is part of creation's design — not an afterthought or a social arrangement, but an act of God in making humanity. The second establishes that marriage creates a new ontological unity: "the two shall become one flesh" (hoi duo eis sarka mian). The word "one" (mian) is emphatic — what was two separate persons has become a single entity. Jesus then draws his conclusion: "What God has joined together (ho theos synezeuxen), no person is to separate (anthropos me chorizeto)." The verb synezeugnymi means to yoke together, as oxen are yoked to a plow. God is the one who performs the joining; human beings are prohibited from undoing it.

The structure of Jesus's argument is worth noting. He acknowledges what Moses said but treats it as a sub-category within the larger story. The hierarchy is: God's original purpose in creation stands above Moses's legislative concession to human failure. This is not a rejection of Torah; it is an interpretation that reads Torah in light of its own opening chapters. The argument assumes that Genesis 1-2 establishes the normative framework and that later provisions must be understood in relation to that norm. The Pharisees read Deuteronomy 24 as the governing text; Jesus reads Genesis 1-2 as the governing text and Deuteronomy 24 as evidence of the problem.

"And in the house the disciples again began questioning Him about this. And He said to them, 'Whoever divorces his wife and marries another woman commits adultery against her; and if she herself divorces her husband and marries another man, she is committing adultery.'"

The private instruction to the disciples is harsher than the public teaching. In the public exchange, Jesus argued from creation theology that marriage is indissoluble. In private, he names the consequence: remarriage after divorce is adultery (moicheia). The statement is blunt and admits no exceptions in this text. The phrase "commits adultery against her" (moichatai ep' auten) is striking — in Jewish legal tradition, a man could commit adultery against another man by sleeping with his wife, but he could not technically commit adultery against his own wife. Jesus overturns this asymmetry. The husband who divorces and remarries commits adultery against the wife he has put away. The woman's dignity and standing are recognized in a way that the prevailing legal framework did not accommodate.

The addition that a woman who divorces her husband and remarries also commits adultery reflects a legal context broader than Palestinian Judaism, where women generally could not initiate divorce. In Roman law, however, women could. The author is writing for an audience that includes Gentile believers who live under Roman legal norms. The symmetry of the statement — both husband and wife are held to the same standard — reinforces the creation-theology argument: both are equally bound by the one-flesh union God has made.

Verses 13-16: Children and the Kingdom — Who Can Receive It

"And they were bringing children to Him so that He would touch them; but the disciples rebuked them."

The transition from the divorce teaching to the children episode is not accidental. The author has just presented Jesus's teaching on marriage, which grounded human relationships in God's original creative act. Now people bring children (paidia) — the direct result of the male-female union — for Jesus's touch. The disciples' response is to rebuke (epetimesan) them. The verb epitimao is the same word used for Jesus's silencing of demons (1:25; 3:12), his calming of the storm (4:39), and Peter's attempt to redirect him from the cross (8:32, where Peter "began to rebuke" Jesus). It is a word of sharp authority. The disciples treat the children's approach as an interruption, an intrusion into serious business.

"But when Jesus saw this, He was indignant and said to them, 'Allow the children to come to Me; do not forbid them, for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these.'"

Jesus responds with indignation (eganaktesen). This is the only place in Mark where Jesus is described as indignant with the disciples (the word will appear again at 10:41, but there it is the ten who are indignant with James and John). The disciples have made a grave error: they have placed themselves as gatekeepers to Jesus, deciding who merits access and who does not. And they have excluded precisely the category of person Jesus declared, just one chapter earlier, to be the model of receiving him: "Whoever receives one child like this in My name receives Me" (9:37). The disciples heard that teaching and immediately failed to practice it.

"The kingdom of God belongs to such as these" (ton gar toiouton estin he basileia tou theou). The phrase "such as these" (toiouton) indicates that children are not merely permitted into the kingdom — they are its characteristic inhabitants. The kingdom does not merely include children; it belongs to the kind of person a child represents.

"Truly I say to you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child will not enter it at all."

The "truly I say to you" (amen lego hymin) formula marks this as a solemn pronouncement. The statement operates as a condition with an absolute consequence: receiving the kingdom like a child is the prerequisite for entering it. The negative formulation — "whoever does not... will not enter it at all" (ou me eiselthe eis auten) — uses the double negative (ou me) for the strongest possible denial. There is no entrance for those who do not receive as children do.

What does it mean to receive the kingdom "like a child" (hos paidion)? The author has not defined this, but the context provides strong clues. In the first century, children had no social standing, no legal rights, no capacity to earn or achieve. A child does not negotiate the terms of reception; a child does not bring credentials or accomplishments. A child receives what is given because a child has nothing to offer in exchange. The contrast with the wealthy man who follows immediately (vv. 17-22) makes the meaning concrete: the rich man has everything and cannot enter; the child has nothing and the kingdom belongs to him. The kingdom is received, not achieved. It comes as gift, not as wage.

"And He took them in His arms and began blessing them, laying His hands on them."

Jesus does not merely permit the children's approach; he embraces them (enagkalisamenos — taking into his arms, the same word used at 9:36). He blesses them and lays hands on them. The gesture is deliberate and tender. The king of the kingdom does not stand above its smallest members; he gathers them into his arms. The disciples wanted to send them away; Jesus takes them in.

Verses 17-31: The Wealthy Man and the Impossibility of Entering the Kingdom

The Man's Question and Jesus's Response (vv. 17-22)

"As He was setting out on a journey, a man ran up to Him and knelt before Him, and asked Him, 'Good Teacher, what shall I do so that I may inherit eternal life?'"

The phrase "as He was setting out on a journey" (ekporeuomenou autou eis hodon) marks the resumption of physical movement toward Jerusalem. The man's approach is urgent and reverential: he runs and kneels. His question — "What shall I do so that I may inherit eternal life?" — combines two concepts that sit in tension. The verb "do" (poieso) implies action, effort, achievement. The verb "inherit" (kleronomeso) implies reception of something given, not earned. An inheritance is bestowed by the will of another, not purchased by the heir's labor. The man's question reveals a confusion at its core: he wants to do something to earn what can only be received. This tension connects directly to the preceding passage: the kingdom belongs to those who receive it like children.

The address "Good Teacher" (didaskale agathe) provokes Jesus's first response.

"But Jesus said to him, 'Why do you call Me good? No one is good except God alone.'"

This statement has been the subject of much later theological speculation, but within the flow of the author's argument, its function is to redirect the man's attention. The man has come with flattery and a question about what he can do. Jesus's counter-question forces him to reckon with the meaning of his own words. If "good" belongs to God alone, then calling Jesus "good" is either careless flattery or an unwitting confession of something the man has not considered. Jesus does not deny being good; he challenges the man to think about what he is saying.

"'You know the commandments: Do not murder, Do not commit adultery, Do not steal, Do not give false testimony, Do not defraud, Honor your father and mother.'"

Jesus lists commandments from the second table of the Decalogue — the commandments governing relationships between human beings (Exodus 20:12-16; Deuteronomy 5:16-20). He omits the first table (no other gods, no idols, no taking God's name in vain, Sabbath observance) — the commandments governing the relationship between human beings and God. This omission will prove significant. The one commandment not found in the standard Decalogue list is "Do not defraud" (me apostere-ses). This may refer to Deuteronomy 24:14 ("You shall not oppress a hired servant who is poor and needy") or to the broader Torah prohibitions against economic exploitation (Leviticus 19:13: "You shall not oppress your neighbor, nor rob him. The wages of a hired worker are not to remain with you all night until morning"). The inclusion of this command may be tailored to the man's particular situation — as the narrative will reveal, he is wealthy, and Jesus may be testing whether his wealth was gained justly.

"And he said to Him, 'Teacher, I have kept all these things from my youth.'"

The man's response is confident and apparently sincere. He has observed the commandments that govern human relationships from a young age. The author does not dispute this claim, and neither does Jesus.

"Looking at him, Jesus showed love to him and said to him, 'One thing you lack: go and sell all you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow Me.'"

The author's note that Jesus "showed love to him" (egapesen auton) is remarkable. This is the only time in the document that the author describes Jesus loving a specific individual. The love is not contingent on the man's future response — Jesus loves him before the man answers, indeed before the man fails. The look (emblepsas auto) is the same penetrating gaze described in other pivotal moments (see 8:33, where Jesus "looked at His disciples" before rebuking Peter).

Jesus identifies "one thing" (hen) lacking. The man has kept the second table of the law — the commandments governing human relationships. But the first table — the commandments governing relationship with God — remains untested. The instruction to sell everything and give to the poor is not a general requirement imposed on all followers (elsewhere in the document, people follow Jesus while retaining their homes — Simon and Andrew's house in Capernaum serves as a base of operations throughout the Galilean ministry). This is a specific diagnosis directed at this specific man. His possessions are the thing that stands between him and total allegiance to God. The first commandment is "You shall have no other gods before Me" (Exodus 20:3). Jesus is testing whether this man's wealth functions as his god.

"You will have treasure in heaven" — the language of heavenly treasure contrasts with earthly possessions. The exchange Jesus proposes is not loss but transfer: what the man relinquishes on earth he gains in heaven. The final words — "and come, follow Me" (deuro akolouthei moi) — use the same call language from the beginning of the document. "Follow Me" is the summons issued to Simon and Andrew (1:17), to Levi (2:14). The call to discipleship is the same; the cost, for this man, includes everything he owns.

"But he was deeply dismayed by these words, and he went away grieving; for he was one who owned much property."

The man's response is grief (lypoumenos) and departure. He "went away" (apelthen) — the opposite of following. The author explains: "he was one who owned much property" (en gar echon ktemata polla). The explanation is causal: his possessions caused his departure. He could not do the one thing Jesus asked because the one thing Jesus asked required him to abandon what he valued most. The children of the preceding passage had nothing and the kingdom was theirs; this man has everything and the kingdom slips from his grasp. The contrast is precise and devastating.

The Disciples' Astonishment and Jesus's Teaching on Wealth (vv. 23-27)

"And Jesus, looking around, said to His disciples, 'How hard it will be for those who are wealthy to enter the kingdom of God!'"

Jesus draws a general principle from the specific case. The difficulty is not unique to this man; it applies to everyone who possesses wealth. The phrase "kingdom of God" (basileia tou theou) ties back to the children passage: the kingdom belongs to those who receive it like children (v. 14), and those with wealth find entering it extraordinarily difficult.

"And the disciples were amazed at His words."

The disciples' amazement (ethambounton) reveals their assumptions. In first-century Judaism, wealth was widely understood as a sign of divine blessing. Deuteronomy 28 explicitly promised material prosperity as a reward for covenant faithfulness: abundant crops, numerous livestock, overflowing storehouses (Deuteronomy 28:1-14). The Psalms frequently associate the righteous with prosperity (Psalm 1:3: "He will be like a tree firmly planted by streams of water, which yields its fruit in its season"). If wealth is a sign of God's favor, then the wealthy should be first in line for the kingdom. Jesus has just overturned this assumption. If the rich find it hard to enter, the entire calculus of blessing and merit collapses.

"But Jesus responded again and said to them, 'Children, how hard it is to enter the kingdom of God!'"

Jesus addresses the disciples as "children" (tekna) — the same category he has just elevated as models of kingdom reception. The address is tender but pointed: even you, who have left your nets and tax booths, must hear this. And the statement broadens: it is not only hard for the wealthy; it is hard for anyone. The difficulty of entering the kingdom is universal.

"It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God."

The image is deliberately absurd. The largest animal familiar to the audience is forced through the smallest opening they know. There is no evidence from first-century sources for a "small gate" interpretation — no gate called "the eye of the needle" has been identified in first-century Jerusalem, and such an explanation would domesticate Jesus's deliberate hyperbole. The point is impossibility, not difficulty. A camel cannot go through the eye of a needle. Period.

"And they were even more astonished, and said to Him, 'Then who can be saved?'"

The disciples' question reveals that they have understood Jesus correctly. If the wealthy — those with every visible marker of divine blessing — cannot enter, then who can? The shift from "enter the kingdom of God" to "be saved" (sothenai) indicates that the disciples understand these as equivalent. Entering the kingdom and being saved are the same reality. Their question is not rhetorical; it is a genuine expression of dismay.

"Looking at them, Jesus said, 'With people it is impossible, but not with God; for all things are possible with God.'"

The answer is the chapter's theological center. The word "impossible" (adynaton) confirms what the camel-and-needle image implied: human beings cannot save themselves. No amount of commandment-keeping, wealth-accumulating, or effort-exerting can produce entrance into the kingdom. The impossibility is absolute on the human side. But "with God" (para theo), all things are possible. The phrase echoes Genesis 18:14, where YHWH asks Sarah, "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" — a question posed in the context of an impossible birth (Isaac from a barren, elderly woman). God's capacity to accomplish the impossible is grounded in Israel's experience: God brought a nation from a barren womb, bread from heaven, water from rock, freedom from Egypt. The salvation Jesus describes is in the same category: it is God's work, not human achievement. This is the theological ground of the children-passage: the kingdom is received because it cannot be earned.

Peter's Question and Jesus's Promise (vv. 28-31)

"Peter began to say to Him, 'Behold, we have left everything and have followed You.'"

Peter's statement is partly a question and partly a claim. The wealthy man could not leave everything; Peter and the others did. The implicit question is: what then is our situation? Have we met the requirement?

"Jesus said, 'Truly I say to you, there is no one who has left house or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or farms, for My sake and for the gospel's sake, but that he will receive a hundred times as much now in the present age, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and farms, along with persecutions; and in the age to come, eternal life.'"

The "truly I say to you" (amen lego hymin) marks another solemn declaration. Jesus affirms that sacrifice for his sake and the gospel's sake will be repaid — abundantly, a hundredfold. The list of what is sacrificed (house, family members, farms) corresponds to the list of what is received, but with two critical additions.

First, the phrase "along with persecutions" (meta diogmon) interrupts the promise of abundance. The hundredfold return comes not in a context of ease but in a context of suffering. The new community of believers provides houses, family, and fields (the early communities of shared possessions described in Acts 2 and 4 would fulfill this concretely), but it provides them under the conditions of persecution. The promise is not prosperity theology; it is community-amid-suffering theology. The author refuses to separate blessing from cost.

Second, the temporal distinction between "now in the present age" (nyn en to kairo touto) and "in the age to come" (en to aioni to erchomeno) is fundamental to first-century Jewish eschatology. The present age is the era of trial, persecution, and partial fulfillment. The age to come is the era of full restoration. In the present age, the disciple receives community and persecution together. In the age to come, the disciple receives eternal life (zoen aionion) — the very thing the wealthy man asked about in verse 17. What the wealthy man wanted to earn by doing, the disciple receives by sacrificing.

"But many who are first will be last, and the last, first."

This reversal saying closes the section and connects it back to 9:35: "If anyone wants to be first, he shall be last of all and servant of all." The wealthy man was first by every worldly measure — possessions, commandment-keeping, social standing — and he went away empty. The children, who were last by every worldly measure, had the kingdom given to them. The disciples, who left everything, will receive abundantly. But the reversal is not a new hierarchy that replaces the old; it is a warning that the calculus of first and last is always subject to God's rearrangement. "Many" (polloi) who are first will be last — not all, but many. Position is not permanently secured by any human calculation.

Verses 32-34: The Third Passion Prediction — The Road Narrows

"Now they were on the road going up to Jerusalem, and Jesus was walking on ahead of them; and they were amazed, and those who followed were fearful."

The geographic detail is vivid and loaded with meaning. They are on the road (en te hodo) — the same word (hodos) that has structured the journey narrative since 8:27 ("on the way" to Caesarea Philippi). Jerusalem is the destination, and Jesus is "walking on ahead" (en proagon autous). The verb proago means to go before, to lead the way. Jesus does not lag behind his followers or walk among them; he strides ahead, leading them toward the place where he will die. The two emotional responses — amazement from the disciples and fear from "those who followed" (a larger group) — suggest that something about Jesus's bearing communicates the gravity of what lies ahead. The man walking at the front of the column knows where the road ends.

"And again He took the twelve aside and began to tell them what was going to happen to Him, saying, 'Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of Man will be handed over to the chief priests and the scribes; and they will condemn Him to death and will hand Him over to the Gentiles. And they will mock Him and spit on Him, and flog Him and kill Him; and three days later He will rise from the dead.'"

This is the third passion prediction in the document, and it is the most detailed. Each prediction has added specificity:

  • First prediction (8:31): "The Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again."
  • Second prediction (9:31): "The Son of Man is to be handed over to men, and they will kill Him; and when He has been killed, He will rise three days later."
  • Third prediction (10:33-34): "The Son of Man will be handed over to the chief priests and the scribes; and they will condemn Him to death and will hand Him over to the Gentiles. And they will mock Him and spit on Him, and flog Him and kill Him; and three days later He will rise from the dead."

The progression is telling. The first prediction named the agents of rejection (elders, chief priests, scribes) and stated that Jesus would be killed. The second simplified the agency to "men" and emphasized the "handing over" (paradidotai). The third now adds three elements: (1) the Jewish leaders will condemn him to death and then hand him to the Gentiles — implicating both Jew and Gentile in the killing; (2) the specific forms of abuse are itemized — mocking, spitting, flogging — the details of Roman punishment; (3) the language of being "handed over" (paradosousin) is used twice, creating a chain of transfer: Jewish leaders hand him to Gentiles, who then carry out the execution. The vocabulary of mocking, spitting, and flogging echoes the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 50:6: "I gave My back to those who strike Me, and My cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I did not cover My face from insults and spitting." The author's Jesus describes his coming death in language drawn from Isaiah's servant songs.

Each of the three predictions has been followed by a demonstration of the disciples' failure to understand. After the first, Peter rebuked Jesus (8:32). After the second, the disciples argued about which of them was the greatest (9:34). After the third, James and John will request seats of honor (10:35-37). The pattern is consistent and deliberate: Jesus announces his death, and the disciples immediately reveal that they are thinking about status. The gap between what Jesus is saying and what the disciples are hearing is the central dramatic tension of this section of the document.

Verses 35-45: James and John's Request and the Redefinition of Power

"James and John, the two sons of Zebedee, came up to Jesus, saying to Him, 'Teacher, we want You to do for us whatever we ask of You.' And He said to them, 'What do you want Me to do for you?' They said to Him, 'Grant that we may sit, one on Your right and one on Your left, in Your glory.'"

The timing is extraordinary. Jesus has just described in graphic detail how he will be mocked, spit upon, flogged, and killed. James and John respond by requesting the two highest positions in his coming kingdom. Their request reveals what they heard in the passion prediction: not the suffering, but the glory. They heard "three days later He will rise from the dead" and concluded that the resurrection means enthronement. They want to secure the best seats now, before the other disciples can claim them.

The phrase "in Your glory" (en te doxe sou) shows they are thinking of the Son of Man's future exaltation — the same glory mentioned in 8:38 ("when He comes in the glory of His Father with the holy angels"). They believe Jesus will arrive at that glory and they want to be next to him when he does. They are not wrong that glory is coming; they are catastrophically wrong about the path to it.

Note also that their opening demand — "we want You to do for us whatever we ask" — is a blank-check request, the language of entitlement. It contrasts sharply with Bartimaeus's approach later in the chapter (v. 51), where Jesus asks the same question — "What do you want Me to do for you?" — to a blind beggar who asks only for mercy.

"But Jesus said to them, 'You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, or to be baptized with the baptism with which I am baptized?'"

Jesus introduces two metaphors for his approaching death.

The cup (to poterion). In Israel's prophetic literature, the cup is a consistent image for God's judgment. Isaiah 51:17: "Rouse yourself! Rouse yourself! Arise, O Jerusalem, you who have drunk from the LORD's hand the cup of His anger; the chalice of staggering you have drained to the dregs." Jeremiah 25:15-16: "Take this cup of the wine of wrath from My hand and cause all the nations to whom I send you to drink it. They will drink and stagger and go insane because of the sword that I am sending among them." Psalm 75:8 (LXX 74:9): "For a cup is in the hand of the LORD, and the wine foams... surely all the wicked of the earth must drain and drink down its dregs." The cup Jesus drinks is the cup of divine wrath poured out on sin. This image will recur at Gethsemane: "Abba! Father! All things are possible for You; remove this cup from Me" (14:36). The cup is not a metaphor for generic suffering; it is the specific cup of God's judgment.

Baptism (to baptisma). Baptism as a metaphor for overwhelming catastrophe draws on the imagery of drowning in deep waters. Psalm 69:1-2 (LXX 68:2-3): "Save me, O God, for the waters have threatened my life. I have sunk in deep mire, and there is no foothold; I have come into deep waters, and a flood overflows me." Psalm 42:7 (LXX 41:8): "All Your breakers and Your waves have passed over me." Isaiah 43:2: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and through the rivers, they will not overflow you." To be baptized with Jesus's baptism is to be overwhelmed by the same catastrophic suffering he will undergo.

"They said to Him, 'We are able.' And Jesus said to them, 'The cup that I drink you shall drink; and you shall be baptized with the baptism with which I am baptized.'"

James and John declare themselves able without understanding what they have agreed to. Their confidence mirrors Peter's declaration in 14:29-31 ("Even if all fall away, I will not... even if I have to die with You, I will not deny You"). The disciples are consistently willing to promise what they have not yet comprehended. Jesus does not dispute their claim; he confirms it. They will indeed drink his cup and share his baptism. James was the first of the twelve to be martyred (Acts 12:2, killed by Herod Agrippa). John, according to early tradition, endured exile and persecution. The prediction comes true — they share in Jesus's suffering, as he said they would.

"But to sit on My right or on My left is not Mine to give; but it is for those for whom it has been prepared."

Jesus affirms that the positions of honor exist but denies that he distributes them. They have been "prepared" (hetoimastai) — the passive voice implies that God has prepared them. The statement is a reminder that the kingdom's arrangements belong to God's sovereignty, not to any human negotiation, and not even to Jesus's personal prerogative in this context. The disciples cannot lobby for position. The structure of the kingdom is determined by God, and Jesus does not override that arrangement even when directly petitioned.

"Hearing this, the other ten began to feel indignant with James and John."

The ten are indignant (eganaktesen) — the same word used for Jesus's indignation when the disciples turned away the children (v. 14). But the ten's indignation is not righteous. They are not angry because James and John misunderstood the kingdom; they are angry because James and John tried to claim positions the ten wanted for themselves. The argument about who is greatest (9:34) is still running.

"Calling them to Himself, Jesus said to them, 'You know that those who are recognized as rulers of the Gentiles domineer over them; and their people in high position exercise authority over them. But it is not this way among you; rather, whoever wants to become prominent among you shall be your servant; and whoever wants to be first among you shall be slave of all.'"

Jesus assembles all twelve and delivers a teaching that directly addresses the greatness dispute from 9:33-34 and the power-grab of James and John. His argument proceeds in three moves:

First, he describes how power operates in the Gentile world. The rulers "domineer" (katakyrieousin) and the great ones "exercise authority" (katexousiazousin). Both verbs carry the prefix kata-, which intensifies the action: this is not mere governance but domination, the exercise of power over others for the benefit of the powerful. The first-century audience knew this pattern intimately — Rome's client kings, tax collectors, military governors, and the entire apparatus of imperial control operated on this principle.

Second, he states flatly: "It is not this way among you" (ouch houtos de estin en hymin). The Gentile model of power is explicitly excluded from the community Jesus is forming. The sentence is prescriptive, not merely descriptive. This is not how things happen to be; this is how things must not be.

Third, he inverts the hierarchy. The one who wants prominence (megas) must become a servant (diakonos). The one who wants to be first (protos) must become a slave (doulos). The word diakonos means one who serves at table, who attends to another's needs. The word doulos means a slave — a person with no rights, no standing, no claim to dignity by worldly measures. Jesus does not abolish the desire for greatness; he redefines it. Greatness is measured by service, and the greatest is the one who serves most completely.

"For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many."

This verse is the theological climax of the chapter and one of the most concentrated christological statements in the document.

"The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve." The Son of Man — the figure from Daniel 7:13-14 to whom "dominion, glory, and a kingdom" are given, and whom "all the peoples, nations, and languages" serve (Daniel 7:14, LXX: elatreusan auto) — came not to receive that service but to render it. The inversion is total. The one to whom all service is owed becomes the one who serves all. The disciples want to sit in positions of honor beside a glorified ruler; the ruler came to kneel and serve.

"To give His life as a ransom for many" (dounai ten psychen autou lytron anti pollon). The word lytron means a ransom-price, the payment made to release a captive or redeem a slave. In the LXX, the related words lytron, lytrosis, and lytroo are used for Israel's redemption from Egypt (Exodus 6:6: "I will redeem you with an outstretched arm"; Deuteronomy 7:8: "the LORD redeemed you... from the house of slavery"). The exodus paradigm is the background: God paid a price — in plagues, in the Passover lamb, in the sea's destruction of Pharaoh's army — to free his people from bondage. Jesus's life is the ransom that secures the release of "many" (pollon).

The word "many" (pollon) echoes Isaiah 53:11-12: "By His knowledge the Righteous One, My Servant, will justify the many, for He will bear their wrongdoings... He Himself bore the sin of many, and interceded for the wrongdoers." The Servant of Isaiah 53 bears the sin of the many and is counted among wrongdoers. Jesus's self-description aligns with this figure: his death is not an accident of political hostility but a purposeful act of substitutionary service. The preposition "for" (anti) means "in place of" or "in exchange for." The Son of Man's life is given in exchange for the many — his death in place of theirs, his suffering as the price of their release.

This single verse ties together the chapter's themes. The wealthy man was asked to give his possessions; Jesus gives his life. The disciples argued about who would be served; Jesus came to serve. The children received the kingdom as a gift; the gift is made possible by the ransom the Son of Man pays. The kingdom of God, which human beings cannot enter by their own effort (v. 27), is opened by God's act of redemption through the death of his Son.

Verses 46-52: Bartimaeus — The Blind Man Who Sees

"Then they came to Jericho. And later, as He was leaving Jericho with His disciples and a large crowd, a beggar who was blind named Bartimaeus, the son of Timaeus, was sitting by the road."

Jericho is the last stop before Jerusalem. The road from Jericho to Jerusalem ascends steeply through the wilderness of Judea — it is the final stretch. Bartimaeus is identified by name (the only person healed in Mark who is named besides Jairus, whose daughter was healed), by his father's name (Timaeus), and by his condition (blind, beggar, sitting by the road). He is sitting "by the road" (para ten hodon) — beside the way, not on it. The word hodos (road, way) has been the dominant metaphor for discipleship throughout this section. Bartimaeus is beside the way but not yet on it.

"And when he heard that it was Jesus the Nazarene, he began to cry out and say, 'Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!'"

The title "Son of David" (huie Dauid) is striking. This is the first time in the document that anyone addresses Jesus with this title. Peter called him "the Christ" (8:29); the crowds have called him a prophet or a teacher. Bartimaeus, a blind beggar, identifies Jesus with the Davidic royal line. The title carries messianic expectation: the "son of David" is the awaited king who will restore Israel's fortunes. In 2 Samuel 7:12-16, God promised David an everlasting dynasty. In the Psalms of Solomon 17 (a first-century Jewish text), the coming "son of David" is described as the one who will purge Jerusalem of Gentile occupiers and reign in righteousness. Bartimaeus's use of the title reveals that he perceives — without physical sight — something the sighted disciples have struggled to grasp. He knows who Jesus is and what he can do.

The cry "have mercy on me" (eleeson me) echoes the psalmic tradition of appeal to God: "Have mercy on me, O God" (Psalm 51:1, LXX 50:3); "Have mercy on me, O LORD, for I am weak" (Psalm 6:2, LXX 6:3). Bartimaeus approaches Jesus with the language of prayer.

"Many were sternly telling him to be quiet, but he kept crying out all the more, 'Son of David, have mercy on me!'"

The crowd tries to silence Bartimaeus, just as the disciples tried to turn away the children (v. 13). In both cases, people around Jesus attempt to exclude those they consider unworthy of his attention — children without status, a blind beggar without dignity. And in both cases, the excluded party gains access because Jesus overrides the gatekeepers. Bartimaeus refuses to be silenced. His persistence — crying out "all the more" (pollo mallon) — mirrors the persistence required of those who would receive from God (cf. the parable of persistent prayer in Luke 18:1-8, which shares thematic territory with this episode).

"And Jesus stopped and said, 'Call him here.' So they called the man who was blind, saying to him, 'Take courage, stand up! He is calling for you.'"

Jesus stops. The entire procession toward Jerusalem halts for a blind beggar. The crowd's tone shifts instantly from hostility to encouragement: "Take courage, stand up! He is calling for you" (tharsei, egeire, phonei se). The verb egeire (rise, stand up) is the same word used for resurrection. The crowd does not know it, but their words anticipate the pattern of what Jesus does: he calls, and the one who is called rises.

"And throwing off his cloak, he jumped up and came to Jesus."

Bartimaeus's response contrasts with the wealthy man's in every detail. The wealthy man had many possessions and went away grieving. Bartimaeus has one possession — his cloak (himation), likely the garment that served as his mat, his blanket, his entire worldly goods — and he throws it away without hesitation. The wealthy man could not let go of everything; Bartimaeus lets go of the only thing he has. The wealthy man walked away from Jesus; Bartimaeus jumps up and comes to Jesus.

"And replying to him, Jesus said, 'What do you want Me to do for you?' And the man who was blind said to Him, 'Rabboni, I want to regain my sight!'"

The question — "What do you want Me to do for you?" (ti soi theleis poieso) — is word-for-word identical to the question Jesus asked James and John in verse 36. The two scenes are deliberately parallel:

| Element | James and John (v. 35-37) | Bartimaeus (v. 46-51) | |---------|---------------------------|----------------------| | Approach | "We want You to do for us whatever we ask" | Cries "have mercy on me" | | Jesus's question | "What do you want Me to do for you?" | "What do you want Me to do for you?" | | Request | Seats of power and glory | Sight | | Address | "Teacher" | "Rabboni" (a more intimate form of "Rabbi") | | Outcome | Told they do not know what they ask | Immediately healed |

James and John, who can see, are blind to what Jesus has been telling them. Bartimaeus, who is blind, sees who Jesus is. James and John ask for status; Bartimaeus asks for mercy. James and John are part of the inner circle; Bartimaeus is a roadside beggar. The author has structured the chapter so that the two scenes interpret each other: the sighted are blind and the blind sees.

"And Jesus said to him, 'Go; your faith has made you well.' And immediately he regained his sight and began following Him on the road."

The healing is attributed to Bartimaeus's faith (he pistis sou sesoken se). The verb sozo means both "to heal" and "to save" — the same word the disciples used in verse 26 ("Then who can be saved?"). Bartimaeus has been both healed and saved. His faith — his persistent crying out, his refusal to be silenced, his identification of Jesus as the Son of David, his willingness to abandon his cloak and come — is the instrument through which God's power operates. This is the answer to the question of verse 27: "With people it is impossible, but not with God." God does what is impossible, and faith is the posture of reception.

The final detail is the most important: Bartimaeus "began following Him on the road" (ekolouthei auto en te hodo). The verb akoloutheo (to follow) is the author's consistent word for discipleship. The road (hodos) is the road to Jerusalem — the road to the cross. The wealthy man went away from Jesus; Bartimaeus follows him. The wealthy man had everything and could not follow; Bartimaeus had nothing and could not be stopped. The blind man who received his sight becomes a disciple. The chapter that began with crowds gathering to hear Jesus ends with a single healed man following him toward his death.

The Architecture of Mark 10: A Unified Argument About Kingdom Entry

The author has constructed Mark 10 as a single sustained argument, not a collection of unrelated episodes. Each scene addresses the same question — what does the kingdom of God require? — and each scene provides part of the answer by contrast with the others:

| Scene | Character(s) | What They Have/Lack | Response to Jesus | Kingdom Lesson | |-------|-------------|---------------------|-------------------|----------------| | vv. 2-12 | Pharisees | Legal expertise, hardened hearts | Test Jesus, receive correction | God's design trumps human concession | | vv. 13-16 | Children | Nothing — no status, no standing | Brought by others, received by Jesus | The kingdom belongs to those who receive as children | | vv. 17-22 | Wealthy man | Many possessions, moral record | Goes away grieving | Possessions can be the barrier; entry requires letting go | | vv. 23-31 | Disciples | Have left everything | Amazed, Peter claims sacrifice | Entry is impossible for humans; God makes it possible | | vv. 35-45 | James and John | Ambition for status | Demand seats of honor | Greatness is servanthood; the Son of Man gives his life | | vv. 46-52 | Bartimaeus | Nothing — blind, begging | Throws away cloak, follows | Faith receives what effort cannot earn |

The movement from the Pharisees to Bartimaeus traces a steady narrowing. The Pharisees come with legal arguments and hardened hearts and learn nothing. The wealthy man comes with sincerity and possessions and leaves empty. The disciples have sacrificed much but still grasp for power. Bartimaeus comes with nothing but faith and receives everything — sight, salvation, and the road.

Three interlocking principles emerge:

The kingdom is received, not achieved. Children do not earn their place; they receive it. The wealthy man tried to earn eternal life and could not buy it. The disciples' sacrifices will be repaid a hundredfold, but the repayment is God's initiative, not a wage they have earned. Bartimaeus's faith is not a work he performed; it is the open hand into which God places the gift.

The barrier to the kingdom is whatever competes with God for ultimate allegiance. For the wealthy man, it was property. For the Pharisees, it was hardened hearts shaped by traditions that overrode God's design. For James and John, it was ambition for status. For the disciples more broadly, it was the instinct to dominate rather than serve. The chapter does not identify a single universal barrier; it catalogues the many forms that idolatry takes. The first commandment — "You shall have no other gods before Me" — stands behind every episode.

The path to the kingdom runs through the cross. Jesus is heading to Jerusalem to die. The passion prediction at the chapter's center (vv. 32-34) is not an interruption of the kingdom teaching; it is its foundation. The Son of Man came to serve and give his life as a ransom for many (v. 45). The kingdom that human beings cannot enter by their own effort is opened by the death of the king. The ransom verse (v. 45) is the mechanism by which the impossible salvation of verse 27 becomes possible. God makes all things possible — and the means God chooses is the self-offering of his Son.

The chapter begins with a crowd gathering to hear Jesus and ends with a single man following him on the road. Between those two bookends, every form of human self-reliance — legal mastery, moral achievement, material wealth, social ambition — has been weighed and found inadequate. What remains is what Bartimaeus had: nothing but a cry for mercy, a willingness to throw away the only thing he owned, and the faith to follow a man headed for his death.
