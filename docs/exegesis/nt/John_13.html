John 13 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (John 12): Chapter 12 marks the end of Jesus's public ministry in this document. The chapter opens with the anointing at Bethany, where Mary anoints Jesus's feet with costly perfume — an act Jesus interprets as preparation for his burial (12:7). Judas objects on the pretense of concern for the poor, and the author interrupts to note that Judas "was a thief, and as he had the money box, he used to pilfer what was put into it" (12:6). The entry into Jerusalem follows (12:12-19), with the crowd waving palm branches and crying "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel" (12:13, citing Psalm 118:25-26). The Pharisees' exasperated remark — "You see that you can do nothing; look, the world has gone after him" (12:19) — is immediately confirmed by the arrival of Greeks who seek Jesus (12:20-22). Their arrival prompts Jesus to declare that "the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" (12:23), the first time in the document that the "hour" is said to have arrived rather than "not yet come" (2:4, 7:30, 8:20). Jesus then speaks of the grain of wheat that must fall into the earth and die to bear fruit (12:24), of losing one's life to keep it (12:25), and of being "lifted up from the earth" to "draw all people" to himself (12:32). The author notes that the crowd cannot reconcile this talk of death with their understanding of the Messiah: "We have heard from the Law that the Christ remains forever; how can you say, 'The Son of Man must be lifted up'?" (12:34). Jesus urges them to believe in the light while the light is among them (12:35-36a), and then "he departed and hid himself from them" (12:36b).

The author then provides a retrospective summary (12:37-43) explaining why, despite the signs, "they were not believing in him" (12:37). The explanation draws on two texts from Isaiah. First, Isaiah 53:1 (LXX): "Lord, who has believed our report? And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?" — the Servant Song's acknowledgment that the message of God's saving work would not be universally received. Second, Isaiah 6:10 (LXX): "He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart, so that they would not see with their eyes and perceive with their heart, and be converted and I heal them" — the prophetic commission of judicial hardening. The author then adds a remarkable gloss: "These things Isaiah said because he saw his glory, and he spoke of him" (12:41), identifying the glory Isaiah saw in the temple vision (Isaiah 6:1-3, "I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and lifted up") as the glory of Jesus. The section concludes with the observation that "many even of the rulers believed in him, but because of the Pharisees they were not confessing him, for fear that they would be put out of the synagogue; for they loved the approval of men rather than the approval of God" (12:42-43). Finally, Jesus makes a last public declaration summarizing the themes of the entire public ministry: believing in him means believing in the one who sent him (12:44); seeing him means seeing the one who sent him (12:45); he has come as light so that those who believe will not remain in darkness (12:46); his word, not he himself, will judge on the last day (12:48); the Father who sent him gave him commandment regarding what to say and speak, and this commandment is eternal life (12:49-50).

Chapter 12 thus closes the public phase of the document. Every major theme has been stated: the relationship between Father and Son, the light-darkness division, the role of the signs, the judicial hardening of Israel's leaders, the hour of glorification through death, the drawing of all people through the "lifting up," and the cost of confession. The public ministry ends in a mixed verdict: many signs, widespread unbelief, some belief that remains hidden for fear of expulsion.

What the author argues immediately after (John 14): Chapter 14 opens with Jesus continuing to speak to the disciples in the same setting: "Do not let your heart be troubled; you believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father's house are many dwelling places; if it were not so, I would have told you; for I go to prepare a place for you" (14:1-2). The chapter continues the private instruction that began in chapter 13, developing the themes of Jesus's departure and return, the relationship between the disciples and the Father, and the promise of the Spirit. Thomas asks "Lord, we do not know where you are going; how do we know the way?" and Jesus responds: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life; no one comes to the Father but through me" (14:5-6). Philip asks to see the Father, and Jesus replies: "He who has seen me has seen the Father" (14:9). The promise of "another Helper" (allon parakleton), the Spirit of truth, is given for the first time (14:16-17, 26). Jesus promises: "I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you" (14:18). The mutual indwelling theme from 10:38 is extended to include the disciples: "In that day you will know that I am in my Father, and you in me, and I in you" (14:20). The chapter concludes with Jesus repeating his commandment of love (14:21, 23-24), promising peace (14:27), and announcing his departure: "I go away, and I will come to you... I go to the Father, for the Father is greater than I" (14:28).

How chapter 13 connects the surrounding argument: Chapter 12 ended with Jesus's withdrawal from public view ("he departed and hid himself from them," 12:36b) and a final public declaration that concluded the themes of the public ministry. Chapter 13 opens a fundamentally new section of the document. The audience shifts from the crowds, the religious authorities, and the public at large to the intimate circle of Jesus's own disciples. The setting shifts from the temple courts, the festival crowds, and the public spaces of Jerusalem to a private supper. The temporal horizon shifts from the ongoing work of signs and teaching to the immediate threshold of Jesus's death — "before the Feast of the Passover, Jesus knowing that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father" (13:1). The thematic focus shifts from the question "Who is Jesus?" (answered through signs, discourses, and confrontations) to the question "What must the disciples know and become before Jesus leaves?"

Chapter 13 is the hinge between these two phases. It performs three essential functions. First, it enacts the transition from public ministry to private instruction through a symbolic action — the washing of the disciples' feet — that embodies the meaning of Jesus's impending death and establishes the pattern for the community's life after his departure. Second, it identifies and removes the betrayer from the community, drawing a line between those who are "clean" and the one who is not, so that the teaching that follows in chapters 14-17 is addressed only to those who belong to Jesus. Third, it introduces the "new commandment" — to love one another as Jesus has loved them — which will be the defining mark of the community and the theme that pervades the remainder of Jesus's private instruction (14:15, 21, 23-24; 15:9-17; 17:26). The foot-washing, the departure of Judas, and the new commandment together establish the conditions under which the farewell discourse of chapters 14-17 takes place.

The chapter also fulfills and extends themes from the public ministry. The good shepherd who "lays down his life for the sheep" (10:11, 15, 17-18) now demonstrates what that laying-down looks like in practice: taking the form of a servant, washing feet, loving "to the end" (eis telos, 13:1). The grain of wheat that falls into the earth and dies (12:24) is about to fall. The "hour" that had "not yet come" throughout the first twelve chapters (2:4, 7:30, 8:20) has now arrived (12:23, 13:1). The light that shone in the public ministry is now concentrated on the small circle that will carry it forward, and the darkness is embodied not in the hostile authorities but in one of Jesus's own: "it was night" (13:30).

The Passover Setting and the Author's Theological Introduction (Verses 1-3)

"Now before the Feast of the Passover, Jesus knowing that his hour had come that he would depart out of this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end. During supper, the devil having already put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon, to betray him, Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come forth from God and was going back to God..."

"Before the Feast of the Passover" (pro de tes heortes tou pascha). The temporal marker places the events of chapter 13 before the Passover meal itself. This creates a chronological tension with the Synoptic accounts, where the Last Supper is the Passover meal. In this document, the Passover has not yet occurred at the time of the supper described here; Jesus will be crucified on the day of preparation for the Passover, at the time the Passover lambs are being slaughtered in the temple (19:14, 31, 42). The author has structured the entire narrative so that Jesus dies as the Passover lamb — John the Baptist's identification of Jesus as "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" (1:29) and the notation that no bone of his body was broken (19:36, echoing the Passover instructions of Exodus 12:46 and Numbers 9:12) converge in the timing: the true Passover lamb dies at the hour the temple lambs are killed. This chronological framing means the supper of chapter 13 is not the Passover meal but a meal that precedes it, and the foot-washing and the discourse that follows are set in the shadow of the approaching Passover.

The Passover itself is the foundational narrative of Israel's deliverance. Exodus 12 recounts how God brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt through the blood of the lamb applied to the doorposts, the angel of death passing over the marked houses, and the exodus itself — the departure "out of" (ek) Egypt into freedom. The Passover is therefore a narrative of departure, deliverance, and transition from bondage to a new identity as God's people. The author opens chapter 13 by describing Jesus's departure "out of this world to the Father" (ek tou kosmou toutou... pros ton patera) in language that echoes the exodus pattern: a departure from one condition to another, accomplished through the death of the lamb. Jesus is simultaneously the lamb whose death effects the deliverance and the leader who goes ahead, as Moses went ahead of Israel.

"Jesus knowing that his hour had come that he would depart out of this world to the Father" (eidos ho Iesous hoti elthen autou he hora hina metabe ek tou kosmou toutou pros ton patera). Three elements require attention.

First, "knowing" (eidos). The participle indicates Jesus's full awareness. He is not surprised by what is about to happen; he acts with complete knowledge. This continues the pattern of sovereign awareness the author has maintained: Jesus "knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was that would betray him" (6:64), "knew what he was going to do" before the feeding of the multitude (6:6), and declared that he lays down his life "on my own initiative" (10:18). Everything that follows in this chapter — the foot-washing, the identification of the betrayer, the new commandment — is performed by one who knows exactly where the evening will lead.

Second, "his hour had come" (elthen autou he hora). The "hour" (hora) has been the document's central temporal concept since 2:4, where Jesus told his mother at Cana, "My hour has not yet come." The same "not yet" appeared at the Feast of Tabernacles: "No one seized him, because his hour had not yet come" (7:30, 8:20). In 12:23, Jesus declared for the first time that "the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." Now the arrival of the hour is stated as accomplished fact (elthen, aorist: "has come"). The hour is the hour of his departure through death and return to the Father — it encompasses the cross, the resurrection, and the ascension as a single movement. In 12:27, Jesus had acknowledged the anguish of this hour ("Now my soul has become troubled; and what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I came to this hour") and then submitted to it. Now the hour is here, and Jesus acts within it with full purpose.

Third, "depart out of this world to the Father" (metabe ek tou kosmou toutou pros ton patera). The verb metabaino means "to pass over, to cross from one place to another, to make a transition." It appeared in 5:24: "He who hears my word, and believes him who sent me, has eternal life, and does not come into judgment, but has passed out of death into life" (metabebeken ek tou thanatou eis ten zoen). The same verb now describes Jesus's own passage: he crosses from this world to the Father, just as the believer crosses from death to life. The death is not an end but a crossing — a passover, in the deepest sense of the word. The language frames the entire Passion not as tragedy but as transition: Jesus goes from here to the Father.

"Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end" (agapesas tous idious tous en to kosmo, eis telos egapesen autous). This is the author's summary statement, the interpretive key for everything that follows in chapters 13-17. Several elements are critical.

"His own" (tous idious): The phrase "his own" (ta idia / hoi idioi) has appeared before in this document with two different valences. In 1:11, "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him" — there "his own" referred to Israel, who rejected him. In 10:3-4, the shepherd "calls his own sheep by name and leads them out... he goes ahead of them, and the sheep follow him because they know his voice" — there "his own" meant the sheep who hear and follow. The usage here in 13:1 is the latter: those who belong to Jesus, who have heard his voice and followed. They are distinguished from "the world" (ton kosmon) in which they exist but to which they do not belong — a distinction that will be developed extensively in chapters 14-17 (especially 15:18-19: "If the world hates you, you know that it has hated me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, because of this the world hates you"; and 17:14-16).

"He loved them to the end" (eis telos egapesen autous): The phrase eis telos can mean "to the end" in two senses: temporally ("to the very last moment, until the end") and qualitatively ("to the uttermost, completely, to the fullest extent"). Both senses operate. Temporally, Jesus loves his own until the moment of his departure — he does not withdraw his love even as betrayal, denial, and abandonment approach. Qualitatively, the love reaches its ultimate expression in what is about to be performed: the foot-washing as enacted love, and the cross as love's full measure. The phrase signals that everything in the chapter — the basin, the towel, the warning about the betrayer, the new commandment — is an expression of this love carried to its completion.

"During supper, the devil having already put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon, to betray him" (deipnou ginomenou, tou diabolou ede beblekotos eis ten kardian hina paradoi auton Ioudas Simonos Iskariotes). The author interrupts the narrative to note that the devil has already placed the intention of betrayal into Judas's heart. The verb beblekotos (perfect participle of ballo, "to throw, put, cast") indicates a completed action: the decision has already been made, the die already cast. The devil (ho diabolos, "the slanderer, the accuser") appeared in 8:44, where Jesus told the unbelieving authorities: "You are of your father the devil, and you want to do the desires of your father. He was a murderer from the beginning, and does not stand in the truth because there is no truth in him." The devil's work is murder and falsehood; Judas's betrayal will lead to murder, and the betrayal itself is an act of deception from within the circle of trust.

The identification of Judas by patronymic — "son of Simon" (Simonos) — distinguishes him from other figures named Judas (14:22: "Judas, not Iscariot") and anchors the betrayer as a specific, known individual. The author has already flagged Judas's character: he was a thief who pilfered from the common purse (12:6), and the earlier notation that "Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was that would betray him" (6:64) has been shadowing the narrative since the bread-of-life discourse. The betrayal is not a surprise but a known element in the drama, part of what Jesus acts within.

"Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come forth from God and was going back to God" (eidos ho Iesous hoti panta edoken auto ho pater eis tas cheiras, kai hoti apo theou exelthen kai pros ton theon hypagei). This second "knowing" clause is extraordinary in its juxtaposition with what Jesus is about to do. The one who knows that all things are in his hands — all authority, all power, the cosmos itself (1:3, "all things came into being through him") — is about to kneel on the floor and wash feet. The one who knows his origin (he came forth from God) and his destination (he is going back to God) performs the act of a household slave. The author constructs the contrast deliberately: the greater the authority, the more astonishing the self-lowering. The claim is not that Jesus washes feet despite his authority but because his authority allows him to do what no one else's status permits. Only the one who holds all things in his hands can freely empty those hands to hold a basin and towel.

The phrase "the Father had given all things into his hands" (panta edoken auto ho pater eis tas cheiras) echoes 3:35 ("The Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand") and 10:29 ("My Father, who has given them to me"). The "hands" into which all things have been given are the same hands that will wash the disciples' feet. The hand imagery from 10:28-29 — "no one will snatch them out of my hand... no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand" — is now enacted in a different register: the hand of power becomes the hand of service. The shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep first takes up a towel and serves them.

Verses 4-5: The Foot-Washing

"Got up from supper, and laid aside his garments; and taking a towel, he girded himself. Then he poured water into the basin, and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the towel with which he was girded."

"Got up from supper, and laid aside his garments" (egeiretai ek tou deipnou kai tithesi ta himatia). Two verbs carry symbolic weight beyond their literal meaning.

The verb "got up" (egeiretai) is a form of egeiro, which throughout this document and the broader apostolic literature is the verb used for resurrection — raising from the dead (2:19-22, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up"; 5:21, "For just as the Father raises the dead and gives them life"; 12:1, 9, 17, of the raised Lazarus). While here it means simply "rose from the table," the reader who has been trained by this author's habit of layered language can hear a faint resonance with the resurrection: the one who rises from the supper to serve will rise from the tomb to live.

The verb "laid aside" (tithesi) is the same verb used in 10:11, 15, 17-18 for the shepherd laying down his life: "the good shepherd lays down (tithesi) his life for the sheep." In 10:18: "I lay it down (tithemi) on my own initiative." The deliberate use of the same verb for setting aside garments and laying down life creates a verbal link: this laying aside is a preview of that laying down. The garments stand for the life itself — the external covering, the visible identity that will be stripped away at the cross (19:23-24, where the soldiers divide his garments). Jesus's voluntary removal of his garments at the supper anticipates his voluntary submission to their removal at the crucifixion.

The word "garments" (himatia, the outer robe or cloak) is the same word used in 19:23 when the soldiers "took his outer garments" (ta himatia autou). The parallel is unmistakable for the reader of the complete document: the garments laid aside at the supper are the garments stripped at the cross. In both cases, the removal is Jesus's own act in the deeper sense — he lays them aside voluntarily (10:18).

"Taking a towel, he girded himself" (labon lention diezosen heauton). The lention ("towel, linen cloth") was the working garment of a slave. The verb diazonnymi ("to gird, to tie around the waist") describes the posture of a servant preparing to work. In the LXX, "girding" is associated with preparation for labor or battle (Exodus 12:11, "your loins girded" for the Passover meal; 1 Kings 18:46, Elijah girded his loins to run before Ahab). But the towel identifies the specific labor: not battle, not travel, but domestic service. The one who in the prologue was identified as the Word through whom all things were made, who was with God and was God (1:1-3), now wears the garment of a household slave.

"He poured water into the basin, and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the towel" (eita ballei hydor eis ton niptera, kai erxato niptein tous podas ton matheton kai ekmassein to lentio ho en diezomenos). The action is described with physical specificity: pouring water, washing feet, drying them. The verb nipto ("to wash" a part of the body, as opposed to louo, "to bathe the whole body" — a distinction that will become significant in verse 10) describes the cleansing of the feet, the lowest and dirtiest part of the body. In first-century Palestine, where roads were unpaved and sandals were the standard footwear, feet accumulated dust, mud, and filth. Foot-washing was performed upon entering a home and was universally regarded as the task of the lowest-ranking servant. In Jewish practice, the duty of washing a master's feet could be imposed on Gentile slaves or on the disciples of a rabbi, but it was considered too demeaning for a Jewish slave. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on Exodus 21:2 (though codified later, reflecting earlier practice) distinguishes between duties a Hebrew slave must perform and duties that only a Canaanite slave can be required to perform, and foot-washing falls in the latter category. A rabbi might have his disciples carry his sandals, but to wash feet was beneath even a disciple's expected service.

Jesus, the teacher (13:13, "You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for so I am"), performs the act that was beneath the lowest disciple. The act is not merely humble; it violates the social hierarchy that defined teacher-student relationships in first-century Judaism. The teacher does not serve the student; the student serves the teacher. By reversing this, Jesus does not merely model humility as a virtue; he overturns the entire framework of honor and status that structured communal life.

The verb ekmasso ("to wipe off, dry") appeared in 11:2 and 12:3, where Mary wiped Jesus's feet with her hair after anointing them. The verbal connection links the two scenes: Mary served Jesus by anointing and wiping his feet; Jesus now serves the disciples by washing and wiping theirs. What Mary did for Jesus out of devotion, Jesus now does for his disciples out of love. The pattern of self-lowering service flows from Jesus and extends to all who follow him.

Verses 6-11: The Exchange with Peter

"So he came to Simon Peter. He said to him, 'Lord, do you wash my feet?' Jesus answered and said to him, 'What I do you do not realize now, but you will understand hereafter.' Peter said to him, 'Never shall you wash my feet!' Jesus answered him, 'If I do not wash you, you have no part with me.' Simon Peter said to him, 'Lord, then wash not only my feet, but also my hands and my head.' Jesus said to him, 'He who has bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean; and you are clean, but not all of you.' For he knew the one who was betraying him; for this reason he said, 'Not all of you are clean.'"

"Lord, do you wash my feet?" (Kyrie, sy mou nipteis tous podas). Peter's question emphasizes the pronouns with force: "You — my feet?" The word order in Greek places the emphatic contrast on "you" (sy) and "my" (mou). Peter's objection is not about the act of foot-washing itself but about who is performing it and for whom. The Lord washes the servant's feet — this inverts everything Peter understands about the relationship between teacher and disciple, master and follower. Peter's resistance is not disobedience but a recognition of the scandal: the one Peter has confessed as Lord (6:68-69, "You are the Holy One of God") cannot be the one kneeling at his feet with a basin.

"What I do you do not realize now, but you will understand hereafter" (ho ego poio sy ouk oidas arti, gnose de meta tauta). The word arti ("now, at this present moment") is contrasted with meta tauta ("after these things, hereafter"). Jesus acknowledges that the meaning of the foot-washing is not transparent; it will be understood only in light of what comes after — the cross, the resurrection, the coming of the Spirit who "will teach you all things" (14:26) and "guide you into all the truth" (16:13). The foot-washing is a sign that requires future events to interpret it, just as the signs throughout the document required discourse to interpret them (the feeding required the bread-of-life discourse; the blind man's healing required the shepherd discourse). The full meaning of the foot-washing will become clear only when the disciples see the garments laid aside on the cross, the body broken for them, and the risen Lord who returns and breathes the Spirit upon them (20:22). The phrase meta tauta also echoes the author's frequent use of "after these things" as a narrative transition (2:12, 3:22, 5:1, 6:1, 7:1, 19:28, 21:1), connecting this moment to the larger sequence of events the document narrates.

"Never shall you wash my feet!" (ou me nipseis mou tous podas eis ton aiona). Peter uses the strongest possible negation: ou me ("by no means, never") plus eis ton aiona ("unto the age, forever"). The absoluteness of Peter's refusal mirrors the absoluteness of the promises throughout the document (6:35, "will never hunger"; 8:51, "will never see death"; 10:28, "will never perish"; 11:26, "will never die"). Peter uses the language of permanence and totality to refuse what Jesus is doing. The irony is that Peter's refusal, motivated by reverence, would place him outside the community: if Jesus does not wash him, he has no part with Jesus.

"If I do not wash you, you have no part with me" (ean me nipso se, ouk echeis meros met' emou). The word meros ("part, share, portion") carries deep significance in Israel's scriptures. The word is used in the LXX for the allocation of inheritance among the tribes. Deuteronomy 10:9: "Levi has no portion (meros) or inheritance with his brothers; the LORD is his inheritance." Deuteronomy 12:12: "the Levite... has no portion (meros) or inheritance with you." Psalm 73:26 (LXX 72:26): "God is... my portion (meris) forever." The meros is one's share in the covenant inheritance, one's place in the community of God's people. To have no meros with Jesus is to be excluded from the relationship, the community, and the inheritance that Jesus is establishing. Jesus's statement reframes the foot-washing from an optional act of humility to a necessary condition of belonging: the disciples must receive Jesus's service in order to have a part with him.

This is a reversal of the usual direction of service. Throughout the document, people have come to Jesus seeking what he can give — life, healing, bread, water, sight. Here Jesus tells Peter that he must receive something he does not want and does not think he needs. The act of receiving Jesus's lowly service is itself a condition of belonging. The disciple who refuses to be served by the master cannot be the master's disciple. The community Jesus is forming is one in which the leader serves and the followers must accept being served — a framework that directly challenges the honor-shame dynamics of the ancient world.

"Lord, then wash not only my feet, but also my hands and my head!" (Kyrie, me tous podas mou monon alla kai tas cheiras kai ten kephalen). Peter swings from absolute refusal to excessive demand. If washing confers belonging, Peter wants as much washing as possible. The response, while expressing devotion, reveals that Peter still does not understand the act. He treats it as a quantitative matter (more washing = more belonging) rather than grasping its qualitative significance.

"He who has bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean" (ho leloumenos ouk echei chreian ei me tous podas nipsasthai, all' estin katharos holos). Jesus introduces a distinction between two kinds of washing. The verb louo ("to bathe the whole body") describes a complete cleansing already accomplished. The verb nipto ("to wash a part of the body") describes the removal of incidental defilement — the dust picked up on the journey from the bath to the house. The one who has bathed (ho leloumenos, perfect participle — indicating a completed action with ongoing results) is already wholly clean (katharos holos). Only the feet — the part that contacts the ground — need additional washing.

The language operates on two levels. On the literal level, it describes a common practice: a guest who had bathed at home before attending a banquet needed only to have the dust washed from his feet upon arrival. On the deeper level, the "bathing" represents the cleansing already accomplished through the disciples' relationship with Jesus — through hearing his word and believing. Jesus said in 15:3, "You are already clean because of the word which I have spoken to you." The cleansing "by the word" is the bath (louo); the foot-washing (nipto) addresses the ongoing defilement that contact with the world produces. The disciples do not need to be re-cleansed from the ground up; they need the continuous attention to the contamination that daily life in a hostile world produces. The foot-washing thus points to an ongoing practice of mutual care and renewal within the community, not a one-time initiation.

"And you are clean, but not all of you" (kai hymeis katharoi este, all' ouchi pantes). The pronouns shift from the singular "you" (addressed to Peter) to the plural "you" (hymeis, addressed to all the disciples). They are clean (katharoi) — the same word used in the Levitical purity system for ritual cleanness (Leviticus 11-15, where clean/unclean [katharos/akathartos] distinguished what was permitted from what was forbidden). But "not all" (ouchi pantes) introduces the exception.

"For he knew the one who was betraying him; for this reason he said, 'Not all of you are clean'" (edei gar ton paradidonta auton; dia touto eipen hoti ouchi pantes katharoi este). The author explains: the exception is Judas. The one who is "not clean" is the one whose heart the devil has already entered (13:2). The uncleanness is not ritual but moral — it is the uncleanness of betrayal, of treachery against the one who has served you. The present participle ton paradidonta ("the one betraying") indicates an ongoing condition: Judas is in the act of betrayal even now, at the table, as Jesus washes his feet. Jesus washes even the feet of the one he knows will hand him over. The foot-washing does not discriminate: the basin reaches Judas as well as Peter. But the washing does not change Judas's condition because cleanliness, in this document's framework, requires reception of Jesus's word and abiding in it (15:3, 8:31). The water touches Judas's feet, but the word has not entered his heart; the devil has occupied that space instead (13:2).

The statement "not all of you are clean" also establishes a theme that will recur throughout the farewell discourse: not everyone who appears to be in the community genuinely belongs to it. The vine metaphor of chapter 15 will develop this: "Every branch in me that does not bear fruit, he takes away" (15:2); "If anyone does not abide in me, he is thrown away as a branch and dries up" (15:6). Judas is the first and most dramatic instance of a branch that is "in" the vine in appearance but does not bear fruit and will be removed. His presence at the table, his receipt of the washing, and his departure into the night (13:30) enact what the vine metaphor will describe.

Verses 12-17: The Interpretation of the Foot-Washing

"So when he had washed their feet, and taken his garments and reclined at the table again, he said to them, 'Do you know what I have done to you? You call me Teacher and Lord; and you are right, for so I am. If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I gave you an example that you also should do as I did to you. Truly, truly, I say to you, a slave is not greater than his master, nor is one who is sent greater than the one who sent him. If you know these things, you are blessed if you do them.'"

"Taken his garments and reclined at the table again" (elaben ta himatia autou kai anepesen palin). The verb "taken" (elaben, from lambano, "to take, take up, receive") reverses the earlier "laid aside" (tithesi). Jesus takes up what he set down. The verbal pattern — setting aside / taking up again — mirrors 10:17-18 precisely: "I lay down my life so that I may take it again (hina palin labo auten). No one has taken it from me, but I lay it down on my own initiative. I have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it up again (palin labein auten)." The garments laid aside and taken up enact in miniature the life laid down and taken up again. The foot-washing is a compressed dramatization of the entire movement from incarnation through death to resurrection: the divine one sets aside his glory, takes the form of a servant, performs the work of redemption, and resumes his place.

"You call me Teacher and Lord; and you are right, for so I am" (hymeis phoneite me ho didaskalos kai ho kyrios, kai kalos legete; eimi gar). Jesus accepts both titles without reservation. "Teacher" (didaskalos, equivalent to "Rabbi") was the standard address for a Jewish sage by his students. "Lord" (kyrios) carried a range of meanings from polite address ("sir") to the divine title used in the LXX to render the tetragrammaton YHWH. In this document, kyrios is used for Jesus with increasing weight: from the respectful address of the Samaritan woman (4:11, 15, 19) and the royal official (4:49) to Thomas's climactic confession, "My Lord and my God" (ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, 20:28). Jesus affirms: the disciples are correct to call him Teacher and Lord. These titles are not courtesies; they describe what he is.

"If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet" (ei oun ego enipsa hymon tous podas ho kyrios kai ho didaskalos, kai hymeis opheilete alleton niptein tous podas). The argument is from the greater to the lesser (qal va-homer, the same reasoning pattern used in 7:23 and 10:34-36). If the one who holds the higher position has performed the act, those who hold the lower position are obligated (opheilete, "you ought, you owe it, you are indebted to") to perform it for one another. The word opheilete carries the sense of debt — something owed, not merely recommended. The foot-washing creates an obligation: the community of Jesus's disciples is bound to serve one another in the way Jesus has served them.

The phrase "one another" (allelon) is critical. Jesus does not say "you ought to wash the feet of those outside the community" or "you ought to wash my feet." The obligation is reciprocal within the community: "one another's feet." This establishes the pattern for the "one another" commands that will follow: "love one another" (13:34-35; 15:12, 17). The community's defining activity is mutual service and mutual love among its members.

"For I gave you an example that you also should do as I did to you" (hypodeigma gar edoka hymin hina kai hymeis poiete kathos ego epoiesa hymin). The word hypodeigma ("example, model, pattern") indicates that the foot-washing is not merely an isolated act but a paradigm — a pattern to be replicated. The conjunction kathos ("just as, in the same manner as") establishes correspondence: as Jesus did, so the disciples should do. The example is not restricted to the literal act of washing feet (though it includes that possibility); it extends to the entire posture of self-lowering service that the foot-washing embodies. The one who has received service from the Lord is obligated to extend the same kind of service to others within the community.

"Truly, truly, I say to you, a slave is not greater than his master, nor is one who is sent greater than the one who sent him" (amen amen lego hymin, ouk estin doulos meizon tou kyriou autou, oude apostolos meizon tou pempsantos auton). The double amen marks the statement as authoritative. The proverb about the slave and master appeared earlier in 15:20 (though later in narrative order), and a version appears in Matthew 10:24 and Luke 6:40. In this context, the proverb cuts in a specific direction: if the master has washed feet, the slave cannot claim to be above foot-washing. The slave is not greater than the master; if the master serves, the slave must serve. The claim to be "too important" for menial service is foreclosed by the master's own act.

The word apostolos ("one who is sent") appears only here in this document (the author typically uses the verb apostello or pempo rather than the noun). Its use here connects the disciples' mission to Jesus's own mission: as the Father sent Jesus (and Jesus served), so Jesus sends the disciples (and they must serve). The chain of sending — Father sends Son, Son sends disciples — carries with it the chain of service. The sent one is not greater than the sender, and the sender has established the paradigm by washing feet.

"If you know these things, you are blessed if you do them" (ei tauta oidate, makarioi este ean poiete auta). The word makarioi ("blessed, fortunate, happy") echoes the beatitude form found throughout Israel's wisdom literature and the Psalms (Psalm 1:1, "Blessed is the man"; Psalm 32:1, "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven"; Psalm 119:1, "Blessed are those whose way is blameless"). The blessing is conditional on doing (poiete, present tense — ongoing practice), not merely knowing. Knowledge without practice does not produce blessedness. The distinction between knowing and doing is the same as the distinction between hearing and obeying that runs through the document: in 13:17, as in 8:31-32 ("if you continue in my word, you are truly my disciples"), the condition of blessedness is enacted faithfulness, not theoretical assent.

Verses 18-20: The Betrayer and the Fulfillment of Scripture

"'I do not speak of all of you. I know the ones I have chosen; but it is that the Scripture may be fulfilled, "He who eats my bread has lifted up his heel against me." From now on I am telling you before it comes to pass, so that when it does occur, you may believe that I am. Truly, truly, I say to you, he who receives whomever I send receives me; and he who receives me receives him who sent me.'"

"I do not speak of all of you" (ou peri panton hymon lego). The blessing of verse 17 — "you are blessed if you do them" — does not apply universally to those present at the table. One of those present will not do what Jesus has commanded; one is excluded from the blessing. The qualification echoes verse 10: "you are clean, but not all of you." The author repeatedly marks the distinction between the community as it appears and the community as it truly is.

"I know the ones I have chosen" (ego oida hous exelexamen). The verb exelexamen (aorist of eklegomai, "to choose, select, elect") indicates that Jesus's choice of the Twelve was deliberate, including the choice of Judas. This echoes 6:70: "Did I myself not choose you, the twelve, and yet one of you is a devil?" The choosing of Judas was not a mistake but part of the larger purpose, as the next clause makes explicit.

"But it is that the Scripture may be fulfilled, 'He who eats my bread has lifted up his heel against me'" (all' hina he graphe plerothe, ho trogon mou ton arton eperen ep' eme ten pternan autou). The quotation is from Psalm 41:9 (LXX 40:10). The psalm is attributed to David and describes a situation where a trusted intimate has turned against him: "Even my close friend, in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted up his heel against me." The Hebrew reads: gam ish shelomi asher batachti bo okhel lachmi higdil alai aqev. The LXX renders this: kai gar ho anthropos tes eirenes mou, eph' hon elpisa, ho esthion artous mou, emegalynen ep' eme pternismon.

Several details are significant for the first-century hearer. First, the psalm describes betrayal by a table companion — one who shared the intimacy of eating together. In the ancient Near East, sharing a meal created a bond of trust and obligation. To eat someone's bread and then turn against them was among the most grievous violations of social covenant. The betrayal is not from an enemy but from a trusted friend, making it exponentially worse. Second, the phrase "lifted up his heel" (eperen pternan) is a gesture of contempt or violence — like an animal kicking, or the image of someone turning their back with a final act of disdain. The heel is the part of the body associated with the serpent's attack in Genesis 3:15 ("he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel"), though whether the author intends this connection is not certain.

In the psalm's original context, David speaks of his own experience of betrayal by a counselor or companion. The traditional identification connects this to Ahithophel, David's trusted advisor who sided with Absalom's rebellion (2 Samuel 15:12, 31; 16:20-17:3). Ahithophel ate at David's table, was David's intimate counselor, and betrayed him to his own son. Ahithophel's end was suicide by hanging (2 Samuel 17:23) — a detail that resonates with the tradition about Judas's death recorded in Matthew 27:5.

Jesus applies the psalm to Judas: the one who has been eating with him — sharing the most intimate fellowship of the teacher-disciple relationship — is about to raise his heel in betrayal. The word trogon ("eating, chewing, gnawing") is the same word used in 6:54, 56, 57, 58 for eating Jesus's flesh in the bread-of-life discourse. The verbal echo is striking: the one who "eats my bread" has been present at every meal, has shared in the fellowship, and yet will turn traitor. The betrayal comes from within, not from without — not from the Pharisees or the chief priests but from one of Jesus's own chosen.

"From now on I am telling you before it comes to pass, so that when it does occur, you may believe that I am" (ap' arti lego hymin pro tou genesthai, hina pisteusete hotan genetai hoti ego eimi). The phrase "that I am" (hoti ego eimi) is the absolute ego eimi — the divine self-identification that has appeared throughout the document (8:24, "unless you believe that I am, you will die in your sins"; 8:28, "when you lift up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am"; 8:58, "before Abraham was, I am"). Jesus tells the disciples in advance so that when the betrayal occurs, rather than destroying their faith, it will confirm it: the one who predicted the betrayal is the one who is — the one whose identity is disclosed by the divine name.

This establishes a pattern that the author will employ throughout the farewell discourse: Jesus tells the disciples what will happen before it happens so that the events, when they occur, will not devastate but confirm (14:29, "Now I have told you before it happens, so that when it happens, you may believe"; 16:4, "These things I have spoken to you, so that when their hour comes, you may remember that I told you of them"). The disciples' faith will be tested by the coming events — betrayal, arrest, crucifixion — and Jesus's prior announcement transforms potential faith-destroyers into faith-confirming evidence.

"Truly, truly, I say to you, he who receives whomever I send receives me; and he who receives me receives him who sent me" (amen amen lego hymin, ho lambanon an tina pempso eme lambanei, ho de eme lambanon lambanei ton pempsanta me). This saying establishes the chain of authorization that will ground the disciples' future mission: the one who receives a sent disciple receives Jesus, and the one who receives Jesus receives the Father. The chain works in both directions: from the Father through Jesus to the disciples, and from the disciples back through Jesus to the Father. The saying assumes that the disciples will be sent (which Jesus will make explicit in 17:18, "As you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world," and 20:21, "As the Father has sent me, I also send you"). Their authority derives not from themselves but from the one who sends them, just as Jesus's authority derives from the Father who sent him. The pattern of authorized representation — where the agent carries the authority of the sender — is well established in Jewish law (the concept of the shaliach, the authorized emissary whose words and acts carry the sender's authority).

Verses 21-30: The Identification and Departure of the Betrayer

"When Jesus had said this, he became troubled in spirit, and testified and said, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, that one of you will betray me.' The disciples began looking at one another, at a loss to know of which one he was speaking. There was reclining on Jesus' bosom one of his disciples, whom Jesus loved. So Simon Peter gestured to him, and said to him, 'Tell us who it is of whom he is speaking.' He, leaning back thus on Jesus' breast, said to him, 'Lord, who is it?' Jesus then answered, 'That is the one for whom I shall dip the morsel and give it to him.' So when he had dipped the morsel, he took and gave it to Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot. After the morsel, Satan then entered into him. Therefore Jesus said to him, 'What you do, do quickly.' Now no one of those reclining at the table knew for what purpose he had said this to him. For some were supposing, because Judas had the money box, that Jesus was saying to him, 'Buy the things we have need of for the feast'; or else, that he should give something to the poor. So after receiving the morsel he went out immediately; and it was night."

"He became troubled in spirit" (etarachthe to pneumati). The verb tarasso ("to trouble, agitate, disturb, stir up") appeared in 11:33, where Jesus "was deeply moved in spirit and was troubled" (etaraxen heauton) at Lazarus's tomb, and in 12:27, "Now my soul has become troubled" (he psyche mou tetaraktai). The troubled spirit is not a sign of doubt or weakness but of the cost of what Jesus is about to do. He is about to identify the betrayer, which means he is about to set in motion the events that lead to the cross. The trouble is the trouble of one who loves and who faces the betrayal of love.

"Testified and said" (emartyrese kai eipen). The verb martyreo ("to testify, bear witness") is the author's technical term for authoritative testimony throughout the document (1:7, 8, 15, 32, 34; 3:11, 26, 32, 33; 5:31-37; 8:13-18; 15:27; 18:37; 19:35; 21:24). By using it here, the author marks Jesus's statement about the betrayal not as a mere prediction or emotional outburst but as formal testimony — a solemn declaration with the weight of a witness in court.

"One of you will betray me" (heis ex hymon paradosei me). The verb paradidomi ("to hand over, deliver up, betray") is used throughout the document for the act of betrayal (6:64, 71; 12:4; 13:2, 11, 21; 18:2, 5, 36; 19:11, 16, 30). The disciples' response — "looking at one another, at a loss" (eblepon eis allelous hoi mathetai aporoumenoi) — indicates that Judas's betrayal was not obvious to the group. The other disciples did not suspect him.

"There was reclining on Jesus' bosom one of his disciples, whom Jesus loved" (en anakeimenos heis ek ton matheton autou en to kolpo tou Iesou, hon egapa ho Iesous). This is the first appearance of the figure the author identifies only as "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (also in 19:26, 20:2, 21:7, 21:20). The phrase en to kolpo ("in the bosom, on the chest") echoes 1:18, where the Son is described as being "in the bosom of the Father" (eis ton kolpon tou patros). The positional language creates a parallel: as the Son is in intimate relationship with the Father, so this disciple is in intimate relationship with Jesus. The disciple's proximity to Jesus gives him access to information the others do not have — he can ask privately what the group cannot determine publicly.

The reclining posture reflects Roman dining practice, which had been adopted in the Hellenized Jewish world for formal meals. Diners reclined on their left side, leaning on the left elbow, with the right hand free for eating. The person to the right of the host would recline with his back toward the host's chest, placing him "in the bosom" of the host. This was the position of honor and intimacy.

"Simon Peter gestured to him" (neuei oun touto Simon Petros). Peter, unable to ask directly, signals to the beloved disciple to inquire. This establishes a pattern that recurs: Peter acts through the beloved disciple as an intermediary (see also 20:2-8, where Peter and the beloved disciple run to the tomb; 21:7, where the beloved disciple identifies the risen Lord and Peter leaps into the sea). The two disciples function as a pair, with the beloved disciple having access to understanding or recognition that Peter lacks.

"That is the one for whom I shall dip the morsel and give it to him" (ekeinos estin ho ego bapso to psomion kai doso auto). The psomion ("morsel, piece of bread") dipped and given to a guest was an act of hospitality and honor at a meal. The host would dip bread into a common dish and hand it to a guest as a gesture of friendship and welcome. Jesus identifies the betrayer not by public accusation but by offering him an act of fellowship. Even the identification of the betrayer is performed through an act of service and honor. The irony is severe: the gesture of friendship singles out the one who will perform the ultimate act of betrayal.

"After the morsel, Satan then entered into him" (kai meta to psomion tote eiselthen eis ekeinon ho Satanas). The author escalates from "the devil" (diabolos) of verse 2 to "Satan" (Satanas). In verse 2, the devil "put into the heart" of Judas the intention to betray; now, after the morsel, Satan "enters into" Judas. The progression is from external temptation to full possession. The moment of the morsel — the moment of the host's act of honor — becomes the moment of Satan's entry. The one who receives Jesus's gesture of fellowship is simultaneously filled by the adversary of God. The juxtaposition is deliberate: at the very point where Jesus extends love, Judas receives something other than love. The morsel enters his mouth; Satan enters his being.

The name "Satan" (from the Hebrew satan, "adversary, accuser") appears in the LXX primarily in Job (1:6-12, 2:1-7), Zechariah 3:1-2, and 1 Chronicles 21:1. In Job, the Satan is a figure in the divine court who tests human loyalty; in Zechariah, the Satan stands to accuse the high priest Joshua. In the broader first-century Jewish context (reflected in the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, Jubilees, and the Qumran literature), Satan/Belial was understood as the chief adversary of God, the leader of hostile spiritual forces. The author of this document uses "the devil" and "Satan" as synonymous designations for the same figure whose character was defined in 8:44: "a murderer from the beginning" who "does not stand in the truth."

"What you do, do quickly" (ho poieis poieson tacheion). Jesus's command to Judas is startling. He does not prevent the betrayal; he accelerates it. The imperative "do quickly" (poieson tacheion) is not permission but a sovereign direction of events. Jesus, who has declared that he lays down his life on his own initiative (10:18), now tells the instrument of his death to proceed without delay. The hour has come (13:1); the departure is underway; the shepherd is orchestrating the laying down of his own life. Judas is not the author of the plot but its tool; Jesus remains the agent even in his own handing over.

"No one of those reclining at the table knew for what purpose he had said this to him." The other disciples' ignorance confirms that Judas's role was not publicly known. Their guesses — that Jesus sent Judas to buy supplies for the feast or to give to the poor — are mundane and reveal how thoroughly Judas's treachery was concealed. That some supposed Judas should "buy the things we have need of for the feast" also confirms that the Passover meal has not yet occurred (the feast is still approaching), consistent with this document's chronology.

"So after receiving the morsel he went out immediately; and it was night" (labon oun to psomion ekeinos exelthen euthys; en de nyx). The three-word sentence "it was night" (en de nyx) is one of the most powerful statements in the document. The author has constructed an elaborate light-darkness framework from the prologue onward: "In him was life, and the life was the light of men. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it" (1:4-5); "This is the judgment, that the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light" (3:19); "I am the light of the world" (8:12, 9:5); "Are there not twelve hours in the day? If anyone walks in the day, he does not stumble" (11:9); "Walk while you have the light, so that darkness will not overtake you" (12:35). Throughout the document, light is the presence and activity of Jesus; darkness is the absence of Jesus and the domain of opposition to God.

When Judas goes out, he goes out from the presence of Jesus into the night. He leaves the light and enters the darkness. The statement is simultaneously literal (it is nighttime) and theological (he enters the realm of darkness, the domain of the one who has just entered him). Judas's departure from Jesus's presence is a departure from the light into the darkness — the same darkness that the prologue said "did not comprehend" the light (1:5). The brevity of the statement — en de nyx — gives it the force of a door slamming shut.

The departure of Judas also purifies the audience for what follows. From this point forward, everything Jesus says in chapters 14-17 is addressed to those who are "clean" (13:10) — the disciples who remain. The betrayer has been removed. The community is now composed of those who truly belong, and Jesus can speak to them without reserve about the Father's house, the vine and branches, the Spirit, the world's hatred, and his own return.

Verses 31-35: The New Commandment

"Therefore when he had gone out, Jesus said, 'Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him; if God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and will glorify him immediately. Little children, yet a little while I am with you. You will seek me; and as I said to the Jews, "Where I am going, you cannot come," now I also say to you. A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another, even as I have loved you, that you also love one another. By this all men will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.'"

"Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him" (nyn edoxasthe ho huios tou anthropou, kai ho theos edoxasthe en auto). The word "now" (nyn) is emphatic: at this moment, with Judas's departure, the glorification has begun. The verb doxazo ("to glorify") appears in the aorist (edoxasthe, "has been glorified, was glorified") — a past tense used for an event that is so certain it can be spoken of as already accomplished. This is the "hour" of glorification that Jesus announced in 12:23 ("The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified"). The glorification is not the opposite of the death but includes it: the cross is the means of glory, not the obstacle to it.

The title "Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthropou) has appeared throughout the document in connection with the "lifting up" (3:14, "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up"; 8:28, "When you lift up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am"; 12:34). The double meaning of "lifted up" (hypsoo) — lifted on the cross and exalted to glory — is operative here. The Son of Man is glorified precisely in the act of being handed over, tried, and crucified. The glory is not post-death vindication but is present in the death itself. The background is Daniel 7:13-14 (LXX), where "one like a son of man" (hos huios anthropou) comes to the Ancient of Days and receives "dominion and glory (doxa) and a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations, and languages should serve him." The son of man's glory in Daniel is a glory received from God; here the glorification is mutual: the Son of Man is glorified, and God is glorified in him.

"God will also glorify him in himself, and will glorify him immediately" (kai ho theos doxasei auton en auto, kai euthys doxasei auton). The future tense (doxasei, "will glorify") points beyond the cross to the resurrection and exaltation. God will glorify the Son "in himself" (en auto, "in God's own presence/being") — which is to say, by receiving the Son back into the divine presence (17:5, "Now, Father, glorify me together with yourself, with the glory which I had with you before the world was"). The word "immediately" (euthys) signals that the glorification will not be delayed: the cross leads directly to resurrection, and resurrection leads directly to exaltation. There is no interval of defeat between the death and the glory; the death is the glory.

"Little children" (teknia). This is the only time Jesus uses this form of address in the document (it is characteristic of the Johannine letters: 1 John 2:1, 12, 28; 3:7, 18; 4:4; 5:21). The diminutive teknia ("little children, dear children") conveys intimacy and tenderness. At the moment when the events of betrayal and death are set in motion, Jesus addresses his disciples with the language of a parent speaking to children. The relational warmth is heightened by the announcement that follows: he is about to leave them.

"Yet a little while I am with you. You will seek me; and as I said to the Jews, 'Where I am going, you cannot come,' now I also say to you" (eti mikron meth' hymon eimi. zetesete me, kai kathos eipon tois Ioudaiois hoti hopou ego hypago hymeis ou dynasthe elthein, kai hymin lego arti). The phrase "a little while" (mikron) will recur in 14:19, 16:16-19, each time provoking confusion. Jesus had said to "the Jews" in 7:33-34 ("Yet a little while I am with you, and then I go to him who sent me. You will seek me, and will not find me; and where I am, you cannot come") and 8:21 ("I go away, and you will seek me, and will die in your sin; where I am going, you cannot come"). Now he says the same thing to the disciples, but the meaning is different. For the hostile authorities, "you cannot come" was a statement of permanent exclusion — they would seek and not find because they had rejected the one who was going. For the disciples, "you cannot come" is a statement of temporal limitation — they cannot come now, but they will come later (14:3, "I will come again and receive you to myself, that where I am, there you may be also"; 17:24, "Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, be with me where I am"). Peter will make this distinction explicit in verse 36 by asking "Lord, where are you going?" and receiving the answer: "Where I go, you cannot follow me now, but you will follow later."

"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another, even as I have loved you, that you also love one another" (entolen kainen didomi hymin, hina agapate allelous, kathos egapesa hymas hina kai hymeis agapate allelous). The word "new" (kainen) requires examination. The command to love is not new in itself — Leviticus 19:18 commands "You shall love your neighbor as yourself," and this was understood as a central obligation of the Torah. What is "new" is the standard of the love: not "as yourself" but "as I have loved you" (kathos egapesa hymas). The standard is not self-love projected onto others but the specific love Jesus has demonstrated — the love that washes feet, that lays down life, that loves "to the end" (eis telos, 13:1). The newness is in the measure: the love is to be patterned on Jesus's own self-giving love, which is qualitatively different from the Levitical command because it is modeled on a specific, concrete act of self-sacrifice.

The word entole ("commandment") echoes 10:18, where the Father gave Jesus a "commandment" (entolen) regarding the laying down and taking up of his life, and 12:49-50, where the Father's "commandment" (entole) is eternal life. The chain of commandment continues: the Father commanded the Son (10:18, 12:49-50); the Son now commands the disciples (13:34). The content of the commandment is love; the model of the love is Jesus's own; the scope is "one another" (allelous) — the members of the community.

The commandment is "new" also in the sense of Jeremiah 31:31-34 (LXX 38:31-34), where God promises "a new covenant" (diatheken kainen): "I will put my law within them, and on their heart I will write it; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people... for they will all know me, from the least of them to the greatest of them." The new commandment belongs to the new covenant. What was written on stone is now enacted in flesh — in the specific flesh of Jesus washing feet, and in the flesh of the community that follows his example.

"By this all men will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (en touto gnosontai pantes hoti emoi mathetai este, ean agapen echete en allelois). The identifying mark of Jesus's community is not doctrine, not ritual, not ethnic identity, not miraculous power, but mutual love. The verb gnosontai ("will know") indicates that this love will be visible — recognizable to those outside the community. The love among the members will function as testimony, as a sign that these people belong to the one who loved them to the end. In a first-century context where religious communities were identified by their observances (Sabbath, circumcision, dietary laws, temple worship, philosophical schools), Jesus identifies a radically different marker: the quality of their love for one another.

Verses 36-38: Peter's Boast and the Prediction of Denial

"Simon Peter said to him, 'Lord, where are you going?' Jesus answered, 'Where I go, you cannot follow me now; but you will follow later.' Peter said to him, 'Lord, why can I not follow you right now? I will lay down my life for you.' Jesus answered, 'Will you lay down your life for me? Truly, truly, I say to you, a rooster will not crow until you deny me three times.'"

"Lord, where are you going?" (Kyrie, pou hypageis). Peter asks the question that the farewell discourse will answer across four chapters. The question reveals that Peter has heard Jesus's announcement of departure but has not grasped its meaning. He thinks of a physical journey to a location. The answer — "where I go, you cannot follow me now, but you will follow later" (hopou hypago ou dynasai moi nyn akolouthesai, akoloutheseis de hysteron) — uses the verb akoloutheo ("to follow"), which has been the defining activity of discipleship throughout the document (1:37, 38, 40, 43; 8:12; 10:4, 5, 27; 12:26). Peter will follow — but not now. The "later" (hysteron) points beyond the immediate crisis to the time after the resurrection, when Peter's following will resume and ultimately lead to his own death (21:18-19, "when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will gird you, and bring you where you do not wish to go" — which the author glosses: "Now this he said, signifying by what kind of death he would glorify God").

"I will lay down my life for you" (ten psychen mou hyper sou theso). Peter uses Jesus's own language. The verb tithemi ("to lay down") and the preposition hyper ("for, on behalf of") are the exact words Jesus used in 10:11, 15, 17-18 for the good shepherd laying down his life for the sheep. Peter claims to be willing to do for Jesus what Jesus has said he will do for the sheep. The claim is sincere but premature. Peter believes he can do what only the shepherd can do — lay down his life. But within hours, Peter will not lay down his life; he will deny three times that he even knows Jesus (18:17, 25, 27).

The irony is severe. The shepherd lays down his life for the sheep; the sheep cannot lay down its life for the shepherd. Peter's boast reverses the roles. In this document's framework, the act of laying down life belongs to the shepherd by unique authority and commandment from the Father (10:18). The sheep's role is to hear, follow, and receive — precisely what the foot-washing has just demonstrated. Peter's inability to accept the foot-washing (v. 8) and his claim to lay down his life (v. 37) are the same error in different forms: he resists receiving and insists on giving, when the entire evening has been structured to teach that the disciple must first receive from Jesus before doing anything.

"A rooster will not crow until you deny me three times" (ou me alektor phonesei heos hou arnesai me tris). The double negative ou me ("by no means, certainly not") makes the prediction absolute. The word phonesei ("will crow") is from phoneo ("to call out, to cry, to sound") — cognate with phone ("voice"), which has been the critical word in the shepherd discourse (10:3-5, 16, 27: the sheep hear the shepherd's voice). The cock's voice will mark the moment when Peter refuses to acknowledge that he has heard and followed the shepherd's voice. The verb arnesai ("you will deny") means "to say no, to disown, to refuse to acknowledge." Peter, who has just professed willingness to die for Jesus, will instead three times refuse to admit that he knows him.

The prediction does not end the relationship. Jesus has already said Peter "will follow later" (v. 36). The denial is real and devastating, but it is not final. The document will record Peter's restoration by the risen Lord at the Sea of Tiberias, where Jesus asks Peter three times, "Do you love me?" (21:15-17) — one question for each denial. The prediction of failure is given within the context of "having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end" (13:1). The love does not stop at Peter's denial; it persists through it and beyond it.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. The chapter marks the transition from public ministry to private instruction. The public phase of the document, in which Jesus performed signs, engaged in public debates, and confronted the religious authorities, ended in 12:36b ("he departed and hid himself from them"). Chapter 13 opens a new section addressed exclusively to "his own" (tous idious, 13:1) — the circle of disciples who have heard his voice and followed. The shift in audience corresponds to a shift in subject: from the question of Jesus's identity (answered through signs and discourses) to the question of how the community will live after Jesus's departure.

2. The foot-washing enacts in advance the meaning of Jesus's death. The sequence of actions — rising from the table, laying aside garments, taking the form of a servant, washing feet, then taking up garments again — mirrors the sequence of the incarnation, crucifixion, and resurrection that the document narrates. The verbal parallels are precise: "laid aside" (tithesi) in verse 4 uses the same verb as "lays down" (tithesi) his life in 10:11; "took" (elaben) his garments in verse 12 uses the same verb as "take it up again" (labein) in 10:18. The foot-washing is a compressed enactment of the gospel the document tells: the divine one descends, serves, dies, and rises.

3. The foot-washing establishes the pattern for the community's life. The "example" (hypodeigma, v. 15) Jesus gives is not merely about humility as an abstract virtue but about a specific practice: mutual service modeled on the self-lowering of the Lord. The community is defined not by hierarchy but by reciprocal service ("wash one another's feet," v. 14), and this service is not optional but obligatory (opheilete, "you ought," v. 14). The community's internal life is to replicate the pattern Jesus has enacted.

4. The chapter draws a line between those who are "clean" and the one who is not. The repeated qualification — "you are clean, but not all of you" (v. 10); "I do not speak of all of you" (v. 18) — identifies Judas as the exception within the group. Despite being chosen (v. 18), having eaten with Jesus (v. 18), and having his feet washed (v. 5), Judas remains "not clean." The author attributes this to the devil's entry into his heart (vv. 2, 27). The foot-washing touches Judas but does not transform him because cleanness in this document is a function of receiving Jesus's word and remaining in it (15:3, 8:31), and Judas has not done so. His departure into the night (v. 30) removes the unclean element from the community and prepares the audience for the intimate teaching of chapters 14-17.

5. The betrayal fulfills Scripture and confirms Jesus's identity. The quotation from Psalm 41:9 (v. 18) places the betrayal within the pattern of Israel's history — specifically, the experience of David, the anointed king, betrayed by a trusted companion. The betrayal does not disprove Jesus's messiahship; it follows the pattern of the Davidic king's own experience. Jesus tells the disciples in advance (v. 19) so that the betrayal, when it occurs, will confirm rather than undermine their belief that he is the "I am" (ego eimi). The events of the Passion are not evidence against Jesus's identity but evidence for it.

6. The "new commandment" establishes love as the defining mark of the community. The commandment to "love one another as I have loved you" (v. 34) takes the Levitical command to love one's neighbor (Leviticus 19:18) and transforms it by providing a new standard: not self-love but Jesus's own self-sacrificing love. The love that washes feet, that serves the betrayer, that loves "to the end" — this is the measure. The visibility of this love ("by this all men will know," v. 35) makes it the community's public testimony, replacing the signs and discourses of the public ministry as the means by which the world recognizes Jesus's disciples.

7. Peter's exchange with Jesus in verses 36-38 reveals the distance between intention and capacity. Peter claims willingness to lay down his life, using the shepherd's own language (cf. 10:11, 15, 17-18). But Jesus predicts denial, not death. The disciple's role at this stage is not to die for the master but to receive from the master — to accept the foot-washing, to hear the word, to abide in the vine. Peter's willingness is real but his capacity is not yet what it will become. The prediction of denial is given within the framework of ongoing love ("he loved them to the end," 13:1) and future restoration ("you will follow later," v. 36; cf. 21:15-19).

8. The temporal and symbolic markers unify the chapter around the theme of passage. The Passover setting (v. 1) evokes the exodus — the departure from bondage through the blood of the lamb. Jesus's own "departure out of this world to the Father" (metabaino, v. 1) is a personal exodus. The "hour" that has finally come (v. 1) is the hour toward which the entire document has been moving. Judas's departure into the "night" (v. 30) is the departure into darkness that the document's light-darkness framework has been describing since the prologue. The glorification that begins "now" (v. 31) is the fulfillment of the promise made in 12:23. Every temporal marker in the chapter points to the same reality: the decisive transition is underway. The lamb is about to be slain. The shepherd is about to lay down his life. The light is about to enter the darkness and overcome it (1:5).

9. The chapter's pronouns carefully delineate the boundaries of the community. "His own" (tous idious, v. 1) identifies those who belong to Jesus. "You are clean, but not all of you" (v. 10) distinguishes the truly belonging from the apparently belonging. "Not all of you" (v. 18) excludes the betrayer from the blessed. "Love one another" (vv. 34-35) defines the community's internal obligation. "All men will know" (v. 35) distinguishes the community from those outside it. The pronoun tracking reveals a community that is known, loved, served, and commanded — but that includes, until the moment of self-revelation, a member who does not truly belong. Judas's departure is the moment when the visible community and the true community become coextensive, and the teaching that follows is addressed to the true community alone.

10. The chapter's argument flows as a single unbroken movement. The author's sequence is: (a) the theological introduction establishing that Jesus acts with full knowledge and love (vv. 1-3); (b) the enacted parable of the foot-washing (vv. 4-5); (c) the exchange with Peter that interprets the washing as necessary for belonging (vv. 6-11); (d) the explicit teaching about the meaning of the washing as an example for mutual service (vv. 12-17); (e) the identification and departure of the betrayer, fulfilling Scripture and removing the one who does not belong (vv. 18-30); (f) the glorification announcement and new commandment, establishing the community's identity and mission (vv. 31-35); (g) Peter's premature claim and Jesus's prediction of denial, tempering the community's confidence with realism about their immediate capacity (vv. 36-38). There is no change of subject: every section flows from the one before it. The foot-washing leads to the teaching about cleanness, which leads to the betrayer's uncleanness, which leads to the betrayer's removal, which opens the space for the new commandment, which defines what the community will look like after Jesus departs. The entire chapter is a preparation for departure — Jesus preparing his disciples for his absence by showing them what his presence means and what their life together must become.
