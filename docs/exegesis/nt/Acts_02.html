Acts 2 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 1): Acts 1 occupied the space between promise and fulfillment. Jesus presented himself alive over forty days, teaching about the kingdom of God. He commanded the apostles to remain in Jerusalem and wait for "what the Father had promised" — the baptism with the Holy Spirit (1:4-5). When the disciples asked whether this meant the restoration of the kingdom to Israel, Jesus redirected them from timing to mission: "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you shall be My witnesses both in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8). Jesus ascended into the cloud of divine presence, and two angelic figures promised his return in the same manner. The community — the Eleven, the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers, about 120 persons in all — gathered in the upper room with one mind, continually devoting themselves to prayer. Peter addressed the need to replace Judas, citing the psalms as prophetic testimony about the betrayer's fate and the necessity of filling his office. Matthias was selected by lot and added to the Eleven, restoring the apostolic body to twelve. Every element in Acts 1 was preparatory: the commission was given, the promise was reiterated, the community was gathered and unified, the apostolic number was restored. The chapter ended with a community fully prepared to receive what had been promised. Everything pointed forward to what was about to happen.

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 3): Peter and John go up to the temple at the hour of prayer, and Peter heals a man lame from birth "in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene" (3:6). The healing demonstrates that the power promised in 1:8 and received in 2:1-4 is now operative through the apostles. Peter's second public address follows, with the same structure as his Pentecost speech: he interprets a visible event (the healing) through Israel's scriptures, declares that "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the God of our fathers, has glorified His Servant Jesus" (3:13), and calls the audience to repentance. Peter explicitly names the audience's ignorance as a factor — "I know that you acted in ignorance, just as your rulers also did" (3:17) — and holds out the prospect that if they repent and return, God will send "times of refreshing" from his presence and will send Jesus, "whom heaven must receive until the period of restoration of all things" (3:20-21). The speech in Acts 3 extends the argument begun in Acts 2: the same Jesus, the same witnesses, the same call to repentance, the same promise of forgiveness. But Acts 3 adds a new element — the citation of Moses from Deuteronomy 18:15-19, warning that "every soul that does not listen to that prophet shall be utterly destroyed from among the people" (3:23). The tone sharpens: the offer of Acts 2:38-39 remains open, but the stakes are made more explicit.

How chapter 2 connects these: Acts 1 ended with a community that was ready. Acts 3 opens with a community that is active, exercising the power they have received. Acts 2 is the chapter in which the transition happens — in which the promised Holy Spirit arrives, the power is received, and the mission begins. The programmatic statement of 1:8 receives its initial fulfillment: the Spirit comes upon them, they speak in languages understood by people from across the known world, and Peter delivers the first public proclamation of the gospel in Jerusalem. The commission of Luke 24:47 — "repentance for forgiveness of sins... proclaimed in His name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem" — is enacted when Peter tells the Jerusalem crowd to repent and be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins (2:38). The community that was described as praying and waiting in Acts 1 is described as teaching, sharing, breaking bread, and growing in Acts 2:42-47. The healing in Acts 3 is possible because of what happened in Acts 2. Acts 2 is the hinge on which the entire narrative turns: it is the chapter in which everything that was promised is delivered and everything that follows becomes possible.

Verses 1-4: The Arrival of the Holy Spirit

"When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place" (2:1).

"The day of Pentecost" (ten hemeran tes pentekostes) is the Greek name for the Jewish festival of Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks. The name pentekostes means "fiftieth" — it falls fifty days after Passover. In the Torah, the festival is described in Leviticus 23:15-21 and Deuteronomy 16:9-12 as the harvest festival, the celebration of the firstfruits of the wheat harvest. The timing is precise within the narrative: Jesus was crucified at Passover, appeared over forty days (Acts 1:3), ascended, and then ten days later the fiftieth day arrives. The Pentecost harvest festival becomes the occasion for the harvest of the first believers.

But by the first century, Shavuot had also become associated with the giving of the Torah at Sinai. The Book of Jubilees (6:17-19) connects the Feast of Weeks to God's covenant-making, and rabbinic tradition (later codified but rooted in earlier associations) identified the fiftieth day after the Exodus as the day the Torah was given. This association is significant for what happens next: as God descended on Sinai with fire and sound to give the Torah to Israel, so now the Spirit descends with fire and sound to inaugurate a new phase of God's action. The parallel between Sinai and Pentecost would not be lost on a Jewish audience familiar with both the festival's agricultural meaning and its covenant-renewal significance.

"They were all together in one place" (esan pantes homou epi to auto). The word pantes ("all") is ambiguous — does it refer to the twelve apostles or to the entire community of about 120 (1:15)? The broader context suggests the full community. Acts 1:14-15 described the entire group — apostles, women, Mary, Jesus's brothers, and others — as being together in prayer. The phrase epi to auto ("in the same place," "together") recurs in Acts as a description of the community's gathered state (1:15, 2:44, 2:47). The unity emphasized in 1:14 (homothymadon, "with one mind") continues here: the community is together, unified, in a single location, when the Spirit comes.

"And suddenly a noise like a violent rushing wind came from heaven, and it filled the whole house where they were sitting" (2:2).

Three elements mark this event. First, it is sudden (aphno) — unpredicted in its exact timing, even though it was promised. The community was waiting for it (1:4-5), but they did not know the day or hour. The arrival is the Father's initiative, not something the community summoned.

Second, the sound is described as "a noise like a violent rushing wind" (echos hosper pheromenes pnoes biaias). The word echos means "sound, noise, reverberation." The comparison (hosper, "as if, like") indicates analogy, not literal wind: it sounded like a violent wind, but it was not ordinary wind. The word pnoe ("wind, breath, blast") is related to pneuma ("spirit, wind, breath") — in both Hebrew (ruach) and Greek, the same word field covers wind, breath, and spirit. The sound of wind evokes the ruach of God that moved over the face of the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2), the ruach that entered the dry bones in Ezekiel 37:9-10 and brought them to life, and the "sound of a gentle blowing" (1 Kings 19:12, LXX: phone auras leptes) that accompanied God's presence to Elijah at Horeb. The phrase "from heaven" (ek tou ouranou) identifies the origin as divine — this comes from God's realm, not from the natural world.

Third, the sound fills "the whole house" (holon ton oikon). The word oikos can mean house, dwelling, or temple. If this is the upper room of 1:13, it is the same space where Jesus shared the Passover meal and where the community has been praying. The filling of the entire house with the sound parallels the filling of the tabernacle with the cloud of God's presence (Exodus 40:34-35) and the filling of Solomon's temple at its dedication (1 Kings 8:10-11, LXX: "the cloud filled the house of the Lord"). What the cloud was to the tabernacle and the temple, the Spirit's presence is now to the house where the community gathers.

"And tongues that looked like fire appeared to them, distributing themselves, and a tongue rested on each one of them" (2:3).

"Tongues that looked like fire" (glossai hosei pyros) — again the language of appearance and analogy (hosei, "as if, like"). These are not literal fire but visible phenomena that looked like fire. The word glossa means both "tongue" (the organ of speech) and "language." The author's word choice is deliberate: these fire-like appearances take the form of tongues — the organ that produces speech — anticipating the multilingual speech that follows immediately in verse 4. The visual sign prefigures the audible result.

Fire carries dense scriptural weight. God appeared to Moses in the burning bush as fire (Exodus 3:2). God descended on Sinai "in fire" (Exodus 19:18). The pillar of fire led Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21-22). Isaiah's lips were purified by a coal of fire from the altar (Isaiah 6:6-7). Malachi described the coming messenger as "a refiner's fire" (Malachi 3:2). And John the Baptist declared that the coming one would "baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Luke 3:16). The fire at Pentecost fulfills John's prophecy directly: the baptism with the Spirit and fire that John predicted is happening.

The phrase "distributing themselves" (diamerizomenai) indicates that the fire-like tongues separated and divided, with individual tongues going to individual persons. The verb diamerizo means "to distribute, to divide into parts." The same verb appears in Luke 22:17 when Jesus took the cup and said "Take this and share it among yourselves" (diamerisate) — a distribution among all participants. The Spirit's coming is not a single corporate experience but a distribution to each person individually.

"A tongue rested on each one of them" (ekathisen eph hena hekaston auton). The verb kathizo ("to sit, to rest, to settle") indicates not a fleeting contact but a settled presence. Each person individually receives a tongue of fire. The phrase "each one of them" (hena hekaston auton) is emphatic: not just some, not just the leaders, but every individual in the room. This detail will matter when Peter interprets the event through Joel's prophecy, which describes the Spirit poured out on "all flesh" — sons, daughters, young, old, male servants, female servants — without distinction.

"And they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak with different tongues, as the Spirit was giving them the ability to speak out" (2:4).

"Filled with the Holy Spirit" (eplesthesan pantes pneumatos hagiou) — the verb pimplemi ("to fill") is a Lukan word for the Spirit's operation. In the Gospel, Elizabeth was "filled with the Holy Spirit" and prophesied (Luke 1:41). Zechariah was "filled with the Holy Spirit" and prophesied (Luke 1:67). Jesus was "full of the Holy Spirit" when he returned from the Jordan (Luke 4:1). The pattern is consistent: being filled with the Spirit produces speech — specifically, prophetic or Spirit-directed speech. The same pattern holds here: they are filled, and they speak.

"Began to speak with different tongues" (erxanto lalein heterais glossais). The word heterais ("different, other") distinguishes these tongues from their native language. As verses 6-11 will clarify, these are not ecstatic utterances but actual human languages — the languages of the peoples gathered in Jerusalem. The Spirit gives them the ability (apophtheggesthai) to speak in languages they do not naturally know. The verb apophtheggomai means "to speak out, to declare, to utter with clarity" — it connotes authoritative, clear, purposeful speech, not unintelligible babble. The same verb is used for prophetic utterance in the LXX (1 Chronicles 25:1, where the temple musicians "prophesied" — apephthengonto — with instruments; Ezekiel 13:9, 19 for prophetic declaration). The Spirit is producing intelligible, purposeful, prophetic speech in identifiable languages.

"As the Spirit was giving them the ability" (kathos to pneuma edidou autois) — the imperfect tense (edidou, "was giving") indicates continuous, ongoing action. The Spirit did not give a one-time ability; it kept giving, sustained the speech, directed the content. The community does not generate this speech on its own; the Spirit produces it through them. This is what Jesus meant by "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you" (1:8) — the power manifests as Spirit-directed speech, the capacity to declare God's deeds in the languages of the nations.

Verses 5-13: The Multinational Audience and Their Response

"Now there were Jews residing in Jerusalem, devout men from every nation under heaven" (2:5).

The author establishes the audience. These are Jews (Ioudaioi) — not Gentiles but members of the Jewish diaspora who have come to Jerusalem. The word katoikountes ("residing, dwelling") could mean permanent residents or temporary residents present for the festival; the list that follows includes both settlers and "visitors" (epidemountes, v. 10). The descriptor "devout" (eulabeis) characterizes them as pious, God-fearing Jews who take their religious obligations seriously — men who have made the pilgrimage to Jerusalem for the festival. The phrase "from every nation under heaven" (apo pantos ethnous ton hypo ton ouranon) is comprehensive: the Jewish diaspora has brought representatives from across the known world to this single location at this single moment. The author presents this as providential — the audience that hears the first gospel proclamation is a cross-section of the entire diaspora.

"And when this sound occurred, the crowd came together and they were bewildered, because each one of them was hearing them speak in his own language" (2:6).

The "sound" (phone) that draws the crowd may be the sound of the wind (v. 2) or the sound of the multilingual speech itself, or both — the word phone can mean sound, voice, or language. The crowd is "bewildered" (synechythe — from syncheō, "to confuse, to confound, to throw into disorder"). The same verb appears in Acts 9:22 when Paul "confounded" the Jews of Damascus, and in Acts 19:32 for the confusion of the mob in Ephesus. The bewilderment is not hostile but cognitive — they cannot account for what they are hearing.

The key phrase is "each one of them was hearing them speak in his own language" (ekousein heis hekastos te idia dialekto lalounton auton). Each individual hearer recognizes his own native language (dialekto — "dialect, language") being spoken by the community. The miracle operates on both sides: the speakers are given the ability to speak languages they do not know, and the hearers recognize their own languages. The word idia ("own, personal, native") emphasizes that each person hears the language of his homeland, the language of his birth.

"They were amazed and astonished, saying, 'Why, are not all these who are speaking Galileans?'" (2:7).

The crowd identifies the speakers as Galileans (Galilaioi). Galileans had a distinctive accent noted by other Jews (Matthew 26:73) and were not regarded as cosmopolitan or multilingual. The amazement lies in the incongruity: uneducated Galileans are speaking in the languages of Parthia, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Rome, and beyond. The crowd's question is rhetorical — of course these are Galileans; that is precisely what makes this inexplicable.

"And how is it that we each hear them in our own language to which we were born?" (2:8).

The phrase "language to which we were born" (te idia dialekto hemon en he egenneethemen) drives the point deeper: this is not formal or literary language but the mother tongue, the language of birth and home. The Spirit enables the speakers to communicate in the most intimate, native register of each hearer's experience.

"Parthians, Medes, and Elamites, and residents of Mesopotamia, Judea, and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia, Phrygia and Pamphylia, Egypt and the parts of Libya around Cyrene, and visitors from Rome, both Jews and proselytes, Cretans and Arabs" (2:9-11a).

The list sweeps from east to west across the known world. It begins with the far east — Parthia, Media, and Elam (modern Iran and Iraq), regions associated with the ancient Assyrian and Babylonian exiles of the northern and southern kingdoms. Mesopotamia includes the Tigris-Euphrates heartland where Abraham originated and where many Jews remained after the Babylonian exile. The list moves westward through the provinces of Asia Minor (Cappadocia, Pontus, Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia), south to Egypt and Libya (the north African diaspora communities), and then to Rome (the heart of the empire). The specification "both Jews and proselytes" (Ioudaioi te kai proselytoi) applied to the Roman visitors indicates that this audience includes both ethnic Jews and Gentile converts to Judaism. The list ends with Cretans and Arabs, adding the Mediterranean islands and the Arabian peninsula.

The geographical scope corresponds to the diaspora map of first-century Judaism. But it also resonates with the Table of Nations in Genesis 10, which catalogued the descendants of Noah and the peoples of the earth. The Pentecost event gathers representatives of the scattered nations into a single hearing of God's mighty deeds — a pattern that reverses the scattering at Babel (Genesis 11:1-9). At Babel, the human community spoke one language and was scattered by the confusion of tongues into mutual incomprehension. At Pentecost, the scattered nations hear the one message in their own tongues. What Babel divided, Pentecost reunites — not by imposing a single language but by the Spirit enabling communication across all languages.

The list also traces, in broad terms, the path that the gospel will follow through Acts: from Jerusalem through Asia Minor to Rome. The nations represented at Pentecost hear the message first; the narrative of Acts will then carry the message to these same regions.

"We hear them speaking in our own tongues of the mighty deeds of God" (2:11b).

The content of the Spirit-inspired speech is identified: "the mighty deeds of God" (ta megaleia tou theou). The word megaleia means "great things, magnificent works, mighty deeds." In the LXX, the same word describes God's acts of deliverance and power. In Deuteronomy 11:2 (LXX), Moses speaks of "the discipline of the Lord your God, His greatness (megalosynen), His mighty hand and His outstretched arm." In the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:3, LXX): "Give greatness (megalosynen) to our God." And the term's closest echo in this author's own work is Mary's Magnificat: "the Mighty One has done great things for me" (Luke 1:49, epoiesen moi megala ho dynatos). The content being declared in all these languages is the same message: God has done mighty things. The specifics of those mighty things — the life, death, resurrection, and exaltation of Jesus — Peter will lay out in his speech that follows.

"And they all continued in amazement and great perplexity, saying to one another, 'What does this mean?' But others were jeering and saying, 'They are full of sweet wine!'" (2:12-13).

The crowd divides into two responses. The majority are perplexed (dieporoun — "to be thoroughly at a loss, to be unable to determine meaning"). Their question "What does this mean?" (Ti thelei touto einai — literally "What does this wish to be?") is genuine inquiry. They recognize that something extraordinary is happening but lack the interpretive framework to understand it.

Others respond with mockery: "They are full of sweet wine" (gleukous memestomenoi eisin). Gleukos is new, sweet wine — not the ordinary wine of daily use but fresh, unfermented or partially fermented wine. The accusation is drunkenness. The mockery serves a narrative function: it provides Peter with his opening. His speech begins by addressing the charge directly and then proceeds to provide the interpretive framework the perplexed crowd is seeking.

Verses 14-21: Peter Interprets the Event Through Joel

"But Peter, taking his stand with the other eleven, raised his voice and declared to them: 'Men of Judea and all you who live in Jerusalem, know this, and pay attention to my words'" (2:14).

Peter stands "with the other eleven" (syn tois hendeka). The Twelve are complete — Matthias was added in 1:26, and the number is now restored. Peter does not speak alone; he speaks as the voice of the full apostolic body. The phrase "raised his voice" (eperen ten phonen autou) indicates formal, public address — the speech is delivered to a large crowd in the open, not in the intimacy of the upper room. The verb apephthegxato ("declared") is the same verb used in verse 4 for the Spirit-directed speech. Peter's declaration is itself a manifestation of the Spirit's work through him — the Spirit that filled them all now directs Peter's proclamation.

The address "Men of Judea and all you who live in Jerusalem" (andres Ioudaioi kai hoi katoikountes Ierousalem pantes) identifies the immediate audience: Jews in and around Jerusalem. The demand to "pay attention" (enotisasthe — literally "give ear to") is a prophetic formula. In the LXX, the prophets regularly demand the audience's hearing: "Give ear, O heavens" (Deuteronomy 32:1, LXX: enotizomai); "Hear, O Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Peter speaks with prophetic authority, calling the crowd to attend to what the Spirit is revealing through him.

"For these people are not drunk, as you assume, since it is only the third hour of the day" (2:15).

The third hour is approximately 9:00 a.m. — far too early for the kind of drunkenness the mockers suggest. The argument is practical and rooted in the cultural pattern: Jews did not typically eat or drink before the morning prayers and the third-hour offering. The accusation is dismissed on simple factual grounds. But the dismissal is brief because Peter's purpose is not to defend the speakers but to interpret the event.

"But this is what has been spoken through the prophet Joel" (2:16).

Peter provides the interpretive key: what the crowd is witnessing is the fulfillment of Joel's prophecy. The phrase "this is that" (touto estin to eiremenon) makes a direct identification — not "this is like what Joel described" but "this is the thing Joel was talking about." The Pentecost event is Joel's prophecy being realized in history.

"'And it shall be in the last days,' God says, 'That I will pour out My Spirit on all mankind; And your sons and your daughters will prophesy, And your young men will see visions, And your old men will have dreams; And even on My male and female servants I will pour out My Spirit in those days, And they will prophesy'" (2:17-18).

Peter's citation of Joel 2:28-29 (LXX 3:1-2) contains several modifications from the Hebrew and LXX text that reveal his interpretive work.

First, Joel begins "It will come about after this" (kai estai meta tauta, LXX). Peter changes this to "in the last days" (en tais eschatais hemerais). This is not a careless alteration but a deliberate interpretive claim. Peter is declaring that the events of Pentecost mark the arrival of the "last days" — the eschatological period in which God's final purposes are set in motion. The phrase "last days" (eschatai hemerai) appears in the LXX in Isaiah 2:2 ("in the last days the mountain of the house of the Lord shall be established") and in Micah 4:1 with similar language. Peter is reading Joel through the lens of the broader prophetic expectation: the outpouring of the Spirit is a marker of the last days, the age in which God's promises come to fulfillment.

Second, Peter adds "God says" (legei ho theos), making explicit what Joel implies — the speaker of these words is God himself. The addition underscores that the Pentecost event is God's own action, announced in advance through the prophet.

Third, Joel says "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" (epi pasan sarka). Peter retains this: "on all mankind" (epi pasan sarka). The scope is comprehensive. "All flesh" (pasa sarx) does not mean every individual human being but removes the previous limitations on the Spirit's activity. In the old dispensation, the Spirit came upon specific individuals for specific tasks: upon judges like Samson (Judges 14:6), upon kings like Saul and David (1 Samuel 10:10, 16:13), upon prophets like Ezekiel (Ezekiel 2:2). Moses himself wished "that all the Lord's people were prophets, that the Lord would put His Spirit upon them" (Numbers 11:29). Joel announced that Moses's wish would be fulfilled. Peter declares that the fulfillment has arrived.

The specifics of the "all flesh" are then enumerated in terms that break down every social division: - "Your sons and your daughters" — male and female, both prophesy. - "Your young men... your old men" — age is no barrier; both young and old receive revelatory experience. - "My male and female servants" (doulous mou kai... doulas mou) — Peter adds "My" (mou), which is present in the LXX but emphasizes that these servants belong to God. Even servants — those of lowest social status — receive the Spirit and prophesy.

Peter adds "and they will prophesy" (kai propheteusousin) at the end of verse 18, where the Hebrew text of Joel does not have it. This addition drives home the point: the prophetic gift is extended to every category of person, without exception. The Spirit's distribution to "each one of them" (v. 3) and the speech produced in "each one" who is filled (v. 4) is the realization of this democratized prophecy.

"'And I will display wonders in the sky above And signs on the earth below, Blood, fire, and vapor of smoke. The sun will be turned into darkness And the moon into blood, Before the great and glorious day of the Lord comes'" (2:19-20).

Peter continues citing Joel 2:30-31 (LXX 3:3-4). These verses describe cosmic signs accompanying the Day of the Lord. Peter modifies Joel's text slightly: he adds "above" (ano) to "sky" and "below" (kato) to "earth," creating a spatial contrast that emphasizes the totality of the signs — above in the heavens, below on earth, everywhere.

The question of how Peter understands these cosmic signs is answered by the text he is citing them within. Peter is not claiming that the sun has already turned to darkness or the moon to blood. He is citing the full Joel passage because he needs to reach the conclusion in verse 21 — the promise that "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved." The cosmic signs in Joel describe the accompaniments of the Day of the Lord, the divine intervention that Joel prophesied. Peter's argument is that the Spirit's outpouring is the leading edge of that eschatological event — the "last days" have begun, and the entire complex of Joel's prophecy has been set in motion. The Spirit's arrival is the first sign; the rest of Joel's sequence belongs to the unfolding future.

The change from Joel's "awesome" (LXX: epiphane, "manifest, conspicuous") to "glorious" (endoxon) is notable. Peter shifts the characterization of the Day of the Lord from its terrifying aspect to its glorious one. For the audience Peter is addressing, the Day of the Lord is presented not primarily as judgment (though that element remains) but as the occasion for deliverance — which is exactly where the citation lands.

"'And it shall be that everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved'" (2:21).

This is the destination of the Joel citation, the verse toward which Peter has been building. In Joel's Hebrew, the phrase is "everyone who calls on the name of YHWH" (kol asher yiqra beshem YHWH). The LXX renders YHWH as kyrios ("Lord"), and Peter cites the LXX form: pas hos an epikalesetai to onoma kyriou sothesetai.

The promise is universal in scope: pas ("everyone"), not limited by ethnicity, gender, age, or social status — the same comprehensive scope established in the "all flesh" of verse 17. The condition is singular: calling on the name of the Lord. The verb epikalesetai (from epikaleomai, "to call upon, to invoke, to appeal to") describes active, deliberate invocation. In the LXX, "calling on the name of the Lord" is the language of prayer, worship, and covenantal appeal (Genesis 4:26, 12:8; Psalm 116:4, 17; Isaiah 12:4).

The critical question is: who is "the Lord" (kyrios) whose name is to be called upon? In Joel, the referent is YHWH. Peter's speech will build toward identifying Jesus as "Lord" (v. 36: "God has made Him both Lord and Christ"), at which point the Joel citation retroactively acquires a christological dimension. The name upon which one must call for salvation is the name of the one whom God has made Lord — Jesus. Peter does not state this yet; he is building his argument layer by layer. But the citation from Joel plants the principle that salvation comes through calling on the name of the Lord, and the speech will conclude by revealing who that Lord is.

The portion of Joel that Peter does not cite is equally significant. Joel 2:32b continues: "For on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there will be those who escape, just as the Lord has said, even among the survivors whom the Lord calls." Peter stops before this clause, but the idea of divine calling is not absent — it will reappear in verse 39: "as many as the Lord our God will call to Himself." The relationship between human calling (on God's name) and divine calling (of those God summons) is held together in Joel and in Peter's speech: salvation involves both the human act of invoking the Lord and the divine act of calling people to himself.

Verses 22-24: Jesus's Ministry, Death, and Resurrection

"Men of Israel, listen to these words: Jesus the Nazarene, a Man attested to you by God with miracles and wonders and signs which God performed through Him in your midst, just as you yourselves know" (2:22).

Peter transitions from the Joel prophecy to its application. The address shifts to "Men of Israel" (andres Israelitai) — the same audience, now addressed by their covenant identity. They are Israel, the people to whom God's promises were made.

Jesus is identified by his human origin ("the Nazarene," ton Nazoraion) and by his divine attestation. The verb apodedeigmenon ("attested, demonstrated, shown forth") means to display publicly, to prove by evidence. God is the active agent: God attested Jesus to Israel through "miracles and wonders and signs" (dynamesin kai terasin kai semeiois). This triad appears together in the LXX for God's acts of deliverance in the Exodus: "the signs and the wonders" (ta semeia kai ta terata) that God performed in Egypt (Deuteronomy 4:34, 6:22, 7:19, 26:8, 29:3). Peter's language places Jesus's ministry within the pattern of God's great acts of deliverance in Israel's history. Just as God attested Moses through signs and wonders in Egypt, God attested Jesus through signs and wonders in Israel's midst.

The phrase "which God performed through Him" (hon epoiesen di autou ho theos) is theologically precise: the miracles were God's work done through Jesus. Jesus is the agent; God is the source. This matches Luke's consistent portrayal: Jesus performed miracles by the power of the Spirit of God (Luke 4:14, 18; 5:17; 11:20).

"Just as you yourselves know" (kathos autoi oidate) — Peter appeals to the audience's own knowledge. They are eyewitnesses of Jesus's public ministry; they saw the signs. The appeal to common knowledge is a rhetorical move that establishes shared ground: what Peter is about to argue is built on facts the audience already possesses.

"This Man, delivered over by the predetermined plan and foreknowledge of God, you nailed to a cross by the hands of godless men and put Him to death" (2:23).

This single sentence holds together divine sovereignty and human responsibility without dissolving either into the other.

"Delivered over by the predetermined plan and foreknowledge of God" (te horismene boule kai prognosei tou theou ekdoton) — the word horismene (from horizo, "to determine, to define, to fix a boundary") means "determined, fixed, decided." The boule is God's "plan, purpose, deliberate counsel." The prognosis is God's "foreknowledge, knowing in advance." The compound phrase asserts that the crucifixion was not an accident or a failure in God's plan; it was the execution of what God had already determined and foreknown. The same theological framework appeared in Luke 22:22: "the Son of Man is going as it has been determined" (kata to horismenon). God's plan included the death of Jesus.

But the second half of the sentence assigns full moral responsibility to the human actors: "you nailed to a cross by the hands of godless men and put Him to death" (dia cheiros anomon prospexantes aneilate). The pronoun "you" (hymeis, implied in the verb aneilate, "you killed") addresses the crowd directly. They are implicated in the crucifixion. The phrase "by the hands of godless men" (dia cheiros anomon — literally "through the hand of lawless ones") refers to the Romans, who were the instruments of the execution but outside the Torah's covenant. The audience — the men of Israel — are charged with responsibility for handing Jesus over to be crucified by lawless Gentiles. The verb prospexantes (from prospegnymi, "to fasten to, to nail") specifies the mode of death: crucifixion, nailing to a cross.

Peter does not resolve the tension between divine determination and human culpability; he holds them together. God determined it; you did it. Both statements are true simultaneously. The audience's guilt is not mitigated by God's plan, and God's plan is not disrupted by human wickedness. This reflects the pattern established in the Joseph narrative (Genesis 50:20: "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good") and in the prayer of Acts 4:27-28, where the community will say that Herod, Pontius Pilate, the Gentiles, and the peoples of Israel did "whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur."

"But God raised Him from the dead, putting an end to the agony of death, since it was impossible for Him to be held in its power" (2:24).

The adversative "but" (hon ho theos anestesen — literally "whom God raised up") sets God's action in direct contrast to the human action of the preceding verse. They killed him; God raised him. The subject of the resurrection is God, not Jesus — God raised Jesus from the dead. This is consistent with the author's theology throughout Luke-Acts: resurrection is God's act of vindication, God's reversal of the unjust human verdict.

"Putting an end to the agony of death" (lysas tas odinas tou thanatou) — the verb lysas ("having loosed, having released") means to unbind, to set free. The phrase "agony of death" (odinas tou thanatou) uses the word odines, which means "birth pangs, labor pains." The image is striking: death is portrayed as being in labor, and the resurrection is the birth that ends the labor pains. The LXX of Psalm 18:5-6 (LXX 17:5-6) uses the phrase "the cords of death" (odines thanatou in some LXX manuscripts, where the Hebrew has "cords" or "pangs"), and the LXX of Psalm 116:3 (LXX 114:3) reads "the pangs of death surrounded me" (perieschon me odines thanatou). Peter is drawing on psalmic language that describes the righteous sufferer's experience of death's grip and God's deliverance from it.

"Since it was impossible for Him to be held in its power" (kathothi ouk en dynaton krateisthai auton hyp autou) — the word adynaton ("impossible") is absolute. Death's hold on Jesus was not merely temporary; it was constitutionally impossible for death to retain him. Peter does not explain why it was impossible at this point; the explanation comes in the psalm citation that follows. The impossibility is grounded in what God promised through David — God's Holy One cannot undergo decay, and therefore death cannot hold him.

Verses 25-28: The Testimony of David — Psalm 16

"For David says of Him, 'I saw the Lord continually before me, Because He is at my right hand, so that I will not be shaken. Therefore my heart was glad and my tongue was overjoyed; Moreover my flesh also will live in hope; For You will not abandon my soul to Hades, Nor will You allow Your Holy One to undergo decay. You have made known to me the ways of life; You will make me full of gladness with Your presence'" (2:25-28).

Peter cites Psalm 16:8-11 (LXX 15:8-11) in its entirety from the LXX text. The citation is the scriptural basis for Peter's claim that it was "impossible" for death to hold Jesus (v. 24).

The psalm as a whole is a declaration of trust in God by one who has set the Lord always before him. In its original context, the psalmist expresses confidence that God will not abandon him to Sheol (the abode of the dead) or let him see corruption. The first-century question — and Peter's question — is: who is speaking in this psalm?

Peter's argument will be that David cannot have been speaking about himself (vv. 29-31), and therefore David was speaking prophetically about someone else — the Christ.

The key phrases in the LXX text that Peter cites:

"I saw the Lord continually before me" (prooromen ton kyrion enopion mou dia pantos) — the verb prooran means "to see beforehand, to see before oneself." The psalmist places the Lord always in his field of vision, always before him. The word dia pantos ("continually, at all times") emphasizes unbroken awareness of God's presence.

"Because He is at my right hand, so that I will not be shaken" (hoti ek dexion mou estin hina me saleuthō) — the right hand is the position of protection, help, and strength. With the Lord at his right hand, the speaker is secure — he will not be "shaken" (saleuthō — "moved, disturbed, toppled"). The same word saleuō appears in Luke 6:48 for the house built on rock that "could not be shaken" by the flood. The speaker's stability comes from God's presence at his side.

"Therefore my heart was glad and my tongue was overjoyed" (dia touto euphranthe he kardia mou kai egalliasato he glossa mou) — the LXX translates the Hebrew "my glory" (kebodi) as "my tongue" (he glossa mou). This is significant because it makes the psalm about speech — the tongue rejoices, the tongue exults. In the context of Pentecost, where the Spirit has just produced speech in other tongues, the connection between the rejoicing tongue of the psalm and the Spirit-filled tongues of the upper room is present for the attentive hearer.

"Moreover my flesh also will live in hope" (eti de kai he sarx mou kataskēnosei ep elpidi) — the verb kataskēnoō means "to dwell, to take up residence, to tabernacle." The flesh — the physical body — will dwell securely in hope. This is not a statement about the soul alone but about the body. The speaker's physical body has a future grounded in hope. This phrase becomes the crux of Peter's argument: David's body did undergo decay (v. 29), but the one David spoke about — the Christ — had a body that dwelt in hope because God would not allow it to see decay.

"For You will not abandon my soul to Hades" (hoti ouk egkataleipseis ten psychen mou eis hadēn) — Hades is the Greek equivalent of the Hebrew Sheol, the abode of the dead. The promise is that the speaker's soul (psyche, his life, his person) will not be left in the realm of the dead. The verb egkataleipō means "to abandon, to leave behind, to forsake." The speaker trusts that God will not leave him among the dead permanently.

"Nor will You allow Your Holy One to undergo decay" (oude dōseis ton hosion sou idein diaphthoran) — the word hosion ("Holy One, devout one, pious one") is the LXX translation of the Hebrew chasid ("faithful one, loyal one, devoted one"). The word diaphthora means "decay, corruption, decomposition" — the physical decomposition of the body after death. The promise is specific and physical: God's Holy One will not experience bodily decomposition.

"You have made known to me the ways of life; You will make me full of gladness with Your presence" (egnōrisas moi hodous zōēs; plērōseis me euphrosynēs meta tou prosōpou sou) — the "ways of life" (hodous zōēs) are the paths that lead to life, not death. Knowledge of these paths comes from God. And the ultimate destination is God's presence — face-to-face with God (meta tou prosōpou sou, "with Your face") — a presence that fills with gladness. This final clause points beyond mere resuscitation to exaltation: the one who is not abandoned in Hades enters God's presence and is filled with joy there.

Verses 29-31: David Cannot Be the Subject — Therefore It Is the Christ

"Brothers, I may confidently say to you regarding the patriarch David that he both died and was buried, and his tomb is with us to this day" (2:29).

Peter's argument turns on a simple, verifiable fact. David died. David was buried. David's tomb — known and visible in first-century Jerusalem — contains his remains. Josephus confirms the location: "David's tomb is in Jerusalem" (Antiquities 7.15.3, 13.8.4, 16.7.1), and Nehemiah 3:16 references David's tombs. Peter appeals to what the audience can see with their own eyes: the patriarch's tomb is still there, still occupied. David's body underwent decay. Therefore, when David wrote "You will not allow Your Holy One to undergo decay," he could not have been speaking about himself.

The address "brothers" (andres adelphoi) shifts the tone from the formal "Men of Israel" to a more intimate register. Peter speaks to his audience as fellow members of the covenant community, not as an outsider addressing strangers.

The word "patriarch" (patriarchēs — "founder of a family, ancestor of a people") designates David's role in Israel's history. He is not merely a poet or a king but the ancestor from whose line the promised ruler would come. The title underscores David's prophetic significance — he speaks as the father of the messianic line.

"So because he was a prophet and knew that God had sworn to him with an oath to seat one of his descendants on his throne, he looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ" (2:30-31a).

Peter's reasoning has three steps:

First, David was a prophet (prophetes on). This designation is rooted in scripture. The Spirit of the Lord spoke through David (2 Samuel 23:2: "The Spirit of the Lord spoke by me, and His word was on my tongue"). The psalms are treated throughout the New Testament as prophetic speech, not merely personal devotion. Peter already established this principle in Acts 1:16: "the Scripture had to be fulfilled, which the Holy Spirit foretold by the mouth of David."

Second, David knew the content of God's oath. The oath in view is God's covenant with David recorded in 2 Samuel 7:12-16 and reiterated in Psalm 132:11 (LXX 131:11): "The Lord swore to David a truth from which He will not turn back: 'Of the fruit of your body I will set upon your throne.'" God swore an oath — not merely made a promise but bound himself with an oath — to place one of David's physical descendants (ek karpou tes osphyos autou — "from the fruit of his loins") on his throne. The throne in view is David's royal throne, the seat of the Davidic dynasty, but the oath's scope is eschatological: the descendant will reign forever (2 Samuel 7:13: "I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever").

Third, because David was a prophet who knew this oath, he "looked ahead" (proïdon — "foresaw, saw in advance") and spoke about the resurrection of the Christ. The verb proïdon is the key: David was not writing about his own experience but looking forward to a future event. The word "Christ" (tou Christou) here means "the Anointed One" — the one God had anointed to sit on David's throne. Peter is arguing that Psalm 16 is a messianic psalm: David, as a prophet, wrote it about the future anointed king from his own line.

"That He was neither abandoned to Hades, nor did His flesh suffer decay" (2:31b).

Peter restates the psalm's core promises, now explicitly applied to the Christ. The two negatives correspond to the psalm's two promises: not abandoned to Hades (the person was rescued from death) and no decay (the body was preserved from decomposition). Both were true of Jesus and false of David. The argument is airtight within its own logic: the psalm promised something that David did not experience; therefore the psalm speaks of someone other than David; that someone is the Christ; Jesus of Nazareth, whom God raised, is the one in whom these promises were fulfilled.

Verses 32-36: The Exaltation and the Declaration

"It is this Jesus whom God raised up, a fact to which we are all witnesses" (2:32).

The argument arrives at its focal point. "This Jesus" (touton ton Iesoun) — the same specific, historical person they knew, the Nazarene attested by signs (v. 22), the one they crucified (v. 23). God raised him. The verb anestesen ("raised up") is the same used throughout for resurrection. And the apostles are witnesses (martyres) — the role defined in 1:8 and 1:22. This is the apostolic testimony in its simplest form: God raised Jesus from the dead, and we are witnesses of this fact. The word "all" (pantes) — "we are all witnesses" — emphasizes the collective, unanimous nature of the testimony.

"Therefore, since He has been exalted at the right hand of God, and has received the promise of the Holy Spirit from the Father, He has poured out this which you both see and hear" (2:33).

Peter now connects the resurrection to the Pentecost event through a chain of reasoning:

Step 1: Jesus was exalted. The phrase "exalted at the right hand of God" (te dexia oun tou theou hypsotheis) uses the verb hypsoo ("to lift up, to exalt") — the same verb used in Luke 14:11 and 18:14 ("everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted"). Jesus humbled himself to the point of crucifixion; God exalted him to the highest position — the right hand of God. The "right hand of God" (dexia tou theou) is the position of supreme honor and authority. In Psalm 110:1 (which Peter is about to cite), the Lord says to David's lord "Sit at My right hand." Jesus's exaltation to God's right hand is the fulfillment of this enthronement oracle.

Step 2: At that exalted position, Jesus "received the promise of the Holy Spirit from the Father" (ten epangelian tou pneumatos tou hagiou labōn para tou patros). The "promise of the Holy Spirit" was what Jesus told the disciples to wait for (1:4: "wait for what the Father had promised"). Now Peter reveals the chain of transmission: the Father promised the Spirit; the exalted Jesus received the Spirit from the Father; and Jesus has now distributed the Spirit to the community.

Step 3: The exalted Jesus "poured out this which you both see and hear" (execheen touto ho hymeis kai blepete kai akouete). The verb execheen ("poured out") is the same verb used in the Joel citation: "I will pour out My Spirit" (ekcheo to pneuma mou, v. 17). In Joel, the one who pours out the Spirit is God. Peter now identifies the exalted Jesus as the one who performs this pouring out — the act that Joel attributed to God is performed by the risen, exalted Jesus. The implication is staggering but unstated directly: Jesus is exercising a divine prerogative. He does what Joel said God would do.

"This which you both see and hear" (touto ho hymeis kai blepete kai akouete) — the Pentecost event is presented as empirical evidence. The audience can see the visible phenomena (the tongues of fire, the behavior of the speakers) and hear the multilingual speech. Peter's argument is not based on private mystical experience but on publicly observable events. The Spirit's arrival has produced effects that anyone present can perceive.

"For it was not David who ascended into heaven, but he himself says: 'The Lord said to my Lord, "Sit at My right hand, Until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet"'" (2:34-35).

Peter now introduces a second psalm text, Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1), and applies the same logic he used with Psalm 16. The argument follows the same structure: David could not have been speaking about himself because David did not ascend into heaven. The person described in the psalm — the one invited to sit at God's right hand — is someone other than David.

Psalm 110 is the most cited Old Testament text in the New Testament. In its original context, it is a royal psalm, an oracle spoken to the king (David's lord) at his enthronement. The opening formula is striking: "The Lord said to my lord" (eipen ho kyrios to kyrio mou) — David's God (the first kyrios, YHWH in Hebrew) speaks to David's lord (the second kyrios, adoni in Hebrew). David, the king of Israel, acknowledges a figure who is his own lord — someone who holds a position superior to David himself.

The invitation "Sit at My right hand" (kathou ek dexion mou) is an enthronement command. The right hand of God is the seat of co-regency, the position from which the enthroned one shares in God's authority. The clause "until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet" (heos an tho tous echthrous sou hypopodion ton podon sou) describes a military image drawn from ancient Near Eastern conquest symbolism: the victorious king places his foot on the neck of the conquered (Joshua 10:24). God will subject all opposition to the authority of the one seated at his right hand.

Peter's point is christological: David spoke of someone whom David himself called "my lord" — someone greater than David, someone who would sit at God's right hand. David did not ascend to heaven or sit at God's right hand. But Jesus, whom God raised and exalted (v. 33), has ascended and has taken the seat at God's right hand. Therefore, Jesus is the one David called "my lord" in Psalm 110.

The argument from the two psalms works in tandem: - Psalm 16 establishes that the Christ would not remain in death — his body would not see decay. This was fulfilled in the resurrection. - Psalm 110 establishes that David's lord would sit at God's right hand in authority. This was fulfilled in the exaltation/ascension.

Together, the two psalms describe the full trajectory: resurrection from death (Psalm 16) and enthronement at God's right hand (Psalm 110). Peter is arguing that Jesus has completed both movements — he has been raised, and he has been exalted — and the proof of the exaltation is the Pentecost event itself: the Spirit has been poured out from above because Jesus is now above, at the Father's right hand, and has received the Spirit from the Father to distribute.

"Therefore let all the house of Israel know for certain that God has made Him both Lord and Christ — this Jesus whom you crucified" (2:36).

This is the climactic declaration of the speech, the conclusion toward which every preceding argument has been building. The address is to "all the house of Israel" (pas oikos Israel) — the entire covenant community, not just those present but the nation as a whole. The verb asphalos ("for certain, assuredly, beyond doubt") means with security, without possibility of error. Peter is not offering a hypothesis; he is announcing a fact established by prophetic testimony, historical events, and observable evidence.

"God has made Him both Lord and Christ" (kai kyrion auton kai christon epoiesen ho theos) — the verb epoiesen ("made") does not mean that Jesus became something he was not, as if he were created as Lord and Christ at the resurrection. The making is God's public appointment and installation of Jesus in the offices that the scriptures designated. "Lord" (kyrios) connects to the Joel citation (v. 21: "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved") and to Psalm 110 (v. 34: "The Lord said to my Lord"). Jesus is the kyrios upon whose name one must call. "Christ" (christos) connects to the Psalm 16 argument (v. 31: "the resurrection of the Christ"). Jesus is the anointed descendant of David who was promised an eternal throne.

The final phrase is devastating in its directness: "this Jesus whom you crucified" (touton ton Iesoun hon hymeis estaurosate). The pronoun "you" (hymeis) is emphatic. The audience did not merely hear about a crucifixion; they participated in it. The one they rejected, condemned, and handed over to be nailed to a cross — that one has been made Lord and Christ by God. The contrast between what Israel did to Jesus (crucified him) and what God did with Jesus (made him Lord and Christ) is the hinge of the entire speech. Israel's verdict and God's verdict are in direct opposition. The crucifixion was Israel's "no" to Jesus; the resurrection and exaltation are God's "yes." And God's verdict overrules Israel's.

Verses 37-40: The Response of the Crowd and Peter's Instruction

"Now when they heard this, they were pierced to the heart, and said to Peter and the rest of the apostles, 'Brothers, what are we to do?'" (2:37).

The verb katenygēsan ("were pierced to the heart") is a strong word. The root nyssō means "to pierce, to stab." The compound katanyssomai means to be pierced deeply, to be cut to the core. It appears in the LXX in Genesis 34:7 and Psalm 109:16 (LXX 108:16) for deep emotional and moral disturbance. The crowd is not merely moved or impressed; they are shattered by the realization that they crucified the one whom God has made Lord and Christ. The piercing is the recognition of guilt — the collision between what they did and what God did.

Their question "What are we to do?" (ti poiesomen) is the question of people who have been convicted but not yet condemned, who recognize their guilt and seek a way forward. The address "brothers" (andres adelphoi) — the same term Peter used for them (v. 29) — indicates that even in their guilt, they approach the apostles as members of the same community, the same family of Israel.

"Peter said to them, 'Repent, and each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit'" (2:38).

Peter's answer contains four elements, each building on the previous.

First: "Repent" (metanoesate). The verb metanoeō means "to change one's mind, to turn around, to reverse direction." It is the same word that formed the core of John the Baptist's message (Luke 3:3: "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins"), of Jesus's preaching (Luke 5:32: "I have not come to call the righteous but sinners to repentance"), and of the commission in Luke 24:47: "repentance for forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in His name to all the nations." Peter is now carrying out that commission. The repentance called for is specific: the crowd must reverse their verdict on Jesus. They rejected him; they must now receive him as Lord and Christ.

Second: "Each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ" (baptisthētō hekastos hymōn epi tō onomati Iēsou Christou). The command is individual — "each of you" (hekastos) — and involves baptism "in the name of" (epi tō onomati) or "upon the name of" Jesus Christ. The phrase "in the name of" signifies identification, allegiance, and authority. To be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ is to be publicly identified with the one whom God has made Lord and Christ — the very one the crowd crucified. It is a visible, physical act that corresponds to the internal change of repentance. John's baptism was with water for repentance (Luke 3:3, Acts 1:5); now baptism is "in the name of Jesus Christ," connecting the physical act to the specific person whom God has exalted.

Third: "For the forgiveness of your sins" (eis aphesin tōn hamartiōn hymōn). The preposition eis indicates purpose or result: baptism in Jesus's name is directed toward, results in, or is connected to the forgiveness of sins. This is the exact phrase from Luke 24:47: "repentance for forgiveness of sins" (metanoian eis aphesin hamartiōn). The commission Jesus gave is being fulfilled word for word. The sins in view include, at minimum, the sin Peter has just charged the audience with: the crucifixion of God's anointed one. But the phrase is not limited to that specific act; it encompasses the full scope of sins from which the audience needs release.

Fourth: "You will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (lēmpsesthe tēn dōrean tou hagiou pneumatos). The Holy Spirit is described as a "gift" (dōrea) — not a reward earned or a prize won but a free gift. The same Spirit that fell on the 120 at Pentecost is promised to those who repent and are baptized. The experience of the Spirit is not reserved for a spiritual elite or for the apostles alone; it is part of the response to the gospel that is available to every individual who repents and is baptized. The promise of Joel — "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" — is being extended beyond the initial recipients to all who respond.

The four elements form a sequence: repent (internal change of mind and direction), be baptized in Jesus's name (external, public identification with Jesus), receive forgiveness (the removal of sins), and receive the Holy Spirit (the gift that empowers the new life). This is the content of the gospel as Peter proclaims it — the practical answer to "What must we do?" in light of the fact that the crucified Jesus has been made Lord and Christ.

"For the promise is for you and your children and for all who are far away, as many as the Lord our God will call to Himself" (2:39).

Peter extends the scope of the promise in three concentric circles:

"For you" (hymin) — the immediate audience, the Jews of Jerusalem and the diaspora pilgrims present at the festival. They are the first recipients.

"And your children" (kai tois teknois hymōn) — the next generation, the descendants of the present audience. The promise is not limited to this moment; it extends into the future.

"And for all who are far away" (kai pasin tois eis makran) — the phrase eis makran ("far off, at a distance") in the LXX carries a specific resonance. Isaiah 57:19 reads: "Peace, peace to the one who is far and to the one who is near, says the Lord" (LXX: eirēnēn ep eirēnēn tois makran kai tois engys). In Ephesians 2:13, 17, the language of "far" and "near" is explicitly applied to Gentiles and Jews respectively. In Peter's speech, the phrase most immediately refers to diaspora Jews scattered across the world — those who are geographically "far away" from Jerusalem. But the language carries an inclusiveness that will be progressively realized as Acts unfolds: the "far away" will eventually include the Gentiles, those who are far from the covenant community itself.

The qualifying clause "as many as the Lord our God will call to Himself" (hosous an proskalesētai kyrios ho theos hēmōn) echoes the uncited portion of Joel 2:32: "among the survivors whom the Lord calls." The initiative is God's: the Lord calls people to himself. The human response (repentance, baptism, calling on the Lord's name) is real and necessary, but it operates within the framework of God's prior calling. The promise is not limited by ethnicity, geography, or generation — it extends to everyone whom the Lord calls.

"And with many other words he solemnly testified and kept on urging them, saying, 'Be saved from this perverse generation!'" (2:40).

The author summarizes additional content that he does not reproduce in full. The verbs "testified" (diemartyrato — an intensified form of martyreō, "to bear witness, to testify solemnly") and "kept on urging" (parekalei — "to exhort, to encourage, to appeal") indicate sustained, passionate, repeated appeal. Peter did not deliver a single speech and stop; he kept urging the crowd.

The phrase "this perverse generation" (apo tēs geneas tēs skolias tautēs) draws on Deuteronomy 32:5 (LXX), where Moses describes Israel as a "crooked and perverse generation" (genea skolia kai diestrammenē) in the Song of Moses. The same language appears in Philippians 2:15. Peter is applying Moses's characterization of unfaithful Israel to the present generation — the generation that crucified the Christ. The command to "be saved from" (sōthēte apo) this generation is a call to separate oneself from the corporate guilt of the generation that rejected Jesus. Salvation here is not abstract; it is deliverance from the consequences that will befall the generation that killed God's anointed one — a theme that Luke 19:41-44, 21:20-24, and 23:28-31 have already anticipated in Jesus's warnings about Jerusalem's coming destruction.

Verses 41-47: The Formation of the Community

"So then, those who had received his word were baptized; and that day there were added about three thousand souls" (2:41).

The response is immediate and massive. "Those who had received his word" (hoi men oun apodexamenoi ton logon autou) — the verb apodechomai means "to receive, to accept, to welcome." The word they received is Peter's proclamation — the entire content of his speech about Jesus's identity, death, resurrection, exaltation, and the call to repentance. Reception of the word precedes baptism: those who accepted the message were then baptized. Baptism is the response of those who have already believed.

"About three thousand souls" (psychai hosei trischiliai) — the number is large but not implausible given the festival crowd of diaspora Jews filling Jerusalem. The word psychai ("souls") is used in the LXX for persons, individuals (Genesis 46:27, Exodus 1:5). The number three thousand has a dark echo in Israel's history: at Sinai, after the golden calf incident, about three thousand men fell by the sword of the Levites (Exodus 32:28). At the giving of the Torah, three thousand died; at the giving of the Spirit, three thousand are given life. The parallel, if intentional, underscores the new-Sinai character of Pentecost: what the Torah could not do — give life (Galatians 3:21 makes this argument explicitly) — the Spirit accomplishes.

"They were continually devoting themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer" (2:42).

The author describes the community's life with four defining practices:

First, "the apostles' teaching" (tē didachē tōn apostolōn) — the community is built on instruction from the Twelve. The content of this teaching would include the testimony about Jesus's life, death, and resurrection, the interpretation of the Hebrew scriptures in light of Jesus, and the practical implications of the new life in the Spirit. The apostles are the authoritative teachers because they are the ones who were with Jesus from John's baptism through the ascension (1:21-22) and who received instruction from the risen Jesus during the forty days (1:3).

Second, "fellowship" (tē koinōnia) — the word koinōnia means sharing, participation, partnership, communion. Its concrete expression is described in verses 44-45: shared possessions, mutual care, meeting each other's needs. Koinōnia is not merely social friendliness but material solidarity — a community that shares its resources so that none are in need.

Third, "the breaking of bread" (tē klasei tou artou) — this phrase could refer to ordinary shared meals, but the specific formulation "the breaking of bread" (with the definite article, tē klasei) suggests a distinctive practice. The same phrase appears in Luke 24:35, where the Emmaus disciples described how Jesus "was recognized by them in the breaking of the bread." In Luke 22:19, at the last supper, Jesus "took bread, and when He had given thanks, He broke it and gave it to them." The "breaking of bread" in Acts appears to be a communal meal that carries the memory and meaning of Jesus's last supper — a shared meal in which the risen Lord's presence is recognized. It is practiced "from house to house" (kat oikon, v. 46), in domestic settings, as a regular feature of the community's life.

Fourth, "prayer" (tais proseuchais) — the definite article and plural form (tais proseuchais, "the prayers") may indicate set, regular prayers — the Jewish hours of prayer that the community continued to observe (3:1: Peter and John go to the temple "at the hour of prayer"). The community did not abandon Jewish prayer practice; they continued within it, now understanding it in light of what God had done through Jesus.

The verb "continually devoting themselves" (proskarterountes) is the same word used in 1:14 for the community's prayer life before Pentecost. The same verb, the same sustained commitment — what characterized the community before the Spirit came continues to characterize it after.

"Everyone kept feeling a sense of awe; and many wonders and signs were taking place through the apostles" (2:43).

"Awe" (phobos — literally "fear") pervades the community. This is not terror but reverential awareness of God's active presence among them. The same word describes the response to Jesus's miracles in the Gospel (Luke 5:26, 7:16, 8:37). The community lives in an atmosphere of awareness that God is at work.

"Wonders and signs" (terata kai sēmeia) — the same pairing used in verse 22 to describe God's attestation of Jesus. What God did through Jesus during his ministry, God now does through the apostles. The continuity is intentional: Jesus "began" to do and teach (1:1), and through the apostles he continues. The wonders and signs validate the apostles' testimony and demonstrate that the same power that operated in Jesus now operates through his witnesses.

"And all the believers were together and had all things in common; and they would sell their property and possessions and share them with all, to the extent that anyone had need" (2:44-45).

The community's material sharing is described in two movements. First, "they had all things in common" (eichon hapanta koina) — the word koina ("common, shared") means that private ownership was subordinated to communal need. This is not enforced redistribution but voluntary sharing — the verb "would sell" (epipraskon) indicates a continuing, voluntary practice, not a one-time command. Second, "to the extent that anyone had need" (kathoti an tis chreian eichen) — the distribution is driven by need, not by ideology. The purpose is that no member of the community lacks what is necessary.

The practice echoes the ideal expressed in Deuteronomy 15:4: "there will be no poor among you, since the Lord will surely bless you in the land which the Lord your God is giving you." The Torah's vision of a community with no poverty is being realized — not through legislative enforcement but through the Spirit-generated generosity of the new community. The contrast with the surrounding culture is stark: in a world of sharp economic stratification, the community models an alternative.

"Day by day continuing with one mind in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, they were taking their meals together with gladness and sincerity of heart, praising God and having favor with all the people" (2:46-47a).

The community's life has two locations: the temple and the house. "In the temple" (en tō hierō) — they continue to participate in the temple worship, attending the daily prayer services and teaching in the public spaces of the temple complex. Solomon's Portico (3:11, 5:12) will become a regular gathering place. The community does not separate itself from Israel's institutions; it remains within the temple, the center of Jewish worship, even as it practices its distinctive life in homes.

"Breaking bread from house to house" (kat oikon klōntes arton) — the domestic setting for the community's shared meals. The phrase kat oikon ("house by house, from house to house") indicates multiple homes serving as gathering places, not a single centralized location. The community life is distributed across the households of its members.

The emotional tenor is described with two words: "gladness" (agalliasei — a word for exultant, intense joy, the same root used in Luke 1:14, 44, 47 for the joy surrounding the births of John and Jesus) and "sincerity of heart" (aphelotēti kardias — the word aphelotēs means simplicity, openness, lack of pretense). This is not forced or performative; it is genuine, unaffected joy in shared life.

"Praising God" (ainountes ton theon) echoes the final verse of the Gospel: "continually in the temple praising God" (Luke 24:53). The community that praised God at the end of the Gospel continues to praise God at the beginning of Acts. The continuity is seamless.

"Having favor with all the people" (echontes charin pros holon ton laon) — the community is not yet a persecuted sect. In its earliest days, it enjoys the goodwill of the broader Jewish population. The word laos ("people") is the author's term for Israel as a whole, the people of God. The community is perceived favorably because it lives within the temple, practices generosity, and radiates joy. The hostility will come later (Acts 4-5), but at this stage, the community grows within a context of public approval.

"And the Lord was adding to their number day by day those who were being saved" (2:47b).

The final sentence of the chapter identifies the agent of growth: "the Lord" (ho kyrios) — whether God the Father or the exalted Jesus (the ambiguity may again be intentional) — is the one adding to the community. The community does not recruit or campaign; the Lord adds. Growth is God's work, not the community's achievement.

The phrase "those who were being saved" (tous sōzomenous) uses the present passive participle, indicating an ongoing process. Salvation here is not described as a completed past event but as a present, continuing reality — the Lord keeps adding, and those being added are those who are in the process of being saved. The word sōzō ("to save") connects to the Joel citation in verse 21: "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved." The salvation Joel prophesied is now happening, daily, as the Lord adds people to the community of those who have called on the name of the Lord Jesus Christ.

"Day by day" (kath hēmeran) — not in a single dramatic event but as a continuing daily reality. The three thousand of verse 41 was the beginning, not the end. The community's growth is organic, daily, sustained by the Lord's ongoing activity.

Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. Acts 2 is the fulfillment chapter. Everything promised in Luke 24 and Acts 1 is delivered here. The power from on high arrives (Luke 24:49 / Acts 1:8). The baptism with the Holy Spirit occurs (Acts 1:5). The commission to proclaim repentance and forgiveness in Jesus's name beginning from Jerusalem is carried out (Luke 24:47). The witness to the resurrection begins (Acts 1:22). The chapter is structured as promise fulfilled, point by point.

2. The Pentecost event is interpreted through Joel as the inauguration of the "last days." Peter's modification of Joel's "after this" to "in the last days" makes a specific eschatological claim: the outpouring of the Spirit marks the beginning of the final epoch of God's dealings with the world. The "last days" are not a future period; they have begun. Everything that follows in Acts — and in the life of the community thereafter — takes place within this eschatological framework.

3. The Spirit's distribution breaks all social barriers. Joel's prophecy, as Peter cites it, emphasizes the comprehensive scope of the Spirit's outpouring: sons and daughters, young and old, male servants and female servants. The visible tongues resting on "each one" (v. 3) and the filling of "all" (v. 4) embody this principle. The Spirit is not a possession of elites; it is given to all flesh.

4. Peter's speech is a sustained argument from Israel's scriptures. The speech does not appeal to philosophical reasoning, mystical experience, or personal authority. It argues from Joel, from the Davidic covenant, from Psalm 16, and from Psalm 110. Each citation is used with a specific logical function: Joel interprets the event, Psalm 16 proves the resurrection was prophesied, the Davidic oath provides the framework for messianic expectation, and Psalm 110 establishes the exaltation. The argument proceeds step by step to its conclusion: God has made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ.

5. The speech holds together divine sovereignty and human responsibility without resolving the tension. Jesus was "delivered over by the predetermined plan and foreknowledge of God" (v. 23) — and "you nailed him to a cross and put him to death." God planned it; you did it. The crowd's guilt is real, as demonstrated by their being "pierced to the heart" (v. 37). But the guilt is not final — repentance, baptism, and forgiveness are offered. The speech both convicts and offers hope.

6. The response Peter commands fulfills the commission of Luke 24:47 with exact verbal precision. "Repentance for forgiveness of sins proclaimed in His name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem" becomes "Repent, and each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins." The same words, the same sequence, the same content — now enacted in Jerusalem, the designated starting point.

7. The promise of the Spirit is extended to all who respond, not limited to the initial recipients. Peter's statement in verse 39 — "the promise is for you and your children and for all who are far away, as many as the Lord our God will call to Himself" — makes the Pentecost gift normative, not exceptional. The Spirit is not a one-time event for the founding generation; it is a standing promise for all whom God calls. This establishes the expectation that every subsequent convert throughout Acts will also receive the Spirit.

8. The community described in verses 42-47 is the visible result of the Spirit's work. The four practices — apostolic teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer — define the community's identity. The sharing of possessions demonstrates that the Spirit produces not only speech but also material generosity. The community that has been "saved from this perverse generation" (v. 40) lives as an alternative society within Jerusalem, characterized by joy, unity, worship, and mutual care. The community is both continuous with Israel (they worship in the temple) and distinct (they break bread in homes, share possessions, and center their life on the apostles' testimony about Jesus).

9. The chapter establishes the pattern for conversion throughout Acts. The sequence — hearing the word, being convicted, repenting, being baptized, receiving the Spirit, being added to the community — will recur in various forms throughout the narrative: the Samaritans (8:12-17), the Ethiopian (8:36-38), Cornelius and his household (10:44-48), Lydia (16:14-15), the Philippian jailer (16:30-34), and others. Acts 2 sets the template.

10. The chapter answers Jesus's response to the disciples' question in 1:6. The disciples asked, "Is it at this time You are restoring the kingdom to Israel?" Jesus answered: you will receive power, and you will be witnesses. Acts 2 shows the beginning of this answer taking shape. The kingdom is not being restored through political sovereignty but through the Spirit's outpouring, the proclamation of Jesus as Lord and Christ, and the formation of a community that embodies the Torah's vision of a people among whom there is no poverty. The "restoration" is happening — but it looks different from what the disciples expected.
