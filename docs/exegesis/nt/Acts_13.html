Acts 13 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 12): The narrative of Acts 12 presented a contrast between two kings — one who attacks the people of God, and one who receives the worship due to God. Herod Agrippa I "laid violent hands on some who belonged to the church" (12:1), killed James the brother of John with the sword (12:2), and arrested Peter during Passover, intending to bring him out for public trial afterward (12:3-4). The church prayed earnestly for Peter (12:5), and God answered by sending an angel to release him from prison — chains falling off his hands, gates opening of their own accord (12:6-10). Peter, once free, went to the house of Mary, the mother of John Mark, where believers were gathered in prayer (12:12). He then "departed and went to another place" (12:17). The author immediately turned to Herod, who failed to find Peter, executed the sentries, and then traveled to Caesarea (12:19). There, in an audience with the people of Tyre and Sidon, Herod delivered an oration and accepted the crowd's acclamation: "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" (12:22). The response was swift and final: "Immediately an angel of the Lord struck him down, because he did not give God the glory, and he was eaten by worms and breathed his last" (12:23). The contrast is precise: an angel rescues the apostle who serves God; an angel strikes down the king who accepts God's glory for himself. Human power that opposes God's purposes destroys itself; the community that prays under persecution is delivered. The chapter concluded with two statements: "But the word of God increased and multiplied" (12:24) — the word grows despite the violence intended to suppress it — and "Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem when they had completed their service, bringing with them John, whose other name was Mark" (12:25). Barnabas and Saul are back in Antioch. They have brought John Mark. Everything is in position.

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 14): Paul and Barnabas continue the pattern established in Acts 13. At Iconium, they enter "the Jewish synagogue and spoke in such a way that a great number of both Jews and Greeks believed" (14:1), but "unbelieving Jews stirred up the Gentiles and poisoned their minds against the brothers" (14:2). The pattern from Pisidian Antioch — initial receptivity in the synagogue, followed by Jewish opposition that stirs up Gentile authorities — repeats. They flee to Lystra and Derbe (14:6). At Lystra, Paul heals a man crippled from birth (14:8-10), and the pagan crowd, rather than recognizing the God of Israel, attempts to worship Barnabas and Paul as Zeus and Hermes (14:11-13). Paul and Barnabas tear their garments and redirect the crowd to "a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them" (14:15). Then "Jews came from Antioch and Iconium" — the very cities where Paul had preached — and "having persuaded the crowds, they stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing that he was dead" (14:19). The persecution follows the messengers from city to city. Paul survives, and he and Barnabas retrace their steps, "strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith, and saying that through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God" (14:22). They appoint elders in every community and return to Antioch in Syria, where "they declared all that God had done with them, and how he had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles" (14:27). The first missionary journey ends where it began, with a report that the central accomplishment was Gentile inclusion.

How chapter 13 connects these: Acts 12 ended with the word of God increasing despite royal persecution, and with Barnabas and Saul back in Antioch. Acts 14 will show the pattern of synagogue-first preaching, Jewish rejection, Gentile reception, and persecution spreading across Asia Minor. Acts 13 is the origin point — the chapter where the Spirit commissions the deliberate mission beyond Antioch, where Saul becomes Paul, where the pattern of synagogue preaching followed by Jewish rejection and the turn to the Gentiles is first enacted and theologically interpreted. Everything that happens in chapters 14 through 28 flows from the events and declarations of this chapter. The Spirit's commissioning in 13:2 sets the mission in motion. The confrontation with Bar-Jesus in 13:6-12 enacts the conflict between the word of God and Jewish resistance in miniature. Paul's speech at Pisidian Antioch (13:16-41) provides the theological framework — Israel's history culminates in Jesus, forgiveness through him exceeds what the Law of Moses could accomplish, and rejection of the message fulfills prophetic warning. The declaration in 13:46 — "we are turning to the Gentiles" — is the programmatic announcement that governs the rest of Acts. And the citation of Isaiah 49:6 in 13:47 provides the scriptural warrant: the turn to the Gentiles is not a deviation from Israel's calling but its fulfillment.

Verses 1-3: The Spirit Commissions the First Deliberate Mission

"Now there were in the church at Antioch prophets and teachers, Barnabas, Simeon who was called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen a lifelong friend of Herod the tetrarch, and Saul" (13:1).

The author opens with a list that deserves close attention, because the composition of this leadership group reveals the character of the community from which the Gentile mission will be launched. Five men are named.

Barnabas: introduced in Acts 4:36-37 as "a Levite, a native of Cyprus," who sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet. He was the one who vouched for the converted Saul in Jerusalem when the other disciples were afraid of him (9:27). He was sent by the Jerusalem church to Antioch when they heard that Gentiles were believing there (11:22). He "saw the grace of God" in Antioch and "was glad" (11:23). He is "a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith" (11:24). Barnabas is listed first — the senior figure.

Simeon who was called Niger: the cognomen "Niger" is Latin for "black." The name suggests either African origin or notably dark complexion. His Hebrew name is Simeon; his Latin descriptor indicates he moves in a Roman-influenced world. Some have speculated he may be Simon of Cyrene who carried Jesus's cross (Luke 23:26), though the text does not make this identification.

Lucius of Cyrene: Cyrene was a city in North Africa (in modern Libya) with a substantial Jewish community. The Cyrenians are mentioned in Acts 6:9 as having a synagogue in Jerusalem. More significantly, Acts 11:20 records that "men of Cyprus and Cyrene" were the ones who first preached to Gentiles at Antioch, "preaching the Lord Jesus." Lucius may be one of those pioneering preachers — a man from North Africa who helped create the very community in which the Gentile mission is now being conceived.

Manaen, a syntrophos of Herod the tetrarch: the Greek word syntrophos means literally "nourished together with" — a foster-brother, a childhood companion raised in the same household. Manaen grew up alongside Herod Antipas, the tetrarch who beheaded John the Baptist (Luke 9:9) and mocked Jesus at his trial (Luke 23:8-11). Someone from the court of the ruler who killed Jesus's forerunner is now among the leaders of the church at Antioch. The juxtaposition is striking: Herod's court produced both a killer of prophets and a teacher of the gospel.

Saul: listed last. The man who breathed threats and murder against the disciples (9:1), who was commissioned to carry Jesus's name to "Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel" (9:15), is now the final name in a roster of prophets and teachers. He has been in Antioch for at least a year, teaching alongside Barnabas (11:26). He enters this chapter at the bottom of the list. He will not remain there.

The composition of the group is itself a statement. A Levite from Cyprus. A man with a Latin name meaning "black." A North African. A member of the Herodian court. A Pharisee from Cilicia. The church at Antioch — the church from which the mission to the Gentiles will be launched — is already a boundary-crossing community. Its leadership is ethnically, geographically, and socially diverse. The mission does not emerge from a homogeneous group deciding to reach out to the unlike. It emerges from a community that already embodies the crossing of boundaries.

"While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, 'Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them'" (13:2).

The timing is specific: the commissioning occurs during worship (leitourgounton — from leitourgia, "public service," the word from which "liturgy" derives; in the LXX it describes priestly service in the temple, e.g., Numbers 8:22, 2 Chronicles 31:2) and fasting. The community is engaged in an act of corporate devotion when the Spirit speaks. The initiative is not human. No one volunteers; no one proposes a mission plan. The Spirit speaks — "Set apart for me" (aphorisate de moi). The verb aphorizo ("to set apart, to separate, to mark off by boundaries") is the word used in Leviticus 20:26 (LXX) where God says, "I have separated you from the peoples." The same vocabulary that described Israel's separation from the nations now describes the separation of two men for a mission to the nations. Paul himself will use this word of his own calling in Galatians 1:15: "he who had set me apart before I was born."

The Spirit says "for me" (moi) — the work belongs to the Spirit. And the work is described as something to which Barnabas and Saul have already been "called" (proskekamai — perfect tense, indicating a completed action with ongoing effect). The calling preceded the commissioning. God had already called them; now the church is instructed to release them for what God has already determined.

"Then after fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off" (13:3).

The community responds with additional fasting and prayer, then lays hands on Barnabas and Saul. The laying on of hands (epitithentes tas cheiras) in Acts signifies commissioning for a task and the transfer of community authorization (cf. Acts 6:6, where the seven were commissioned for food distribution). The church does not create the calling; it recognizes and ratifies what the Spirit has declared. The verb apelysan ("sent them off, released them") is straightforward: they let them go, they released them for the work.

Verses 4-5: The Mission Begins in the Synagogues

"So, being sent out by the Holy Spirit, they went down to Seleucia, and from there they sailed to Cyprus" (13:4).

The author immediately clarifies the source of the mission: "being sent out by the Holy Spirit" (ekpemphthentes hypo tou hagiou pneumatos). The church laid hands on them and sent them off; the author attributes the sending to the Spirit. The human act and the divine act are simultaneous but the author names the divine agent as primary. Seleucia was the port city of Antioch, about sixteen miles to the west. Cyprus was Barnabas's homeland (4:36) — a natural first destination, a place where Barnabas had connections and knowledge of the terrain.

"When they arrived at Salamis, they proclaimed the word of God in the synagogues of the Jews. And they had John to assist them" (13:5).

The pattern that will govern the entire missionary journey — and indeed the rest of Acts — is established in this single verse. They begin in the synagogues. The synagogue is their first point of contact in every city, not as a strategic calculation but as a theological conviction that will be articulated later in the chapter: "it was necessary that the word of God be spoken first to you" (13:46). The message goes to Jews first. The synagogue provides both the audience that shares the scriptural framework necessary to understand the message and the God-fearing Gentiles who are already attached to the synagogue community.

"John" is John Mark, mentioned in 12:12 and 12:25 — the one whose mother's house served as a gathering place for the Jerusalem church, and who accompanied Barnabas and Saul from Jerusalem. His role as "assistant" (hypereten — literally "an under-rower," then more generally "a servant, an attendant, a helper") will be short-lived; he will depart in verse 13, and this departure will cause a sharp dispute between Paul and Barnabas in Acts 15:37-39.

Verses 6-12: A Jewish False Prophet, a Gentile Proconsul, and the Name "Paul"

"When they had gone through the whole island as far as Paphos, they came upon a certain magician, a Jewish false prophet named Bar-Jesus. He was with the proconsul, Sergius Paulus, a man of intelligence, who summoned Barnabas and Saul and sought to hear the word of God" (13:6-7).

They traverse the entire island from east (Salamis) to west (Paphos), the seat of the Roman provincial government. Two figures appear who embody the conflict that will define the rest of Acts.

The first is Bar-Jesus — a Jewish man (Ioudaion) who is both a magician (magos) and a false prophet (pseudopropheten). His name means "son of Jesus" or "son of Joshua" in Aramaic, a detail the author records without comment but which a first-century hearer would note. The combination of "Jewish" and "false prophet" and "magician" is deliberately loaded. Israel's scriptures repeatedly warned against false prophets (Deuteronomy 13:1-5, 18:20-22; Jeremiah 14:14, 23:16; Ezekiel 13:1-16) and against sorcery (Deuteronomy 18:10-12). Bar-Jesus is everything Israel's tradition rejected — a practitioner of the very arts the Law prohibited, claiming prophetic authority while opposing the true word of God. He is a Jew who has become the opposite of what a Jew should be.

The second figure is Sergius Paulus — a Roman proconsul, a Gentile holding the highest provincial office. The author describes him as "a man of intelligence" (andri syneto — from syniemi, "to understand, to comprehend"). This characterization is deliberate: the Gentile ruler is discerning; the Jewish court prophet is false. Sergius Paulus "sought to hear the word of God" (epezitesen akousai ton logon tou theou). The verb epiziteo means "to seek after, to desire earnestly." A Roman governor is actively pursuing the word of God. The echo of Cornelius — another Roman official drawn to Israel's God — is unmistakable.

"But Elymas the magician (for that is the meaning of his name) opposed them, seeking to turn the proconsul away from the faith" (13:8).

The author provides Bar-Jesus's working name, Elymas, and notes that it means "magician" (the etymology is debated; it may derive from an Arabic root meaning "wise" or "sage"). The verb anthistato ("opposed, stood against") describes deliberate resistance. Elymas does not merely disagree; he actively works to "turn away" (diastrepsai — "to distort, to pervert, to turn aside") the proconsul "from the faith" (apo tes pisteos). The language of turning and perversion will recur immediately in Paul's rebuke.

This episode parallels the Simon Magus episode of Acts 8:9-24. Both involve a magician who encounters the gospel. Both involve a figure whose spiritual claims are exposed as fraudulent. Simon Magus was in Samaria — the territory between Jewish and Gentile worlds. Bar-Jesus is in a fully Gentile administrative setting. The escalation is geographic and cultural: the confrontation between the word of God and false spiritual power moves from the margins to the center of Roman authority.

"But Saul, who was also called Paul, filled with the Holy Spirit, looked intently at him and said, 'You son of the devil, you enemy of all righteousness, full of all deceit and villainy, will you not stop making crooked the straight paths of the Lord?'" (13:9-10).

This is the moment the author introduces the name change. "Saul, who was also called Paul" (Saulos de, ho kai Paulos). The author has called him "Saul" throughout Acts. From this verse forward, he will be "Paul" exclusively (except in the retelling of his conversion in chapters 22 and 26, where the voice from heaven uses the Hebrew form). The shift is not a conversion name-change — Saul/Paul had been a believer since chapter 9. Many diaspora Jews bore both a Hebrew name and a Greco-Roman name. Saul's Roman cognomen Paulus was likely his from birth or childhood. The author's decision to switch names at this precise moment is a literary marker: the mission has turned toward the Gentile world, and the name that will govern that mission is the Roman one. The timing is also suggestive: Paul emerges as "Paul" in the presence of Sergius Paulus — a coincidence of name that the text neither explains nor conceals.

More significant than the name change is the shift in narrative ordering. Verse 1 listed "Barnabas... and Saul." Verse 7 had "Barnabas and Saul." But from verse 13 forward, the pairing will consistently be "Paul and Barnabas" (13:43, 46, 50; with occasional reversals when the context involves Barnabas's prior relationships, as at 14:14). The junior partner has become the leading figure. The author effects this transition without explanation — it simply happens, and the reader is expected to observe it.

Paul is "filled with the Holy Spirit" (plestheirs pneumatos hagiou) — the same phrase used of Peter before his confrontation with the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:8) and of Stephen before his speech (Acts 6:5, 7:55). The filling of the Spirit precedes prophetic confrontation. Paul "looked intently" (atenisas — the same verb used of Stephen gazing into heaven in 7:55 and of Peter looking intently at the lame man in 3:4). The gaze is deliberate and fixed.

The denunciation is severe and precisely constructed. "Son of the devil" (huie diabolou) directly inverts his name: he is Bar-Jesus ("son of Jesus/salvation") by name, but son of the devil in reality. "Enemy of all righteousness" (echthre pases dikaiosynes) — the one who claims prophetic authority is in fact at war with everything righteous. "Full of all deceit and villainy" (pleres pantos dolou kai pases rhadiourgias) — he is full, but full of the wrong things. The word rhadiourgias means "reckless ease, unscrupulousness, villainy" — a term used in classical Greek for criminal behavior conducted with brazen casualness.

"Will you not stop making crooked the straight paths of the Lord?" (ou pause diastrephon tas hodous tou kyriou tas eutheias). The verb diastrephon ("twisting, perverting, making crooked") is the same root (diastrepsai) used in verse 8 for what Elymas sought to do to the proconsul. The "straight paths of the Lord" (tas hodous tou kyriou tas eutheias) echoes Isaiah 40:3 (LXX): "Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths" — the same text that described John the Baptist's mission (Luke 3:4). John the Baptist prepared the straight way; Bar-Jesus makes it crooked. John pointed people toward the Coming One; Bar-Jesus turns people away from the faith. The false prophet is the anti-Baptist.

"And now, behold, the hand of the Lord is upon you, and you will be blind and unable to see the sun for a time" (13:11a).

"The hand of the Lord" (cheir kyriou) is the standard LXX phrase for God's direct action in judgment or deliverance (cf. Exodus 9:3, 1 Samuel 5:6, 7:13; Isaiah 59:1). The punishment is blindness — and the irony is layered. Paul himself was struck blind on the Damascus road (9:8-9) when he was opposing God's purposes. Paul, the former persecutor who was blinded by the light of Christ and then had his sight restored, now pronounces blindness on a man who is persecuting the word of God. The blindness is "for a time" (achri kairou) — it is disciplinary, not final. The same mercy that was extended to Paul — temporary blindness as the prelude to possible restoration — is extended to Bar-Jesus. The door to repentance is not closed.

"Immediately mist and darkness fell upon him, and he went about seeking people to lead him by the hand" (13:11b).

The immediate fulfillment confirms the prophetic authority of the word. The language echoes Paul's own experience: after the Damascus road encounter, Paul "could see nothing" and had to be "led by the hand" (9:8, cheiragogounes auton — the same root as cheiragogous here). The former blind persecutor pronounces blindness on the current opponent. The reversal is complete.

"Then the proconsul believed, when he saw what had occurred, for he was astonished at the teaching of the Lord" (13:12).

Sergius Paulus believes. The verb episteeusen ("believed") is the same verb used throughout Acts for saving faith (2:44, 4:4, 8:12, 10:43, 11:17). The author attributes his belief to two things: "what had occurred" (to gegonas — the blinding of Elymas) and "the teaching of the Lord" (te didache tou kyriou). The sign confirmed the teaching; the teaching gave meaning to the sign. Neither alone would have sufficed. The proconsul is "astonished" (ekplessomenos — "struck out of his senses, overwhelmed") not at the miracle as a spectacle but at "the teaching" — the content of the message about Jesus.

The episode as a whole enacts in miniature the pattern that the rest of the chapter will narrate at length. A Jewish figure resists the word of God. A Gentile figure receives it. The one who stands in the way is judged. The one who seeks to hear is granted faith. This is the pattern of the Pisidian Antioch synagogue scene compressed into a single dramatic encounter.

Verses 13-15: Pisidian Antioch — An Invitation to Speak

"Now Paul and his companions set sail from Paphos and came to Perga in Pamphylia. And John left them and returned to Jerusalem" (13:13).

The narrative makes three moves in a single sentence. First, the ordering shifts: "Paul and his companions" (hoi peri Paulon — literally "those around Paul"), not "Barnabas and his companions." Paul is now the center of the group. Second, they cross from Cyprus to the mainland of Asia Minor, arriving at Perga in the coastal region of Pamphylia. Third, John Mark departs. The verb apochoresas ("having departed, having withdrawn") indicates a deliberate separation. The author records the fact without explaining the reason. The only commentary will come later, in Acts 15:38, where Paul considers Mark's departure as desertion — he "had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia and had not gone with them to the work." Whatever the reason — homesickness, fear of the journey into the interior, disagreement with the direction of the mission — Mark's departure is recorded as a significant event. The mission continues without him.

"But they went on from Perga and came to Antioch in Pisidia. And on the Sabbath day they went into the synagogue and sat down" (13:14).

Pisidian Antioch (not the Antioch in Syria from which they were sent, but a different city in the interior highlands of Asia Minor) was a Roman colony, a significant administrative and military center in the region of Pisidia. The journey from Perga to Pisidian Antioch involved crossing the Taurus Mountains — a difficult and dangerous route through territory known for bandits (cf. Paul's later reference to "danger from robbers" in 2 Corinthians 11:26). They arrive on the Sabbath and enter the synagogue and "sat down" (ekathisan) — taking seats among the congregation as visiting Jews.

"After the reading from the Law and the Prophets, the rulers of the synagogue sent a message to them, saying, 'Brothers, if you have any word of encouragement for the people, say it'" (13:15).

The synagogue service followed a known order: readings from the Torah (the Law) and from the Prophets (the Haftarah), followed by an exposition or word of exhortation. Visiting teachers were customarily invited to speak — the same practice Jesus took advantage of in Nazareth (Luke 4:16-21). The "rulers of the synagogue" (hoi archisynagogoi — the plural suggests more than one presiding official, as was common in larger synagogues) extend the invitation: "Brothers" (andres adelphoi — "men, brothers," a formal synagogue address), "if you have any word of encouragement" (ei tis estin en hymin logos parakleseos — literally "if there is in you any word of exhortation/encouragement"). The noun paraklesis ("encouragement, exhortation, consolation") is a broad term that encompasses teaching, comfort, and moral appeal. The invitation is open and respectful. They do not yet know what Paul will say.

Verses 16-22: Israel's History as God's Initiative

"So Paul stood up, and motioning with his hand said: 'Men of Israel and you who fear God, listen'" (13:16).

Paul's posture — standing and motioning with his hand — signals a formal address. The gesture (kataseisas te cheiri) is the same one Peter used in 12:17 and Paul will use again in 21:40. It calls for silence and attention.

Paul's audience is explicitly dual: "Men of Israel" (andres Israelitai) and "you who fear God" (hoi phoboumenoi ton theon). The God-fearers — Gentiles who worship Israel's God and attend the synagogue but have not undergone circumcision — are directly addressed as part of the audience. They are not eavesdroppers; they are intended hearers. Paul will address this dual audience again in verse 26, and the distinction between the two groups will become the fault line along which the chapter breaks open in verses 44-47. The speech is crafted for both, but its conclusion will have different implications for each.

"The God of this people Israel chose our fathers and made the people great during their stay in the land of Egypt, and with uplifted arm he led them out of it" (13:17).

Paul begins where Stephen began (Acts 7) and where Peter began in Cornelius's house (Acts 10:36-37): with God as the subject and actor. Every verb in the historical review has God as its subject. God chose (exelexato). God made the people great (hypsosen ton laon). God led them out (en brachioni hypselo exegagen autous). The "uplifted arm" (brachioni hypselo) is drawn directly from the LXX's language for the Exodus: Deuteronomy 5:15, "The LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (en cheiri krataia kai en brachioni hypselo). This is not Paul's original metaphor; it is Israel's liturgical vocabulary for God's saving power, repeated in the Passover liturgy and throughout the Psalms (Psalm 136:12).

"And for about forty years he put up with them in the wilderness" (13:18).

The verb etrophophoresen is textually disputed — some manuscripts read etropophoresn ("he bore with their ways, he put up with them," from tropophoreoo) while others read etrephophoresen ("he nourished them, he carried them as a nurse carries a child," from trophophoreoo). The difference is a single letter in Greek and a significant difference in tone. If "put up with," the emphasis is on Israel's rebellion in the wilderness and God's patient endurance of it. If "nourished," the emphasis is on God's provision despite their complaining. Either reading presents God's action as primary and Israel's wilderness period as sustained by divine patience or divine care.

"And after destroying seven nations in the land of Canaan, he gave them their land as an inheritance" (13:19).

The "seven nations" is a standard formula from the LXX: Deuteronomy 7:1 lists "the Hittites, the Girgashites, the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites — seven nations greater and mightier than you." God destroyed (katheloon — "brought down, pulled down, demolished") these nations and God gave (katekkleronomesen — "distributed as an inheritance") the land. Again, all initiative belongs to God. Israel receives; God acts.

"All this took about 450 years. And after that he gave them judges until Samuel the prophet" (13:20).

The 450-year period is calculated from the patriarchal promise through the conquest. God "gave" (edoken) judges — the verb continues the pattern of divine initiative. The judges were God's provision for a people without a king.

"Then they asked for a king, and God gave them Saul the son of Kish, a man of the tribe of Benjamin, for forty years" (13:21).

The request for a king is presented as Israel's initiative — "they asked" (etesanto) — a detail that echoes 1 Samuel 8:5-6, where the elders' request for a king "displeased Samuel." But even in Israel's questionable request, God acted: "God gave them" (edoken ho theos). The human demand was accommodated by divine concession. The mention of Saul the king by name, in a speech delivered by a man who shares that name and who is also from the tribe of Benjamin, creates an implicit parallel. The first Saul was given to Israel because they asked; this Saul (Paul) was given to the Gentile mission because God called.

"And when he had removed him, he raised up David to be their king, of whom he testified and said, 'I have found in David the son of Jesse a man after my heart, who will do all my will'" (13:22).

God "removed" (metastesas) King Saul and "raised up" (egeiren) David. The verb egeiren ("raised") is the same verb that will be used for Jesus's resurrection in verse 30 (egeiren). The verbal echo is deliberate: God raised David to the throne; God raised Jesus from the dead. The pattern of God raising up what he has chosen runs from David to Jesus.

The quotation attributed to God — "I have found in David the son of Jesse a man after my heart, who will do all my will" — is a composite of two texts. The first half echoes 1 Samuel 13:14 (LXX): "The Lord has sought out for himself a man after his own heart" (ezetesen kyrios heauto anthropon kata ten kardian autou). The second half echoes Psalm 89:20 (LXX 88:21): "I have found David my servant" (heuron Dauid ton doulon mou). Paul weaves the two together into a single divine testimony. The result is a portrait of David defined by two characteristics: alignment with God's heart and obedience to God's will. This portrait of David is not the point of the speech; it is the platform from which the speech will vault to its real subject — David's descendant.

Verses 23-25: From David's Offspring to Jesus

"Of this man's offspring God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised" (13:23).

The transition from David to Jesus is made through a single phrase: "of this man's offspring" (toutou... apo tou spermatos — "from the seed of this one"). The promise to David — that his throne would be established forever (2 Samuel 7:12-16), that God would raise up his offspring — is declared fulfilled. God "has brought" (egagen — from ago, "to lead, to bring") a Savior (sotera). The word soter ("savior, deliverer") is used in the LXX for the judges and deliverers God raised up for Israel (Judges 3:9, 3:15; Nehemiah 9:27). Paul is not introducing an alien concept; he is using Israel's own vocabulary for divine rescue. God brought deliverers to Israel before — Othniel, Ehud, Gideon. Now God has brought the ultimate deliverer, Jesus, "as he promised" (kat' epangelian). The fulfillment language is emphatic: this was promised; this has now been delivered.

"Before his coming, John had proclaimed a baptism of repentance to all the people of Israel. And as John was finishing his course, he said, 'What do you suppose that I am? I am not he. No, but behold, after me one is coming, the sandals of whose feet I am not worthy to untie'" (13:24-25).

Paul introduces John the Baptist as the forerunner whose role was to prepare and to defer. John's baptism was "of repentance" (baptisma metanoias) and directed "to all the people of Israel" (panti to lao Israel). John's message was national in scope — directed to the whole people, not a sect. But as John was "finishing his course" (epleroun ton dromon — literally "fulfilling the race" or "completing the course"), he pointed beyond himself. John's self-denial — "I am not he" (ouk eimi ego) — echoes the language of John 1:20 and the synoptic parallels. The image of the sandals (ton hypodimaton ton podon) is the act of the lowest household slave; John declares himself unworthy even of that servile role in relation to the one coming after him.

Paul's inclusion of John the Baptist serves a specific rhetorical purpose. The synagogue audience would have known of John — his ministry was widely remembered (cf. Acts 18:25, 19:3, where followers of John are encountered in Ephesus). By positioning John as the herald who deferred to Jesus, Paul establishes that Jesus's ministry was not a private affair but a publicly anticipated, prophetically announced event.

Verses 26-29: Jerusalem's Rejection as Prophetic Fulfillment

"Brothers, sons of the family of Abraham, and those among you who fear God, to us has been sent the message of this salvation" (13:26).

Paul pauses the historical narrative to address his audience directly — and again, the address is dual. "Sons of the family of Abraham" (huioi genous Abraam) speaks to the Jewish members of the congregation. "Those among you who fear God" (hoi en hymin phoboumenoi ton theon) speaks to the God-fearers. Both are included in the pronoun "us" (hemin): "to us has been sent the message of this salvation." The message is not for one group to the exclusion of the other. Both the children of Abraham and the God-fearing Gentiles are recipients. The word "salvation" (soterias) connects back to "Savior" (sotera) in verse 23 — the savior has been brought; the message of the salvation he brings has been sent.

"For those who live in Jerusalem and their rulers, because they did not recognize him nor understand the utterances of the prophets, which are read every Sabbath, fulfilled them by condemning him" (13:27).

This is one of the most carefully constructed sentences in the speech. Paul makes three claims simultaneously. First, the inhabitants of Jerusalem and their rulers "did not recognize him" (touton agnoesantes — from agnoeo, "to be ignorant of, to fail to recognize"). The verb does not necessarily imply willful blindness; it can mean genuine failure to perceive. Second, they did not "understand the utterances of the prophets which are read every Sabbath" (tas phonas ton propheton tas kata pan sabbaton anaginoskamenas). The prophetic scriptures were read aloud in the synagogue every Sabbath — the same practice Paul's audience is engaged in right now. The Jerusalem leaders heard those readings and missed their meaning. Third — and this is the rhetorical turn — by condemning Jesus they "fulfilled" (epleroosan) those very prophetic utterances. The rejection was itself the fulfillment. The irony is precise: the act of rejecting the one the prophets described was the act that fulfilled what the prophets predicted. They accomplished God's purposes by opposing them.

This argument parallels Stephen's pattern in Acts 7: Israel's leaders consistently rejected the ones God sent (Joseph, Moses, the prophets), and this pattern of rejection was itself part of the prophetic record. Paul's innovation is to note that the prophetic readings were available every Sabbath — the information was publicly accessible, regularly recited, and still unrecognized. The failure was not one of access but of perception.

"And though they found in him no guilt worthy of death, they asked Pilate to have him executed" (13:28).

The judicial irregularity is stated bluntly: no guilt (medemian aitian thanatou heurontes — "finding no cause of death"), yet they asked (etesanto) Pilate for execution. The verb etesanto ("they asked, they requested") is the same verb used in verse 21 for Israel's request for a king. Israel asked for a king when they should not have; Jerusalem's rulers asked for an execution when there was no basis for one. The verb connects two misguided requests across Israel's history.

"And when they had carried out all that was written of him, they took him down from the tree and laid him in a tomb" (13:29).

"All that was written of him" (panta ta peri autou gegrammena) — the events of Jesus's death are presented as the completion of a script. Everything written about him was "carried out" (etelasan — from teleo, "to complete, to bring to an end, to accomplish"). The word "tree" (xylou) repeats the language of Acts 5:30 and 10:39 — "hanging him on a tree" — the deliberate echo of Deuteronomy 21:22-23, where a body hung on a tree is "cursed by God." Paul does not soften the scandal. The messiah was subjected to the form of death associated with divine curse. They "laid him in a tomb" (ethekan eis mnemeion) — the narrative reaches its lowest point. The promised descendant of David, the savior God brought to Israel, was killed, hung on a tree, and buried.

Verses 30-37: The Resurrection Proven from Scripture

"But God raised him from the dead" (13:30).

The conjunction "but" (ho de theos) carries the entire weight of the speech. Human agents condemned and killed. "But God" (ho de theos) reversed the verdict. The verb egeiren ("raised") is the same verb used in verse 22 for God raising David to the throne. There, God raised David from obscurity to kingship. Here, God raises Jesus from death to life. The pattern is consistent: God raises those he has chosen, and human opposition cannot prevent it.

"And for many days he appeared to those who had come up with him from Galilee to Jerusalem, who are now his witnesses to the people" (13:31).

The resurrection is attested by witnesses — those who traveled with Jesus from Galilee to Jerusalem and who saw him after his death "for many days" (epi hemeras pleious). These witnesses "are now" (hoitines nyn eisin) his witnesses "to the people" (pros ton laon). The word "now" (nyn) indicates ongoing testimony. The witnesses are still alive, still speaking, still testifying. Paul does not count himself among these witnesses — he was not one who came up from Galilee. His role is different, as the next verse shows.

"And we bring you the good news that what God promised to the fathers, this he has fulfilled to us their children by raising Jesus" (13:32-33a).

Paul shifts from third person ("those who are his witnesses") to first person ("we bring you the good news"). The witnesses from Galilee testify to the people in Jerusalem; Paul and Barnabas bring the good news (euangelizometha — "we evangelize, we announce good news") to this diaspora audience. The content of the good news is stated with precision: "what God promised to the fathers" (ten pros tous pateras epangelian genomenen — "the promise that was made to the fathers") has been "fulfilled to us their children" (tois teknois auton hemin ekpepleroken — "to their children, to us, he has completely fulfilled"). The promises to Abraham, to David, to Israel through the prophets — all of them find their fulfillment in the raising of Jesus. The word ekpepleroken (from ekpleroo — "to fill up completely, to fulfill entirely") is an intensified form of the verb. God has not partially fulfilled; he has fulfilled completely.

"As also it is written in the second Psalm, 'You are my Son, today I have begotten you'" (13:33b).

Paul quotes Psalm 2:7. In its original setting, Psalm 2 is a royal enthronement psalm. The nations rage and the rulers plot against "the Lord and against his Anointed" (Psalm 2:2), but God declares: "I have set my King on Zion, my holy hill" (2:6). The king then reports God's decree: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you" (2:7). In Israel's royal theology, the "begetting" language referred to the king's installation — the moment of enthronement, not biological generation. On the day of coronation, the king entered into a new relationship with God as God's adopted son, God's viceroy over Israel.

Paul applies this psalm not to Jesus's birth, not to his baptism, but to his resurrection. The "begetting" — the moment at which God declares "You are my Son" in its fullest sense — is the raising from the dead. The resurrection is Jesus's enthronement. It is the moment when the crucified one is installed as the king whom the nations and rulers opposed. Psalm 2 predicted opposition from rulers and nations against God's Anointed; Acts has narrated exactly that opposition — Herod, Pilate, the Gentile soldiers, and the Jewish leaders (cf. Acts 4:25-28, where the early community explicitly applied Psalm 2 to Jesus's trial). But Psalm 2 also declared that God's response to that opposition was to enthrone his king regardless. The resurrection is that enthronement.

"And as for the fact that he raised him from the dead, no more to return to corruption, he has spoken in this way, 'I will give you the holy and sure blessings of David'" (13:34).

Paul's second scriptural proof addresses the permanence of the resurrection. Jesus was raised "no more to return to corruption" (meketi mellonta hypostrephein eis diaphthoran) — this is not a temporary resuscitation (like Lazarus, who was raised but would die again) but an irreversible transformation. The body that was raised will not decay.

The quotation is from Isaiah 55:3 (LXX): "I will give you the holy and sure blessings of David" (doso hymin ta hosia Dauid ta pista). The LXX renders the Hebrew hesed David — "the steadfast love/covenant faithfulness [shown to] David" — as ta hosia Dauid ta pista — "the holy/consecrated things of David, the faithful/trustworthy ones." The word hosia (from hosios, "holy, consecrated, sacred by divine sanction") is critical because it connects to the next quotation. The "holy things" promised to David — the covenant blessings that God swore would endure — require a living recipient. If David's line ends in the grave, the blessings die with it. A dead descendant cannot receive "sure blessings." The resurrection of Jesus from David's line means that the blessings are alive and operative.

"Therefore he says also in another psalm, 'You will not let your Holy One see corruption'" (13:35).

Paul's third quotation is Psalm 16:10 (LXX 15:10): "You will not let your Holy One see corruption" (ou doseis ton hosion sou idein diaphthoran). The word hosion ("Holy One") is from the same root as hosia ("holy things") in the Isaiah quotation. Paul has chained two texts together by a shared word: the "holy things of David" (ta hosia Dauid) in Isaiah 55:3 depend on the "Holy One" (ton hosion) in Psalm 16:10 not seeing corruption. The logic is: God promised David sacred, enduring blessings. Those blessings require the Holy One not to decay. Therefore the Holy One must be raised.

This is the same psalm Peter quoted at Pentecost (Acts 2:25-31). Peter's argument there was identical in structure: David wrote Psalm 16, but David died and was buried, and his tomb is still with us, so the psalm cannot be about David himself. It must be about David's descendant, whom God would raise before his body saw decay. Paul now makes the same argument explicitly.

"For David, after he had served the purpose of God in his own generation, fell asleep and was laid with his fathers and saw corruption, but he whom God raised up did not see corruption" (13:36-37).

The argument is simple and devastating. David "served the purpose of God in his own generation" (idia genea hypretesas te tou theou boule) — David's service was genuine, his faithfulness real, his role in God's plan significant. But it was bounded: "in his own generation" (idia genea). David's service was for his time. After fulfilling his role, David "fell asleep" (ekoimiethe — the standard euphemism for death, cf. 1 Kings 2:10: "David slept with his fathers") and "was laid with his fathers" (prosetethe pros tous pateras autou) and "saw corruption" (eiden diaphthoran). David's body decayed. His tomb was known and accessible in first-century Jerusalem (cf. Acts 2:29: "his tomb is with us to this day").

"But he whom God raised up did not see corruption" (hon de ho theos egeiren ouk eiden diaphthoran). The contrast is absolute. David decayed. Jesus did not. David served God in his generation. Jesus was raised to serve God's purposes beyond any single generation. The psalm that David wrote about not seeing corruption was not about David; it was about the one God would raise from among David's descendants.

Verses 38-41: Forgiveness Beyond the Law, and a Warning

"Let it be known to you therefore, brothers, that through this man forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you" (13:38).

Paul arrives at the application: "let it be known to you therefore" (gnoston oun esto hymin) — the language of formal proclamation. "Through this man" (dia toutou) — through the crucified, risen, enthroned Jesus — "forgiveness of sins" (aphesis hamartion) is "proclaimed" (katangelletai) to you. The word aphesis ("release, forgiveness, letting go") is the same word used in Luke 4:18, where Jesus read from Isaiah 61 in the Nazareth synagogue: "He has sent me to proclaim release to the captives" (keruxai aichmalotois aphesin). The release that Isaiah promised, that Jesus announced in Nazareth, is now being proclaimed in Pisidian Antioch — to a diaspora audience far from Jerusalem, far from the temple, far from the sacrificial system.

"And by him everyone who believes is freed from everything from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses" (13:39).

This is the most theologically charged statement in the speech. The verb is dikaioo — "to justify, to declare righteous, to acquit, to free." The claim is: "by him" (en touto) — in or through Jesus — "everyone who believes" (pas ho pisteuon) "is freed" (dikaioutai, present passive — "is being justified") "from everything" (apo panton) "from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses" (hon ouk edynethethe en nomo Mouseos dikaiothenai).

The phrase "from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses" requires careful reading. The Greek (apo panton hon ouk edynethethe en nomo Mouseos dikaiothenai) says literally: "from all things from which you were not able in the law of Moses to be justified." The Law of Moses included the sacrificial system, which provided atonement for certain categories of sin. The sin offerings of Leviticus 4-5 addressed unintentional sins. The Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) provided annual corporate cleansing. But the Law itself acknowledged categories of sin for which there was no sacrificial remedy. Numbers 15:30-31 declares that the person who sins "with a high hand" (defiantly, deliberately) "reviles the Lord, and that person shall be cut off from among his people." Deliberate, high-handed transgression had no sacrificial remedy in the Mosaic system. The person was "cut off."

Paul's claim is that through Jesus, justification is available "from everything" — including those things the Law could not resolve. The scope is universal in two senses: universal in the range of sins covered ("everything") and universal in the people eligible ("everyone who believes"). The Law of Moses did not fail in its purpose; it served its function. But its function had limits. What the Law could not accomplish — full justification from all sin for all who believe — is now accomplished through the risen Jesus.

Paul is speaking in a synagogue, to people who live under the Law. He is not attacking the Law; he is declaring that something has arrived that exceeds the Law's capacity. This is not a negation but a surpassing. The Law pointed to atonement but could not complete it for every case. Jesus completes what the Law began.

"Beware, therefore, lest what is said in the Prophets should come about: 'Look, you scoffers, be astounded and perish; for I am doing a work in your days, a work that you will not believe, even if one tells it to you'" (13:40-41).

Paul closes with a warning, quoting Habakkuk 1:5. The original context of this prophecy deserves close attention, because Paul's application depends on it. Habakkuk was writing in the late seventh century BCE, shortly before the Babylonian invasion. God announced to Habakkuk that he was about to do something shocking: "I am raising up the Chaldeans, that bitter and hasty nation, who march through the breadth of the earth, to seize dwellings not their own" (Habakkuk 1:6). God's "work" — the thing that would be unbelievable even if reported — was the use of a pagan nation as God's instrument of judgment against Israel. The scandal was not the judgment itself but the instrument: God was working through Gentiles to accomplish his purposes with Israel.

Paul's application of this text to his own situation is precise. God is again doing a work that involves the Gentiles in a way that Israel will find incredible. In Habakkuk's time, the unbelievable work was God using Babylonians to judge Israel. In Paul's time, the unbelievable work is God using the crucified and risen Jesus to offer justification to both Jews and Gentiles, bypassing the mechanisms of the Law. Both works involve God acting through channels that Israel did not expect and does not want to accept. The warning is: do not be among those who "will not believe even if one tells it to you." The telling is happening right now, in this synagogue, in this speech. The question is whether the hearers will believe it or not.

The LXX version of Habakkuk 1:5 that Paul quotes differs slightly from the Hebrew. The Hebrew has "Look among the nations" (bago'im); the LXX has "Look, you scoffers" (idete, hoi kataphronetai), apparently reading bogo'dim ("you treacherous ones/scoffers") instead of bago'im ("among the nations"). Paul follows the LXX text, which makes the warning more directly personal: you scoffers, you despisers. The LXX turns an observation about the nations into a direct address to those who refuse to believe.

Verses 42-43: The Initial Response

"As they went out, the people begged that these things might be told them the next Sabbath" (13:42).

The initial response is eager and positive. The verb parekaloun ("begged, urged, entreated") indicates genuine desire to hear more. "The people" (ta ethne — some manuscripts read this as "the Gentiles," others as the audience generally) want the message repeated. The request for the "next Sabbath" shows that the message has provoked interest but not yet full understanding. They want to hear it again, to process it, to consider it further.

"And after the meeting of the synagogue broke up, many Jews and devout converts to Judaism followed Paul and Barnabas, who, as they spoke with them, urged them to continue in the grace of God" (13:43).

Two groups respond: "many Jews" (polloi ton Ioudaion) and "devout converts to Judaism" (ton sebomenon proselyton — literally "the worshiping proselytes"). Proselytes were Gentiles who had fully converted to Judaism, undergoing circumcision, baptism, and sacrifice. They had crossed the boundary that God-fearers had not. Both groups "followed" (ekolouthisan) Paul and Barnabas — the verb implies adherence, not merely physical accompaniment.

Paul and Barnabas respond by "urging them to continue in the grace of God" (epeithon autous prosmenein te chariti tou theou). The verb prosmenein ("to remain in, to continue in, to abide in") implies that they have already entered the sphere of grace and must now persist in it. The phrase "the grace of God" (te chariti tou theou) is the summary term for what Paul has been proclaiming: God's unmerited action in raising Jesus, offering forgiveness, and justifying those who believe. The exhortation to "continue" assumes that the response of faith is not a one-time event but an ongoing posture. Grace has been extended; they must remain in it.

Verses 44-47: Jewish Jealousy and the Turn to the Gentiles

"The next Sabbath almost the whole city gathered to hear the word of the Lord" (13:44).

A week has passed. The message has spread beyond the synagogue. "Almost the whole city" (schedon pasa he polis) gathers. The audience is no longer a synagogue congregation of Jews and God-fearers; it is the general population of a Roman colonial city — predominantly Gentile. The "word of the Lord" (ton logon tou kyriou) has drawn a crowd that overwhelms the original setting.

"But when the Jews saw the crowds, they were filled with jealousy and began to contradict what was spoken by Paul, reviling him" (13:45).

The reaction of "the Jews" (hoi Ioudaioi) to the crowds is not theological disagreement but "jealousy" (zelou — from zelos, "zeal, jealousy, envy"). The same word described the Sadducees' reaction to the apostles' popularity in Acts 5:17: "they were filled with jealousy." The jealousy is provoked not by what Paul said — they had heard the same message a week earlier without this reaction — but by the crowds. The Gentile multitude flooding the synagogue threatens the boundaries of the community. If the word of God belongs to everyone, the synagogue's distinctive status is diminished. They "began to contradict" (antelegon — "to speak against, to oppose in speech") and to "revile" (blasphemountes — "to blaspheme, to slander, to speak abusively"). The word blasphemeo is strong: it means to speak injuriously, to defame. They are not offering reasoned counterarguments; they are attacking Paul with abusive speech.

"And Paul and Barnabas spoke out boldly, saying, 'It was necessary that the word of God be spoken first to you. Since you thrust it aside and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we are turning to the Gentiles'" (13:46).

Paul's response is the programmatic declaration of Acts — the statement that provides the theological framework for everything that follows in the book. Three elements require attention.

First: "It was necessary that the word of God be spoken first to you" (hymin en anagkaion proton lalethenai ton logon tou theou). The word anagkaion ("necessary") indicates not strategic preference but divine obligation. The word had to go to Jews first. This priority is rooted in the scriptural narrative Paul has just recited — God chose the fathers, made the people great, gave them the land, raised up David, brought a savior from David's line. The word of God emerges from Israel's story and must be offered to Israel's people before it goes elsewhere. Paul and Barnabas have been faithful to this order: in every city, they went to the synagogue first. The "first to you" is not an arbitrary preference but a covenantal obligation.

Second: "Since you thrust it aside and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life" (epeide apotheisthe auton kai ouk axious krinete heautous tes aioniou zoes). The verbs are active and reflexive. "You thrust it aside" (apotheisthe — "to push away, to reject, to repudiate") — the rejection is their own act. "You judge yourselves unworthy" (ouk axious krinete heautous) — the word "yourselves" (heautous) is emphatic. No one has judged them unworthy. God has not excluded them. They have excluded themselves. The judgment is self-imposed. By rejecting the message, they have rendered their own verdict: we are not worthy of eternal life. Paul does not say God has rejected them; he says they have rejected the message and thereby pronounced their own sentence.

Third: "Behold, we are turning to the Gentiles" (idou strephometha eis ta ethne). The present tense (strephometha — "we are turning") indicates an action underway, not a permanent, irrevocable decree. This is not "God has abandoned the Jews forever." It is "we, right now, in this city, are turning to the Gentiles because you have rejected the word." The pattern will repeat in subsequent cities: Paul will enter the synagogue first, preach, face rejection, and then turn to the Gentile population (cf. Acts 18:6 in Corinth: "Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent. From now on I will go to the Gentiles"; Acts 28:28 in Rome: "Therefore let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen"). The turn to the Gentiles is real and consequential, but it is enacted city by city, not declared as an absolute and permanent national rejection.

"For so the Lord has commanded us, saying, 'I have made you a light for the Gentiles, that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth'" (13:47).

Paul grounds the turn to the Gentiles in scripture — specifically, in Isaiah 49:6. The original context is the second of Isaiah's Servant Songs. The Servant of YHWH, addressed by God, receives a commission that exceeds the restoration of Israel: "It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob and to bring back the preserved of Israel; I will make you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth" (Isaiah 49:6). The Servant's mission has two stages: first, the restoration of Israel; then, light for the nations. Restoring Israel alone is "too light a thing" — too small for the scope of God's purposes. The Servant must also bring salvation to the ends of the earth.

Paul applies this text to himself and Barnabas: "the Lord has commanded us" (entetaltai hemin ho kyrios). The pronoun "us" (hemin) claims participation in the Servant's mission. Paul and Barnabas are not the Servant — Jesus is the Servant of Isaiah — but they carry out the Servant's commission. The one who is the light for the Gentiles has commissioned messengers to carry that light. The turn to the Gentiles is not a human decision made in frustration; it is the fulfillment of the Servant's mandate. Isaiah 49:6 commanded it. Jewish rejection has become the occasion for its enactment, but the command preceded the rejection.

The phrase "to the ends of the earth" (heos eschatou tes ges) repeats the language of Acts 1:8: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." The mission Jesus announced in Acts 1:8 and the mission the Servant received in Isaiah 49:6 are the same mission. Paul has linked the risen Jesus's commission to the Isaianic Servant's commission, and he places himself and Barnabas within that trajectory. The "end of the earth" is the destination; Pisidian Antioch is one stop along the way.

Verse 48: The Response of the Gentiles

"And when the Gentiles heard this, they began rejoicing and glorifying the word of the Lord, and as many as were appointed to eternal life believed" (13:48).

The Gentile response is described in three phrases, each requiring attention.

First, "they began rejoicing and glorifying the word of the Lord" (echairon kai edoxazon ton logon tou kyriou). The joy and glorification parallel the response to the gospel throughout Acts. In Acts 2:46-47, the Jerusalem community ate "with glad and generous hearts, praising God." In Acts 8:8, there was "much rejoicing" in Samaria when Philip preached. In Acts 11:18, the Jerusalem church "glorified God" when they heard about Cornelius. The Gentile response in Pisidian Antioch follows the same pattern. What God did in Jerusalem, in Samaria, and in Caesarea, God is now doing in the interior of Asia Minor.

Second, "as many as were appointed to eternal life believed" (episteusas hosoi esan tetagmenoi eis zoen aionion). This clause has generated enormous discussion, and it must be read carefully within its immediate context.

The verb tetagmenoi is the perfect passive participle of tasso. In Greek usage — military, administrative, and civic — tasso means "to arrange, to place in order, to assign to a position, to enroll." A soldier is "appointed" to a post. A citizen is "enrolled" in a register. An official is "assigned" to a duty. The passive voice indicates that the appointing was done by another agent — the Gentiles did not appoint themselves. The perfect tense indicates a state resulting from a prior action — they were already in this appointed state when they heard the message.

The contrast with verse 46 is structurally important. In verse 46, Paul told the rejecting Jews: "you judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life" (ouk axious krinete heautous tes aioniou zoes). The Jews' rejection is described with active, reflexive verbs — they judge themselves, they thrust the word aside. Their unworthiness is self-determined. In verse 48, the Gentiles' reception is described with a passive verb — they "were appointed" (tetagmenoi). The two halves create a deliberate asymmetry: the rejection is a human act; the reception corresponds to a divine arrangement. The Jews who rejected were not appointed to rejection; they chose it. The Gentiles who believed were appointed to eternal life — their believing corresponds to a prior ordering.

The phrase "eternal life" (zoen aionion) is used in the LXX of Daniel 12:2: "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life (eis zoen aionion) and some to shame and everlasting contempt." In that context, eternal life was the destiny of the righteous at the resurrection. Paul has just preached about the resurrection of Jesus and the forgiveness of sins; "eternal life" is the outcome for those who receive this message. Those who were "appointed to eternal life" were disposed toward, enrolled for, set in order for this outcome — and they believed.

The author does not elaborate on the mechanism or timing of the appointment. He does not say "from before the foundation of the world" or "by an eternal decree." He states a correspondence: the ones who were appointed believed. The ones who rejected excluded themselves. The author's interest is not in constructing a theory about the relationship between divine appointment and human response but in narrating what happened: the Jews rejected and judged themselves unworthy; the Gentiles who were appointed to life believed. Both sides are presented as accountable — the Jews for their rejection, the Gentiles for their believing — but the language of divine arrangement stands behind the Gentile reception in a way that it does not stand behind the Jewish rejection.

Verses 49-52: The Word Spreads, Persecution Comes, Joy Remains

"And the word of the Lord was spreading throughout the whole region" (13:49).

The same phrase that concluded the Jerusalem section — "the word of God increased and multiplied" (12:24) — now appears in a new geography. The word spreads (diephereto — "was being carried through, was being spread abroad") beyond Pisidian Antioch into "the whole region" (di' holes tes choras). The persecution intended to contain the word produces its geographic expansion — the same pattern as Acts 8:1-4, where the scattering after Stephen's death spread the word through Judea and Samaria.

"But the Jews incited the devout women of high standing and the leading men of the city, stirred up persecution against Paul and Barnabas, and drove them out of their district" (13:50).

The opponents use social and political leverage rather than direct violence. "The devout women of high standing" (tas sebomenas gynaikas tas euschemonas) are God-fearing women of the upper class — Gentile women attached to the synagogue who had social influence. The "leading men of the city" (tous protous tes poleos) are the civic authorities. The Jews who rejected the message deploy their social connections to turn the political establishment against the messengers. The pattern echoes Jesus's warning: "they will deliver you up to councils... and you will be dragged before governors and kings for my sake, to bear witness before them and the Gentiles" (Matthew 10:17-18). The persecution is not mob violence but institutional action — driving them out of the district (exebalon autous apo ton horion auton).

"But they shook off the dust from their feet against them and went to Iconium" (13:51).

The gesture of shaking off dust (ektinaxamenoi ton koniorton ton podon) enacts Jesus's instruction to the disciples: "And if anyone will not receive you or listen to your words, shake off the dust from your feet when you leave that house or town" (Matthew 10:14; cf. Luke 9:5, 10:11). The gesture signifies the end of responsibility — the messengers have delivered the word; the rejection belongs to the rejectors. Paul and Barnabas move to Iconium, approximately ninety miles southeast, where the pattern will repeat (Acts 14:1-7).

"And the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit" (13:52).

The final verse describes not Paul and Barnabas (who have departed) but the new believers left behind in Pisidian Antioch — "the disciples" (hoi mathetai). They are "filled with joy" (charas) and "with the Holy Spirit" (pneumatos hagiou). The combination of joy and the Spirit echoes the description of the Jerusalem community in its earliest days (Acts 2:46-47) and the response of the Samaritans to Philip's preaching (Acts 8:8). Persecution has driven the messengers out, but it has not destroyed the community or its joy. The new community, composed of Gentile believers and whatever Jews and proselytes remained faithful (cf. verse 43), persists in the Spirit after the apostles leave. The mission has not only proclaimed the word but generated a community capable of sustaining itself in the Spirit's power.

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 13 divides into three movements that together form a single argument about the direction of the word of God.

The first movement (vv. 1-12) establishes the mission's origin and enacts its conflict in miniature. The Spirit commissions the work. The missionaries go first to the synagogues. A Jewish false prophet opposes the word; a Gentile proconsul receives it. The pattern is set: Jewish resistance, Gentile reception.

The second movement (vv. 13-41) provides the theological argument. Paul's speech in the Pisidian Antioch synagogue traces God's initiative through Israel's history — choosing the fathers, delivering from Egypt, enduring the wilderness, giving the land, providing judges and kings, and culminating in David. From David's line comes Jesus, the Savior. Jesus was rejected by Jerusalem's leaders, but their rejection fulfilled the prophets. God raised Jesus from the dead, and this resurrection fulfills Psalm 2, Isaiah 55, and Psalm 16. Through Jesus, forgiveness and justification beyond what the Law of Moses could provide are now available to everyone who believes. The warning from Habakkuk closes the speech: God is doing an unbelievable work; do not be among those who refuse to believe it.

The third movement (vv. 42-52) narrates the response and draws the conclusion. Initial enthusiasm gives way to Jewish jealousy when the Gentile crowds appear. Paul declares the programmatic shift: the word goes to Jews first, but since they have rejected it, the messengers turn to the Gentiles. Isaiah 49:6 provides the scriptural mandate: the Servant's mission extends to the ends of the earth. The Gentiles believe. The word spreads. Persecution follows. The messengers move on. The community they leave behind is filled with joy and the Holy Spirit.

The chapter's deepest claim is that the turn to the Gentiles is not a departure from Israel's story but its fulfillment. Paul's speech does not contrast Israel's God with a new, Gentile-friendly deity. It traces Israel's God acting through Israel's history, fulfilling Israel's promises, and extending Israel's salvation to the nations — exactly as Israel's prophets said he would. The rejection of the message by some Jews in Pisidian Antioch does not nullify Israel's story; it enacts one of its most persistent patterns — the pattern Stephen described in Acts 7, where Israel's leaders repeatedly rejected the agents God sent. And the reception of the message by Gentiles does not replace Israel; it fulfills the Servant's commission in Isaiah 49:6. The "light for the Gentiles" was always part of God's plan for Israel's Servant. The events of Acts 13 do not invent a Gentile mission; they reveal that the Gentile mission was embedded in Israel's scriptures from the beginning, waiting for the moment when it would be activated by the Servant's death, resurrection, and enthronement.

The Habakkuk quotation that closes Paul's speech binds the chapter's argument together. In Habakkuk's time, God's "unbelievable work" was using Babylonians — Gentiles — as his instrument with Israel. In Paul's time, God's "unbelievable work" is using a crucified and risen messiah to bring justification to Gentiles outside the Law of Moses. Both works scandalize Israel. Both works are God's. The question posed to the synagogue audience in Pisidian Antioch is the same question Habakkuk posed to Judah: will you believe that God is doing this, or will you perish as scoffers who refuse to accept what God himself has declared?
