Galatians 1 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

The document's genre and occasion: This is a letter, written to be read aloud to assembled congregations, from "Paul, an apostle — not from men nor through man" (1:1) to "the assemblies of Galatia" (1:2). It is written in crisis, and it is the angriest surviving document from Paul's hand.

The situation can be reconstructed almost entirely from the letter's own statements, and it is worth setting out plainly before any of the argument is examined, because nearly every difficult sentence in the letter is answering something in it.

Paul founded these congregations. He tells us he arrived among them because of an illness (4:13), stayed, preached, and was received with extraordinary warmth — they would have torn out their own eyes for him (4:15). The people who responded were Gentiles: before hearing him they "did not know God" and were "enslaved to those which by nature are not gods" (4:8), which is a description of pagan idolatry, not of Gentiles attached to a synagogue. They received the Spirit (3:2-5). Paul left.

After his departure, other teachers arrived. The letter never names them and never quotes them directly, so everything we know about their position is Paul's characterization of it. What they were urging is not in doubt: circumcision (5:2-3; 6:12-13). Paul says they "compel" the Galatians toward it (6:12) and that they want to "boast in your flesh" (6:13). He says they are "troubling" the congregations (1:7; 5:10), that they want to "shut you out" (4:17), and that they are motivated by a desire to avoid persecution (6:12). Whether that is a fair account of their motives cannot be checked; the other side of this dispute has not survived.

Why circumcision was not a small request. A modern reader may hear "circumcision" as a minor ritual addition, and the letter's temperature will then seem disproportionate. In the first century it was nothing of the kind. Circumcision was the covenant sign given to Abraham in Genesis 17, and for a Gentile male to receive it was to undergo conversion: he ceased to be a Gentile and became a Jew, a proselyte, with the full obligations of the covenant. Paul states this understanding himself, and states it as something already familiar: "I testify again to every man who receives circumcision that he is a debtor to do the whole law" (5:3). That is the standard formulation of what proselyte conversion entailed. So the question in Galatia was not whether Gentile believers should add a rite. It was whether they had to become Jews in order to belong to the people of God.

This also explains why the letter can be so fierce about circumcision while its author remains, on his own account and on Acts', an observant Jew (Acts 21:20-26). The dispute is not over whether Jews should keep the covenant. It is over whether Gentiles must enter it.

The second charge: Paul's standing. Chapters 1 and 2 are almost entirely autobiography, which is unusual, and the shape of the autobiography tells us what was being said about him. Paul insists that his message came directly from God and not from Jerusalem (1:11-12), that he did not go to Jerusalem for years (1:16-18), that when he finally went he stayed two weeks and saw two people (1:18-19), that he was personally unknown to the Judean congregations (1:22), and that when he did eventually lay his gospel before the Jerusalem leadership they "added nothing" (2:6). Someone had evidently argued that Paul's authority was derivative — that he had received a message from Jerusalem and then abridged it for Gentile consumption, dropping the requirement that made it costly.

Chapter 1 answers the first half of that charge: his commission was not received from Jerusalem, because for years he was not there.

Where and when. The recipients are "the assemblies of Galatia" (tais ekklesiais tes Galatias). Galatia is named for the Galatae, Celtic peoples who migrated into central Asia Minor in the third century BCE and settled around Ancyra (modern Ankara). By Paul's day the name did double duty: it could mean that ethnic territory in the north, or the much larger Roman province of Galatia, which had absorbed southern districts including Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe — the cities Acts 13-14 has Paul evangelizing on his first journey. The two possibilities are called the North and South Galatian hypotheses. The letter itself does not settle the question, and the dating follows from it: on the southern reading Galatians may be the earliest surviving letter of Paul, written before the Jerusalem council of Acts 15; on the northern reading it is later. Nothing in the letter's argument depends on the answer, and it will not be pressed here.

What is missing from the opening, and why it matters. Ancient letters followed conventions, and Paul's letters follow them closely enough that departures are audible. The standard Pauline shape is: sender, recipients, greeting, then a thanksgiving for the recipients ("I give thanks to my God always for you," 1 Corinthians 1:4; "I thank my God in all my remembrance of you," Philippians 1:3; and so through Romans, Colossians, 1 Thessalonians, 2 Thessalonians, 2 Timothy, Philemon).

Galatians has no thanksgiving. At the exact structural point where one belongs, Paul writes "I am astonished" (1:6). A congregation that had heard a Pauline letter read aloud before would register the omission before a single argument had been made. The silence is the first thing the letter does.

What the author argues immediately after (Galatians 2): Chapter 2 continues the autobiography into two scenes. In the first (2:1-10), Paul goes up to Jerusalem after fourteen years, lays out the gospel he preaches among the Gentiles, and reports two outcomes: Titus, a Greek travelling with him, "was not compelled to be circumcised" (2:3), and the leadership recognized a division of spheres — Paul entrusted with the gospel to the uncircumcised as Peter with the gospel to the circumcised (2:7-9). The "pillars" added nothing to him (2:6). In the second scene (2:11-14), at Antioch, Paul opposes Cephas to his face over withdrawal from table fellowship with Gentile believers. Out of that confrontation the letter's theological argument emerges (2:15-21) and runs unbroken into chapter 3.

How chapter 1 connects to what follows: The two chapters make complementary moves and both are needed. Chapter 1 establishes independence: the message did not come from Jerusalem. Chapter 2 establishes confirmation: when it was finally presented in Jerusalem, it was ratified rather than corrected. Independence alone would leave Paul a freelancer; confirmation alone would make him a subordinate. He claims both, in that order, and only then turns to the argument itself.

Verses 1-5: The Greeting

> Paul, an apostle — not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father, who raised him from the dead — and all the brothers who are with me, to the assemblies of Galatia: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, who gave himself for our sins so that he might deliver us out of the present evil age, according to the will of our God and Father, to whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen.

The greeting is the shortest in the Pauline corpus in what it says about the recipients and the longest in what it says about the sender, and the imbalance is deliberate. Paul normally identifies himself in a phrase and then expands warmly on the people he is writing to. Here he interrupts his own name with a denial and gives the Galatians four words.

Verse 1: "Paul, an apostle — not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father, who raised him from the dead."

"Paul" (Paulos) is a Roman name, the cognomen Paullus. Acts gives him a second name, Saul (Saulos, from the Hebrew Sha'ul), and uses it until Acts 13:9, after which it uses Paul. Nothing in the letters treats this as a change of identity — it is normal for a Jew of the diaspora with Roman citizenship (Acts 22:25-28) to carry both a Semitic and a Greco-Roman name. He does not present himself as a man who changed his name when he changed his religion.

"An apostle" (apostolos) is built on apostello, "to send off, to dispatch." In ordinary Greek it could mean a naval expedition or a dispatched envoy. Its weight in this context, however, comes from a Jewish institution: the shaliach, the authorized agent. The principle stated in the Mishnah is that "a man's agent is as himself" (m. Berakhot 5:5) — an agent commissioned to act on another's behalf carries the sender's authority, and dealing with the agent is dealing with the sender. To call oneself an apostolos is therefore to make a claim not about personal qualification but about who stands behind you. This is precisely why the source of the commission is the first thing Paul defends.

The denial has two prepositions and they are not saying the same thing twice. Ouk ap' anthropon ("not from men," plural) uses apo, which marks origin or source: the commission did not originate with any human body — no council appointed him, no congregation delegated him. Oude di' anthropou ("nor through a man," singular) uses dia, which marks agency or channel: it was not conveyed to him by any human intermediary — no one laid hands on him to transmit it. Together the two prepositions close off both ways a human commission could arise: not from a group, not through a person.

Both are then answered positively with a single preposition: alla dia Iesou Christou kai theou patros ("but through Jesus Christ and God the Father"). The dia that was denied of man is affirmed of Jesus Christ — and note that one preposition governs both names. Jesus Christ stands on the divine side of the "not through man" denial, alongside the Father. Nothing is said about how this relation is to be conceived; the grammar simply places him there.

The Father is then identified by a single act: tou egeirantos auton ek nekron ("who raised him from the dead"). This is worth pausing over. In Israel's scriptures God is characteristically identified by what he has done, and the identifying act is the exodus. The Ten Words open: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2; Deuteronomy 5:6). The formula recurs constantly (Leviticus 19:36; 25:38; Numbers 15:41; Deuteronomy 8:14; Judges 6:8; Psalm 81:10). Here the identifying act is the resurrection. God is named by the newest thing he has done rather than the oldest, and a hearer formed on the scriptures would notice the substitution. The phrase ek nekron ("from among dead ones") is plural: Jesus was raised out from among the dead, one drawn out of a company.

Verse 2: "And all the brothers who are with me, to the assemblies of Galatia."

Paul names co-senders in most of his letters, and names them: Sosthenes (1 Corinthians 1:1), Timothy (2 Corinthians 1:1; Philippians 1:1; Colossians 1:1; Philemon 1), Silvanus and Timothy (1 and 2 Thessalonians 1:1). Here the co-senders are an undifferentiated group — hoi syn emoi pantes adelphoi, "all the brothers with me." Whether the anonymity is because no one with him was known to the Galatians, or because Paul wants the weight of a body rather than of named individuals, the text does not say. The effect is that a whole company stands behind the letter without any of them being available to be weighed against Paul.

The address is bare, and the bareness is the point. Compare what Paul usually writes: "to the church of God that is in Corinth, to those sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints, together with all those who in every place call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 1:2); "to all those in Rome who are loved by God and called to be saints" (Romans 1:7); "to the saints and faithful brothers in Christ at Colossae" (Colossians 1:2). Here: "to the assemblies of Galatia." No commendation, no "beloved," no "saints." The withholding begins immediately.

"Assemblies" translates ekklesiai, and the word carries two histories that are both live in a mixed Greek-speaking Jewish and Gentile setting. To a Greek ear, an ekklesia was the citizen assembly of a city — the body of enfranchised men summoned out (the word is built on ek, "out," and kaleo, "to call") to deliberate and decide. Acts 19:32, 39, 41 uses the word in exactly that civic sense of the riotous gathering at Ephesus. To a Jewish ear formed on the Greek scriptures, ekklesia was the standard rendering of the Hebrew qahal, the assembly of Israel gathered before God: the assembly at Horeb, "the day of the assembly" (Deuteronomy 4:10; 9:10; 18:16); the regulations for admission to "the assembly of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 23:1-3); the assembly gathered by Solomon at the temple's dedication (1 Kings 8:14, 22, 55, 65). Both senses are a summoned body, and neither is a building or an institution.

The plural (ekklesiais) means several congregations, which fits either the northern or the southern reconstruction. The letter is addressed to a region, not a city — unusual for Paul, and consistent with a crisis spreading through a network.

Verses 3-5: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, who gave himself for our sins so that he might deliver us out of the present evil age, according to the will of our God and Father, to whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen."

"Grace to you and peace" (charis hymin kai eirene) is Paul's standard formula, and it is a fusion of two conventions. The ordinary Greek letter opened with chairein, "greetings" (literally "rejoice"), which is what the letter in Acts 15:23 and James 1:1 use. Paul substitutes the similar-sounding charis, "grace" — favor freely given, a gift that is not owed and not earned. To this he adds eirene, which is the Greek rendering of the Hebrew shalom. Shalom is not primarily the absence of conflict; it denotes wholeness, soundness, a state in which things are as they should be and nothing is missing. The priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24-26 ends with it: "the LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you shalom." So the greeting joins a Greek courtesy transformed into a theological term with a Hebrew wish for wholeness, which is a fair description of the congregations Paul is writing to.

Both come "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ," again with one preposition governing both.

What is not standard is what happens next. Paul does not usually define Christ's work in a greeting. Here he attaches a relative clause, and it states in one sentence the frame within which everything else in the letter will be argued.

"Who gave himself for our sins" (tou dontos heauton hyper ton hamartion hemon). The verb is didomi, "to give," with the reflexive heauton, "himself" — he was not merely given, he gave himself, which makes the act voluntary. The preposition hyper means "on behalf of, for the sake of, in the interest of." It is the ordinary preposition for acting to someone's benefit and is standard in contexts of sacrifice and of dying for another. The phrase will return in the singular at 2:20 — "who loved me and gave himself for me" — which is the only place in the letter Paul puts the cross in the first person singular.

"So that he might deliver us out of the present evil age" (hopos exeletai hemas ek tou aionos tou enestotos ponerou). Three things in this clause deserve attention, and together they set the letter's horizon.

First, the verb. Exaireo means "to take out, to pluck out, to rescue," and it is the exodus verb of the Greek scriptures. God at the burning bush: "I have come down to deliver them (exelesthai autous) out of the hand of the Egyptians" (Exodus 3:8). Jethro blesses the God "who delivered (exeilato) the people from under the hand of the Egyptians" (Exodus 18:10). The same verb runs through the recitals of the rescue: Judges 6:9; 1 Samuel 10:18; 2 Samuel 22:1; Psalm 34:4 (LXX 33:5). Whatever Paul is describing, he is describing it as a new exodus — a people taken out from where they were held.

Second, what they are taken out of. Not a place, not a power, but an aion — an age, a stretch of time with a character. Jewish thought of this period widely divided history into two: ha-olam ha-zeh, "this age," the present order under which things go wrong, and ha-olam ha-ba, "the age to come," when God sets them right. The framework is visible across the literature of the period and in the Gospels ("this age" and "the age to come," Matthew 12:32; Mark 10:30; Luke 18:30) and in Paul (1 Corinthians 1:20; 2:6, 8; 2 Corinthians 4:4; Ephesians 1:21). Calling the present age poneros, "evil," is not a statement that the material world is bad. It is a statement about the present period of history and about what is operative within it.

Third, the tense of it. The participle enestotos comes from enistemi, "to have set in, to be present," and it is temporal rather than moral: the age that has arrived and is currently standing. Paul uses the word this way at 1 Corinthians 7:26 ("the present distress") and Romans 8:38 ("things present" set against "things to come"). So the rescue is out of an age the recipients are still living in. Something has happened to them that has not yet happened to the world around them.

That single clause governs a great deal of what follows. The letter's talk of slavery and freedom (2:4; 4:1-11; 5:1), of the "elements of the world" (4:3, 9), of a world crucified (6:14) and of "new creation" (6:15) all sit inside this two-age frame, announced in the letter's third sentence before any controversy has been named.

"According to the will of our God and Father" (kata to thelema tou theou kai patros hemon) locates the whole of it in God's purpose rather than in an emergency measure.

"To whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen." A doxology here is unusual; Paul's letters normally reserve them for the body or the close. The phrase eis tous aionas ton aionon, "unto the ages of the ages," is a Semitic superlative construction (the same shape as "holy of holies" or "king of kings") rendered literally into Greek, and it means something like "for ever and ever" with the emphasis on inexhaustibility. Amen transliterates the Hebrew 'amen, from a root meaning firmness or reliability — the word by which a congregation assents to what has been said and makes it its own.

The letter's first movement therefore ends in praise. The next paragraph begins with astonishment.

Verses 6-10: A Different Gospel

> I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting him who called you in the grace of Christ and turning to a different gospel — not that there is another one, but there are some who are troubling you and want to distort the gospel of Christ. But even if we, or an angel from heaven, should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed. As we have said before, so now I say again: if anyone is preaching to you a gospel contrary to the one you received, let him be accursed. For am I now seeking the approval of men, or of God? Or am I trying to please men? If I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ.

Verse 6: "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting him who called you in the grace of Christ, to a different gospel."

"I am astonished" (thaumazo) stands where the thanksgiving belongs, and it is not merely an expression of surprise. Greek letter-writing had a recognized formula of rebuke that opened with thaumazo followed by the offence — surviving papyrus letters use it in exactly this way ("I am astonished that you have not written to me"). It is the conventional opening of a complaint, and putting it in the thanksgiving's place is a substitution a hearer would feel.

"So quickly" (houtos tacheos) states that little time has passed, though the letter does not say little time since what — since Paul's departure, or since the other teachers arrived. Either way the speed is part of the offence.

"You are deserting" translates metatithesthe, and both the verb and its tense matter. Metatithemi means "to transfer, to shift, to move something from one place to another." It is used of moving a boundary marker, of transferring a body from one tomb to another (Acts 7:16), and in the middle voice, as here, of a person changing sides — shifting one's allegiance from one party or philosophy to another. Josephus and later writers use it of political and philosophical defection. The word is not "you have abandoned"; it is "you are changing over."

And it is present tense. This governs the tone of the entire letter. The Galatians are in the process of moving. The transfer is under way and not complete; nobody, on this description, has yet been circumcised (5:2 addresses circumcision as a live possibility, not an accomplished fact). Everything harsh in this letter is written to people the writer believes can still turn around, which is why the harshness never becomes dismissal and why the last word of the letter is "brothers" (6:18).

What they are leaving is described as a person, not a doctrine: apo tou kalesantos hymas ("from the one who called you"). The participle "the one who called" refers to God throughout Paul's usage (1:15; 5:8; Romans 8:30; 9:24; 1 Corinthians 1:9; 1 Thessalonians 5:24). They are not primarily exchanging one theology for another; they are walking away from someone.

"In the grace of Christ" (en chariti Christou) names the sphere they were called into. A few manuscripts omit "of Christ," and one omits "grace," but the sense is not materially affected.

Verse 7: "Not that there is another; but there are some who are troubling you and want to distort the gospel of Christ."

Paul corrects his own sentence in mid-air. He has just said the Galatians are turning to a heteron euangelion, a "different gospel"; he now says ho ouk estin allo — "which is not another."

Greek has two words for "other." Heteros carries the sense of other-of-a-different-kind; allos, other-of-the-same-kind. The distinction is not always pressed in the Greek of this period, and it would be unwise to build much on it alone — but here the two words stand four words apart, in a deliberately self-correcting sentence, which is as close to pressing the distinction as an author can come. The sense: what you are turning to is a different thing, and it is not a second gospel. There are not two gospels between which one might choose. There is one, and there is something else wearing its name.

The teachers are described with two participles. Hoi tarassontes hymas ("those troubling you") uses tarasso, whose root sense is the stirring up of water so that it becomes turbid — it is used of the stirring of the pool at John 5:7 and, figuratively, of agitation and disturbance of mind. The same verb returns in the singular at 5:10. And thelontes metastrepsai to euangelion tou Christou ("wanting to turn about the gospel of Christ"): metastrepho is "to turn around, to reverse." The Greek scriptures use it for the reversal of a state — "the sun shall be turned (metastraphesetai) to darkness and the moon to blood" (Joel 2:31, LXX 3:4). What is threatened is not adjustment but inversion.

Verses 8-9: "But even if we, or an angel from heaven, should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed. As we have said before, so now I say again: if anyone is preaching to you a gospel contrary to the one you received, let him be accursed."

The curse is pronounced twice, and the second time with an explicit note that it is a repetition. Repetition in this position is a formal device: it converts a rhetorical outburst into a standing ruling.

"Let him be accursed" is anathema esto. Anathema in the Greek scriptures renders the Hebrew herem, a term with no clean English equivalent. A thing placed under herem is devoted to God and thereby removed from all ordinary use — withdrawn from the human economy entirely, which in practice often meant destruction. The regulations are at Leviticus 27:28-29: "every devoted thing is most holy to the LORD... no devoted thing that a man devotes to the LORD shall be redeemed." Deuteronomy 7:26 applies it to idolatrous objects; Joshua 6:17-18 places Jericho under it, and Joshua 7 narrates what happened when Achan took what had been devoted. To pronounce anathema is not to insult someone. It is to hand them over to God and withdraw them from the community's dealings.

The scope of the first curse is deliberately absurd in its extremity: "even if we" — Paul first, including himself and his co-senders — "or an angel from heaven." Whatever authority might be invoked on behalf of a message, the message is not thereby authorized. Paul's own apostleship, which he has just spent a whole verse defending, would not protect him. Nor would an origin in heaven.

This establishes the criterion by which everything in the letter is to be judged, and it is worth stating plainly because so much of the letter depends on it: the gospel is authenticated by its content measured against what the Galatians already received, not by the standing of whoever brings it. That is why Paul can argue, three chapters later, from the Galatians' own experience of the Spirit (3:2-5) rather than from his apostolic office. He has ruled out his office as the test.

Note the shift between the two curses. The first is subjunctive — ean hemeis e angelos... euangelizetai, "if we or an angel should preach" — a hypothetical stated for its force. The second is indicative — ei tis hymas euangelizetai, "if anyone is preaching to you" — a real condition describing something happening now.

Verse 10: "For am I now seeking the approval of men, or of God? Or am I trying to please men? If I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ."

The "for" (gar) attaches this to the curse: the double anathema is itself Paul's evidence. A man courting approval does not curse his opponents twice in two sentences.

Two expressions indicate that a charge is being answered. Peitho is "to persuade, to win over, to conciliate," and with a personal object it can mean seeking someone's favour. Anthropois areskein is "to please men" — the phrase for accommodating an audience.

The word eti ("still") in "if I were still pleasing men" implies a past in which he was. Read against verses 13-14, which describe a career in which he excelled and advanced beyond his contemporaries, the reference may be to the standing he once had in a movement whose approval he enjoyed.

What the accusation actually was is not stated, but the letter supplies a mirror image of it at 6:12: the other teachers, Paul says, "want to make a good showing in the flesh" and press circumcision "only so that they may not be persecuted for the cross of Christ." The charge Paul answers at the beginning — that he softens the message to win people — is the charge he turns back on his opponents at the end. Whoever started that exchange, the letter's opening and closing are its two ends.

"Servant of Christ" is Christou doulos, and doulos is not a hired servant but a slave — a person owned. English versions have long softened it, but the word is stark, and Paul uses it of himself deliberately (Romans 1:1; Philippians 1:1). The logic of the sentence depends on the starkness: a slave has one owner and cannot serve the preferences of others. If Paul were still in the business of pleasing people, the relationship he claims would not exist.

Verses 11-12: Not Received, Not Taught

> For I would have you know, brothers, that the gospel that was preached by me is not according to man. For I did not receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but through a revelation of Jesus Christ.

Verse 11: "For I would have you know, brothers, that the gospel that was preached by me is not according to man."

Gnorizo de hymin ("I make known to you") is a formula introducing something the writer intends to establish rather than assume. Paul uses it at 1 Corinthians 12:3 and 15:1 and 2 Corinthians 8:1, always to open a matter he is about to set out.

"Not according to man" is ouk estin kata anthropon. The preposition kata with the accusative indicates conformity to a standard: the gospel is not measured by, patterned on, or of a piece with human thinking. The phrase does not yet say where it came from; that is the next verse.

The address "brothers" (adelphoi) appears here for the first time in the letter's body. It will recur throughout, including in the letter's last line, and it is worth noting that the severity never displaces it.

Verse 12: "For I did not receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but through a revelation of Jesus Christ."

Two denials, and both use technical terms.

Oude gar ego para anthropou parelabon auto ("for neither did I receive it from a man") uses paralambano. This verb and its partner paradidomi ("to hand on, to deliver over") are the standard vocabulary for the transmission of received tradition. Paul uses the pair precisely that way in his account of the resurrection: "I delivered (paredoka) to you as of first importance what I also received (parelabon)" (1 Corinthians 15:3), and again of the Lord's supper at 1 Corinthians 11:23. The same two-verb structure corresponds to the Hebrew pair qibbel and masar, "to receive" and "to transmit," used in rabbinic literature for the chain of tradition ("Moses received Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua," m. Avot 1:1).

This is important for reading the denial correctly. Paul is not claiming that he received nothing by tradition — he elsewhere says explicitly that he did. He is claiming that this did not come that way.

The second denial, oute edidachthen ("nor was I taught it"), closes the other route: not by transmission, and not by instruction.

What he affirms is di' apokalypseos Iesou Christou — "through a revelation of Jesus Christ." Apokalypsis is built on apo ("away") and kalypto ("to cover") — an uncovering, an unveiling of what was there but hidden.

The genitive "of Jesus Christ" can be read two ways, and this ambiguity will recur at several crucial points in the letter. As an objective genitive, the phrase means a revelation whose content is Jesus Christ: God unveiled his Son. As a subjective genitive, it means a revelation given by Jesus Christ, with Christ as the revealer. Verse 16 leans toward the first, since there God is the agent and the Son is what is disclosed ("to reveal his Son in me"). But the phrase as it stands supports both, and Paul does not disambiguate. The same construction will govern the far more consequential phrase pistis Iesou Christou at 2:16.

Verses 13-17: The Former Life, and the Call

> For you have heard of my former manner of life in Judaism, how I persecuted the assembly of God violently and tried to destroy it. And I advanced in Judaism beyond many of my own age among my people, being far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers. But when he who had set me apart from my mother's womb and called me through his grace was pleased to reveal his Son in me, so that I might preach him among the Gentiles, I did not immediately consult with flesh and blood, nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me, but I went away into Arabia, and returned again to Damascus.

Verse 13: "For you have heard of my former manner of life in Judaism, how I persecuted the assembly of God violently and tried to destroy it."

"You have heard" (ekousate) indicates that Paul's past was known to the Galatians — either because he had told them or because it was widely reported (verse 23 will say that it circulated as a report even among congregations that had never met him).

Anastrophe is "manner of life, conduct, way of going about" — how a person lives, not merely what he believes.

Ioudaismos. This word, translated "Judaism," is rare and it is not the neutral term a modern reader will hear. In the whole of the Greek Bible it appears here, in the next verse, and in 2 Maccabees (2:21; 8:1; 14:38), and nowhere else. It is worth setting out what it means there, because the passages are the word's earliest surviving uses and they define it.

2 Maccabees narrates the crisis under Antiochus IV Epiphanes in the 160s BCE, when the Seleucid ruler outlawed Torah observance, forbade circumcision on pain of death, and installed pagan worship in the Jerusalem temple. Some in Jerusalem cooperated, adopting Greek customs — building a gymnasium, disguising their circumcision. 2 Maccabees calls that programme Hellenismos, "Hellenism" (4:13), and it calls the armed resistance to it Ioudaismos. The book praises those "who fought bravely for Ioudaismos" (2:21), speaks of the Maccabean forces at 8:1, and at 14:38 describes a man who "had been accused of Ioudaismos, and had risked body and life for Ioudaismos with all constancy."

The word was coined, in other words, for a partisan movement — for the party of resistance in a struggle over whether Israel would remain distinct. It names a cause one fights for, not a religion one belongs to.

"I persecuted the assembly of God violently and tried to destroy it" — kath' hyperbolen edioken ten ekklesian tou theou kai eporthoun auten. Kath' hyperbolen means "to excess, beyond measure" (the English "hyperbole" comes from it). Dioko is "to pursue, to chase down, to persecute." Portheo, in the imperfect eporthoun, is a violent word: to ravage, to sack, to lay waste — what an army does to a city. The imperfect tense makes it ongoing: he was in the business of laying it waste.

Verse 14: "And I advanced in Judaism beyond many of my own age among my people, being far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers."

Proekopton ("I was advancing") comes from prokopto, which originally meant to cut a way forward — to hack a path through obstacles — and came to mean progress or advancement. He was not a marginal figure; he was outstripping his contemporaries (synelikiotas, "those of the same age") within his people (en to genei mou).

"Zealous for the traditions of my fathers" contains two terms that a reader should not pass over, because between them they specify what Paul is describing.

Zelotes ("zealot, one who is zealous"). Zeal in Israel's scriptures is not enthusiasm; it is decisive, often violent action taken for the boundary of the covenant people. The paradigm is Phinehas, who in Numbers 25 sees an Israelite man bring a Midianite woman into the camp, follows them, and runs a spear through both — and is commended: "he was zealous for his God" (Numbers 25:13), and it "was counted to him as righteousness from generation to generation" (Psalm 106:30-31). The second paradigm is Elijah: "I have been very zealous for the LORD, the God of hosts" (1 Kings 19:10, 14), said immediately after he has slaughtered the prophets of Baal. And the Maccabean literature applies the language to itself: when Mattathias kills the Jew who sacrifices at the pagan altar, he is said to have "burned with zeal... as Phinehas did" (1 Maccabees 2:24-26), and he calls out, "Let every one who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out with me" (2:27).

Paul places this word between two descriptions of what he did with it — he persecuted and laid waste (v. 13), and he advanced beyond his contemporaries (v. 14). The self-description is coherent and it is specific.

Paradoseis ("traditions"). This is not nomos, "law," and the difference is substantial. Paradosis is "that which is handed over" — transmitted custom, the accumulated practice passed from one generation to the next. It is the word used for "the tradition of the elders" in the dispute at Mark 7:3-5 and Matthew 15:2-6, where practices not written in the Torah are observed alongside it. Josephus uses it the same way of the Pharisees: they "have passed on to the people certain regulations handed down by former generations and not recorded in the law of Moses" (Antiquities 13.297). And the modifier here is patrikas, "ancestral, of my fathers."

So when Paul specifies the object of his surpassing zeal, he names ancestral tradition, using the word for handed-down custom rather than the word for Torah.

What Paul is describing as his "former manner of life." Three readings are possible and the interpreter must choose.

He is describing Judaism as such, which he then left. On this reading, verses 13-14 are a conversion narrative: he changed religions.

He is describing a zealot-traditionalist movement within Judaism, which he then left, remaining a Jew. On this reading, what he abandoned was the programme of violent boundary enforcement, not the covenant.

The distinction is anachronistic and Paul would not have separated the two.

The vocabulary bears on the choice, and it should be weighed before the first reading is assumed as the obvious one. Ioudaismos carries a Maccabean history of partisan struggle; paradoseis is handed-down custom rather than Torah; zelotes carries the Phinehas and Elijah background of violent action. And the next verse describes what happened to him in the language of prophetic commissioning rather than of conversion.

Verses 15-16a: "But when he who had set me apart from my mother's womb and called me through his grace was pleased to reveal his Son in me, so that I might preach him among the Gentiles."

The language here is drawn from two prophets, and the borrowing is close enough to be unmistakable.

Jeremiah 1:5 (Greek): "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you came out of the womb I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations" (propheten eis ethne).

Isaiah 49:1 (Greek): "The LORD called me from my mother's womb (ek koilias metros mou ekalesen to onoma mou)"; and 49:6: "I have given you as a light to the nations (eis phos ethnon), that you may be my salvation to the end of the earth."

Paul's sentence — aphorisas me ek koilias metros mou kai kalesas dia tes charitos autou, "having set me apart from my mother's womb and called me through his grace" — combines the elements of both, and the purpose clause supplies the third: the nations.

Two consequences follow, and both matter for how the whole letter is read.

First, this is the form of a prophetic commissioning, not of a conversion. Nothing here describes guilt relieved, beliefs exchanged, or a religion left. There is no confession of sin, no account of being forgiven, no before-and-after of unbelief and belief. There is an appointment: a man set apart before birth, called, and sent. That is the shape of Jeremiah's call, of Isaiah's servant, of Samuel's, of Moses'. Whatever happened to Paul on the road, the way he describes it here is the way a prophet describes being commissioned.

Second, the Gentile mission is inside the commission. Hina euangelizomai auton en tois ethnesin — "so that I might announce him among the nations." It is not a later development, not a strategic redirection after Jewish audiences proved unresponsive, not a concession. It is the stated content of what he was called to do. By the time the letter's argument begins, the inclusion of the nations is not a conclusion Paul reached but the assignment he was given.

Eudokesen ("was pleased") is the verb of God's good pleasure — a determination made with satisfaction rather than reluctance.

The phrase apokalypsai ton huion autou en emoi ("to reveal his Son in me") is ambiguous in its preposition. En emoi may mean "to me" (Paul as recipient of the revelation), "in me" (the revelation occurring inwardly), or "in my case, through me" (Paul as the medium by which the Son is revealed to others). The purpose clause that immediately follows — so that I might preach him among the nations — fits the third comfortably: God unveiled his Son in Paul in order that Paul might display him to the nations.

Verses 16b-17: "I did not immediately consult with flesh and blood, nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me, but I went away into Arabia, and returned again to Damascus."

The whole point of the itinerary is negative. It is a list of where Paul did not go.

Ou prosanethemen sarki kai haimati ("I did not confer with flesh and blood") uses prosanatithemi, "to lay a matter before someone, to consult, to submit for another's judgment." The simple form of this verb, anatithemi, will appear at 2:2 for what Paul eventually did do in Jerusalem ("I set before them the gospel"), and the compound will appear again at 2:6 in the sense "to add" ("they added nothing to me"). Paul's careful use of this verb family across two chapters is part of the argument: he did not go for a ruling, and when he came they did not give one.

"Flesh and blood" (sarx kai haima) is a Semitic idiom for human beings considered in their creatureliness and limitation. It appears at Matthew 16:17 ("flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven") in a context strikingly parallel to this one — a revelation contrasted with human information.

The specific denial follows: he did not go up to Jerusalem pros tous pro emou apostolous ("to those who were apostles before me"). Note the concession embedded in it. Paul grants their chronological priority without qualification — they were apostles before he was. What he denies is that this made them his source.

Instead: apelthon eis Arabian ("I went away into Arabia"). Arabia in this period designates the Nabatean kingdom, a substantial Arab state extending south and east of Damascus, with its capital at Petra, ruled in this period by Aretas IV. It was Gentile territory.

What Paul did there is not stated. The text says he went and that he returned to Damascus. Several reconstructions have been offered — that he began preaching to Gentiles immediately, since Nabatea was Gentile; that he withdrew for a period of reflection; that he is echoing Elijah's flight to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8), particularly given that 4:25 will place Mount Sinai "in Arabia." A related datum is 2 Corinthians 11:32-33, where the ethnarch under King Aretas guarded Damascus in order to seize Paul and he escaped over the wall in a basket, which suggests the period ended in trouble with Nabatean authorities.

None of these is established by the text, and the argument does not require any of them. What the verse establishes is only the negative that Paul needs: not Jerusalem.

Verses 18-24: Jerusalem at Last, and Then Away Again

> Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days. But I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother. (In what I am writing to you, before God, I do not lie!) Then I went into the regions of Syria and Cilicia. And I was still unknown by face to the assemblies of Judea that are in Christ; they only were hearing, "He who used to persecute us is now preaching the faith he once tried to destroy." And they glorified God because of me.

Verses 18-19: "Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days. But I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother."

Every element of this sentence is doing argumentative work, and it is worth taking them in order.

"Then after three years." Epeita meta tria ete. The interval establishes that any contact with Jerusalem came long after the commission and long after the preaching had begun. Whether the three years are counted from the call or from the return to Damascus is not specified; ancient reckoning would count partial years, so the actual span may be shorter than three full years.

"To visit Cephas." The verb is historesai, and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. It is the aorist infinitive of historeo, from which the English "history" descends by way of histor, "one who knows, an inquirer." The verb means "to inquire into, to get information about," and with a personal object "to visit for the purpose of making someone's acquaintance," or "to visit a person or place of note." It is emphatically not the vocabulary of receiving instruction — no one is a student of a man he goes to historesai. Paul had a wide choice of verbs and chose this one.

"Cephas" is the Aramaic Kepha, "rock," which John 1:42 reports as the name given to Simon and glosses into Greek as Petros. Paul uses the Aramaic form here and throughout chapter 2 (2:9, 11, 14), and Petros only at 2:7-8.

"Fifteen days." Hemeras dekapente. The number is given because its smallness is the point. Two weeks is a visit. It is not a period of training, and no one reading the letter could construe it as one.

"None of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother." Whether the "except" (ei me) means that Paul saw one apostle besides Cephas — namely James — or that he saw no other apostles but did see James, who is not being counted among them, is grammatically undecidable. The status of James in relation to the apostolic circle is not what the sentence is about. What the sentence is about is that in fifteen days Paul met two people.

"The Lord's brother" (ton adelphon tou kyriou) identifies this James as a member of Jesus' family, distinguishing him from the two apostles of that name. He appears as a leading figure in the Jerusalem community here, at 2:9 and 2:12, and in Acts 15:13 and 21:18.

Verse 20: "(In what I am writing to you, before God, I do not lie!)"

An oath interrupts the narrative. Enopion tou theou hoti ou pseudomai — "before God, that I do not lie."

Oaths of this kind are rare in Paul (compare Romans 9:1; 2 Corinthians 1:23; 11:31; 1 Thessalonians 2:5) and they appear where a claim is under challenge. Nobody swears to facts nobody disputes. The presence of the oath here is therefore evidence about the situation: the chronology in verses 15-19 — the years of absence, the brevity of the visit, the two people seen — was being contested. Someone in Galatia was telling the story differently.

Verses 21-22: "Then I went into the regions of Syria and Cilicia. And I was still unknown by face to the assemblies of Judea that are in Christ."

The itinerary continues away from Jerusalem. Syria and Cilicia are the regions north of Judea; Cilicia contains Tarsus, which Acts names as Paul's birthplace (Acts 21:39; 22:3). Whatever Paul was doing in these years, he was doing it at a distance.

Verse 22 makes a stronger claim than verse 18 did. Not only did Paul not train in Jerusalem; he was emen de agnooumenos to prosopo ("I was being unknown by face") to the assemblies of Judea. The construction is a periphrastic imperfect — an ongoing state rather than a momentary fact. He was not merely unacquainted with the apostles; he was personally unknown throughout the region. His independence was not a brief interval before an apprenticeship. It was the standing condition of his ministry in this period.

The qualification tais en Christo ("that are in Christ") distinguishes these Judean assemblies from the synagogues — Paul was certainly not unknown in those.

Verses 23-24: "They only were hearing, 'He who used to persecute us is now preaching the faith he once tried to destroy.' And they glorified God because of me."

The report is quoted in direct speech, in the present tense, as those congregations passed it around: ho diokon hemas pote nyn euangelizetai ten pistin hen pote eporthei.

Two verbs return from verse 13 — dioko ("persecute") and portheo ("ravage, lay waste") — and the repetition makes the report an echo of Paul's own self-description. The people he had hunted are describing him in the words he uses of himself.

Ten pistin ("the faith") appears here with the article as a term for the message believed rather than for the act of believing — the content, what is elsewhere called "the gospel." The formulation is compact and ironic: he announces the faith he once ravaged.

The chapter closes: kai edoxazon en emoi ton theon — "and they were glorifying God in me," which is to say, on account of what had happened in his case.

The rhetorical function of this last line is precise, and it is easy to miss. Paul has spent nineteen verses establishing his distance from Jerusalem. That distance could easily be read as estrangement — as though he were a rogue operator at odds with the movement's centre. The closing sentence forecloses that reading. The congregations closest to Jerusalem, who had the strongest reason to be suspicious of the man who had tried to destroy them, and who had no personal acquaintance with him to soften the judgment, praised God for him. Independence, therefore, without hostility. That is exactly the position chapter 2 needs in order to work: Paul can go to Jerusalem, be received, and still owe them nothing.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1. The chapter's function is to establish that Paul's message did not come from Jerusalem, and its instrument is chronology. Three years before any visit; fifteen days when it came; two men seen; unknown by face to the Judean assemblies afterward; then away north to Syria and Cilicia. Each item narrows the window in which instruction could have occurred, and together they close it. The oath at verse 20 tells us that these dates were themselves in dispute — Paul does not swear to what nobody denies.

2. The missing thanksgiving is structural and would have been heard. Every other Pauline letter moves greeting, thanksgiving, body. This one moves greeting, "I am astonished." And thaumazo was itself a conventional opening for a complaint in Greek letters. The letter registers its emergency before making a single argument.

3. The defection is described as in progress, and this governs the letter's tone throughout. Metatithesthe (1:6) is present tense: they are changing over, not gone. Nobody has yet been circumcised. Everything harsh in the six chapters that follow is written to people the writer believes can still turn back, which is why the severity never becomes dismissal and why the last word of the letter is "brothers."

4. The double curse sets the criterion for the entire letter, and it excludes appeal to authority — including Paul's own. Not the apostle who founded them, not an angel from heaven. The measure is the message the Galatians already received. This is why the argument in chapter 3 can begin from their own experience of the Spirit rather than from Paul's office: he has ruled his office out as the test.

5. The vocabulary of 1:13-14 is more specific than "Judaism" and "the law," and the specificity should be weighed before it is smoothed away. Ioudaismos is the word 2 Maccabees coined for the armed resistance to Hellenization — a cause fought for, not a religion belonged to. Paradoseis is ancestral handed-down custom, the word used for "the tradition of the elders," and not nomos. Zelotes carries the Phinehas and Elijah background of violent action for the covenant boundary, and Paul brackets it with what he did: persecuted, laid waste. Whether he is describing leaving Judaism or leaving a partisan movement within it is a decision the reader must make — but these three words are the evidence for making it, and they are not neutral.

6. What Paul describes in 1:15-16 has the form of a prophetic commissioning, not a conversion. The language is Jeremiah's and Isaiah's: set apart from the womb, called, sent to the nations. There is no guilt relieved, no belief exchanged, no religion left. There is an appointment. And the Gentile mission is stated as the content of the call itself, not as a later adjustment — which means that before the letter's argument begins, the inclusion of the nations is already established as an assignment received rather than a conclusion reached.

7. The greeting has already stated the letter's frame in miniature (1:4), and it is an apocalyptic frame. Christ gave himself "so that he might deliver us out of the present evil age." The verb is the exodus verb of the Greek scriptures; the object is an aion, an age, not a place or a power; and the age is one the recipients are still standing in. Everything the letter goes on to argue about law, promise, slavery, freedom and inheritance is argued inside a two-age structure announced in the third sentence — and the letter will return to it at the close with "new creation" (6:15).

8. The charge Paul answers at 1:10 is the charge he returns at 6:12. He asks whether he is trying to please people; at the end he says his opponents press circumcision to make a good showing in the flesh and avoid persecution for the cross. The accusation of accommodation runs in both directions, and only one side of that exchange has survived. The letter is the sole surviving source for this dispute, and the other party's account of themselves is not available.
