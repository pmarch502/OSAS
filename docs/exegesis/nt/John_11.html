John 11 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (John 10): Chapter 10 presented Jesus as the good shepherd of Ezekiel 34 — the one whom God promised to raise over Israel after indicting the nation's failed leaders. The chapter opened without a scene break from chapter 9, interpreting the Pharisees' expulsion of the healed blind man as evidence that they are thieves and hired hands, not true shepherds. Jesus declared himself the door of the sheep (10:7, 9) and the good shepherd (10:11, 14), the one who calls his own sheep by name, whose sheep hear his voice and follow him. He announced that the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep — voluntarily, deliberately, on his own initiative, with authority both to lay it down and to take it up again (10:17-18). He declared that his sheep will never perish, that no one can snatch them from his hand or from the Father's hand, and that "I and the Father are one" (10:30). The chapter also extended the scope of the shepherd's mission beyond Israel: "I have other sheep, which are not of this fold; I must bring them also, and they will hear my voice; and they will become one flock with one shepherd" (10:16). At the Feast of Dedication, the authorities demanded a plain declaration of his messiahship, provoked by Jesus's response to attempt stoning a second time (after 8:59), and then attempted to seize him. Jesus withdrew beyond the Jordan to where John had baptized, creating a geographic inclusio with the beginning of his public ministry (1:28). Many believed in him there. The chapter's final word was belief; its sustained argument was that the shepherd's death is not defeat but the deliberate act of the one who has authority over life and death.

What the author argues immediately after (John 12): Chapter 12 opens with Jesus returning to Bethany six days before Passover, where Mary anoints his feet with expensive perfume — an act Jesus interprets as preparation for his burial (12:7). Lazarus is present at the table, a living witness to the power just demonstrated, and the chief priests make plans to kill Lazarus as well because "on account of him many of the Jews were going away and believing in Jesus" (12:10-11). Jesus enters Jerusalem to the acclaim of crowds who specifically cite the raising of Lazarus as the reason for their coming out (12:17-18). The Pharisees say to one another, "You see that you are not doing any good; look, the world has gone after him" (12:19) — a statement immediately followed by Greeks coming to seek Jesus (12:20-21), which fulfills the "other sheep not of this fold" promise of 10:16 and the "gather together into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" of 11:52. Jesus responds to the Greeks' arrival by declaring that "the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" (12:23) and employs the grain-of-wheat image: "unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (12:24). The one who raised Lazarus from the dead now announces that he himself must die in order to give life on the largest scale. Chapter 12 thus translates the events of chapter 11 into their consequences: the sign that demonstrated Jesus's power over death precipitates the events that will bring about his own death, which will in turn bring about the universal gathering of life.

How chapter 11 connects the surrounding argument: Chapter 10 ended with the shepherd declaring his authority to lay down his life and take it up again. Chapter 11 now demonstrates, in the most dramatic sign yet, that this authority is real: the one who claims power over death raises a man who has been dead four days. The claim is no longer abstract. The shepherd who says he can give life to the dead now does so — publicly, before witnesses, in a village two miles from Jerusalem where the authorities will inevitably hear of it. And the result of this supreme demonstration of life-giving power is the decision to kill Jesus (11:47-53). The chapter thus functions as the hinge of the entire document: it is the climactic sign that proves who Jesus is, and it is the event that sets in motion the passion narrative that will prove what the shepherd meant by "I lay down my life for the sheep." The one who gives life to the dead will be put to death by those who refuse to believe, and that death will itself become the means of giving life to the world.

The chapter also completes a series of sign-and-discourse pairings that have structured the document. In chapter 5, Jesus healed a paralytic and then discoursed on the Son's authority to give life and raise the dead (5:21-29). In chapter 6, Jesus fed the multitude and then discoursed on himself as the bread of life (6:35-58). In chapter 9, Jesus gave sight to the blind man and then discoursed on the good shepherd (10:1-18). Now in chapter 11, the discourse comes first — Martha and Jesus exchange words about resurrection and life (11:21-27) — and then the sign follows (11:38-44). The pattern is inverted: the theological declaration ("I am the resurrection and the life") precedes and governs the interpretation of the act. The reader is told what the sign means before the sign is performed.

The Lazarus Narrative in Israel's Scriptural Tradition

Before proceeding to the text, several scriptural precedents must be established, because the author's first-century audience would have heard them resonating throughout the chapter.

1. Elijah raises the son of the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:17-24). The child became ill and "there was no breath left in him" (17:17). The widow accused the prophet: "What do I have to do with you, O man of God? You have come to me to bring my iniquity to remembrance and to put my son to death!" (17:18). Elijah stretched himself upon the child three times and cried to the LORD, "O LORD my God, I pray you, let this child's life return to him" (17:21). The LORD heard, and "the life of the child returned to him and he revived" (17:22). The widow responded: "Now I know that you are a man of God and that the word of the LORD in your mouth is truth" (17:24). The pattern is: death of a loved one, an accusation or reproach directed at the prophet, the prophet's prayer, the raising, and a confession of the prophet's identity. John 11 follows this pattern precisely: Lazarus dies, Martha reproaches Jesus ("if you had been here, my brother would not have died"), Jesus prays to the Father, Lazarus is raised, and the witnesses respond.

2. Elisha raises the son of the Shunammite woman (2 Kings 4:18-37). The child died, and the mother went to the prophet at Mount Carmel and said, "Did I ask for a son from my lord? Did I not say, 'Do not deceive me'?" (4:28). Elisha came to the house, found the child dead, shut the door on the two of them, and prayed to the LORD (4:33). He stretched himself upon the child, and the child sneezed seven times and opened his eyes (4:35). As with the widow of Zarephath, the elements include: death, the mother's confrontation of the prophet, prayer, and the raising.

3. Ezekiel's valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37:1-14). God set Ezekiel in a valley full of dry bones and asked, "Son of man, can these bones live?" (37:3). God commanded Ezekiel to prophesy to the bones: "Thus says the Lord GOD to these bones: 'Behold, I will cause breath to enter you that you may come to life'" (37:5). The bones came together, sinews and flesh grew, skin covered them, breath entered, and they stood on their feet as a vast army (37:10). God interpreted the vision: "These bones are the whole house of Israel... Behold, I will open your graves and cause you to come up out of your graves, my people; and I will bring you into the land of Israel. Then you will know that I am the LORD" (37:11-13). The parallels with John 11 are striking: the question of whether the dead can live, the command to the dead to come forth, the response of recognition ("you will know that I am the LORD"), and the connection between raising the dead and the identity of the one who raises them.

4. Daniel 12:2. "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake, these to everlasting life, but the others to disgrace and everlasting contempt." Martha's statement in 11:24 — "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" — reflects precisely this expectation of a general resurrection at the end of the age. Jesus does not deny this expectation but redefines it: the resurrection is not only a future event but a present person.

5. Isaiah 25:8 and 26:19. "He will swallow up death for all time, and the Lord GOD will wipe tears from all faces" (25:8). "Your dead will live; their corpses will rise. You who lie in the dust, awake and shout for joy" (26:19). The defeat of death and the raising of the dead are marks of God's own eschatological action. When Jesus raises Lazarus, he performs what Isaiah attributed to God himself.

Verses 1-4: The Setting and the Purpose Statement

"Now a certain man was sick, Lazarus of Bethany, the village of Mary and her sister Martha. It was the Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was sick. So the sisters sent word to him, saying, 'Lord, behold, he whom you love is sick.' But when Jesus heard this, he said, 'This sickness is not to death, but for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified by it.'"

"A certain man was sick, Lazarus of Bethany." The name Lazarus is the Greek form of the Hebrew Eleazar (El'azar), meaning "God has helped" or "God is my help." The name is significant: the man whose name means "God helps" will receive the ultimate divine help — restoration from death. Bethany is identified as "the village of Mary and her sister Martha." This Bethany is near Jerusalem (11:18, "about fifteen stadia," approximately two miles), distinct from the Bethany beyond the Jordan where John baptized (1:28, 10:40). The proximity to Jerusalem is essential to the narrative: the sign will occur close enough to the center of religious authority that its consequences will be immediate and unavoidable.

"It was the Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair." The author identifies Mary by an event that has not yet been narrated in the document — the anointing will occur in 12:1-8. This anticipatory identification suggests that the audience already knows the story, either from oral tradition or from other accounts (a similar anointing appears in the Synoptic traditions, though the details differ). The author uses the anointing as a marker of identity: this Mary is known for this act. The detail that she wiped Jesus's feet with her hair will be narrated in full in 12:3 and connects to the foot-washing of 13:1-17, where Jesus performs the servant's act for his disciples. The anointing foreshadows death and burial; by introducing it here, the author places the entire Lazarus narrative under the shadow of Jesus's own approaching death.

"Lord, behold, he whom you love is sick" (kyrie, ide hon phileis asthenei). The sisters' message does not contain a request. They report a fact: the one you love is sick. The word for love here is phileo ("to love, to have affection for, to be fond of"), denoting personal friendship and attachment. The sisters assume that this information alone — that the one Jesus loves is in danger — will be sufficient. The message appeals to the relationship, not to Jesus's power. It is a statement of confidence: you love him; therefore you will act. The verb asthenei ("is sick, is weak") comes from asthenes ("without strength"), which in the LXX describes both physical illness and broader human frailty.

"This sickness is not to death, but for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified by it" (haute he astheneia ouk estin pros thanaton all' hyper tes doxes tou theou, hina doxasthe ho huios tou theou di' autes). Jesus's response echoes his statement in 9:3 about the man born blind: the blindness existed "so that the works of God might be displayed in him." Here the sickness exists "for the glory of God." In both cases, human suffering is interpreted not as punishment but as occasion for divine self-revelation.

The phrase "not to death" (ouk estin pros thanaton) is paradoxical: Lazarus will in fact die. The statement is true at a deeper level — the sickness is not aimed at death as its final destination. Death will occur but will not be the outcome. The outcome is glory. The word doxa ("glory") in this document refers to the visible manifestation of God's character and power. It was used in 2:11 for the sign at Cana: "This beginning of his signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee, and manifested his glory" (ephanerosen ten doxan autou). The glory Jesus manifested through the signs is the glory he had with the Father before the world existed (17:5). Each sign is a partial unveiling of that glory; the raising of Lazarus will be the most complete unveiling yet.

The phrase "so that the Son of God may be glorified" (hina doxasthe ho huios tou theou) introduces a double meaning. The Son will be glorified through the raising of Lazarus — the sign will reveal his identity and power. But the Son will also be glorified through his own death, which the raising of Lazarus will precipitate. In this author's usage, "glory" and "glorification" consistently point toward the cross as the supreme revelation of God's character: 12:23 ("The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified"), 12:28 ("Father, glorify your name"), 13:31 ("Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him"), 17:1 ("Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son, that the Son may glorify you"). The glory revealed in raising Lazarus and the glory revealed in Jesus's death are not two different glories but two aspects of the same reality: the life-giving power of the one who lays down his life.

Verses 5-6: The Deliberate Delay

"Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So when he heard that he was sick, he then stayed two days longer in the place where he was."

"Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus" (egapa de ho Iesous ten Marthan kai ten adelphen autes kai ton Lazaron). The verb here is agapao, the other Greek word for love, distinct from the phileo used in the sisters' message (v. 3). Whether the author intends a meaningful distinction between the two verbs is debated; in this document, the two seem largely interchangeable (cf. 21:15-17, where both are used in the same exchange between Jesus and Peter). The statement's function in context is to establish a causal connection — or more precisely, to expose the paradox of what follows. Jesus loves this family. And therefore (oun, "so, therefore, accordingly") he stayed two days longer.

"He then stayed two days longer in the place where he was" (tote men emeinen en ho en topo dyo hemeras). The conjunction oun ("so, therefore") connecting Jesus's love to his delay is one of the most jarring transitions in the document. The reader expects: "Jesus loved them, so he immediately went to them." Instead: "Jesus loved them, so he stayed where he was." The delay is deliberate, not accidental. The author presents it as flowing from Jesus's love, not contradicting it. The two-day delay will mean that by the time Jesus arrives, Lazarus will have been in the tomb four days (v. 17). The four-day detail matters: a widespread first-century Jewish belief held that the soul lingered near the body for three days, hoping to return, but departed permanently on the fourth day when the body began to decompose (this belief is attested in later rabbinic sources, including Genesis Rabbah, though the tradition may reflect first-century popular understanding). The delay ensures that no one can claim Lazarus was merely in a coma, merely sleeping, merely revivable by ordinary means. By the fourth day, decomposition has begun (Martha says so explicitly in v. 39: "Lord, by this time there will be a stench"). The delay ensures that the sign will be unambiguous.

The pattern of deliberate delay is established throughout the document. Jesus delays his departure for Galilee (1:43, 2:1-4, where his mother says "they have no wine" and he responds "my hour has not yet come"). He arrives at the pool of Bethesda long after the paralytic has been waiting (5:5, "thirty-eight years"). He delays his departure for the Feast of Tabernacles (7:6-10, "my time is not yet here"). In each case, the delay is not negligence but sovereign timing — Jesus acts at the moment the Father's purpose requires, not at the moment human urgency demands. The delay is itself part of the sign.

Verses 7-10: The Decision to Return to Judea

"Then after this he said to the disciples, 'Let us go to Judea again.' The disciples said to him, 'Rabbi, the Jews were just now seeking to stone you, and are you going there again?' Jesus answered, 'Are there not twelve hours in the day? If anyone walks in the day, he does not stumble, because he sees the light of this world. But if anyone walks in the night, he stumbles, because the light is not in him.'"

"Let us go to Judea again." After the two-day delay, Jesus initiates the return. The disciples' reaction — "the Jews were just now seeking to stone you" (10:31, 39) — reveals the danger. The word "again" (palin) recalls the violence of the recent visit. The disciples' protest is reasonable by any human calculation: returning to Judea means returning to lethal hostility.

"Are there not twelve hours in the day?" Jesus's response reframes the question. The issue is not safety but timing. The twelve-hour day is a fixed period; the one who walks during it does not stumble because the light is present. The metaphor operates on two levels. On the surface, it addresses the practical concern: while it is "day" — while Jesus's appointed time of ministry has not ended — he cannot be cut short. The hour of his death is determined by the Father, not by the intentions of the authorities. On the deeper level, the light-darkness language continues the sustained theme from chapters 1, 3, 8, and 9. Jesus is the light of the world (8:12, 9:5), and while the light is in the world, those who walk with it do not stumble. The disciples' fear assumes that the darkness can overcome the light; Jesus's answer states that it cannot (cf. 1:5: "the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not overcome it").

"But if anyone walks in the night, he stumbles, because the light is not in him." The warning applies to those who walk without the light — not merely those who walk at nighttime but those who are not oriented toward the light. The phrase "the light is not in him" (to phos ouk estin en auto) distinguishes between the one who carries the light within (who walks without stumbling) and the one who does not (who stumbles). This connects to the judgment language of 3:19-21: those who love darkness rather than light do evil, and those who practice truth come to the light. The disciples are being told: follow me into Judea, because walking with me is walking in the light, regardless of the external danger.

Verses 11-16: "Lazarus Has Fallen Asleep"

"This he said, and after that he said to them, 'Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep; but I go, so that I may awaken him out of sleep.' The disciples then said to him, 'Lord, if he has fallen asleep, he will recover.' Now Jesus had spoken of his death, but they thought that he was speaking of literal sleep. So Jesus then said to them plainly, 'Lazarus is dead, and I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, so that you may believe; but let us go to him.' Therefore Thomas, who is called Didymus, said to his fellow disciples, 'Let us also go, so that we may die with him.'"

"Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep" (Lazaros ho philos hemon kekoimetal). Jesus uses the verb koimaomai ("to fall asleep, to sleep"), a well-attested euphemism for death in both Jewish and Greek usage. In the LXX, the phrase "slept with his fathers" describes the death of kings (1 Kings 2:10, David; 1 Kings 11:43, Solomon; 2 Kings 14:22, Azariah). Daniel 12:2: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake." The metaphor of sleep for death implies the possibility of waking — which is precisely Jesus's point. He calls Lazarus "our friend" (ho philos hemon), using philos, the noun form of the same verb (phileo) the sisters used in their message. The relationship is personal, not professional. Jesus is going to raise a friend, not perform a clinical demonstration.

"I go, so that I may awaken him out of sleep" (poreuomai hina exypniso auton). The verb exypnizo ("to awaken from sleep") appears only here in the New Testament. The prefix ex- ("out of") combined with hypnos ("sleep") creates a vivid image: to pull someone out of sleep. If death is sleep, then what Jesus intends is to wake Lazarus from death. The confidence of the statement is remarkable: not "I will try to wake him" or "perhaps God will wake him through me" but "I go so that I may wake him." The purpose of the journey is stated as a fact.

"Lord, if he has fallen asleep, he will recover." The disciples' misunderstanding is characteristic of this author's technique. Throughout the document, Jesus speaks on one level and his interlocutors hear on another: Nicodemus misunderstands "born again/from above" as re-entering the womb (3:4), the Samaritan woman misunderstands "living water" as flowing well water (4:11-12), the crowd misunderstands "eat my flesh" as literal cannibalism (6:52). The misunderstandings serve a literary function: they force Jesus to make explicit what was implicit, thereby advancing the reader's understanding. Here the disciples hear "sleep" and think of natural rest that will restore health. Their misunderstanding forces Jesus to speak plainly.

"Lazarus is dead" (Lazaros apethanen). The aorist verb apethanen ("he died") is unambiguous. The euphemism is dropped. The plain statement confirms what "sleep" meant: death.

"And I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, so that you may believe" (kai chairo di' hymas, hina pisteusete, hoti ouk emen ekei). Jesus's gladness is not about Lazarus's death but about what the death will make possible. Had Jesus been present, presumably he would have healed Lazarus before death, as the sisters themselves will suggest (vv. 21, 32). A healing would have been a sign, but it would have been the same kind of sign already performed in chapters 4, 5, and 9 — healing the sick. The death of Lazarus creates the occasion for a greater sign: raising the dead. And this greater sign serves a greater purpose: "so that you may believe" (hina pisteusete). The subjunctive pisteusete could be translated "so that you may come to believe" (if they do not yet believe) or "so that you may believe more deeply" (if they already have some level of belief). Given that the disciples have been following Jesus throughout the document, the latter seems more likely: the sign is intended to deepen and solidify their belief, especially in preparation for the crisis that the passion will bring.

"Therefore Thomas, who is called Didymus, said to his fellow disciples, 'Let us also go, so that we may die with him'" (agomen kai hemeis hina apothanomen met' autou). Thomas (Aramaic for "twin"; Didymus is the Greek equivalent) speaks for the first time in the document. His statement is ambiguous: "die with him" could mean "die with Jesus" (who is walking into lethal danger) or "die with Lazarus" (who is already dead). The more natural reading is "die with Jesus" — Thomas recognizes the danger of returning to Judea and expresses a willingness to share Jesus's fate, even if that fate is death. The statement has a quality of resigned courage — not faith that all will be well, but loyalty that does not demand safety. Thomas's willingness to die with Jesus anticipates the language of the good shepherd discourse: the shepherd lays down his life, and the sheep follow. Thomas follows, even into danger. His statement also carries dramatic irony: when the time for dying with Jesus actually arrives, the disciples will scatter (16:32), and Thomas himself will not believe the resurrection until he sees the wounds (20:24-29). The courage expressed here will not hold.

Verses 17-20: Arrival at Bethany

"So when Jesus came, he found that he had already been in the tomb four days. Now Bethany was near Jerusalem, about fifteen stadia off; and many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary, to console them concerning their brother. Martha therefore, when she heard that Jesus was coming, went to meet him, but Mary stayed at the house."

"He had already been in the tomb four days" (tessaras ede hemeras echonta en to mnemelo). The four-day detail is stated with precision. Combined with the two-day delay (v. 6), the travel time from beyond the Jordan to Bethany (approximately a day's journey), and the time for the message to reach Jesus (approximately a day), the chronology suggests that Lazarus may have died around the time the message was sent — or even before it reached Jesus. If so, Jesus's declaration "this sickness is not to death" (v. 4) was spoken when Lazarus was already dead or dying, making it not a prognosis but a prophetic interpretation: death is not the destination; glory is.

The four days also place the raising beyond any natural explanation. As noted above, the popular belief that the soul departed after three days means that at four days, the death is considered final and irreversible by the standards of the time. Martha's statement in verse 39 — "by this time there will be a stench" — confirms that decomposition is expected to have begun. The sign that follows will not be a resuscitation of the recently dead but a reversal of advanced death.

"About fifteen stadia." Approximately 1.7 miles (2.8 kilometers). The proximity to Jerusalem means that many from the city have come to mourn. These mourners will become witnesses to the sign, and their reports will reach the authorities in Jerusalem almost immediately.

"Many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary, to console them." The "Jews" (Ioudaioi) here appear in a neutral or sympathetic role — mourners fulfilling the obligation of consolation. This is one of many instances where the author's use of "the Jews" does not denote hostility but describes the Jewish community of Jerusalem. The practice of communal mourning was well established: visitors would come to the house of the bereaved during the period of sitting shiva (seven days of mourning), to weep with the mourners and offer comfort. The presence of these mourners is essential to the narrative: they provide witnesses for the sign and messengers who will carry the report back to the Pharisees (v. 46).

"Martha therefore, when she heard that Jesus was coming, went to meet him, but Mary stayed at the house." The two sisters respond differently. Martha goes out; Mary stays. The author distinguishes the sisters' temperaments without evaluating either response. Martha's initiative parallels the portrait of her in Luke 10:38-42 as the active, practical one. Mary's remaining at the house is consistent with the custom of mourning — the bereaved sat in the house and received visitors rather than going out. Martha's going out to meet Jesus is an act of initiative that breaks from mourning convention.

Verses 21-27: Martha's Confession

"Martha then said to Jesus, 'Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. Even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you.' Jesus said to her, 'Your brother will rise again.' Martha said to him, 'I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.' Jesus said to her, 'I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in me will live even if he dies, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?' She said to him, 'Yes, Lord; I have believed that you are the Christ, the Son of God, even he who comes into the world.'"

"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died" (kyrie, ei es hode ouk an apethanen ho adelphos mou). Martha's statement is a reproach and a confession simultaneously. The reproach: you were absent, and your absence cost my brother his life. The confession: your presence would have been sufficient to prevent death. She believes Jesus could have healed Lazarus had he been there. The conditional sentence (a contrary-to-fact condition using ei with the imperfect and the aorist with an) implies: "you were not here, and therefore he died." The statement limits Jesus's power to physical proximity — a limitation the sign will demolish. Martha does not yet understand that the one who gives life does not need to be physically present to do so (cf. 4:46-54, where Jesus healed the royal official's son from a distance, saying "Go; your son lives," and the child recovered at the moment Jesus spoke).

"Even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you" (alla kai nyn oida hoti hosa an aitese ton theon dosei soi ho theos). This statement is harder to interpret. It appears to express an open-ended confidence in Jesus's petition: whatever you ask, God will grant. But does Martha mean that Jesus could ask God to raise Lazarus from the dead? Her subsequent response in verse 24 suggests she does not expect an immediate raising — she deflects to the general resurrection at the last day. The statement may express a hopeful but unfocused faith: I know you have a special relationship with God, and I believe God responds to you, but I do not know what to expect in this specific situation. The faith is real but its object is unclear.

"Your brother will rise again" (anastesetai ho adelphos sou). Jesus's response is deliberately ambiguous. The verb anistemi ("to raise up, to rise again") is used in the LXX and in Jewish discourse for both ordinary rising and eschatological resurrection. Martha hears the familiar theological category: the resurrection on the last day.

"I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" (oida hoti anastesetai en te anastasei en te eschate hemera). Martha's response is theologically correct. She holds the Pharisaic belief in a general, future resurrection of the dead (cf. Daniel 12:2; 2 Maccabees 7:9, 14, 23; Acts 23:8, where the Pharisees affirm the resurrection while the Sadducees deny it). Her statement reflects the mainstream of first-century Jewish eschatological expectation: God will raise the dead at the end of the age. She understands resurrection as a doctrine about the future. Jesus is about to redefine it.

"I am the resurrection and the life" (ego eimi he anastasis kai he zoe). This is the fifth "I am" declaration with a predicate in the document (after "bread of life" in 6:35, "light of the world" in 8:12, "the door" in 10:7/9, and "the good shepherd" in 10:11/14). The statement does not deny the future resurrection that Martha confessed. It redefines resurrection from an event to a person. Martha said: "He will rise at the last day" — a statement about a future happening. Jesus says: "I am the resurrection" — a statement about a present reality embodied in a person. The resurrection is not merely something that will happen; it is someone who is here. The future event has entered the present in the person of the one speaking.

The pairing of "the resurrection and the life" (he anastasis kai he zoe) joins two concepts that this document has been developing. Resurrection (anastasis) addresses death — it is the reversal of death, the raising of the dead. Life (zoe) addresses the ongoing condition after death is overcome — not merely resuscitation but the quality of existence that characterizes God himself. In 5:26, "the Father has life in himself, and has granted the Son also to have life in himself." In 1:4, "in him was life, and the life was the light of men." The two terms cover the entire scope of Jesus's claim: he is both the act of overcoming death and the ongoing reality of the life that follows.

"He who believes in me will live even if he dies" (ho pisteuon eis eme kan apotheane zesetai). The first half of the promise addresses physical death. The person who believes in Jesus will die physically (kan apotheane, "even if he dies") but will live (zesetai, future tense). This is the resurrection promise: physical death does not end the life of the one who believes. Lazarus is about to demonstrate this visibly — he died, and he will live again.

"And everyone who lives and believes in me will never die" (kai pas ho zon kai pisteuon eis eme ou me apotheane eis ton aiona). The second half addresses spiritual reality. The one who "lives and believes" — that is, the one who is presently alive in the sense this document gives to "life" (zoe, the life that comes from God, the eternal life of 3:16, 5:24, 6:47) and who is presently believing — will never die (ou me apotheane, using the emphatic double negative). The phrase eis ton aiona ("into the age, forever") intensifies the negation: such a person will not experience the ultimate death, the death that is separation from God. Physical death is acknowledged (the first clause says "even if he dies"); ultimate death is denied (the second clause says "will never die"). The two clauses are not contradictory but complementary: the believer may die physically but will not die ultimately, because the resurrection and the life is not a future event but a present relationship with the person standing before Martha.

The two present participles — "the one living" (ho zon) and "the one believing" (ho pisteuon) — describe ongoing states, consistent with the present-tense pattern throughout the document. The promise is for the one who is living and believing, not for one who once believed and then stopped. This matches the grammar of 3:16 (ho pisteuon, "the one believing"), 6:35 (ho pisteuon eis eme, "the one believing in me"), 6:54 (ho trogon mou ten sarka kai pinon mou to haima, "the one eating my flesh and drinking my blood"), and 10:27 (ta probata ta ema tes phones mou akouousin, "my sheep hear my voice"). The condition described is continuous.

"Do you believe this?" (pisteueis touto;). Jesus asks Martha directly. The question is not rhetorical; it demands a personal response. The theological discussion about resurrection becomes a personal confrontation: do you believe that I am what I have just claimed to be?

"Yes, Lord; I have believed that you are the Christ, the Son of God, even he who comes into the world" (nai, kyrie; ego pepisteuka hoti sy ei ho Christos ho huios tou theou ho eis ton kosmon erchomenos). Martha's confession is the fullest christological declaration in the document thus far, rivaling Peter's confession in the Synoptic accounts (Matthew 16:16, Mark 8:29, Luke 9:20) and paralleling Peter's confession within this document itself (6:68-69: "You are the Holy One of God"). Martha's confession contains three titles:

1. "The Christ" (ho Christos). The Messiah, the anointed one, the king of David's line whom Israel expected (1:41, 4:25-26, 7:26-27, 31, 41-42).

2. "The Son of God" (ho huios tou theou). The title that carries both royal-messianic significance (2 Samuel 7:14, Psalm 2:7) and the deeper meaning this document has been developing: the Son who shares the Father's nature, authority, and life-giving power (5:19-26, 10:30, 36).

3. "He who comes into the world" (ho eis ton kosmon erchomenos). The "coming one" is a messianic designation drawn from Psalm 118:26 (LXX 117:26): "Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the LORD." The phrase "into the world" (eis ton kosmon) connects to the prologue's declaration that the true light "was coming into the world" (1:9) and to Jesus's self-description as the one "whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world" (10:36).

Martha's confession uses the perfect tense pepisteuka ("I have believed and continue to believe"), indicating a settled, established conviction rather than a spontaneous reaction to the present conversation. Her faith was not generated by Jesus's "I am the resurrection" statement; it preceded it. What she expresses is not new belief but existing belief that has now been given a deeper object. She believed Jesus was the Christ; she is now learning what that means.

Verses 28-32: Mary Comes to Jesus

"When she had said this, she went away and called Mary her sister, saying secretly, 'The Teacher is here and is calling for you.' And when she heard it, she got up quickly and was coming to him. Now Jesus had not yet come into the village, but was still in the place where Martha met him. Then the Jews who were with her in the house, and consoling her, when they saw that Mary got up quickly and went out, they followed her, supposing that she was going to the tomb to weep there. Therefore, when Mary came where Jesus was, she saw him, and fell at his feet, saying to him, 'Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.'"

"The Teacher is here and is calling for you" (ho didaskalos parestin kai phonei se). Martha calls Jesus "the Teacher" (ho didaskalos), the same title used by the disciples (1:38, where Rabbi is translated "Teacher," and 13:13-14, "You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right"). The verb phonei ("calls") echoes the shepherd's calling: in 10:3, the shepherd "calls his own sheep by name" (phonei kat' onoma). Mary is called by name, as a sheep is called by the shepherd.

"She got up quickly and was coming to him." Mary's response is immediate. The adverb tachy ("quickly") conveys urgency.

"Now Jesus had not yet come into the village." Jesus remains outside the village at the point where Martha met him. This geographic detail creates a sequence: Martha went out to meet Jesus, returned to call Mary, and Mary now goes out to the same location. Jesus does not come to the house; the sisters come to him. The one who summons must be sought out.

"The Jews who were with her in the house, and consoling her... followed her, supposing that she was going to the tomb to weep there." The mourners' assumption is natural: a bereaved woman leaving the house suddenly would be going to the tomb. Their following ensures that they will be present to witness what happens next. The author carefully positions witnesses for the sign.

"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." Mary repeats Martha's words almost exactly (cf. v. 21). The identical wording suggests either a shared family conviction ("if only he had come sooner") or a set phrase that both sisters had been repeating during the days of mourning. The repetition also invites comparison between the two encounters: Martha's confession led to a theological exchange; Mary's identical words lead in a different direction — to Jesus's emotional response.

Verses 33-37: Jesus's Response to Grief

"When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews who came with her also weeping, he was deeply moved in spirit and was troubled, and said, 'Where have you laid him?' They said to him, 'Lord, come and see.' Jesus wept. So the Jews were saying, 'See how he loved him!' But some of them said, 'Could not this man, who opened the eyes of the blind man, have kept this man also from dying?'"

"He was deeply moved in spirit and was troubled" (enebrimesato to pneumati kai etaraxen heauton). The verb embrimaomai is difficult to translate. In its other uses (Mark 1:43, 14:5; Matthew 9:30), it carries the sense of stern warning or indignation. It is used in the LXX to describe the snorting of horses (a sound associated with anger or agitation). The word does not primarily denote grief or sadness; it denotes agitation, anger, or deep disturbance. Jesus does not merely feel sad; he is stirred to the point of visible turmoil. The question is: at what is this agitation directed?

Several possibilities present themselves from the immediate context. Jesus may be moved by the power of death itself — its hold on the creation, its capacity to break human lives, its domination of the world he came to liberate. He may be responding to the grief of Mary and the mourners — not merely sympathizing with it but being angered by the reality that causes it. He may be responding to the unbelief implied in the mourning: these people weep as though death is final, as though the one who gives life is not standing among them. The text does not specify a single object for the emotion; the breadth of the verb allows all of these to be in play.

"And was troubled" (kai etaraxen heauton). The verb tarasso ("to trouble, agitate, disturb") is used of Jesus in 12:27 ("Now my soul is troubled") and 13:21 ("he was troubled in spirit" when he announced the betrayal). The reflexive heauton ("himself") makes the action deliberate: "he troubled himself." This is consistent with the author's portrait of Jesus as sovereign over his own emotional states, just as he is sovereign over his own death (10:18, "I lay it down on my own initiative"). Even Jesus's grief is a chosen entry into the human condition, not an involuntary reaction.

"Where have you laid him?" The question is not for information — the one who knew Lazarus was dead before the message arrived (v. 4, if the chronology is read this way), who knew Lazarus had "fallen asleep" (v. 11), does not need to ask where the tomb is. The question serves the narrative: it invites the mourners to lead him to the site, making them participants in what is about to happen.

"Jesus wept" (edakrysen ho Iesous). The verb dakryo ("to shed tears") appears only here in the New Testament. It differs from klaio ("to wail, weep loudly"), the verb used for Mary's weeping (v. 33) and the mourners' weeping. Klaio is demonstrative, vocal lamentation; dakryo is the quiet shedding of tears. The shortest verse in many English translations, it is among the most theologically dense. The one who is about to demonstrate absolute authority over death first weeps at its devastation. The one who has declared "I am the resurrection and the life" stands before a tomb and sheds tears. The tears are not evidence that Jesus doubts his power to raise Lazarus; they are evidence that the one who has life in himself enters fully into the grief of those who do not yet see what he sees.

In Israel's scriptures, God's grief over death and suffering is not absent. Isaiah 63:9: "In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them." Judges 10:16: "He could bear the misery of Israel no longer." The God of Israel is not impassive in the face of human suffering. Jesus's tears at the tomb of Lazarus place him in continuity with the God who weeps over the affliction of his people.

"See how he loved him!" The mourners interpret the tears as evidence of love — phileo love, personal attachment, friendship. Their interpretation is correct as far as it goes: Jesus loved Lazarus. But the tears carry more than personal grief; they carry the weight of the one who has life confronting the domain of death.

"Could not this man, who opened the eyes of the blind man, have kept this man also from dying?" Some of the mourners voice the same question Martha and Mary expressed: if Jesus has power (evidenced by the healing of the blind man, chapter 9), why did he not use it in time? The question assumes that prevention would have been better than cure — that healing Lazarus before death would have been preferable to raising him after death. Jesus's deliberate delay (v. 6) has already answered this question from the reader's perspective: the greater sign requires the greater extremity. But the mourners do not know this yet.

Verses 38-44: The Raising of Lazarus

"So Jesus, again being deeply moved within, came to the tomb. Now it was a cave, and a stone was lying against it. Jesus said, 'Remove the stone.' Martha, the sister of the deceased, said to him, 'Lord, by this time there will be a stench, for he has been dead four days.' Jesus said to her, 'Did I not say to you that if you believe, you will see the glory of God?' So they removed the stone. Then Jesus raised his eyes, and said, 'Father, I thank you that you have heard me. I knew that you always hear me; but because of the people standing around I said it, so that they may believe that you sent me.' When he had said these things, he cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come forth.' The man who had died came forth, bound hand and foot with wrappings, and his face was wrapped around with a cloth. Jesus said to them, 'Unbind him, and let him go.'"

"Again being deeply moved within" (palin embrimomenos en heauto). The same intense emotion from verse 33 recurs as Jesus approaches the tomb. The repetition underscores that what Jesus is about to do is not a dispassionate exercise of power but an act performed in the midst of profound emotional engagement.

"Now it was a cave, and a stone was lying against it." The tomb is described as a cave (spelaion) with a stone placed against the entrance, a common first-century Judean burial arrangement. Rock-cut tombs with rolling stones or blocking stones have been extensively documented in the Jerusalem area. The detail establishes the physical reality of the burial: this is a real tomb, with a real stone, containing a real body.

"Remove the stone" (arate ton lithon). Jesus commands the bystanders to remove the stone. He does not remove it himself. The pattern is consistent with the document: human participation is involved in the divine act. In chapter 2, the servants filled the water jars. In chapter 6, the disciples distributed the bread. In chapter 9, the blind man went and washed. Here, the mourners remove the stone. The human action does not produce the miracle, but the miracle incorporates human cooperation.

"Lord, by this time there will be a stench, for he has been dead four days" (kyrie, ede ozei, tetartaios gar estin). Martha's protest is both practical and theological. Practically: the body has been decomposing for four days; opening the tomb will expose the mourners to the odor of decay. Theologically: the four-day detail confirms that Lazarus is beyond the point of natural recovery. Martha, who had just confessed Jesus as "the Christ, the Son of God" (v. 27), now objects to the very action that will fulfill that confession. Her faith, though genuine, encounters a practical limit when confronted with the reality of decomposition. The gap between theological confession and practical trust is one of the most realistic psychological details in the narrative.

"Did I not say to you that if you believe, you will see the glory of God?" Jesus's response recalls verse 4 ("this sickness is not to death, but for the glory of God") and the broader theme of the relationship between believing and seeing in this document. In 2:11, the disciples saw the glory at Cana. In 1:14, the community testified: "We saw his glory." In 12:41, the author will say that Isaiah "saw his glory." The pattern throughout is that the glory of God is visible to those who believe — not that belief produces the glory, but that belief enables the perception of it. Martha is being told: believe, and you will see what God is about to reveal.

"Then Jesus raised his eyes" (eren oun ho Iesous tous ophthalmous ano). The upward gaze is the posture of prayer, directing attention toward God. The gesture is public and visible — the bystanders can see that Jesus is addressing the Father.

"Father, I thank you that you have heard me. I knew that you always hear me; but because of the people standing around I said it, so that they may believe that you sent me." The prayer is remarkable for what it reveals about the relationship between Jesus and the Father. Several elements deserve attention:

1. "I thank you that you have heard me" (eucharisto soi hoti ekousas mou). The aorist ekousas ("you heard") indicates that the hearing has already occurred. Jesus is not making a request; he is thanking the Father for having already heard. This implies that the prayer — whatever its specific content — was offered at some earlier point, perhaps during the two-day delay (v. 6) or in the silent communion between Father and Son that this document presupposes as continuous (5:19-20, 8:29, "He who sent me is with me; he has not left me alone, for I always do the things that are pleasing to him").

2. "I knew that you always hear me" (ego de edein hoti pantote mou akoueis). The statement goes beyond this single occasion to establish a general principle: the Father always hears the Son. The verb akouo carries the same weight it has carried throughout the shepherd discourse — hearing that leads to response. The Father hears the Son as the sheep hear the shepherd's voice. The relationship of hearing is reciprocal: the Son hears the Father (5:30, "I can do nothing on my own initiative; as I hear, I judge"), and the Father hears the Son. The "always" (pantote) excludes the possibility of refusal or inattention. This distinguishes Jesus from every prophet who preceded him: Elijah prayed and God heard (1 Kings 17:22); Elisha prayed and God heard (2 Kings 4:33); but Jesus declares that God always hears him — not as a special grace but as a permanent condition of their relationship.

3. "Because of the people standing around I said it, so that they may believe that you sent me" (dia ton ochlon ton periestota eipon, hina pisteusosin hoti sy me apesteilas). The prayer is spoken aloud for the sake of the witnesses. The purpose is not to inform God but to inform the crowd. They need to understand that what is about to happen comes from the Father, through the Son. The sign is not an independent display of Jesus's power but a work performed by the one the Father sent. The phrase "that you sent me" (hoti sy me apesteilas) is the document's central claim about Jesus's identity: he is the sent one, the apostle of the Father (3:17, 34; 5:36, 38; 6:29, 57; 7:29; 8:42; 10:36). The purpose of the sign is to produce belief in this specific claim: Jesus acts on behalf of the Father who sent him.

"He cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come forth'" (phone megale ekraugasen, Lazare, deuro exo). The command is issued with a "great voice" (phone megale), a loud cry audible to all present. The volume is deliberate — this is a public act, not a whispered incantation. The command to Lazarus echoes 5:25: "Truly, truly, I say to you, an hour is coming and now is, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live." What was announced in chapter 5 as a coming reality is now enacted: a dead man hears the voice of the Son of God and lives. The "hour that is coming and now is" has arrived at this tomb.

The command also echoes 5:28-29: "Do not marvel at this; for an hour is coming, in which all who are in the tombs will hear his voice, and will come forth" (ekporeusontai). The raising of Lazarus is a preview of the general resurrection. One man in one tomb hears one command and comes out; at the last day, all who are in the tombs will hear the same voice and come out. Lazarus's raising is not the final resurrection but a sign pointing to it — a demonstration, in a single instance, of the power that will eventually operate universally.

The fact that Jesus calls Lazarus by name connects to the shepherd discourse: "He calls his own sheep by name" (10:3). The shepherd's voice, heard and recognized, is what brings life. The dead man, who cannot hear naturally, hears the shepherd's call and responds. The power of the voice overcomes death itself.

"The man who had died came forth, bound hand and foot with wrappings, and his face was wrapped around with a cloth" (exelthen ho tethnekos dedemenos tous podas kai tas cheiras keiriais, kai he opsis autou soudario periededeto). The description emphasizes both the reality of the death and the reality of the raising. The man who had died (ho tethnekos, perfect participle, indicating a completed state of death) came forth (exelthen) still bound in burial cloths. The wrappings (keiriai, strips of linen) and the face cloth (soudarion, a cloth for wiping sweat, used to cover the face of the dead) are the physical evidence of death and burial. Lazarus emerges from the tomb in the condition of a corpse — wrapped for burial, bound hand and foot. The raising does not undo the burial procedure; it overcomes the death while leaving the physical evidence of death's reality in place. The wrappings must be removed by human hands; the life was restored by the divine voice.

The face cloth (soudarion) will appear again in 20:7, at the empty tomb of Jesus, where Peter sees "the face-cloth which had been on his head, not lying with the linen wrappings, but rolled up in a place by itself." The contrast is deliberate: Lazarus came out still bound; Jesus's tomb shows the wrappings left behind, neatly arranged. Lazarus was raised to resume mortal life (he would die again); Jesus rose to immortal life and left the trappings of death behind permanently.

"Unbind him, and let him go" (lysate auton kai aphete auton hypagein). Again, Jesus involves human action: the bystanders must unwrap the burial cloths. The raising is Jesus's work; the unbinding is the community's work. The verb lyo ("to loose, unbind, release") is the same verb used in 5:18, where Jesus was accused of "loosing" (breaking) the Sabbath, and in 10:35, where Jesus said "the Scripture cannot be broken" (lythenai). The dead are bound by death; Jesus commands their release. The community participates in the liberation that the shepherd's voice has made possible.

Verses 45-46: The Division Among Witnesses

"Therefore many of the Jews who came to Mary, and saw what he had done, believed in him. But some of them went to the Pharisees and told them the things which Jesus had done."

"Many of the Jews... believed in him." The sign produces belief, fulfilling the purpose stated in verse 15 ("so that you may believe") and verse 42 ("so that they may believe that you sent me"). The witnesses saw (theasamenoi, from theaomai, "to behold, to observe carefully" — this is attentive seeing, not a casual glance) what Jesus had done, and they believed. The sign functions as the author stated in 20:30-31: "These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name." The seventh and greatest sign produces faith in those who see it rightly.

"But some of them went to the Pharisees and told them." Not all who witnessed the sign believed. Some became informants. The same event — the raising of a dead man — produces faith in some and hostility-by-proxy in others. The division is consistent with the pattern throughout the document: the light illuminates some and blinds others (9:39); the shepherd's voice is heard by some and not by others (10:26-27). The sign does not compel universal belief; it reveals the existing orientation of the heart. Those who are disposed toward the light come to it; those who are not carry the report to those who oppose the light.

Verses 47-53: The Council's Decision

"Therefore the chief priests and the Pharisees convened a council, and were saying, 'What are we doing? For this man is performing many signs. If we let him go on like this, all men will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation.' But one of them, Caiaphas, who was high priest that year, said to them, 'You know nothing at all, nor do you take into account that it is expedient for you that one man die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish.' Now he did not say this on his own initiative, but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus was going to die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but in order that he might also gather together into one the children of God who are scattered abroad. So from that day on they planned together to kill him."

"The chief priests and the Pharisees convened a council" (synegagon oun hoi archiereis kai hoi Pharisaioi synedrion). The two groups that have opposed Jesus throughout the document — the chief priests (Sadducean aristocracy associated with the temple) and the Pharisees (Torah-interpreters associated with the synagogue) — now unite in formal deliberation. The word synedrion ("council, Sanhedrin") refers to the supreme judicial body of the Jewish nation. The raising of Lazarus has escalated the confrontation beyond individual encounters and street-level disputes. The authorities now gather in an official capacity.

"What are we doing? For this man is performing many signs." The question is not about the reality of the signs. The council does not deny that Jesus performs signs; they acknowledge it openly ("this man is performing many signs," polla poiei semeia). The issue is not whether the signs are genuine but what consequences they will have. The authorities' problem is not insufficient evidence but an unwillingness to follow the evidence where it leads. Their concern is political, not theological.

"If we let him go on like this, all men will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation." The fear is concrete: popular enthusiasm for Jesus will provoke Roman intervention. "Our place" (hemon ton topon) likely refers to the temple (the "place" par excellence in Jewish usage; cf. Acts 6:13-14, 21:28, where "this place" means the temple, and 2 Maccabees 5:19: "the Lord did not choose the nation for the sake of the place, but the place for the sake of the nation"). "Our nation" (to ethnos) refers to the political entity of Judea under Roman tolerance. The authorities fear that a messianic movement — and the signs are generating exactly the kind of popular excitement that Rome watched for — will trigger a military response. Rome had little patience for messianic agitation in the provinces. The fear is not irrational; the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE proved that Roman patience with Jewish agitation had limits. The council's reasoning is political pragmatism: better to suppress one man than to risk the entire nation.

The irony is profound: the authorities fear losing the temple and the nation. Within a generation of this decision, both will be lost. The temple will be destroyed in 70 CE, and the nation will be devastated. The very catastrophe they sought to prevent by eliminating Jesus will occur regardless — not because they killed Jesus but because they rejected the shepherd God sent and continued on the path that led to confrontation with Rome.

"But one of them, Caiaphas, who was high priest that year." Caiaphas (Joseph bar Caiaphas) served as high priest from approximately 18-36 CE, the longest tenure of any first-century high priest. The phrase "that year" (tou eniautou ekeinou) does not mean Caiaphas was high priest for only one year (the author repeats the phrase in vv. 51 and 18:13); it identifies the specific year — the year in which these events occurred, the year of Jesus's death. The author marks the year by its high priest, as one might mark a year by a consulate or a kingship.

"You know nothing at all" (hymeis ouk oidate ouden). Caiaphas's contempt for his colleagues is blunt. They are deliberating, weighing options, agonizing over strategy. Caiaphas cuts through the discussion with pragmatic decisiveness.

"It is expedient for you that one man die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish" (sympherei hymin hina heis anthropos apotheane hyper tou laou kai me holon to ethnos apoletai). Caiaphas's statement is pure political calculation: it is better (sympherei, "it is advantageous, profitable, expedient") for one man to die than for the whole nation to be destroyed. The logic is utilitarian: sacrifice the individual to save the collective. The preposition hyper ("for, on behalf of") is the same preposition Jesus used in the good shepherd discourse: "I lay down my life for [hyper] the sheep" (10:11, 15) and in the bread of life discourse: "the bread which I will give for [hyper] the life of the world is my flesh" (6:51). Caiaphas speaks the word hyper in the sense of political expediency; the author hears it in the sense of sacrificial substitution. The high priest says more than he knows.

"Now he did not say this on his own initiative, but being high priest that year, he prophesied" (touto de aph' heautou ouk eipen, alla archiereus on tou eniautou ekeinou epropheteuse). The author's editorial comment is extraordinary. Caiaphas did not speak "from himself" (aph' heautou) — the same phrase used in 10:18, where Jesus said "I lay it [my life] down from myself" (ap' emautou). Jesus acts from his own sovereign initiative; Caiaphas speaks from a source beyond himself, unwittingly. The high priest, by virtue of his office (archiereus on, "being high priest"), prophesied. The role of the high priest in Israel included, in certain traditions, an oracular function — the high priest bore the Urim and Thummim (Exodus 28:30) and could inquire of the LORD. The author sees in Caiaphas's cynical political calculation a genuine prophetic utterance: Jesus was going to die for the nation.

"And not for the nation only, but in order that he might also gather together into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" (kai ouch hyper tou ethnous monon, all' hina kai ta tekna tou theou ta dieskorpismena synagage eis hen). The author extends Caiaphas's prophecy far beyond what the high priest intended. Jesus would die not only for the Jewish nation but to gather God's scattered children into unity. Several phrases require attention:

1. "The children of God who are scattered abroad" (ta tekna tou theou ta dieskorpismena). The phrase "children of God" (tekna tou theou) connects to the prologue: "to those who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God" (1:12). The "scattered" children are those who will come to belief but are currently dispersed among the nations — the "other sheep" of 10:16 who are "not of this fold." The verb diaskorpizo ("to scatter, disperse") is used in the LXX for the scattering of Israel among the nations (Deuteronomy 30:3, Ezekiel 34:5-6, Jeremiah 10:21), but here the scope is broader than ethnic Israel. The scattered children include all who will be gathered by the shepherd's voice.

2. "Gather together into one" (synagage eis hen). The verb synago ("to gather together, to assemble") is the root of "synagogue" (synagoge, "assembly, gathering"). The gathering of the scattered is a central promise of the prophets: Ezekiel 34:13, "I will bring them out from the peoples and gather them from the countries"; Isaiah 43:5-6, "I will bring your offspring from the east, and gather you from the west. I will say to the north, 'Give them up!' and to the south, 'Do not hold them back.' Bring my sons from afar and my daughters from the ends of the earth"; Micah 2:12, "I will surely assemble all of you, Jacob, I will surely gather the remnant of Israel." Jesus's death accomplishes the gathering that the prophets promised God would perform. The "one flock, one shepherd" of 10:16 is achieved through the shepherd's death.

The entire passage is the document's clearest statement of the purpose of Jesus's death. The shepherd does not lay down his life merely as an act of love or courage; he dies to gather the scattered children of God into unity. The death is the mechanism of the gathering. How the death accomplishes the gathering is not explained here in abstract terms; the document will develop it through the foot-washing (13:1-17), the farewell discourse (14-17), and the passion narrative (18-19). But the fact is stated: the death is for gathering.

"So from that day on they planned together to kill him" (ap' ekeines oun tes hemeras ebouleusanto hina apokteinosin auton). The verb bouleuo ("to plan, to take counsel, to resolve") indicates a formal decision, not merely hostile intention. The council has resolved on Jesus's death. The supreme demonstration of life-giving power — raising a dead man — has produced the decision to kill the life-giver. The irony is the deepest in the document: the one who gives life to the dead will be put to death by those who fear the consequences of his life-giving. And the death they plan will itself become the means by which life is given to the world.

Verses 54-57: Withdrawal and the Approaching Passover

"Therefore Jesus no longer continued to walk publicly among the Jews, but went away from there to the country near the wilderness, into a city called Ephraim; and there he stayed with the disciples. Now the Passover of the Jews was near, and many went up to Jerusalem out of the country before the Passover to purify themselves. So they were seeking for Jesus, and were saying to one another as they stood in the temple, 'What do you think; that he will not come to the feast at all?' Now the chief priests and the Pharisees had given orders that if anyone knew where he was, he was to report it, so that they might seize him."

"Jesus no longer continued to walk publicly among the Jews." The withdrawal from public activity echoes earlier strategic retreats: 6:15, when the crowd tried to make him king; 7:1, when he remained in Galilee because "the Jews were seeking to kill him"; 10:40, when he withdrew beyond the Jordan. Each retreat is temporary, and each precedes a return at the divinely appointed moment.

"To the country near the wilderness, into a city called Ephraim." Ephraim is identified as near the wilderness (eremos), likely the Judean wilderness northeast of Jerusalem. The name Ephraim carries scriptural associations: Ephraim was the name of one of Joseph's sons (Genesis 41:52) and one of the northern tribes of Israel. Hosea's prophecy addressed Ephraim extensively as a wayward child God nevertheless loved: "How can I give you up, Ephraim? How can I hand you over, Israel?" (Hosea 11:8). Whether the author intends these associations is uncertain, but the withdrawal to a place named Ephraim, near the wilderness, creates a sense of liminal existence — between the public ministry and the final Passover, between the signs and the passion.

"Now the Passover of the Jews was near." This is the third Passover in the document (the first in 2:13, the second in 6:4, and now the third in 11:55). If the unnamed feast of 5:1 was also a Passover, it is the fourth. The Passover — the feast that commemorates God's deliverance of Israel from Egypt through the death of the firstborn and the blood of the lamb — is the festival at which Jesus will die. The author has identified Jesus as "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" (1:29). The lamb of the first Passover was slaughtered so that the firstborn might live; the Lamb of God will be slaughtered so that the scattered children might be gathered and given life.

"Many went up to Jerusalem out of the country before the Passover to purify themselves." Ritual purification before the feast was required for those who had become ceremonially unclean (Numbers 9:6-10 describes provisions for those unable to observe Passover due to corpse contamination). The pilgrims gather in Jerusalem, creating the enormous crowd that will be present for the final events.

"What do you think; that he will not come to the feast at all?" The question among the pilgrims reveals that Jesus's presence is the subject of public speculation. The tension is palpable: will the one who raised the dead come to the feast where the authorities plan to kill him? The question parallels the one in 7:11 at the Feast of Tabernacles: "Where is he?" The crowd senses that the confrontation between Jesus and the authorities has reached a decisive point.

"The chief priests and the Pharisees had given orders that if anyone knew where he was, he was to report it." The authorities have issued a warrant. Information about Jesus's whereabouts is to be reported so that they can arrest him. The shepherd who laid down his life "on his own initiative" (10:18) is now the object of a coordinated search. But the arrest, when it comes, will happen on Jesus's terms, at Jesus's chosen moment, at the Passover feast where the lamb is slain.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1. The chapter is the climactic sign of the document — the seventh and greatest. It demonstrates, in the most extreme possible terms, the claim Jesus has been making since chapter 5: "As the Father raises the dead and gives them life, even so the Son also gives life to whom he wishes" (5:21). The claim has been progressive: healing a paralytic (ch. 5), feeding a multitude (ch. 6), giving sight to the blind (ch. 9), and now raising a man dead four days. Each sign escalates the demonstration. The feeding showed Jesus as the bread of life; the sight-giving showed him as the light of the world; the raising shows him as the resurrection and the life. The signs are not disconnected wonders; they are an escalating argument, each building on the one before.

2. The "I am the resurrection and the life" declaration (v. 25) redefines resurrection from a future event to a present person. Martha's theology was correct but incomplete: she believed in the resurrection on the last day (v. 24). Jesus does not deny the future resurrection but relocates its source: the resurrection is not an impersonal event scheduled for the end of the age; it is a person standing before her. The future tense of eschatological hope is not abolished but grounded in the present reality of relationship with the one who has life in himself (5:26). The last day is not merely something that will come; its power has already arrived in the person of the Son.

3. The deliberate delay (vv. 5-6) demonstrates that Jesus's love operates according to the Father's timing, not human urgency. The author presents the delay as flowing from love, not contradicting it. Human expectation says: if you love, you act immediately. The narrative says: if you love, you act at the moment that will produce the greatest revelation of glory and the deepest foundation for faith. The delay ensures that the sign will be unambiguous — a four-day-dead man raised, not a coma patient revived. The delay is an act of love because it serves the purpose that love intends: "so that you may believe" (v. 15).

4. Martha's confession (v. 27) is the fullest christological declaration in the document up to this point. She confesses Jesus as the Christ, the Son of God, and the one who comes into the world — three titles that encompass the royal-messianic, the divine-filial, and the prophetic dimensions of Jesus's identity. Her confession precedes the sign; she does not need to see the raising in order to confess. Her faith is tested by the reality of decomposition (v. 39) and strengthened by Jesus's reminder (v. 40), but it was established before the sign occurred. The sign confirms her faith; it does not create it.

5. Jesus's prayer at the tomb (vv. 41-42) reveals the nature of his relationship with the Father. The prayer is thanksgiving, not petition — the Father has already heard. The relationship is one of permanent mutual hearing: "I knew that you always hear me." The prayer is spoken aloud not for God's benefit but for the witnesses', so that they may understand the sign as the work of the one the Father sent. The sign is not an autonomous display of Jesus's power; it is the Father's work through the Son, demonstrating the unity of purpose and action that "I and the Father are one" (10:30) declared.

6. The raising of Lazarus directly precipitates the decision to kill Jesus (vv. 47-53). The supreme demonstration of life is met with the decision to impose death. The pattern is the document's central paradox: the life-giver must die so that life can be given on the widest possible scale. The council's political calculation — one man must die for the nation — is reinterpreted by the author as unwitting prophecy: Jesus's death will accomplish the gathering of God's scattered children into unity (v. 52). The death that the authorities intend as the suppression of a threat becomes the mechanism of universal salvation.

7. Caiaphas's prophecy (vv. 49-52) provides the document's clearest statement of the purpose of Jesus's death. The shepherd lays down his life not only for "this fold" (Israel) but for "the children of God who are scattered abroad." The death accomplishes what 10:16 promised: the other sheep, not of this fold, will be brought in, and there will be one flock and one shepherd. The gathering into unity is the purpose; the death is the means. The document does not yet explain the mechanism — how one death gathers many — but it states the fact. The explanation will unfold in the farewell discourse and the passion narrative.

8. The chapter completes the sign-sequence that structures the first half of the document. The document has been organized around seven signs: water to wine (2:1-11), healing the official's son (4:46-54), healing the paralytic (5:1-9), feeding the multitude (6:1-14), walking on the sea (6:16-21), healing the man born blind (9:1-7), and raising Lazarus (11:1-44). The seventh sign is the climax; it demonstrates the fullest extent of Jesus's power — authority over death itself. After this sign, there will be no more signs in the document. The remainder of the narrative (chapters 12-21) will move from signs to the reality to which the signs pointed: Jesus's own death and resurrection. The signs were previews; the cross and the empty tomb are the substance.

9. The connection between life-giving and death-receiving runs through the entire chapter as its structural principle. Jesus gives life to Lazarus, and this act of giving life brings about the decision that Jesus himself must die. The one who has authority to "take up" his life again (10:18) first demonstrates that authority by taking up Lazarus's life. The shepherd who declared his power over death now exercises it, and the exercise provokes the very death he promised to undergo. The chapter thus functions as the hinge between the signs and the passion: it proves that Jesus can give life, and it initiates the sequence of events in which he will lay down his life. The two acts — giving life and laying down life — are not in tension but are the two aspects of a single reality: the shepherd's self-giving love for the flock.

10. The present-tense grammar of believing continues as the chapter's consistent pattern. "The one believing in me will live even if he dies" (ho pisteuon eis eme, v. 25). "Everyone who lives and believes in me will never die" (pas ho zon kai pisteuon eis eme, v. 26). The promise is addressed to the one who is presently believing and living, not to one who believed once in the past. The continuous nature of the relationship — hearing, following, believing, living — remains the condition under which the promises operate, consistent with 3:16, 6:35, 6:54, and 10:27-28.

11. The chapter's emotional depth — Jesus's agitation, his tears, his troubled spirit — reveals that the one who has sovereign authority over death is not indifferent to its devastation. The life-giver weeps at the tomb. The one who will command the dead to rise first enters into the grief of those who mourn. The tears are not weakness but the fullest expression of the shepherd's love: he does not merely rescue from a distance but enters the suffering of the sheep. The sovereignty and the compassion are not opposed but united in the same person. The God of Israel who was "afflicted in all their affliction" (Isaiah 63:9) is present at the tomb of Lazarus, weeping before commanding the dead to live.
