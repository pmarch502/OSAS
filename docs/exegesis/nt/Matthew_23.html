Matthew 23

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 22): The confrontation between Jesus and the religious leadership that began with his entry into Jerusalem (21:1-11) and intensified through the parables of indictment (21:28-46) reached its climax in a series of challenges and counter-challenges. Jesus told the parable of the wedding feast (22:1-14), in which a king invited guests to his son's wedding banquet, the invited guests refused and killed the king's messengers, the king destroyed those murderers and burned their city (22:7), and then sent servants to gather "both bad and good" (22:10) from the streets. A guest without a wedding garment was cast into "the outer darkness" (22:13), and Jesus concluded: "For many are called, but few are chosen" (22:14). The parable extended the vineyard tenants parable of 21:33-46 by moving from the rejection of prophets to the question of who will participate in the kingdom and on what terms. Then came four successive exchanges: the Pharisees and Herodians tested Jesus on paying taxes to Caesar (22:15-22), the Sadducees tested him on the resurrection (22:23-33), a lawyer tested him on the greatest commandment (22:34-40), and Jesus himself posed a question about David's Son and David's Lord from Psalm 110:1 (22:41-46). The result of this final exchange was decisive: "No one was able to answer him a word, nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions" (22:46). The leadership's challenge has been silenced. Every faction — Pharisees, Herodians, Sadducees, lawyers — has had its opportunity and been answered. The initiative now passes entirely to Jesus.

What comes after (Matthew 24): Jesus left the temple, and his disciples came to point out the temple buildings. Jesus responded: "Do you not see all these things? Truly I say to you, not one stone here will be left upon another, which will not be torn down" (24:2). Sitting on the Mount of Olives, facing the temple, the disciples asked privately, "When will these things happen, and what will be the sign of your coming, and of the end of the age?" (24:3). Jesus then delivered the extended discourse on the destruction of the temple, the tribulation that will accompany it, and the coming of the Son of Man — the fifth and final major discourse in the document. The discourse warns of false messiahs (24:4-5), wars and famines (24:6-8), persecution and betrayal (24:9-13), the "abomination of desolation" standing in the holy place (24:15), and the cosmic signs accompanying the Son of Man's coming (24:29-31). The discourse is a direct continuation of the themes of chapter 23: the judgment pronounced on the scribes, Pharisees, and Jerusalem in chapter 23 finds its concrete historical outworking in the destruction prophesied in chapter 24. The lament over Jerusalem that closes chapter 23 ("your house is left to you desolate," 23:38) is the emotional anticipation of the temple's destruction that opens chapter 24 ("not one stone here will be left upon another," 24:2).

How chapter 23 connects them: Chapter 22 ended with the silencing of all challengers (22:46). No one dares to question Jesus further. In chapter 23, Jesus takes the offensive. Having answered every challenge, he now delivers an extended and devastating assessment of the scribes and Pharisees — not in response to any question, but on his own initiative. The chapter functions as the prosecution's closing argument after the cross-examination is complete. It gathers themes that have run throughout the document — hypocrisy (6:2, 5, 16; 15:7; 22:18), the failure of Israel's leadership to produce the fruit God requires (3:8; 7:16-20; 12:33; 21:19, 43), the killing of the prophets (21:35-36), the inversion of external piety and internal reality (15:1-20), the criterion of doing the Father's will versus merely speaking it (7:21-27; 21:28-32) — and concentrates them into a single sustained indictment. The chapter then closes with the lament over Jerusalem (23:37-39), which pivots directly into the temple destruction prophecy of chapter 24. The movement is from the silencing of the challengers (22:46) through the formal indictment of the leadership (23:1-36) to the prophetic verdict on the institution they administer (23:37-39, leading into 24:1-2). The three chapters form a continuous sequence: confrontation, indictment, judgment.

Address to the Crowds and Disciples: The Scribes and Pharisees on Moses's Seat (vv. 1-4)

"Then Jesus spoke to the crowds and to his disciples, saying, 'The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses's seat (epi tes Moseos kathedras ekathisan).'"

The opening word "Then" (tote) connects this speech to what has just occurred: the silencing of all challengers in chapter 22. The audience is specified carefully: "the crowds and his disciples" (tois ochlois kai tois mathetais autou). This is not a private instruction to the twelve, nor is it addressed to the scribes and Pharisees themselves (though they are presumably present, since the speech is delivered in the temple, cf. 24:1). Jesus speaks about the scribes and Pharisees to those who live under their authority — the ordinary people and his own followers.

"Moses's seat" (he Moseos kathedra) refers to the chair of authoritative teaching in the synagogue. Archaeological evidence from first-century synagogues (notably the Chorazin synagogue) confirms the existence of a stone seat, positioned facing the congregation, from which the Torah was read and interpreted. The phrase denotes the scribes' and Pharisees' role as the authoritative interpreters of the Torah of Moses. By saying they "sit on Moses's seat," Jesus acknowledges their institutional position as Torah teachers. They are the recognized authorities through whom Moses's instruction reaches the people.

"'So practice and observe whatever they tell you (panta oun hosa ean eiposin hymin poiesate kai tereite) — but do not do according to their works (kata de ta erga auton me poieite). For they preach but do not practice (legousin gar kai ou poiousin).'"

The instruction is striking in its dual structure. First, Jesus tells the crowds and disciples to do what the scribes and Pharisees say — insofar as they transmit the teaching of Moses, their instruction carries the authority of the seat they occupy. The seat confers legitimacy on the teaching even when the teachers themselves are deficient. This is consistent with the author's presentation of Jesus as one who came "not to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to fulfill" them (5:17). The Torah remains authoritative.

But then comes the sharp distinction: "do not do according to their works." The separation of speech from action is the parable of the two sons (21:28-32) applied to the leadership as a class. The second son said "I go, sir" but did not go. The scribes and Pharisees teach the Torah but do not live it. The verb poieo (to do) appears in both halves of the instruction — "do what they say" (poiesate) but "do not do according to their works" (me poieite) — creating a deliberate wordplay that isolates the gap between their teaching and their conduct. This gap is what the author has consistently called "hypocrisy" (hypokrisis), a word that will appear repeatedly in this chapter (vv. 13, 15, 23, 25, 27, 29).

"'They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear (phortia barea kai dysbastakta), and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger (auto de to daktylo auton ou thelousin kinesai auta).'"

The image is of physical load-bearing. The word phortion (burden, load) appeared at 11:30, where Jesus said, "my burden (phortion) is light." The contrast is pointed: the scribes and Pharisees impose heavy burdens; Jesus offers a light one. The "burdens" are the detailed halakhic requirements — the oral tradition's expansion of Torah obligations into minute prescriptions covering every aspect of daily life. The accusation is not that they teach Torah (Jesus has just told the crowds to observe their teaching) but that they multiply requirements beyond Torah and refuse to help those who struggle under the weight. The phrase "not willing to move them with their finger" (ou thelousin kinesai) indicates not inability but unwillingness. They could provide assistance, interpretation, leniency, practical help — but they choose not to. The burden-imposers do not share the burden. This is the opposite of the servant-leadership Jesus taught at 20:25-28, where greatness is defined by bearing others' burdens rather than adding to them.

The Love of Display: Public Piety as Performance (vv. 5-12)

"'They do all their deeds to be seen by others (pros to theathenai tois anthropois). For they make their phylacteries broad (platynousin gar ta phylakteria auton) and their fringes long (megalynousin ta kraspeda).'"

The phrase "to be seen by others" (pros to theathenai tois anthropois) uses the same verb (theaomai, to behold, to view) that appeared in 6:1, where Jesus warned the disciples: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before people to be seen by them (pros to theathenai autois)." The Sermon on the Mount's warning is now applied as a diagnosis. What Jesus warned his followers against is precisely what the scribes and Pharisees practice. The verb theathenai carries theatrical connotations — from it comes the word theatron (theater). Their piety is a performance staged for an audience.

The phylacteries (phylakteria, also called tefillin) are small leather boxes containing scripture passages (Exodus 13:1-10, 13:11-16; Deuteronomy 6:4-9, 11:13-21), worn on the forehead and left arm during prayer in response to the command of Deuteronomy 6:8: "You shall bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes." The scribes and Pharisees "make them broad" (platynousin) — larger and more conspicuous than necessary, so that their devotion is visible. The fringes (kraspeda, also called tzitzit) are the tassels commanded by Numbers 15:38-39: "Speak to the people of Israel, and tell them to make tassels on the corners of their garments throughout their generations." The purpose of the fringes, according to Numbers 15:39, is "to look at and remember all the commandments of the LORD, to do them." The scribes and Pharisees "make them long" (megalynousin — literally, "make them great") — again, enlarging the visible marker of piety. In both cases, items intended as personal reminders of obedience have been converted into public displays of status.

Jesus wore fringes himself — the woman with the hemorrhage touched "the fringe (kraspedou) of his garment" (9:20, 14:36). The issue is not the wearing of fringes or phylacteries but the enlargement of them for purposes of display. The external marker of faithfulness has been inflated into a badge of superiority.

"'They love the place of honor at feasts (protoklisian en tois deipnois) and the best seats in the synagogues (protokathedrias en tais synagogais) and greetings in the marketplaces (aspas-mous en tais agorais) and being called rabbi by others (kaleisthai hypo ton anthropon, Rabbi).'"

Four specific loves are listed, and the progression moves from formal settings to casual ones. The "place of honor at feasts" (protoklisia) is the reclining position closest to the host at a banquet — the position of highest social rank. The "best seats in the synagogues" (protokathedria) are the seats facing the congregation, near the Torah ark, where the most prominent members sat and were visible to all. The "greetings in the marketplaces" (agorais) are the public acknowledgments of status — the respectful salutations that confirm one's standing in the community. And "being called Rabbi" (Rabbi — from the Hebrew rav, great, meaning "my great one" or "my master") is the title of honor that formally recognizes teaching authority. Each of these loves is oriented toward visibility and recognition — they want to be seen, positioned, saluted, and titled. The pattern is consistent: external markers of religious authority are pursued for the social honor they confer.

"'But you are not to be called rabbi (hymeis de me klethete, Rabbi), for you have one teacher (heis gar estin hymon ho didaskalos), and you are all brothers (pantes de hymeis adelphoi este). And call no man your father on earth (patera me kalesete hymon epi tes ges), for you have one Father, who is in heaven (heis gar estin hymon ho pater ho ouranios). Neither be called instructors (mede klethete kathegetai), for you have one instructor, the Christ (heis gar hymon estin ho kathegetes, ho Christos).'"

Jesus now turns from description of the scribes and Pharisees to direct instruction to his own community. Three titles are prohibited: "rabbi" (teacher), "father" (pater), and "instructor" (kathegetes). In each case, the prohibition is grounded in the same logic: the community has one who uniquely holds that role, and human claims to the title create hierarchies that replicate the very pattern Jesus is denouncing.

The word didaskalos (teacher) is the Greek equivalent of rabbi. Jesus has been called didaskalos throughout the document (8:19, 9:11, 12:38, 17:24, 19:16, 22:16, 24, 36). The prohibition is not against the function of teaching (the author will present teaching as a commission in 28:20, "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you") but against the assumption of the title as a marker of hierarchical authority that elevates the teacher above the community. "You are all brothers" (pantes de hymeis adelphoi este) is the positive statement: the community is a fraternity of equals under one teacher. No member holds a rank that elevates him above another.

"Father" (pater) as a religious title was used for revered teachers and patriarchal figures. The Talmud records that prominent rabbis were addressed as "Abba" (father) by their students. Jesus prohibits this because "you have one Father, who is in heaven" (heis gar estin hymon ho pater ho ouranios). The "Father in heaven" (ho pater ho ouranios) is the author's characteristic designation for God — it has appeared at 5:16, 45, 48; 6:1, 9, 14, 26, 32; 7:11, 21; 10:32-33; 12:50; 15:13; 16:17; 18:10, 14, 19, 35. To call a human being "father" in the sense of ultimate religious authority is to assign to a person what belongs to God alone.

"Instructor" (kathegetes — literally, one who leads the way, a guide) is a rarer word. The prohibition is that the community has one kathegetes, "the Christ" (ho Christos). This is one of the few places in the document where Jesus uses the title "Christ" in reference to himself in his own speech rather than receiving it from others (cf. 16:16, 20). The title here designates the one who has the exclusive right to guide and direct the community's understanding.

The threefold prohibition establishes a community structure that is radically flat. No human teacher, no patriarchal figure, no authoritative guide holds a position that places him above the rest. The community has one teacher (Christ), one father (God), and is composed entirely of siblings (adelphoi). This is the direct antithesis of the scribes' and Pharisees' love of titles and positions.

"'The greatest among you shall be your servant (ho de meizon hymon estai hymon diakonos). Whoever exalts himself will be humbled (hostis de hypsose heauton tapeinothesetai), and whoever humbles himself will be exalted (kai hostis tapeinose heauton hypsothestai).'"

This restates the servant-leadership teaching of 20:26-27 ("whoever would be great among you must be your servant") and connects it to the reversal principle that pervades the document. The language of exaltation and humbling (hypsoo / tapeinoo) draws on the Magnificat tradition in Luke (1:52, "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate"), but the author has established this principle independently: the first will be last and the last first (19:30, 20:16); the kingdom belongs to the childlike (18:1-4, 19:14); the tax collectors and prostitutes enter the kingdom before the religious leaders (21:31). The greatest is the servant — not the one with the largest phylacteries, the best seat, or the most impressive title. The verb tapeinoo (to humble, to bring low) is the verb from which tapeinos (lowly) derives — the word Jesus used to describe himself at 11:29: "I am gentle (praus) and lowly (tapeinos) in heart."

The Seven Woes: A Sustained Indictment (vv. 13-36)

The section that follows consists of seven pronouncements, each beginning with "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites" (ouai hymin, grammateis kai Pharisaioi, hypokritai). The "woe" (ouai) is a form with deep roots in Israel's prophetic tradition. Isaiah 5:8-23 delivers a series of woes against those who join house to house, those who rise early to run after strong drink, those who call evil good and good evil, those who are wise in their own eyes. Habakkuk 2:6-19 contains five woes against the unjust oppressor. Amos 5:18 and 6:1 pronounce woes against those who desire the day of the LORD and those who are at ease in Zion. The woe oracle is not a wish for harm but a prophetic announcement of impending disaster. It declares that those addressed are in a state of grave peril because of their conduct. Jesus is speaking as a prophet in the tradition of Isaiah, Amos, and Habakkuk — and his woes, like theirs, are directed at the leadership of God's people.

The word hypokrites (hypocrite, actor, one who plays a part) has appeared at 6:2, 5, 16 (those who practice piety for display); 7:5 (the one who sees the speck in another's eye but not the log in his own); 15:7 (quoting Isaiah 29:13, "this people honors me with their lips but their heart is far from me"); and 22:18 (Jesus perceiving the Pharisees' malice, "why do you test me, hypocrites?"). The word denotes a fundamental disconnect between external appearance and internal reality — between what is presented and what is.

First Woe: Shutting the Kingdom (v. 13)

"'But woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you shut the kingdom of heaven in people's faces (kleiete ten basileian ton ouranon emprosthen ton anthropon). For you neither enter yourselves nor allow those who would enter to go in (hymeis gar ouk eiserchesthe, oude tous eiserchomenous aphiete eiselthein).'"

The first woe concerns the kingdom of heaven (he basileia ton ouranon), the document's central subject since 3:2 ("Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand") and 4:17 (Jesus's identical announcement). The scribes and Pharisees "shut" (kleiete, from kleio, to close, to lock) the kingdom in people's faces. The accusation is twofold: they do not enter the kingdom themselves, and they prevent others from entering. The image is of a gatekeeper who bars the door — the very people who should be opening the way to God's reign are blocking access to it. This connects to the key of knowledge: in the parallel passage in Luke 11:52, Jesus says, "You have taken away the key of knowledge; you did not enter yourselves, and you hindered those who were entering." The scribes' function was to open the scriptures to the people; instead, their interpretation has become an obstacle.

The phrase "those who are entering" (tous eiserchomenous — present participle, indicating people in the process of entering) is significant. There are people who would enter the kingdom, who are on their way in, and the scribes and Pharisees block them. This is the sin of the gatekeepers: not merely personal failure but the active obstruction of others. This recalls the condemnation at 18:6-7, where causing "one of these little ones who believe in me to stumble" warrants a millstone around the neck and drowning. The scribes and Pharisees are stumbling-blocks on a national scale.

Second Woe: Making Converts Worse (v. 15)

"'Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte (poiesai hena proselyten), and when he becomes a proselyte, you make him twice as much a child of hell as yourselves (poieite auton huion geennes diplouton hymon).'"

The Pharisees were known for missionary activity — Josephus notes their influence and their desire to bring people under the Torah's authority (Antiquities 13.288, 20.38-48). The effort described — crossing sea and land for a single convert — is extraordinary. The zeal is not in question; the result is. The proselyte (proselytos — a Gentile convert to Judaism) becomes "twice as much a child of Gehenna" (huion geennes diploun) as the Pharisees themselves. The phrase "son of Gehenna" (huios geennes) is a Semitic idiom meaning one who belongs to Gehenna, one whose destiny is Gehenna — the valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, which served as the image of divine judgment (see 5:22, 29-30; 10:28; 18:9). The convert, having been shaped by the Pharisees' distorted version of faithfulness, is worse off than before because he now has all the apparatus of religion without the substance — he has been made into a more zealous practitioner of the very hypocrisy that characterizes his teachers. Zeal without right orientation compounds the problem rather than solving it.

Third Woe: Swearing by the Temple and the Altar (vv. 16-22)

"'Woe to you, blind guides (hodegoi typhloi), who say, "If anyone swears by the temple (naos), it is nothing, but if anyone swears by the gold of the temple (chryso tou naou), he is bound by his oath." You blind fools (moroi kai typhloi)! For which is greater, the gold or the temple that has made the gold sacred (ho naos ho hagiasas ton chryon)?'"

The address shifts from "hypocrites" to "blind guides" (hodegoi typhloi). The metaphor of blindness for failed leadership appeared at 15:14: "They are blind guides of the blind (typhloi eisin hodegoi typhlon). And if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit." The blindness is spiritual — inability to discern what matters, to see the relative weight of things.

The issue addressed is the casuistry of oath-taking. The scribes had developed elaborate distinctions about which oaths were binding and which were not. Swearing by the temple itself (naos — the inner sanctuary, the sacred structure) was considered non-binding, but swearing by the gold of the temple (the gold decorations, the treasury) was binding. Similarly, swearing by the altar was non-binding, but swearing by the gift on the altar was binding. These distinctions created a system in which oaths could be taken with hidden escape clauses — one could appear to swear solemnly while knowing the oath was technically void.

Jesus dismantles this system by pointing to the logical absurdity. The temple sanctifies the gold, not the reverse. The altar sanctifies the gift, not the reverse. The container is greater than the contained. To say that an oath by the gold is binding but an oath by the temple is not reverses the hierarchy of holiness. The argument proceeds by strict logic: "which is greater?" (ti gar meizon). The word meizon (greater) is the comparative of megas — the same word used in the servant-greatness teaching (20:26, 23:11). The scribes cannot even discern which is greater in their own temple.

"'And whoever swears by the altar swears by it and by everything on it. And whoever swears by the temple swears by it and by him who dwells in it (to katoikounti auton). And whoever swears by heaven swears by the throne of God and by him who sits upon it.'"

Jesus's conclusion eliminates the possibility of a non-binding oath. Every oath, properly understood, invokes God — because the temple is God's dwelling, heaven is God's throne (5:34, "do not swear by heaven, for it is the throne of God"), and every sacred object derives its sanctity from the one who inhabits the sacred space. The casuistry that distinguished binding from non-binding oaths was a mechanism for evading truthfulness. This connects directly to 5:33-37, where Jesus taught, "Do not swear at all... Let your 'yes' be 'yes' and your 'no' be 'no'; anything more than this comes from the evil one." The oath-casuistry of the scribes is precisely the "anything more" that Jesus condemned in the Sermon on the Mount — an elaborate system that makes truthfulness optional rather than absolute.

Fourth Woe: Tithing Minutiae While Neglecting Justice (v. 23-24)

"'Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you tithe mint and dill and cumin (apodekatoute to hedyosmon kai to anethon kai to kyminon), and have neglected the weightier matters of the law (aphekate ta barytera tou nomou): justice and mercy and faithfulness (ten krisin kai to eleos kai ten pistin). These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others.'"

The tithe (apodekatoo, to give a tenth) was required by Torah on agricultural produce (Leviticus 27:30, Deuteronomy 14:22-23). The Pharisaic tradition extended the tithing requirement to the smallest garden herbs — mint (hedyosmon), dill (anethon), and cumin (kyminon) — going beyond the letter of the Torah to ensure complete compliance even on items of trivial value. Jesus does not condemn the practice ("these you ought to have done") but condemns the prioritization. The herbs are tithed while "the weightier matters of the law" (ta barytera tou nomou) are neglected.

The word barytera (weightier, more important) is the comparative of barys (heavy). The rabbis themselves distinguished between "light" (kal) and "heavy" (chamer) commandments — the principle that some commandments carry more weight than others was not foreign to Pharisaic thought (cf. the lawyer's question at 22:36, "which is the great commandment in the Law?"). Jesus identifies three "weightier matters": justice (krisis), mercy (eleos), and faithfulness (pistis).

These three terms resonate deeply within the prophetic tradition. Justice (krisis) — the rendering of right judgment, the protection of the vulnerable, the establishment of equity — is the demand of Amos 5:24 ("let justice roll down like waters"), Isaiah 1:17 ("seek justice, correct oppression"), and Micah 6:8. Mercy (eleos) is the word from Hosea 6:6, which the author has already cited twice at pivotal moments: 9:13 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice") and 12:7 (the same quotation in the grain-field controversy). Faithfulness (pistis) — reliability, trustworthiness, fidelity — corresponds to the Hebrew emunah, which Habakkuk 2:4 declares is the condition of life: "the righteous shall live by his faithfulness." The triad of justice, mercy, and faithfulness echoes Micah 6:8: "What does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" The scribes have perfected the periphery and abandoned the center.

"'You blind guides, straining out a gnat (diylizentes ton konopa) and swallowing a camel (ten de kamelon katapinontes)!'"

The metaphor is vivid and may involve a wordplay in Aramaic: the Aramaic word for gnat is galma, and the word for camel is gamla — the two are nearly identical in sound. The image is of someone who carefully strains his drink through a cloth to avoid ingesting an unclean insect (gnats were unclean under Leviticus 11:20-23) while swallowing whole a camel, the largest unclean animal in Palestine (Leviticus 11:4). The absurdity makes the point: meticulous attention to trivial purity regulations combined with total disregard for the massive matters of justice, mercy, and faithfulness. The gnat and the camel are both unclean, but the disproportion between what is strained out and what is gulped down exposes the distortion of priorities.

Fifth Woe: Clean Outside, Foul Inside (vv. 25-26)

"'Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you clean the outside of the cup and the plate (to exothen tou poteriou kai tes paropsidos), but inside they are full of greed and self-indulgence (esothen de gemousin ex harpages kai akrasias). You blind Pharisee! First clean the inside of the cup and the plate (katharison proton to entos tou poteriou), that the outside also may be clean (hina genetai kai to ektos autou katharon).'"

The cup (poterion) and plate (paropsis) are everyday domestic items, but they function here as metaphors for the person. The Pharisaic purity regulations included detailed instructions for the cleaning of vessels (cf. Mark 7:3-4). The charge is that they attend to the outside (to exothen) while the inside (esothen) is filled with greed (harpage — rapacity, plunder, the seizure of what belongs to others) and self-indulgence (akrasia — lack of self-control, intemperance). The outside/inside distinction was the subject of Jesus's extended argument at 15:1-20, where he taught that "what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and this defiles a person" (15:18). The principle established at 15:1-20 — that defilement is internal, not external — is now applied as a specific diagnosis of the Pharisees' condition. Their external purity is impeccable; their internal reality is rapacious.

The remedy Jesus prescribes is to "first clean the inside" (katharison proton to entos). The word proton (first) indicates priority of sequence and importance. If the inside is clean, the outside will follow. The reverse is not true: cleaning the outside does nothing for the inside. This is the exact principle of 15:18-20, now stated as a direct imperative.

Sixth Woe: Whitewashed Tombs (vv. 27-28)

"'Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs (taphois kekoniamenois), which outwardly appear beautiful (exothen men phainontai horaioi), but within are full of dead people's bones and all uncleanness (esothen de gemousin osteon nekron kai pases akatharsias). So you also outwardly appear righteous to others (houtos kai hymeis exothen men phainesthe tois anthropois dikaioi), but within you are full of hypocrisy and lawlessness (esothen de este mestoi hypokriseos kai anomias).'"

It was customary to whitewash tombs before Passover so that pilgrims, who would be rendered unclean by contact with a tomb (Numbers 19:16, "whoever in the open field touches someone who was killed with a sword or who died naturally, or touches a human bone or a grave, shall be unclean seven days"), could identify and avoid them. The whitewash made the tombs visible and even attractive in appearance. But the contents remained: bones and uncleanness (akatharsias). The metaphor extends the outside/inside contrast of the previous woe into a more extreme register. A cup with greed inside is unattractive; a tomb with corpses inside is a source of ritual contamination. The scribes and Pharisees do not merely have a dirty interior — they are ritually defiling. Contact with them, despite their beautiful exterior, makes others unclean.

The pairing of "hypocrisy and lawlessness" (hypokriseos kai anomias) at the end is telling. Anomia (lawlessness) is a startling accusation against the very people who claim to be the most rigorous keepers of the law. The word appeared at 7:23, where Jesus declared, "Depart from me, you workers of lawlessness (hoi ergazomenoi ten anomian)" — addressed to those who prophesied in his name, cast out demons, and did many mighty works, but whom he never knew. At 13:41, the Son of Man will send his angels to gather "all causes of sin and all workers of lawlessness" (pantas tous poiountas ten anomian) out of his kingdom. At 24:12, "because lawlessness will be increased, the love of many will grow cold." The scribes and Pharisees, for all their outward rigor, are lawless — they violate the substance of God's law (justice, mercy, faithfulness) while meticulously performing its externals. Their lawlessness is the more dangerous because it is disguised as its opposite.

Seventh Woe: Building Tombs for the Prophets (vv. 29-36)

"'Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you build the tombs of the prophets (oikodomeite tous taphous ton propheton) and decorate the monuments of the righteous (kosmeite ta mnemia ton dikaion), saying, "If we had lived in the days of our fathers, we would not have taken part with them in shedding the blood of the prophets (ouk an emetha autois koinonoi en to haimati ton propheton)." Thus you witness against yourselves (hoste martyreite heautois) that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets (hoti huioi este ton phoneusanton tous prophetas).'"

The seventh and final woe is the longest and most consequential. It addresses the scribes' and Pharisees' relationship to Israel's history of prophet-killing. They build and beautify the tombs of the prophets — an act of apparent reverence. They declare that they would not have participated in the murders their ancestors committed. But Jesus turns this declaration into self-indictment. By saying "our fathers" (hoi pateres hemon) killed the prophets, they acknowledge their lineage: "you witness against yourselves that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets." The sonship is not merely genealogical; in Semitic idiom, to be a "son of" something is to share its character (cf. "sons of the kingdom," 8:12, 13:38; "sons of the evil one," 13:38; "son of Gehenna," 23:15; "sons of the bridegroom," 9:15). They are the offspring of prophet-murderers, and as the narrative will demonstrate, they will do what their fathers did — they will kill the one standing before them.

The claim "we would not have participated" is exposed as false by the very plot already in motion. At 21:46 and 22:15, the chief priests and Pharisees were already seeking to seize and destroy Jesus. They are at this moment planning the death of the one who stands in the prophetic succession of those whose tombs they adorn. The tomb-building is itself an act of unwitting self-exposure: they honor dead prophets while preparing to kill the living one.

"'Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers (plerosate to metron ton pateron hymon).'"

The imperative "fill up" (plerosate) is a prophetic command of grim permission: complete what your fathers started. The "measure" (metron) is the full account of sin that, when complete, triggers judgment. The concept appears in Genesis 15:16, where God tells Abraham, "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" — divine judgment waits until the measure of sin is full. The same concept operates in 1 Thessalonians 2:16, where Paul says of those who hinder the gospel, they are "always filling up the measure of their sins; but wrath has come upon them at last." Jesus tells the scribes and Pharisees to complete the pattern their ancestors began. The killing of Jesus and the persecution of his messengers (vv. 34-36) will be the act that fills the measure.

"'You serpents, you brood of vipers (opheis, gennemata echidnon)! How are you to escape being sentenced to Gehenna (pos phygete apo tes kriseos tes geennes)?'"

The phrase "brood of vipers" (gennemata echidnon) was first used in this document by John the Baptist, addressing the Pharisees and Sadducees who came to his baptism: "You brood of vipers! Who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (3:7). At 12:34, Jesus used the identical phrase against the Pharisees who accused him of casting out demons by Beelzebul: "You brood of vipers! How can you speak good, when you are evil?" The threefold use (3:7, 12:34, 23:33) of this precise phrase tracks the escalation of the confrontation with the Pharisees across the entire document: John warned them, Jesus diagnosed them, and now, in his final public address, he pronounces the verdict. The question "how are you to escape being sentenced to Gehenna?" (pos phygete — how will you flee?) echoes John's "who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (3:7). The answer, implicitly, is that they will not escape.

"'Therefore I send you prophets and wise men and scribes (dio idou ego apostello pros hymas prophetas kai sophous kai grammateis), some of whom you will kill and crucify, and some you will flog in your synagogues and persecute from town to town (dioxete apo poleos eis polin).'"

The "therefore" (dio) connects what follows to what precedes: because the pattern of prophet-murder continues, Jesus will send more messengers, and the pattern will be repeated. The statement "I send" (ego apostello) is striking. In the parallel in Luke 11:49, it is "the Wisdom of God" who says, "I will send them prophets and apostles." Here, Jesus himself is the sender. The ego is emphatic: it is Jesus who dispatches these prophets, wise men, and scribes — a claim that places him in the position of divine authority. The language of sending (apostello) is the verb from which "apostle" (apostolos) derives.

The triad — "prophets and wise men and scribes" (prophetas kai sophous kai grammateis) — uses categories recognizable within Judaism. These are not outsiders imposing foreign ideas; they are Israel's own categories of spiritual leadership. The messengers Jesus sends will be killed, crucified, flogged in synagogues, and persecuted from city to city. This is a preview of what the author's community will experience: the book of Acts records the stoning of Stephen (Acts 7:58-60), the persecution by Saul (Acts 8:1-3), and the scattering of the community from Jerusalem. The future tense ("you will kill," "you will flog") turns the indictment into prophecy. The pattern of the fathers will be repeated by the sons.

"'So that on you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth (hopos elthe eph' hymas pan haima dikaion ekchynnomenon epi tes ges), from the blood of righteous Abel (apo tou haimatos Habel tou dikaiou) to the blood of Zechariah the son of Barachiah (heos tou haimatos Zachariou huiou Barachiou), whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar (hon ephoneusate metaxy tou naou kai tou thysiasteriou).'"

The phrase "so that" (hopos) indicates purpose or result: the consequence of filling up the measure of the fathers is that the accumulated guilt of all righteous blood falls upon this generation. The span "from Abel to Zechariah" is not random. Abel is the first murder victim in the Hebrew scriptures (Genesis 4:8). Zechariah the son of Barachiah is most likely a reference to Zechariah the son of Jehoiada (2 Chronicles 24:20-22), who was stoned in the court of the temple by order of King Joash after he prophesied against the king: "Thus God said, 'Why do you transgress the commandments of the LORD, so that you cannot prosper? Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you.'" As he died, Zechariah said, "May the LORD see and avenge!" (2 Chronicles 24:22). In the Hebrew Bible, 2 Chronicles is the last book (the Hebrew canon ends with Chronicles, not Malachi). The span from Abel (Genesis, the first book) to Zechariah (2 Chronicles, the last book) therefore encompasses the entire sweep of righteous blood in the Hebrew scriptures — from beginning to end.

The identification "son of Barachiah" (huiou Barachiou) presents a difficulty, since the Zechariah murdered in the temple was the son of Jehoiada, not Barachiah. The prophet Zechariah (the author of the biblical book of Zechariah) was the son of Berechiah (Zechariah 1:1). The author may be conflating the two figures, or there may be a textual tradition that identified the martyred Zechariah differently. What is certain from the text is that the author intends the Zechariah who was killed "between the sanctuary and the altar" — a detail that matches 2 Chronicles 24:21, where Zechariah was stoned "in the court of the house of the LORD."

The verb "murdered" (ephoneusate) is in the second person plural: "whom you murdered." This generation is held accountable not only for its own crimes but for the entire history of prophet-killing, because it is this generation that fills the measure full. The logic is corporate and cumulative: the guilt builds across generations and comes to a head in the generation that consummates the pattern by killing the final messenger — and the one who sent him.

"'Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation (hēxei tauta panta epi tēn genean tautēn).'"

The phrase "this generation" (he genea haute) has appeared throughout the document as a term of indictment. At 11:16, "to what shall I compare this generation?" At 12:39, "an evil and adulterous generation seeks a sign." At 12:41-42, the Ninevites and the Queen of the South will rise up in judgment "with this generation" and condemn it. At 12:45, "so also will it be with this evil generation." At 16:4, "an evil and adulterous generation seeks a sign." At 17:17, "O faithless and twisted generation." The phrase is not a neutral chronological marker; it designates the specific generation of Israelites who have witnessed Jesus's ministry, heard his teaching, seen his works, and refused to respond. The prediction "all these things will come upon this generation" will find its concrete fulfillment within the narrative of chapter 24, where the destruction of the temple is prophesied, and historically in the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE.

The Lament over Jerusalem (vv. 37-39)

"'Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her (he apokteinousa tous prophetas kai lithobolousa tous apestalmenous pros auten)! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings (hon tropon ornis episynagei ta nossia autes hypo tas pterygas), and you were not willing (kai ouk ethelesate)!'"

The double address "Jerusalem, Jerusalem" (Ierousalem, Ierousalem) follows a pattern of solemn prophetic address: "Martha, Martha" (Luke 10:41), "Simon, Simon" (Luke 22:31), "Saul, Saul" (Acts 9:4). The repetition conveys deep emotion — grief, urgency, tenderness. Jesus is not addressing the city's buildings but its people, its leadership, its corporate identity as the center of Israel's religious life. The characterization — "the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her" — is a present-tense description. Jerusalem's prophet-killing is not past but characteristic; it is what Jerusalem does.

The image of the hen (ornis) gathering her chicks (nossia) under her wings (pterygas) draws on a rich scriptural tradition of God's protective wing. Psalm 17:8: "Keep me as the apple of your eye; hide me in the shadow of your wings." Psalm 36:7: "How precious is your steadfast love, O God! The children of mankind take refuge in the shadow of your wings." Psalm 57:1: "In the shadow of your wings I will take refuge, till the storms of destruction pass by." Psalm 61:4: "Let me take refuge under the shelter of your wings." Psalm 91:4: "He will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge." Ruth 2:12: Boaz told Ruth, "The LORD repay you for what you have done, and a full reward be given you by the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge." Deuteronomy 32:11 describes God as an eagle that "stirs up its nest, that flutters over its young, spreading out its wings, catching them, bearing them on its pinions." In each of these texts, it is God who provides the refuge of wings. Jesus applies this image to himself: "How often would I have gathered your children together" — he claims the role that the scriptures assign to God. The gathering is protective, nurturing, an act of sheltering from danger.

The critical phrase is "and you were not willing" (kai ouk ethelesate). The verb thelo (to will, to desire, to choose) marks a deliberate refusal. Jesus's desire to gather was met by Jerusalem's refusal to be gathered. The failure is not divine inability but human unwillingness. The dynamic matches what has been building throughout the document: Jesus offered, taught, healed, and demonstrated the kingdom; the leadership of Jerusalem refused to respond. The "you" (hymeis) is emphatic — you, Jerusalem, you were not willing. The grief in Jesus's voice is the grief of one whose persistent offer of protection has been persistently rejected.

"'See, your house is left to you desolate (idou aphietai hymin ho oikos hymon eremos).'"

"Your house" (ho oikos hymon) is the temple. The word oikos (house) was used at 21:13, where Jesus declared, "My house shall be called a house of prayer" — citing Isaiah 56:7. There, it was "my house" (ho oikos mou) — God's house. Now it is "your house" (ho oikos hymon). The possessive has shifted. The temple is no longer God's house; it has become theirs. God is vacating it. The word "desolate" (eremos — deserted, abandoned, empty) is the word used for the wilderness (eremos) where John the Baptist preached (3:1) and where Jesus was tempted (4:1). The temple, the center of Israel's worship, the place where God's presence was said to dwell, will be left as a wasteland. This is the Shekinah's departure — the withdrawal of divine presence. In Ezekiel 10-11, the prophet saw the glory of the LORD depart from the temple and from the city, moving from the cherubim over the threshold (10:4), to the east gate (10:19), to the Mount of Olives (11:23) — a staged withdrawal. Jesus is now seated on the Mount of Olives (cf. 24:3) as the temple he has abandoned stands across the valley. The imagery resonates with Ezekiel's vision: the glory leaves, and the house is left desolate.

The verb aphietai (is left, is abandoned) is in the present tense — the abandonment is happening now, in the act of Jesus's pronouncement. This is not a future prediction alone; it is a present reality being declared. Jeremiah used similar language: "I have forsaken my house; I have abandoned my heritage; I have given the beloved of my soul into the hands of her enemies" (Jeremiah 12:7). The prophetic precedent is exact: God abandons his own house because those who inhabit it have rejected his messengers.

"'For I tell you, you will not see me again, until you say, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord (eulogemenos ho erchomenos en onomati kyriou)."'"

The citation is Psalm 118:26 — the same verse the crowds shouted at Jesus's entry into Jerusalem (21:9). At the entry, the crowds spoke these words in celebration. Now Jesus quotes them as a condition for his return. The word "until" (heos) creates a temporal boundary: there will be a period in which Jesus is absent ("you will not see me"), and that period will end when Jerusalem speaks the words of Psalm 118:26 — the blessing of the one who comes in the Lord's name.

The implication is that Jerusalem's desolation is not permanent but conditional. There is an "until" — a point at which the relationship could be restored. But the condition is that Jerusalem itself must recognize and bless the one it has rejected. The rejected stone must be acknowledged as the cornerstone (21:42). This is not a simple prediction of what will happen but a statement of what must happen: recognition of Jesus as the one who comes in the LORD's name.

This verse ends Jesus's public teaching in the document. From this point forward (chapters 24-25), Jesus speaks only to his disciples. Chapter 26 begins the passion narrative. The last words spoken to Jerusalem as a whole are words of grief, judgment, and conditional hope.

The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. It gathers and concentrates the document's critique of religious hypocrisy into a single sustained address. From the Sermon on the Mount's warnings about practicing piety "to be seen by others" (6:1-18), through the denunciation of the Pharisees' tradition at 15:1-20, to the parables of indictment in 21:28-22:14, the author has been building a case against the religious establishment. Chapter 23 is the summation. Every theme — the gap between speech and action, the inversion of priorities, the love of honor, the failure to produce fruit, the killing of prophets — is collected and stated with finality. The seven woes are the closing argument in a trial that has been conducted across the entire document.

2. It demonstrates that the leaders' failure is not ignorance but willful refusal. The scribes and Pharisees sit on Moses's seat — they have access to the Torah. They build the prophets' tombs — they know the prophetic tradition. They tithe with meticulous care — they are familiar with the law's details. Their failure is not a deficit of knowledge or zeal but a distortion of both. They know the law but neglect its weightier matters. They honor dead prophets but kill living ones. They display piety but harbor greed. At every point, the indictment is not that they are ignorant of God's requirements but that they have substituted external performance for genuine obedience. The distinction between what they know and what they do is the definition of hypocrisy the author has been constructing since 6:1.

3. It establishes the community structure that stands opposite the Pharisaic model. The positive instructions of vv. 8-12 — no one called rabbi, no one called father, no one called instructor; one teacher (Christ), one Father (God), all brothers; the greatest is the servant — define the alternative community Jesus is forming. The scribes' love of titles and positions is answered by a community of radical equality under a single authority. The community does not abolish teaching, parenting, or guidance as functions; it abolishes the titles that convert those functions into markers of hierarchical status. This vision is continuous with the servant-leadership teaching of 20:25-28 and the child-as-model teaching of 18:1-4: the community of the kingdom inverts the categories of greatness that characterize both Gentile political structures and the religious establishment of Israel.

4. It locates Jesus within the prophetic tradition while simultaneously placing him above it. Jesus speaks in the form of the prophets — the woe oracle is Isaiah's form, Amos's form, Habakkuk's form. He pronounces judgment on the leadership of God's people, as Jeremiah did at the temple gate (Jeremiah 7). He laments over the city, as the weeping prophet lamented over Jerusalem. But he also exceeds the prophetic role: he says "I send you prophets" (v. 34), claiming the authority to commission the very prophets whose killing he denounces. He applies to himself the image of God's sheltering wings (v. 37). He declares "your house is left to you desolate" (v. 38), using language that in Jeremiah and Ezekiel described God's own withdrawal. The chapter presents Jesus as one who stands in the prophetic succession and yet exercises the prerogatives that the prophets attributed to God.

5. It provides the theological basis for the temple's destruction, which chapter 24 will describe. The lament of vv. 37-39 is the emotional and theological prelude to the prediction of 24:2 ("not one stone here will be left upon another"). The temple's destruction is not arbitrary divine violence; it is the consequence of a sustained pattern of rejecting God's messengers, a pattern that reaches its climax in the rejection and killing of God's son. The "desolation" announced at 23:38 is the withdrawal of divine presence from an institution that has become "your house" rather than "my house" — a house managed for the benefit of its administrators rather than for the purposes of its owner. The movement from chapter 23 to chapter 24 is seamless: the indictment (chapter 23) produces the verdict (chapter 24), and the verdict has been earned across the entire history surveyed in 23:35 — from Abel to Zechariah, and now to Jesus himself.

6. It completes the first-last reversal as applied to Israel's leadership. The scribes and Pharisees, who consider themselves the guardians of God's covenant, the authorized interpreters of his law, the spiritual elite of his people, are identified as serpents and a brood of vipers heading toward Gehenna (v. 33). The tax collectors and prostitutes enter the kingdom before them (21:31). The blind and lame are healed in the temple while the chief priests are indignant (21:14-15). Children recognize the Son of David while the experts do not (21:15-16). The "first" in Israel's religious hierarchy have become "last" — not because God rejected them arbitrarily, but because they rejected his messengers, neglected justice and mercy, and converted their position of service into an instrument of self-exaltation. The reversal is complete: those who claimed to stand closest to God are furthest from him, and those whom the religious establishment despised — the ignorant, the sinful, the marginal — have responded and entered.

7. It preserves the distinction between the authority of Torah and the failure of its interpreters. Jesus does not repudiate the Torah or the institution of its teaching. He begins by affirming that the scribes and Pharisees sit on Moses's seat and that the people should do what they teach (v. 3). The Torah is not the problem; the teachers are. The vineyard is not destroyed; the tenants are replaced (21:41). Moses's seat remains; those who occupy it are judged. This distinction is essential to the author's argument: Jesus came not to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to fulfill them (5:17), and the judgment on the scribes and Pharisees is rendered precisely by the standard of the Torah they claim to teach. They are condemned not by an external standard but by their own — by the weightier matters of the very law they profess to uphold.
