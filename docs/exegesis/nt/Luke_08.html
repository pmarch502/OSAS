Luke 8 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 7): Luke 7 was organized around the question of who recognizes what God is doing through Jesus and who does not. The answer systematically overturned expected categories. A Gentile centurion displayed faith greater than anything Jesus found in Israel. The raising of a widow's son at Nain caused the crowd to declare that "God has visited His people," echoing the Elijah pattern from 1 Kings 17. John the Baptist, confused that Jesus's ministry looked like mercy rather than judgment, sent from prison to ask whether Jesus was "the Coming One." Jesus answered with Isaiah's restoration prophecies and pronounced a blessing on anyone not offended by a messiah who heals rather than judges. The editorial comment of 7:29-30 stated the chapter's thesis directly: tax collectors and ordinary people accepted God's purpose by submitting to John's baptism, while Pharisees and lawyers rejected God's purpose for themselves. The anointing at Simon's house dramatized this division in a single scene: the sinful woman who knew the size of her debt and had experienced its cancellation responded with extravagant love, while the Pharisee who considered his debt small responded with minimal hospitality. Jesus declared that the woman's faith had saved her. Chapter 7 thus demonstrated that outsiders and sinners were closer to God than the religious insiders, and that faith — not ethnicity, status, or moral reputation — was the operative reality connecting people to God's saving action.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 9): Luke 9 marks a turning point. Jesus sends the twelve out on a mission, giving them power and authority over demons and diseases and commissioning them to proclaim the kingdom of God. When they return, Jesus feeds the five thousand — a sign recalling the wilderness manna and demonstrating his provision for the gathered people of God. Peter confesses Jesus as "the Christ of God," but Jesus immediately redefines what this means: the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and be raised on the third day. He then calls all who would follow him to deny themselves, take up their cross daily, and follow him. The transfiguration follows, where Moses and Elijah appear and discuss Jesus's "departure" (exodos) that he is about to accomplish in Jerusalem. The rest of the chapter shows the disciples failing to understand: they cannot cast out a demon, they argue about who is greatest, and they want to call fire down on a Samaritan village. Chapter 9 thus moves from empowerment and revelation to suffering and misunderstanding, beginning the shift toward Jerusalem and the cross.

How chapter 8 connects these: Luke 7 demonstrated who recognizes God's action and who does not; chapter 8 now explains why responses to the word vary and provides further demonstrations of Jesus's authority that demand a response. The parable of the soils (8:4-15) is the explanatory mechanism: the same word is sown, but outcomes differ based on the condition of the hearer. Some hear and fall away under pressure, some are choked by the cares and riches of life, and some hear with a good and honest heart and bear fruit with perseverance. This directly accounts for the varied responses catalogued in chapter 7 — why the centurion believed and the Pharisees did not, why tax collectors accepted God's purpose and lawyers rejected it. The chapter then redefines Jesus's true family as "those who hear the word of God and do it" (8:21), providing a criterion that matches the soils parable's distinction between hearing that endures and hearing that does not. Four demonstrations of authority follow — over nature, demons, chronic illness, and death — each of which provokes a response from those who witness it, pressing the question of whether they will be good soil or not. This positions chapter 9, where the twelve are sent out to proclaim the very word whose reception chapter 8 has analyzed, and where the question of understanding and response deepens into the question of whether the disciples themselves truly grasp what they are following.

Verses 1-3: The Ministry Entourage

"Soon afterward, He began going around from one city and village to another, proclaiming and preaching the kingdom of God. The twelve were with Him, and also some women who had been healed of evil spirits and sicknesses: Mary who was called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out, and Joanna the wife of Chuza, Herod's steward, and Susanna, and many others who were contributing to their support out of their private means."

This summary statement establishes the setting for the chapter. Jesus is on an itinerant ministry through cities and villages, and the content of his proclamation is identified as "the kingdom of God" (tēn basileian tou theou). The verb kēryssōn ("proclaiming, heralding") carries the sense of a public announcement — the kind of declaration a herald makes on behalf of a king. The verb euangelizomenos ("preaching good news") is drawn from the same root as euangelion ("good news, gospel") and links Jesus's activity to Isaiah 61:1, which he claimed in the Nazareth synagogue: "He has anointed Me to bring good news to the poor" (4:18).

Two groups accompany him. First, "the twelve" (hoi dōdeka) — the apostles selected in 6:13. Their presence as a defined group is noted without elaboration; they are traveling with Jesus, observing and learning.

Second, and more remarkable for a first-century audience, "some women" (gynaikes tines). The author names three and notes that there were "many others." These women are identified by two characteristics: they had been healed, and they were financially supporting the ministry.

Mary called Magdalene (Maria hē kaloumenē Magdalēnē) is introduced as one "from whom seven demons had gone out." The number seven in this context indicates completeness or severity — a thoroughgoing demonic affliction from which she has been delivered. This is the first time Mary Magdalene appears in Luke's narrative, and the author is careful to distinguish her from the unnamed sinful woman of 7:36-50. The text makes no connection between the two figures.

Joanna (Iōanna) is identified as "the wife of Chuza, Herod's steward." Chuza (Chouzas) is Herod Antipas's epitropos — a household manager or estate steward, a position of significant influence and wealth. This means Joanna comes from the highest social tier of the Herodian court. Her presence among Jesus's followers is extraordinary: a woman from the court of the very ruler who imprisoned John the Baptist and who will later seek to see Jesus (9:9, 23:8) is funding Jesus's ministry. The author's inclusion of this detail continues the theme of unexpected insiders and outsiders.

Susanna (Sousanna) is named but not further described.

The women were "contributing to their support out of their private means" (diēkonoun autois ek tōn hyparchontōn autais). The verb diakoneō means "to serve, to minister, to provide for." The word hyparchonta refers to one's possessions or resources. These women are not passive followers but active supporters — patrons of the ministry. In the Greco-Roman world, wealthy women frequently served as patrons of philosophical and religious movements. What is notable here is that women who had been healed — recipients of Jesus's ministry — become sustainers of that ministry. Their response to what they received was not passive gratitude but active participation. This models the kind of "good soil" response that the parable of the sower will describe.

Verses 4-8: The Parable of the Sower and the Soils

"When a large crowd was coming together, and those from the various cities were journeying to Him, He spoke by way of a parable: 'The sower went out to sow his seed; and as he sowed, some fell beside the road, and it was trampled under foot and the birds of the air ate it up. Other seed fell on rocky soil, and as soon as it grew up, it withered away, because it had no moisture. Other seed fell among the thorns; and the thorns grew up with it and choked it out. Other seed fell into the good soil, and grew up, and produced a crop a hundred times as great.' As He said these things, He would call out, 'He who has ears to hear, let him hear.'"

A large crowd is assembling from multiple cities. The growing scale of Jesus's audience provides the occasion for a parable about how audiences receive his message. The parable form (parabolē) was a standard tool in Jewish teaching — a story drawn from everyday life used to illuminate a spiritual or moral truth. The mashal tradition in Hebrew wisdom literature (Proverbs, Ezekiel 17, 2 Samuel 12:1-4) employed the same method.

The parable describes a single sower performing a single action — sowing seed — with four different outcomes determined entirely by the soil on which the seed falls. The sower and the seed are constant; only the ground varies.

First soil: seed falls "beside the road" (para tēn hodon). Roads in first-century Palestine were not paved surfaces but beaten paths through or alongside fields. Seed falling on this packed earth cannot penetrate the surface. It is trampled (katepatēthē) and eaten by birds. The seed never enters the ground at all.

Second soil: seed falls "on rocky soil" (epi tēn petran). This refers not to soil mixed with stones but to a thin layer of earth over a limestone shelf — a common feature of Palestinian terrain. The seed germinates and sprouts quickly (because the shallow soil warms fast), but the plant cannot develop roots. It withers because it has "no moisture" (dia to mē echein ikmada). Luke uses "moisture" where the parallel accounts use "depth of earth" — the result is the same: the plant has no sustaining root system and dies when conditions become hostile.

Third soil: seed falls "among the thorns" (en mesō tōn akanthōn). The thorns are already present in the soil — they grow up with (synphyeisai) the good seed and choke it (apepnixan auton). The word apopnigō means "to choke, to strangle, to suffocate." The plant germinates, grows, and survives the initial stage but is eventually overwhelmed by competing growth. It lives longer than the rocky-soil plant but is equally unfruitful.

Fourth soil: seed falls "into the good soil" (eis tēn gēn tēn agathēn). This seed grows and produces "a crop a hundred times as great" (karpon hekatontaplasiona). A hundredfold yield was extraordinary — ancient agricultural sources suggest that a tenfold or even sevenfold return was considered good. The hundredfold figure signals an abundant, almost miraculous productivity.

Jesus concludes with a public call: "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (ho echōn ōta akouein akouetō). This formula appears frequently in prophetic and wisdom literature (Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 6:9-10; Ezekiel 3:27; 12:2). It simultaneously invites hearing and implies that not everyone will actually hear. The call places responsibility on the listener: the message is available, but reception depends on the hearer.

Verses 9-10: The Purpose of Parables

"His disciples began questioning Him as to what this parable meant. And He said, 'To you it has been granted to know the mysteries of the kingdom of God, but to the rest it is in parables, so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand.'"

The disciples ask about the meaning of the parable. Jesus's answer distinguishes two groups: "you" (hymin) — the disciples — and "the rest" (tois loipois). To the disciples, "it has been granted to know the mysteries of the kingdom of God" (dedotai gnōnai ta mystēria tēs basileias tou theou). The passive "it has been granted" (dedotai) is a divine passive — the granting comes from God. The word mystēria does not mean "puzzles" or "enigmas" in the modern sense. In Jewish apocalyptic usage (Daniel 2:18-19, 27-30, 47; 1 Enoch; the Dead Sea Scrolls), mystērion refers to God's purposes that are hidden from ordinary perception but revealed to those whom God grants understanding. Daniel uses the word for Nebuchadnezzar's dream and its interpretation — the mystery was the divine plan for the course of empires, which God revealed to Daniel. The "mysteries of the kingdom" are the realities of God's reign that are not self-evident but require revealed understanding.

To "the rest," these realities come "in parables" (en parabolais). Jesus then quotes Isaiah 6:9: "so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand" (hina blepontes mē blepōsin kai akouontes mē syniōsin). The original context of Isaiah 6 is critical. In Isaiah 6:9-10, God commissions the prophet Isaiah and tells him that his preaching will harden Israel rather than convert them — their ears will be heavy, their eyes shut, their hearts fat. This was not the purpose of Isaiah's preaching in the sense that God wanted Israel to be deaf; rather, the preaching would function as a dividing agent, revealing who was already hardened and who was receptive. The word of God through the prophet would clarify the distinction that already existed within Israel.

The same dynamic is at work here. The parables do not create blindness in people who would otherwise see clearly. They reveal blindness that already exists. Those who are genuinely seeking will press further — as the disciples do by asking for explanation. Those who are indifferent or hostile will hear a story about farming and walk away. The parable functions as a filter: it invites the receptive deeper while leaving the unreceptive undisturbed in their incomprehension.

This connects directly to 7:29-30, where the author noted that the people and tax collectors "justified God" while the Pharisees and lawyers "rejected God's purpose for themselves." The parable mechanism explains how the same ministry can produce both responses simultaneously.

Verses 11-15: The Interpretation of the Parable

"Now the parable is this: the seed is the word of God. Those beside the road are those who have heard; then the devil comes and takes away the word from their heart, so that they will not believe and be saved. Those on the rocky soil are those who, when they hear, receive the word with joy; and these have no firm root; they believe for a while, and in time of temptation fall away. The seed which fell among the thorns, these are the ones who have heard, and as they go on their way they are choked with worries and riches and pleasures of this life, and bring no fruit to maturity. But the seed in the good soil, these are the ones who have heard the word in an honest and good heart, and hold it fast, and bear fruit with perseverance."

Jesus provides the key: the seed is "the word of God" (ho logos tou theou). This is the same word (logos) that has been preached throughout Luke's narrative — the proclamation of the kingdom. The four soils represent four types of human response to this word.

The road (v. 12): These are people who hear (akousantes) but from whom "the devil" (ho diabolos) removes the word "from their heart" (apo tēs kardias autōn). The heart (kardia) in Hebrew and Jewish thought is not primarily the seat of emotion but the center of the whole person — mind, will, and understanding. The word never penetrates this center. The purpose clause is stark: "so that they will not believe and be saved" (hina mē pisteusantes sōthōsin). Two elements are joined: believing (pisteusantes) and being saved (sōthōsin). The construction implies that believing is the means by which saving occurs, and that preventing belief prevents salvation. This matches 7:50, where Jesus told the sinful woman, "Your faith has saved you." The road-soil hearers never reach the point of faith.

The rocky soil (v. 13): These hear and "receive the word with joy" (meta charas dechontai ton logon). Their initial response is positive and emotional. But they "have no firm root" (rhizan ouk echousin). The metaphor of rootedness describes an internal depth and stability. These people "believe for a while" (pros kairon pisteuousin) — their faith is real but temporary, lasting only "for a while" (pros kairon, "for a season"). The critical phrase is "in time of temptation fall away" (en kairō peirasmou aphistantai). The word peirasmos means "testing, trial, temptation" — any circumstance that puts faith under pressure. The verb aphistantai is from aphistēmi, meaning "to depart, to withdraw, to fall away, to apostatize." The word is used in the LXX for Israel's departure from God (Deuteronomy 32:15; Jeremiah 3:14; Ezekiel 20:8; Daniel 9:9). These are not people who never believed; they believed genuinely but temporarily. When external pressure came, they departed.

This is a critical observation within Luke's narrative. The author presents a category of person who believes and then falls away. The believing is real — the same word pisteuousin is used as in verse 12 where believing leads to being saved. But the falling away is also real. The implication is that initial faith does not automatically guarantee final perseverance. The soil that matters is the one that endures.

The thorny soil (v. 14): These also hear. Their problem is not external persecution but internal competition. They are "choked with worries and riches and pleasures of this life" (hypo merimnōn kai ploutou kai hēdonōn tou biou sympnigontai). Three things choke the word: merimnai ("worries, anxieties, cares"), ploutos ("wealth, riches"), and hēdonai ("pleasures"). The word bios ("life" in the sense of livelihood, daily existence) distinguishes these from spiritual concerns — these are the ordinary preoccupations of human life. The result is that they "bring no fruit to maturity" (ou telesphorousin). The verb telephoreō means "to bring to completion, to bear fruit to maturity." The plant grows but never produces a ripe harvest. These hearers are not described as falling away in the manner of the rocky soil; they remain in some sense present but unfruitful. The word is alive in them but overwhelmed.

This connects directly to the warnings in Jesus's Sermon on the Plain (6:24-26) — "Woe to you who are rich" — and anticipates later warnings about wealth (12:13-21, the rich fool; 16:19-31, the rich man and Lazarus; 18:18-25, the rich ruler). Riches are not condemned as inherently evil but as competing loyalties that choke the word's productivity.

The good soil (v. 15): These hear "in an honest and good heart" (en kardia kalē kai agathē). The heart here returns to its full sense — the center of the person. The adjectives kalos ("beautiful, noble, honest") and agathos ("good, virtuous") describe the internal condition of receptivity. But hearing alone is not sufficient. Two further actions are required: they "hold it fast" (katechousin) and they "bear fruit with perseverance" (karpophorousin en hypomonē).

The verb katechō means "to hold down, to hold firmly, to retain, to possess." It describes a deliberate, ongoing grip on the word — not a passive reception but an active holding. The word hypomonē means "endurance, patient perseverance, steadfast continuance." It is the opposite of the rocky soil's temporary faith. The good soil does not merely hear, or even believe initially; it holds fast and endures. Fruit-bearing is the result of this sustained faithfulness.

The parable as a whole establishes a spectrum of responses to the word of God, and the decisive factor is not the quality of the word (which is the same in every case) but the condition and perseverance of the hearer. The author's point is that hearing is necessary but not sufficient; what matters is the kind of hearing — whether it penetrates, takes root, survives competition, and produces lasting fruit.

Verses 16-18: The Lamp and the Warning about Hearing

"Now no one after lighting a lamp covers it over with a container, or puts it under a bed; but he puts it on a lampstand, so that those who come in may see the light. For nothing is hidden that will not become evident, nor anything secret that will not be known and come to light. So take care how you listen; for whoever has, to him more shall be given; and whoever does not have, even what he thinks he has shall be taken away from him."

These sayings, which might initially appear disconnected, continue the argument about hearing and responding to the word.

The lamp saying: a lamp (lychnos) is lit for the purpose of illumination. It is placed on a lampstand (lychnias), not hidden under a vessel (skeuei) or a bed (klinēs). The word of God — the seed of the parable — is not given to be concealed. It is meant to be visible, to illuminate. This has a double application. First, what has been given to the disciples as "mysteries" (v. 10) is not meant to remain private forever; it is entrusted to them so that it will eventually be made public. Second, the truth of the word will ultimately be revealed in the lives of those who hear it — the quality of the soil will become visible in the fruit, or lack thereof.

The hiddenness saying reinforces this: "nothing is hidden that will not become evident" (ou gar estin krypton ho ou phaneron genēsetai). Whatever is now concealed will be brought to light. In the context of the soils parable, this means that the internal condition of the hearer — whether road, rock, thorns, or good soil — will eventually be exposed by results. The person who appears to believe but has no root will be revealed when testing comes. The person who appears fruitful but is being choked will be revealed when maturity fails to arrive.

The warning about hearing: "take care how you listen" (blepete oun pōs akouete). The adverb pōs ("how") indicates that the manner of hearing matters, not just the fact of hearing. This connects directly to the soils: all four types hear, but they hear differently. The consequences are stated in terms of having and not having: "whoever has, to him more shall be given; and whoever does not have, even what he thinks he has shall be taken away from him." The one who hears rightly and holds fast (the good soil) will receive more understanding. The one who hears superficially (the road or rocky soil) will lose even what seemed to be gained. The verb dokei ("thinks, supposes") is important — "even what he thinks he has" (ho dokei echein). The person may believe they possess understanding, but if they have not truly received and held the word, even that supposed possession will be lost.

This principle of accumulation and loss through the quality of hearing runs through the entire chapter and provides the lens for reading the episodes that follow.

Verses 19-21: Jesus's True Family

"And His mother and brothers came to Him, and they were unable to get to Him because of the crowd. And it was reported to Him, 'Your mother and Your brothers are standing outside, wishing to see You.' But He answered and said to them, 'My mother and My brothers are these who hear the word of God and do it.'"

Jesus's biological family — his mother and brothers (hē mētēr kai hoi adelphoi autou) — arrive and cannot reach him because of the crowd. Someone reports their presence to Jesus.

Jesus's response redefines family on the basis of response to the word: "My mother and My brothers are these who hear the word of God and do it" (mētēr mou kai adelphoi mou houtoi eisin hoi ton logon tou theou akouontes kai poiountes). Two participles define the true family: akouontes ("hearing") and poiountes ("doing"). This is the same pairing that concluded the Sermon on the Plain: the one who comes to Jesus, hears his words, and does them is like the person who builds on rock (6:47-48); the one who hears and does not do is like the person who builds without a foundation (6:49).

The statement does not reject Jesus's biological family. It establishes a new criterion for belonging to the family of God that transcends biological and ethnic ties. This is consistent with the centurion episode (7:1-10), where a Gentile's faith surpassed Israel's, and with the parable of the soils, where the decisive factor was not the hearer's identity but the quality of their hearing and response. The "good soil" of verse 15 — those who hear in an honest and good heart, hold fast, and bear fruit with perseverance — are here identified as Jesus's true kin. The word of God, not bloodline, creates the family.

This is also a subtle but significant statement about Israel's identity. The children of Abraham assumed that covenant membership was determined by descent. Jesus is asserting that membership in the renewed people of God is determined by hearing and doing the word. This does not abolish Israel's covenant but redefines who constitutes the faithful within it.

Verses 22-25: Authority over Nature — Stilling the Storm

"Now on one of those days Jesus and His disciples got into a boat, and He said to them, 'Let us go over to the other side of the lake.' So they launched out. But as they were sailing along He fell asleep; and a fierce gale of wind descended on the lake, and they began to be swamped and to be in danger. They came to Jesus and woke Him up, saying, 'Master, Master, we are perishing!' And He got up and rebuked the wind and the surging waves, and they stopped, and it became calm. And He said to them, 'Where is your faith?' They were fearful and amazed, saying to one another, 'Who then is this, that He commands even the winds and the water, and they obey Him?'"

The narrative shifts from teaching to demonstration. Jesus initiates a journey across the Sea of Galilee (the lake, tēn limnēn). He falls asleep — a detail that emphasizes his full humanity and physical exhaustion from the itinerant ministry described in verse 1.

A "fierce gale" (lailaps anemou) descends. The Sea of Galilee, situated in a basin surrounded by hills, is notoriously subject to sudden, violent storms as wind funnels through the surrounding valleys. The disciples — several of whom were fishermen by trade (5:1-11) and therefore experienced on this lake — are genuinely frightened. They wake Jesus with a double vocative: "Master, Master" (Epistatēs, Epistatēs). The word epistatēs is used exclusively in Luke among the Gospel writers; it means "master, commander, one who stands over." Their cry is raw: "we are perishing" (apollymetha).

Jesus's response is described with a verb loaded with significance: he "rebuked" (epetimēsen) the wind and the surging waves. The verb epitimaō ("to rebuke, to command with authority") is the same word used for Jesus's rebukes of demons (4:35, 4:41). The use of this verb for natural forces treats the storm as something that must submit to Jesus's authoritative command in the same way that unclean spirits do. The storm does not gradually subside; "they stopped, and it became calm" (epausanto, kai egeneto galēnē). The cessation is immediate and total.

The scriptural background is Psalm 107:23-30 (106:23-30 LXX), where sailors in a storm cry out to the LORD, and "He caused the storm to be still, so that the waves of the sea were hushed. Then they were glad because they were quiet, and He guided them to their desired haven." In the Psalms, the authority to command the sea and the wind belongs exclusively to God (see also Psalm 89:9 [88:10 LXX]: "You rule the swelling of the sea; when its waves rise, You still them"; Job 38:8-11; Jonah 1:15). The first-century hearer, steeped in these texts, would recognize that what Jesus does here is what the Psalms say only God does.

Jesus's question to the disciples is pointed: "Where is your faith?" (Pou hē pistis hymōn). This is not a gentle pastoral inquiry but a rebuke. They have been with Jesus, heard his teaching, witnessed his healings and authority over demons. Yet when danger threatened them personally, their response was panic rather than trust. The question connects back to the soils parable: they have received the word but have not yet held it fast in a way that produces the fruit of trust under pressure. They resemble the rocky soil — believing when conditions are favorable but shaken when tested.

Their response is "fearful and amazed" (phobēthentes ethaumasan), and they ask one another: "Who then is this, that He commands even the winds and the water, and they obey Him?" (Tis ara houtos estin hoti kai tois anemois epitassei kai tō hydati, kai hypakouousin autō). The verb epitassō means "to command, to give orders to." The verb hypakouō means "to obey, to listen submissively." The disciples are confronting a reality that exceeds their existing categories. They know Jesus is a teacher and a healer. But authority over the sea and the wind belongs to God alone. The question "Who then is this?" is not rhetorical; it is the central question the author wants every reader to confront. The answer is implied by the scriptural background but not stated — the reader must supply it.

Verses 26-33: Authority over the Demonic — The Gerasene Demoniac

"Then they sailed to the country of the Gerasenes, which is opposite Galilee. And when He came out onto the land, He was met by a man from the city who was possessed with demons; and who had not put on any clothing for a long time, and was not living in a house, but in the tombs. Seeing Jesus, he cried out and fell before Him, and said in a loud voice, 'What business do we have with each other, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I beg You, do not torment me.' For He had commanded the unclean spirit to come out of the man. For it had seized him many times; and he was bound with chains and shackles and kept under guard, and yet he would break his bonds and be driven by the demon into the desert. And Jesus asked him, 'What is your name?' And he said, 'Legion'; for many demons had entered him. They were imploring Him not to command them to go away into the abyss. Now there was a herd of many swine feeding there on the mountain; and the demons implored Him to permit them to enter the swine. And He gave them permission. And the demons came out of the man and entered the swine; and the herd rushed down the steep bank into the lake and was drowned."

Jesus crosses the lake to Gentile territory — the country of the Gerasenes, "opposite Galilee" (antipera tēs Galilaias). This geographical note is significant: Jesus has left Jewish territory. The presence of a large herd of swine (choirōn) confirms the Gentile setting, since pigs were unclean animals under Torah (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8) and would not be kept in a Jewish area.

The man who meets Jesus is described in terms that emphasize the totality of his dehumanization. He wears no clothing (himatia ouk enedidysketo) — stripped of the most basic marker of human dignity and social participation. He does not live in a house (ouk en oikia emenen) — excluded from all domestic and communal life. He lives "in the tombs" (en tois mnēmasin) — among the dead, in a place of ritual impurity. Everything about him is inverted: naked instead of clothed, homeless instead of housed, dwelling with the dead instead of the living. He exists in a state of complete social and personal disintegration.

The demons speak through the man, addressing Jesus as "Son of the Most High God" (Huie tou theou tou hypsistou). The title "Most High God" (theos hypsistos) is used in the Hebrew scriptures as a name for Israel's God, particularly in contexts where God is being distinguished from the gods of other nations (Genesis 14:18-22, where Melchizedek is priest of El Elyon; Daniel 3:26; 4:2; 5:18, 21). The demons recognize Jesus's identity and authority more clearly than any human character in the narrative has. Their plea — "do not torment me" (mē me basanisēs) — uses the verb basanizō, which means "to torture, to examine by torture." The demons anticipate a judgment that they believe Jesus has the authority to execute.

Jesus asks the man's name, and the response is "Legion" (Legiōn), "for many demons had entered him." The word legiōn is a Latin military term (legio) for a Roman military unit of approximately five to six thousand soldiers. The use of this Roman military term in Gentile territory occupied by Rome carries unmistakable resonance: the man is occupied by a force that shares its name with the occupying army. Whether the author intends a direct political allegory is uncertain, but the association would not be lost on first-century hearers living under Roman rule.

The demons beg not to be sent "into the abyss" (eis tēn abysson). The word abyssos in Jewish apocalyptic literature refers to the place of imprisonment for rebellious spiritual beings (1 Enoch 10:4-6, 11-13; Jubilees 5:6-10). The demons recognize that Jesus has the authority to send them there — that is, to execute final judgment on them — and they plead for a lesser sentence.

Jesus permits them to enter the swine, and the herd — about two thousand animals according to the parallel in Mark 5:13 — rushes down the steep bank and drowns. The destruction of the swine demonstrates that the demonic force was real and destructive. The herd's drowning in the lake echoes, for Jewish hearers, the drowning of Pharaoh's army in the sea (Exodus 14:27-28; 15:4-5) — a destruction of an occupying force in water.

Verses 34-39: The Response of the Gerasenes

"When the herdsmen saw what had happened, they ran away and reported it in the city and out in the country. The people went out to see what had happened; and they came to Jesus, and found the man from whom the demons had gone out, sitting down at the feet of Jesus, clothed and in his right mind; and they became frightened. Those who had seen it reported to them how the man who was demon-possessed had been made well. And all the people of the country of the Gerasenes and the surrounding district asked Him to leave them, for they were gripped with great fear; and He got into a boat and returned. But the man from whom the demons had gone out was begging Him that he might accompany Him; but He sent him away, saying, 'Return to your house and describe what great things God has done for you.' So he went away, proclaiming throughout the whole city what great things Jesus had done for him."

The contrast between the healed man and the local population is sharp. The man who was naked, homeless, and living among the dead is now "sitting down at the feet of Jesus, clothed and in his right mind" (kathēmenon himatismenon kai sōphronounta para tous podas tou Iēsou). Every element of his dehumanization has been reversed: he is seated (restored to calm), clothed (restored to dignity), in his right mind (sōphronounta — thinking clearly, restored to rational self-possession), and at the feet of Jesus (in the posture of a disciple). The phrase "at the feet of" (para tous podas) is the same used for Mary sitting at Jesus's feet in 10:39 — it denotes discipleship and learning.

The population's response is fear (ephobēthēsan, v. 35; phobō megalō synechonto, v. 37). They ask Jesus to leave. This is remarkable: they have witnessed an act of total restoration — a man who was the most extreme case of human ruin has been completely healed — and their response is not gratitude or worship but fear and rejection. The loss of the swine herd (a significant economic asset) may contribute to their reaction, but the text emphasizes the fear, not the financial loss.

The healed man begs to go with Jesus, but Jesus refuses and gives him a different commission: "Return to your house and describe what great things God has done for you" (hypostrephe eis ton oikon sou kai diēgou hosa soi epoiēsen ho theos). Two elements are noteworthy. First, Jesus sends the man back to his house — restoring him to the domestic and social world from which he had been expelled. Second, Jesus tells him to proclaim what "God" has done. The man, however, "went away, proclaiming throughout the whole city what great things Jesus had done for him" (kēryssōn kath' holēn tēn polin hosa epoiēsen autō ho Iēsous). The author records the substitution without comment: what Jesus attributes to God, the man attributes to Jesus. The interchangeability of "God" and "Jesus" in this narrative is consistent with the pattern throughout Luke where Jesus acts in the place of God — forgiving sins (5:20-21; 7:48-49), stilling the sea (Psalm 107), and commanding unclean spirits.

This man becomes the first evangelist to the Gentiles in Luke's narrative — sent by Jesus to proclaim God's saving action in Gentile territory.

Verses 40-42a: Two Urgent Needs Intertwined

"And as Jesus returned, the people welcomed Him, for they had all been waiting for Him. And there came a man named Jairus, and he was an official of the synagogue; and he fell at Jesus's feet, and began to implore Him to come to his house; for he had an only daughter, about twelve years old, and she was dying."

Jesus returns to the Jewish side of the lake and is welcomed. The contrast with the Gerasene population is immediate: they asked him to leave; these people have been waiting for him.

Jairus (Iairos) is identified as "an official of the synagogue" (archōn tēs synagōgēs). The synagogue ruler was responsible for organizing worship services and managing the synagogue building — a position of religious authority and social prominence. This man is an insider, a pillar of the religious establishment. Yet he falls at Jesus's feet (pesōn para tous podas tou Iēsou) — the same posture of the healed demoniac (v. 35), and a posture of complete humility and desperation. His status does not prevent him from seeking Jesus.

His daughter is described with three details that maximize the urgency: she is his "only daughter" (thygatēr monogenēs), she is "about twelve years old" (hōs etōn dōdeka), and she is dying (apethnēsken). The word monogenēs ("only, unique") was used of the widow's son at Nain (7:12). Its reappearance connects the two stories: in both cases, a parent faces the loss of an only child. The age of twelve is significant — she is on the threshold of adulthood, about to enter marriageable age in first-century Jewish culture. She is dying at the moment her life is about to begin.

Verses 42b-48: The Woman with a Flow of Blood

"But as He went, the crowds were pressing against Him. And a woman who had a hemorrhage for twelve years, and could not be healed by anyone, came up behind Him and touched the fringe of His cloak, and immediately her hemorrhage stopped. And Jesus said, 'Who is the one who touched Me?' And while they were all denying it, Peter said, 'Master, the people are crowding and pressing in on You.' But Jesus said, 'Someone did touch Me, for I was aware that power had gone out of Me.' When the woman saw that she had not escaped notice, she came trembling and fell down before Him, and declared in the presence of all the people the reason why she had touched Him, and how she had been immediately healed. And He said to her, 'Daughter, your faith has saved you; go in peace.'"

The author interrupts the Jairus story to insert a second healing. This narrative intercalation — a story begun, interrupted by another story, then completed — is a deliberate literary technique. The two stories interpret each other.

The woman has had "a hemorrhage" (en rhysei haimatos) for twelve years. The number twelve connects her to Jairus's daughter, who is twelve years old. This woman has been suffering for the entire duration of the girl's life. Under the purity regulations of Leviticus 15:25-30, a woman with a chronic flow of blood was in a state of continuous ritual impurity. Everything she touched became unclean; anyone who touched her became unclean; she was excluded from the temple, from normal social participation, and from physical contact with others. For twelve years she has lived in a state of enforced isolation and ritual exclusion from the community of Israel. She has spent everything on physicians (Luke notes she "could not be healed by anyone") and her condition has not improved.

She approaches Jesus from behind and touches "the fringe of His cloak" (tou kraspedou tou himatiou autou). The kraspedon is the tassel or fringe that Numbers 15:38-39 and Deuteronomy 22:12 required Israelites to wear on the corners of their garments as a reminder of God's commandments. She is reaching for the part of Jesus's clothing that represents his covenant faithfulness. In Malachi 4:2 (3:20 LXX), the prophet declares that "the sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings" (pterygōn) — the word "wings" also means the corners or edges of a garment. The woman's touch of the garment's fringe may carry this associative resonance: she is reaching for healing from the one in whom God's righteousness is active.

The healing is immediate (parachrēma estē hē rhysis tou haimatos autēs). Jesus perceives that "power had gone out" (dynamin exelēlythuian) from him and asks who touched him. Peter's response — the crowds are pressing against you — treats the question as absurd. But Jesus distinguishes between incidental contact and purposeful touch. The woman, seeing that she cannot remain hidden, comes forward trembling and falls before him.

Her public declaration of her condition and her healing is significant. She has been living in hiding — her condition was socially shameful and ritually isolating. Jesus's insistence on identifying her forces the healing into the public sphere. He does not allow her to receive healing in secret and slink away. He brings her out of concealment so that her restoration is witnessed and her reintegration into the community is established publicly.

Jesus's words to her are identical in structure to his words to the sinful woman in Simon's house (7:50): "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (hē pistis sou sesōken se; poreuou eis eirēnēn). He addresses her as "Daughter" (Thygatēr) — a term of affection and inclusion. The word sōzō here encompasses both physical healing and the fuller sense of rescue and restoration. She came isolated, impure, and invisible; she leaves healed, publicly restored, and addressed as a member of the family.

The parallel between the two uses of "your faith has saved you" (7:50 and 8:48) is deliberate. In both cases, the person who receives this declaration is someone who would have been excluded by the prevailing religious categories: a woman known as a sinner, and a woman in a state of chronic ritual impurity. Both approach Jesus despite the social risks. Both are told that faith — not ritual status, not moral reputation, not social standing — is what connected them to saving power.

Verses 49-56: Authority over Death — Raising Jairus's Daughter

"While He was still speaking, someone came from the house of the synagogue official, saying, 'Your daughter has died; do not trouble the Teacher anymore.' But when Jesus heard this, He answered him, 'Do not be afraid any longer; only believe, and she will be saved.' When He came to the house, He did not allow anyone to enter with Him, except Peter and John and James, and the girl's father and mother. Now they were all weeping and mourning for her; but He said, 'Stop weeping, for she has not died, but is asleep.' And they began laughing at Him, knowing that she had died. He, however, took her by the hand and called, saying, 'Child, arise!' And her spirit returned, and she got up immediately; and He gave orders for something to be given her to eat. Her parents were amazed; but He instructed them to tell no one what had happened."

The interruption of the hemorrhaging woman has introduced a delay, and during this delay the girl dies. A messenger arrives with the news: "Your daughter has died; do not trouble the Teacher anymore" (tethnēken hē thygatēr sou; mēketi skylle ton didaskalon). The verb skyllō means "to bother, to trouble, to weary." The messenger's logic is clear: death is final; there is no point in continuing to impose on Jesus.

Jesus's response to Jairus is a direct command: "Do not be afraid any longer; only believe, and she will be saved" (mē phobou; monon pisteuson, kai sōthēsetai). The verb pisteuson is aorist imperative — a decisive, one-time command: believe now. The verb sōthēsetai ("she will be saved/made well") is future passive. Jesus is asking Jairus to believe in the face of evidence that believing is pointless. This is the crisis of faith that the soils parable described: will Jairus hold fast to the word under the most extreme testing, or will he fall away? The woman with the hemorrhage has just modeled faith that persists despite twelve years of failure; now Jairus must model faith that persists despite death itself.

Jesus restricts who enters the house to Peter, John, James, and the parents. The mourners (who in first-century Jewish culture were often professional mourners hired for funerals) are weeping and mourning. Jesus says, "She has not died, but is asleep" (ouk apethanen alla katheudei). The mourners' laughter (kategelōn autou) shows they understand this as absurd — they know a dead person when they see one. The statement is not a medical diagnosis (the author confirms she had died); it is a redefinition of her condition from the perspective of Jesus's authority. For someone who has the power to raise the dead, death is not a permanent state but a temporary one — analogous to sleep, from which one can be awakened.

Jesus takes her by the hand (kratēsas tēs cheiros autēs) and says, "Child, arise!" (hē pais, egeire). As with the touch of the bier at Nain (7:14) and the touch of the leper (5:13), contact with death does not defile Jesus; instead, his touch reverses death. "Her spirit returned" (epestrepsen to pneuma autēs) — the language recalls the returns of life in the Elijah and Elisha narratives (1 Kings 17:22: "the life of the child returned to him"). She rises immediately (anestē parachrēma), and Jesus orders that she be given food — a practical detail confirming the physical reality of her restoration and his care for her bodily needs.

The instruction to tell no one (parēngeilen autois mēdeni eipein to gegonos) contrasts sharply with the command to the Gerasene demoniac to proclaim what God had done (v. 39). In Gentile territory, proclamation was commanded; in Jewish territory, silence is ordered. The reason may be pragmatic — the fame is already generating crowds that impede Jesus's ministry (cf. 5:15-16) — or it may relate to the nature of Jesus's messianic identity, which at this stage of the narrative he controls carefully rather than allowing public proclamation.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 8 is organized around two interlocking themes: the varied human response to the word of God, and the comprehensive scope of Jesus's authority.

The parable of the soils (vv. 4-15) provides the analytical framework for the entire chapter and for the preceding narrative. It explains why the same word produces different outcomes: the variable is the condition of the hearer, not the quality of the word. Four types of hearing are identified, and only one produces lasting fruit — the hearing that takes place in an honest and good heart, holds fast to the word, and bears fruit with perseverance. The rocky soil is particularly significant: it describes people who believe genuinely but temporarily, falling away when testing comes. This establishes that initial faith does not by itself guarantee a final outcome; perseverance is required.

The lamp and hearing sayings (vv. 16-18) extend the soils teaching by warning that what is hidden will be revealed and that the manner of hearing determines whether one receives more or loses what one has.

The true family declaration (vv. 19-21) applies the soils principle to the question of belonging: Jesus's family consists of those who hear the word and do it — the good-soil hearers. This redefines the people of God around response to the word rather than biological or ethnic identity.

The four demonstrations of authority then press the question of response by placing witnesses before undeniable displays of power:

  • The stilling of the storm (vv. 22-25) reveals authority over nature — an authority that the Psalms assign to God alone. The disciples' fear and Jesus's rebuke ("Where is your faith?") show that even those closest to Jesus have not yet become good soil under pressure.
  • The Gerasene demoniac (vv. 26-39) reveals authority over the entire demonic realm — a "Legion" of unclean spirits in Gentile territory. The healed man becomes the first Gentile proclaimer. The local population, however, responds with fear and asks Jesus to leave — they are the road-side soil, unable to receive what has happened.
  • The woman with the hemorrhage (vv. 42b-48) reveals authority over chronic illness and ritual impurity. Her twelve years of suffering mirror the twelve years of the girl's life, connecting the two stories. She models the faith that perseveres despite long failure, and Jesus declares that her faith has saved her — the same declaration made to the sinful woman in chapter 7.
  • The raising of Jairus's daughter (vv. 49-56) reveals authority over death itself. Jairus is challenged to believe even after receiving news that his daughter has died. The command "only believe, and she will be saved" distills the chapter's argument to its essence: the word demands trust that persists even when circumstances make trust appear absurd.

The chapter thus moves from explanation (why responses differ) to demonstration (what demands response) to challenge (will you be good soil?). It connects backward to chapter 7's catalogue of varied responses by providing the explanatory framework, and it connects forward to chapter 9, where the twelve will be sent out to proclaim the very word whose reception has been analyzed here, and where the question of understanding will deepen as Jesus begins to speak of suffering and death.

The women of verses 1-3 quietly model the good-soil response from the chapter's opening: healed by Jesus, they respond not with passive gratitude but with active service, sustaining the ministry from their own resources. They hear, hold fast, and bear fruit with perseverance. They are, in the parable's terms, the hundredfold crop.
