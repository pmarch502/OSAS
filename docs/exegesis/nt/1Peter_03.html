1 Peter 3 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Peter 2:11-25): Beginning at 2:11, the author turned from the community's corporate identity (living stones, royal priesthood, holy nation) to the community's conduct within a hostile social environment. The governing instruction was: "Keep your behavior excellent among the Gentiles, so that in the thing in which they slander you as evildoers, they may because of your good deeds, as they observe them, glorify God on the day of visitation" (2:12). Good conduct in the face of slander is the community's primary witness. The author then applied this principle to specific social relationships. First, submission to human institutions — the emperor and his governors — "for the Lord's sake" (2:13-17). Second, household servants (oiketai) submitting to their masters, including harsh ones, because innocent suffering borne with God-awareness "finds favor" with God (2:18-20). This led to the christological foundation: Christ himself suffered unjustly, leaving the community an example (hypogrammos) to follow in his steps (2:21). Drawing on Isaiah 53, the author described Christ's sinlessness, his refusal to retaliate, his entrusting of himself to the righteous Judge, and his bearing of sins on the cross so that the community might "die to sin and live for righteousness" (2:22-24). The section concluded with the sheep-and-shepherd metaphor: the recipients were once straying like sheep but have now returned to "the Shepherd and Guardian of your souls" (2:25).

The structure is a sequence of ordered-conduct instructions, each grounded in the same logic: submit, do good, endure unjust treatment if it comes, and trust God's justice rather than retaliating. The christological passage (2:21-25) is not a digression but the foundation on which every specific instruction rests. Christ is both the pattern for conduct (how to endure suffering) and the agent of redemption (why the community can afford to suffer without retaliating — their ultimate vindication is secured).

What the author argues immediately after (1 Peter 4:1-6): The author continues the argument about suffering and its relationship to sin: "Since Christ has suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves also with the same purpose, because the one who has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin" (4:1). The logic is that suffering in the flesh — enduring hardship rather than yielding to the desires that once governed their behavior — is the mechanism by which the community breaks free from sin's power. The remainder of 4:1-6 describes the contrast between the community's former way of life ("the desire of the Gentiles" — sensuality, lusts, drunkenness, carousing, drinking parties, abominable idolatries, 4:3) and their present conduct, which astonishes and angers their former companions (4:4). The passage culminates in the statement that "the gospel has been preached even to those who are dead, so that though they are judged in the flesh as people, they may live in the spirit according to God" (4:6) — a passage that stands in close relationship to the difficult material at the end of chapter 3 concerning Christ's proclamation to "spirits in prison."

How chapter 3 connects these: Chapter 3 continues the same pattern of ordered-conduct instructions that began at 2:13. The opening word "in the same way" (homoios, 3:1) binds the section on wives to the previous instructions about citizens (2:13) and servants (2:18), applying the same logic of submission and good conduct to yet another social relationship. After addressing wives (3:1-6) and husbands (3:7), the author gathers the entire community under a unified ethical instruction: harmony, sympathy, love, humility, and the refusal to repay evil with evil (3:8-12), grounded in a lengthy citation of Psalm 34:12-16 — the same psalm whose opening verse ("Taste and see that the Lord is good") was echoed in 2:3. The author then moves from ethical instruction to the theology of suffering (3:13-17), asking who will harm those who do good, and affirming that even if suffering comes for doing right, the community is blessed. This leads to the chapter's climactic passage (3:18-22), which grounds the community's experience in Christ's own suffering and vindication: Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, was put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit, proclaimed to spirits in prison from the days of Noah, and has now gone into heaven with all powers subject to him. The Noah reference introduces baptism (3:21) as the community's own passage through judgment-waters into salvation. The entire chapter thus moves from specific conduct instructions (wives, husbands, community) through the theology of innocent suffering to the cosmic scope of Christ's victory — the same trajectory as chapter 2, but expanded to include the full arc from earthly conduct to heavenly enthronement.

Verses 1-2: Wives and the Power of Conduct Without Words

"In the same way, you wives, be subject to your own husbands so that even if any of them are disobedient to the word, they may be won without a word by the behavior of their wives, as they observe your pure and reverent behavior" (3:1-2).

The phrase "in the same way" (homoios) is the structural signal that connects this instruction to the preceding sections. The author used the same connective principle in 2:18: servants were told to submit to their masters in continuity with the submission to governing authorities in 2:13. Now wives are told to submit to their husbands in continuity with both. The pattern is consistent: the community's posture in every social relationship is one of voluntary, ordered conduct — not because the structures they inhabit are inherently just, but because the Lord is served through such conduct (2:13) and because Christ provided the pattern (2:21). The verb "be subject" (hypotassomenai) is the same root (hypotasso) used in 2:13 (submit to human institutions) and 2:18 (servants, be subject to your masters). The consistency of vocabulary is deliberate: the same posture governs every sphere.

The specific situation the author envisions is striking: "even if any of them are disobedient to the word" (ei tines apeithousin to logo). The word apeithousin ("are disobedient, are unbelieving") is the same word used in 2:8 of those who "stumble because they are disobedient to the word" and in 2:7 of "the unbelieving" (apistousin/apeithousin). The author is addressing wives whose husbands have heard the gospel and refused it — or perhaps have not yet heard it, but are outside the community of faith. The phrase "the word" (to logo) is the same logos that has functioned throughout the letter as the gospel message: the "living and enduring word of God" (1:23), the "word which was preached to you" (1:25), the word that the disobedient stumble over (2:8). These husbands stand in relation to the word as the "disobedient" of 2:8 do: they have encountered it (or its effects in their wives' changed behavior) and have not submitted to it.

The author's instruction is not that wives should preach to their husbands or argue them into belief. The strategy is the opposite: "they may be won without a word by the behavior of their wives" (aneu logou dia tes ton gynaikon anastrophes kerdethesontai). The phrase "without a word" (aneu logou) creates a deliberate contrast with the "word" (logos) that the husbands are disobeying. The logos they resist when proclaimed verbally may reach them through a different channel — the anastrophe ("conduct, behavior, way of life") of their wives. The word anastrophe has appeared throughout the letter as a key term: "all your behavior" (1:15), "your futile way of life" (1:18), "your behavior excellent among the Gentiles" (2:12). It denotes the entire visible pattern of daily living, not isolated acts but the sustained, observable shape of a life. The verb kerdaino ("to win, to gain") is used in the sense of winning someone over, persuading them, bringing them to faith — the same usage appears in Matthew 18:15 ("you have won your brother") and 1 Corinthians 9:19-22, where Paul speaks of "winning" Jews, those under the law, those without law, and the weak.

The principle is the same as 2:12: good conduct among outsiders leads them to "glorify God on the day of visitation." The outsider in view here is not a pagan neighbor but the woman's own husband. The verb "as they observe" (epopteuontes) is the same word used in 2:12 (epopteuontes, "watching, being eyewitnesses of"). Outsiders observe the community's conduct and are changed by what they see. Here the observer is the husband, and what he observes is "pure and reverent behavior" (ten en phobo hagnen anastrophen hymon). The word hagne ("pure, chaste, morally clean") describes conduct free from corruption or impurity. The word phobos ("fear, reverence") here carries the same meaning as in 1:17 ("conduct yourselves in fear during the time of your stay") and 2:18 ("servants, be subject to your masters with all respect/fear") — not cowering terror but the reverential awareness of God that shapes all behavior. The wife's conduct is oriented toward God, not toward manipulating her husband, and it is this God-ward orientation that the husband observes and that may win him.

Verses 3-4: The Adornment That God Values

"Your adornment must not be merely external — braiding the hair, wearing gold jewelry, or putting on garments; but let it be the hidden person of the heart, with the imperishable quality of a gentle and quiet spirit, which is precious in the sight of God" (3:3-4).

The word "adornment" (kosmos) means "arrangement, decoration, order" — it is the root of the English "cosmetic." The author does not say external adornment is sinful; the construction "not... but" (ouch... alla) functions here not as absolute prohibition but as a comparison of priorities: the emphasis should fall not on external decoration but on internal character. The three items listed — braiding of hair (emplokes trichon), wearing gold jewelry (peritheseos chrysion), and putting on garments (endyseos himation) — represent the standard markers of wealth and status in the Roman world. Elaborate hairstyles, gold ornaments, and fine clothing were the visible signs of social position and were frequently subjects of moral commentary among both Jewish and Greco-Roman writers. The author is not introducing a novel concern; he is drawing on a widespread tradition that valued inner virtue over external display. Isaiah 3:18-24 catalogs the finery of the "daughters of Zion" — bangles, headbands, earrings, necklaces, veils, sashes — and declares that God will replace these with humiliation. The prophetic tradition consistently treated excessive adornment as evidence of misplaced values.

The contrast is "the hidden person of the heart" (ho kryptos tes kardias anthropos). The phrase is remarkable. The "hidden person" (kryptos anthropos) is the interior self that no one sees — the true person behind the visible exterior. Paul used a similar expression in Romans 2:29: "he is a Jew who is one inwardly (en to krypto)... circumcision is that which is of the heart, by the Spirit." The heart (kardia) is, as throughout the LXX, the seat of will, thought, intention, and character — the core of the person. The "hidden person of the heart" is what God sees when the external adornments are stripped away.

What constitutes this inner adornment is specified: "the imperishable quality of a gentle and quiet spirit" (en to aphtharto tou praeos kai hesychiou pneumatos). The word aphthartos ("imperishable, incorruptible") has appeared twice already in the letter — for the inheritance that is "imperishable" (1:4) and for the "imperishable seed" through which they were born again (1:23). The word creates a deliberate echo: just as the inheritance and the seed of new birth are imperishable, so the inner quality that God values is imperishable. The braided hair, the gold, the fine clothing — these are perishable, belonging to the order of "grass that withers and flower that falls off" (1:24). The gentle and quiet spirit belongs to the imperishable order.

The word praus ("gentle, meek") describes strength under control, power that does not assert itself aggressively. In the LXX, the praeis ("the meek") are those who trust God rather than forcing their own way: "The meek shall inherit the earth" (Psalm 37:11 LXX 36:11), the verse Jesus cited in Matthew 5:5. The word hesychios ("quiet, tranquil, peaceable") does not mean silent or passive but undisturbed, not agitated, not driven to anxious self-assertion. Together, "gentle and quiet" describes a spirit that is at rest because it trusts God's judgment rather than striving to control outcomes — precisely the disposition the author attributed to Christ in 2:23: "He did not threaten, but kept entrusting Himself to Him who judges righteously."

"Which is precious in the sight of God" (ho estin enopion tou theou polyteles) — the word polyteles means "of great value, very costly, precious." The parallel to 2:4 is exact: Christ as the living stone is "choice and precious in the sight of God" (para de theo eklekton entimon). The same evaluative structure governs both: human beings may value external things (rejecting the stone, admiring the gold), but God values what is internal, imperishable, and oriented toward trust in him. The gentle and quiet spirit is "precious in the sight of God" just as the rejected stone is "precious in the sight of God." The community's members are invited to share God's valuation rather than the world's.

Verses 5-6: Sarah as the Exemplar

"For in this way in former times the holy women also, who hoped in God, used to adorn themselves, being subject to their own husbands; just as Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord, and you have become her children if you do what is right without being frightened by any fear" (3:5-6).

The author reaches back into Israel's history for precedent. The "holy women" (hagiai gynaikes) who "hoped in God" (hai elpizousai eis theon) are the matriarchs and other women of the patriarchal narratives. The word hagiai ("holy") applies the same holiness vocabulary that the author has used of the community throughout the letter (1:15-16, "be holy, for I am holy"; 2:5, "holy priesthood"; 2:9, "holy nation"). These women were holy because they were set apart for God, and their hope (elpizousai) was directed toward God — the same posture the author commands in 1:13 ("fix your hope completely on the grace to be brought to you"). The verb "used to adorn themselves" (ekosmoun heautas) picks up the kosmos language of verse 3: their adornment was their conduct, their inner character, their trust in God. The participle "being subject to their own husbands" (hypotassomenai tois idiois andrasin) describes the form this adornment took within their domestic relationships — the same voluntary, ordered conduct the author has been advocating throughout.

Sarah is then singled out: "just as Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord" (hos Sarra hypekousen to Abraam kyrion auton kalousa). The reference is to Genesis 18:12 (LXX), where Sarah, upon hearing that she would bear a child in her old age, laughed and said: "After I have become old, shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?" The word "lord" (kyrios) in Sarah's speech is an address of respect, the normal way a wife referred to her husband in the ancient Near East. The author does not cite Sarah's laughter or her initial disbelief; he cites her use of the respectful address as evidence of her posture of ordered conduct within her marriage.

"And you have become her children if you do what is right without being frightened by any fear" (hes egenethete tekna agathopoiousai kai me phoboumenai medemian ptoesin). The verb egenethete ("you have become") is aorist — it describes something that has already happened. The women addressed in the letter have become Sarah's children (tekna) — they are now part of the matriarch's lineage, not by blood but by conduct. The condition is twofold: "doing what is right" (agathopoiousai) and "not being frightened by any fear" (me phoboumenai medemian ptoesin). The word agathopoieo ("to do good, to do what is right") is a key term in this letter — it appeared in 2:15 ("by doing right you silence the ignorance of foolish people"), 2:20 ("when you do what is right and suffer for it"), and will appear again in 3:17 ("it is better to suffer for doing good"). The word ptoesin ("fear, terror, intimidation") comes from ptoeo, which means "to be startled, to be terrified, to be thrown into panic." The phrase describes external intimidation — threats, pressure, social hostility — that would cause the women to abandon their course. The instruction is: do not let fear of what others might do to you change your conduct. This is the same principle that governs the entire letter: do good, do not retaliate, do not let suffering or intimidation drive you from the right path, and entrust yourself to God.

The echo of Proverbs 3:25 (LXX) may be present: "Do not be afraid of sudden terror (ptoesin)" — the same word ptoesin appears there. The matriarch Sarah, living in a foreign land with Abraham, faced genuine dangers (Genesis 12:10-20, 20:1-18) yet continued in faith. The author presents her as the prototype of the community's women: living among those who do not share their faith, potentially facing hostility, but continuing in good conduct without being paralyzed by fear.

The identity claim is significant. Just as the community as a whole has been given Israel's titles (chosen people, royal priesthood, holy nation, 2:9), the women in the community are given a specific lineage: they are Sarah's children. The matriarchal connection supplements the broader national-identity claim. The women are not merely members of a holy nation in general terms; they stand in a specific line of descent from the mother of the covenant people. This is identity through conduct, not through ethnicity — they "become" (egenethete) Sarah's children by doing what is right, not by birth.

Verse 7: Husbands and the Shared Inheritance

"You husbands in the same way, live with your wives in an understanding way, as with someone weaker, since she is a woman; and show her honor as a fellow heir of the grace of life, so that your prayers will not be hindered" (3:7).

The phrase "in the same way" (homoios) again links this instruction to the preceding chain: citizens (2:13), servants (2:18), wives (3:1), and now husbands. Each group receives instruction within the same framework of ordered, God-oriented conduct. The verb "live with" (synoikountes, literally "dwelling together with") describes the daily domestic reality of shared life. The phrase "in an understanding way" (kata gnosin) means "according to knowledge" — with awareness, insight, and consideration. The husband is to bring understanding (gnosis) to the relationship, not ignorance, insensitivity, or mere assertion of authority.

The phrase "as with someone weaker, since she is a woman" (hos asthenestero skeuei to gynaikeio) is more literally "as with a weaker vessel, the female one." The word skeuos ("vessel, instrument, container") is used metaphorically for a person — Paul used it in 1 Thessalonians 4:4 ("each of you know how to acquire his own vessel in holiness and honor") and in 2 Corinthians 4:7 ("we have this treasure in earthen vessels"). The word asthenestos ("weaker") refers to physical vulnerability in the ancient social context — women occupied a position of lesser physical strength and lesser legal protection in the Roman world. The author is not making a judgment about spiritual or intellectual capacity; he is naming a social and physical reality and commanding the husband to respond to it with consideration, not exploitation.

"And show her honor as a fellow heir of the grace of life" (aponemontes timen hos kai synkleronomois charitos zoes). The instruction to "show honor" (aponemontes timen) places the wife-husband relationship under the same principle that governed the community's relationship to all people in 2:17: "Honor all people." The word time ("honor, value, respect") is the same. But the grounds for honoring the wife go beyond the general command: she is a "fellow heir" (synkleronomos) of "the grace of life" (charitos zoes). The word synkleronomos means "co-inheritor, one who shares the same inheritance." The kleronomia ("inheritance") language connects to 1:4, where the inheritance was described as "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, reserved in heaven." The wife is not merely a dependent or subordinate; she is a co-heir of the same heavenly inheritance. Whatever social hierarchy the author's instructions may reflect, the theological reality is equality of inheritance. The husband and wife stand on the same ground before God as recipients of the same grace and heirs of the same life.

The phrase "the grace of life" (charitos zoes) combines two of the letter's most important concepts. "Grace" (charis) appeared in 1:2 ("grace and peace be multiplied"), 1:10 ("the grace that would come to you"), 1:13 ("the grace to be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ"), 2:19-20 (suffering for doing right "finds favor/grace"), and will continue as a major theme. "Life" (zoe) is the quality that characterizes everything in God's new order: "living hope" (1:3), "living word" (1:23), "living stone" (2:4), "living stones" (2:5). The "grace of life" is the gift of participation in God's own life — the eternal, imperishable order that the community has entered through new birth.

"So that your prayers will not be hindered" (eis to me enkoptesthai tas proseuchas hymon). The verb enkopto means "to cut into, to impede, to hinder, to block the way" (Paul used it in Romans 15:22, Galatians 5:7, 1 Thessalonians 2:18 for being "hindered" from travel or progress). The connection between domestic conduct and prayer is startling but consistent with the letter's logic. In 3:12, the author will cite Psalm 34:15: "the eyes of the Lord are toward the righteous, and His ears attend to their prayer." God listens to the prayers of the righteous — those who conduct themselves rightly. If a husband dishonors his wife, his prayers are obstructed. The relational dimension is not separable from the spiritual: how one treats a fellow heir of grace affects one's access to God. The community's life before God and its life among its members are a single fabric.

Verses 8-9: The Community's Unified Ethic

"To sum up, all of you be harmonious, sympathetic, loving as brothers and sisters, kindhearted, and humble in spirit; not returning evil for evil or insult for insult, but giving a blessing instead; for you were called for the very purpose that you would inherit a blessing" (3:8-9).

The phrase "to sum up" (to de telos) signals that the author is drawing together the specific instructions of 2:13-3:7 into a single comprehensive statement addressed to the entire community ("all of you," pantes). The preceding sections addressed particular groups: citizens, servants, wives, husbands. Now the author addresses everyone, and the virtues he commands are the communal equivalent of the individual instructions.

Five qualities are listed. "Harmonious" (homophrones) — literally "of the same mind, like-minded." The word does not mean uniform opinion on every matter but a shared orientation, a common purpose, minds moving in the same direction. "Sympathetic" (sympatheis) — "suffering with, sharing the feelings of" — the word from which "sympathy" derives. The community is to feel one another's pain and joy. "Loving as brothers and sisters" (philadelphoi) — the word philadelphia ("brotherly/sisterly love") appeared in 1:22 ("sincere love of the brothers and sisters") and describes the affection that characterizes a family. "Kindhearted" (eusplanchnoi) — literally "with good intestines," since the splanchna ("bowels, intestines") were considered the seat of deep emotion in the ancient world. The word means "compassionate, tenderhearted." "Humble in spirit" (tapeinophrones) — "lowly-minded, thinking humbly of oneself." The word tapeinophrosyne ("humility") was not a virtue in the Greco-Roman world; it was generally regarded as the quality of slaves and the weak. The early community transformed it into a central virtue, following the example of Christ who "humbled himself" (Philippians 2:8).

"Not returning evil for evil or insult for insult, but giving a blessing instead" (me apodidontes kakon anti kakou e loidorian anti loidorias, tounantion de eulogountes). This instruction recapitulates in communal terms what was said of Christ in 2:23: "while being abusively insulted, He did not insult in return" (loidoroumenos ouk anteloidorei). The word loidoria ("insult, verbal abuse, reviling") is the noun form of the verb used of Christ. The pattern is explicit: Christ did not return insult for insult; the community must not return insult for insult. Christ entrusted himself to the righteous Judge; the community is to give a blessing instead. The word eulogountes ("blessing") means literally "speaking well of" — the opposite of loidoria ("speaking abusively of"). Where the world repays evil with evil in an escalating cycle of retaliation, the community breaks the cycle by responding with blessing. This is not passive acquiescence but active, countercultural generosity.

"For you were called for the very purpose that you would inherit a blessing" (hoti eis touto eklethete hina eulogian kleronomesete). The verb "called" (eklethete) connects to the calling language that pervades the letter: 1:15 ("the Holy One who called you"), 2:9 ("Him who has called you out of darkness"), 2:21 ("you have been called for this purpose"), and 5:10 ("the God of all grace, who called you to His eternal glory"). The community was called — summoned by God into its new identity — and the purpose (eis touto, "for this very thing") of the calling includes both the giving of blessing to others and the inheriting of blessing from God. The word kleronomesete ("you would inherit") uses the same root as kleronomia ("inheritance") from 1:4. The community inherits blessing; therefore it gives blessing. The logic flows in one direction: those who have been blessed by God become blessers of others, and this activity of blessing is itself part of the inheritance they receive. The two are not in tension; they are the same movement seen from different angles.

Verses 10-12: The Psalm 34 Citation

"For, 'The one who desires life, to love and see good days, must keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit. He must turn away from evil and do good; he must seek peace and pursue it. For the eyes of the Lord are toward the righteous, and His ears attend to their prayer, but the face of the Lord is against those who do evil'" (3:10-12).

The author now grounds his ethical instruction in Psalm 34:12-16 (LXX 33:13-17). This is the same psalm whose opening verse (LXX 33:9, "Taste and see that the Lord is good") was echoed in 2:3 ("if you have tasted the kindness of the Lord"). The psalm has been operating as a background text throughout the letter's middle section, and now the author brings it fully to the surface, quoting it at length. The effect is to show that the conduct he has been commanding — doing good, avoiding evil, speaking without deceit, seeking peace — is not innovation but is rooted in Israel's own wisdom tradition.

"The one who desires life, to love and see good days" (ho gar thelon zoen agapan kai idein hemeras agathas) — the psalm addresses anyone who wants to experience a good life. The word zoen ("life") and the phrase "good days" (hemeras agathas) describe a life characterized by God's blessing. The community might well ask: given our suffering, slander, and social hostility, is a good life even possible for us? The psalm answers: yes, but the path to it runs through specific conduct.

"Must keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit" (pausato ten glossan autou apo kakou kai cheile autou tou me lalesai dolon). The control of speech is the first requirement. The word dolos ("deceit") has been tracked through the letter from the beginning. In 2:1, the community was told to "rid yourselves of all deceit" (dolon). In 2:22, Christ was described as the one in whose mouth "no deceit was found" (oude heurethe dolos en to stomati autou), citing Isaiah 53:9. Now Psalm 34 confirms the same principle: the good life requires lips free from deceit. The author has woven three threads together — the community's own purification from deceit, Christ's exemplary freedom from deceit, and the psalm's instruction to keep lips from deceit. The consistency is the author's commentary on the word's importance: dolos is incompatible with the life God offers at every level.

"He must turn away from evil and do good" (ekklinato apo kakou kai poiesato agathon) — the psalm states the ethical principle in both negative and positive terms. Turning away from evil is necessary but not sufficient; one must also actively do good. The word agathos ("good") connects to the repeated instruction to "do good" (agathopoieo) throughout the letter: 2:15, 2:20, 3:6, and the coming 3:17. The good-doing that the author has been commanding is not merely his own pastoral instruction but the psalm's own requirement for the good life.

"He must seek peace and pursue it" (zetesato eirenen kai dioxato auten) — peace (eirene) is not something that simply occurs; it must be sought (zetesato, "let him seek") and pursued (dioxato, "let him chase, run after, pursue"). The verb dioko means "to chase, to hunt, to pursue vigorously" — the same intensity used for pursuing a fugitive or hunting an animal. Peace requires aggressive effort, not passive waiting. This instruction has particular force for a community under social hostility: the natural response to persecution is to fight back or withdraw. The psalm commands neither; instead, it commands the active, energetic pursuit of peace — seeking reconciliation, extending goodwill, refusing the cycle of retaliation.

"For the eyes of the Lord are toward the righteous, and His ears attend to their prayer" (hoti ophthalmoi kyriou epi dikaious kai ota autou eis deesin auton) — the reason for the ethical instructions is now stated: God watches the righteous and listens to their prayers. The connection to 3:7 is immediate: husbands who dishonor their wives find their prayers "hindered"; the righteous find that God's ears are open to their prayers. The community's ethical conduct is not merely good strategy for social survival; it is the condition for access to God. The God who "impartially judges according to each one's work" (1:17) is the God whose eyes are on the righteous and whose ears are open to their prayers.

"But the face of the Lord is against those who do evil" (prosopon de kyriou epi poiountas kaka) — the contrast is stark. The same Lord whose eyes are turned toward the righteous turns his face against evildoers. The word prosopon ("face") carries the sense of God's presence and attention. When God's face is "toward" someone, it signifies favor and blessing (the Aaronic blessing: "The Lord make His face shine on you," Numbers 6:25). When God's face is "against" someone, it signifies opposition, judgment, and withdrawal of favor. The two outcomes — divine favor toward the righteous, divine opposition toward evildoers — mirror the two responses to the stone in 2:6-8: honor for those who believe, stumbling for those who disobey. The psalm confirms the letter's consistent pattern: the decisive factor is not one's social position or the world's evaluation but God's evaluation, and God evaluates on the basis of conduct.

Verses 13-14: Suffering for Doing Right

"Who is there to harm you if you prove zealous for what is good? But even if you should suffer for the sake of righteousness, you are blessed. 'And do not fear their intimidation, and do not be troubled'" (3:13-14).

The rhetorical question "Who is there to harm you if you prove zealous for what is good?" (kai tis ho kakoson hymas ean tou agathou zeletai genesthe) is not naively optimistic. The author has already acknowledged that the community is suffering (1:6, 2:12, 2:18-20, 3:1). The question functions within the framework the psalm has just established: if God's eyes are on the righteous and God's face is against evildoers, then ultimately no one can truly harm those who pursue good, because God is their protector. The word kakoson ("harming you") does not mean that no suffering will occur; it means that no ultimate, final harm can be inflicted. The word zelotai ("zealous, eager, passionate") is emphatic: not merely doing good half-heartedly but pursuing it with zeal and passion.

"But even if you should suffer for the sake of righteousness, you are blessed" (all ei kai paschoite dia dikaiosynen, makarioi). The optative mood (paschoite, "you should suffer") indicates a possibility, not a certainty — suffering for righteousness may or may not happen, but if it does, the community's status does not change. They are "blessed" (makarioi). The word makarios is the same word used in the beatitudes of Matthew 5 and Luke 6: "blessed are the poor in spirit," "blessed are those who mourn," "blessed are you when people insult you and persecute you." Jesus specifically said: "Blessed are those who have been persecuted for the sake of righteousness" (Matthew 5:10). The author of 1 Peter echoes this teaching directly: suffering for righteousness produces blessedness, not loss. The word makarios describes a state of divine favor — being in a position that God regards with approval, regardless of how the world regards it. The same reversal of values that pervades the letter appears here: the world sees suffering as misfortune; the author sees suffering for righteousness as blessedness.

"And do not fear their intimidation, and do not be troubled" (ton de phobon auton me phobethete mede tarachthete). This is drawn from Isaiah 8:12-13 (LXX). The original context is essential. In Isaiah 8, God warns Isaiah not to call conspiracy what "this people" calls conspiracy: "You are not to fear what they fear or be in dread of it. It is the LORD of hosts whom you should regard as holy. And He shall be your fear, and He shall be your dread" (Isaiah 8:12-13). The passage is from the same chapter that provided the "stone of stumbling and rock of offense" text quoted in 2:8 (Isaiah 8:14). The author is drawing on the same source: Isaiah 8 provides both the stumbling-stone image and the instruction not to fear human threats. In Isaiah's context, the "fear" in question was Judah's terror at the Syro-Ephraimite alliance threatening Jerusalem. God's message through Isaiah was: do not fear the political threats that terrify everyone else; fear God alone. The author of 1 Peter applies this to his community's situation: do not fear the intimidation (phobon) of those who threaten you; do not be thrown into confusion (tarachthete, "troubled, disturbed, agitated"). The only fear that should govern the community is the fear of God (1:17, 2:17).

Verses 15-16: The Defense of Hope

"But sanctify Christ as Lord in your hearts, always being ready to make a defense to everyone who asks you to give an account for the hope that is in you, but with gentleness and reverence; and keep a good conscience so that in the thing in which you are slandered, those who disparage your good behavior in Christ will be put to shame" (3:15-16).

"Sanctify Christ as Lord in your hearts" (kyrion de ton Christon hagiasate en tais kardiais hymon) — this continues the Isaiah 8 citation conceptually. Where Isaiah 8:13 reads "It is the LORD of hosts whom you should regard as holy" (or "sanctify"), the author replaces "the LORD of hosts" with "Christ as Lord." The verb hagiasate ("sanctify, set apart as holy, treat as holy") means to give Christ the position of supreme authority and reverence in the heart. The heart (kardia) is the inner person, the center of thought and will (as in 3:4, "the hidden person of the heart"). This is not an external confession but an internal orientation: Christ is to hold the position of ultimate authority in the believer's inner life, the place from which all decisions and responses flow. When Christ is sanctified as Lord in the heart, the fears that human threats generate lose their power.

"Always being ready to make a defense to everyone who asks you to give an account for the hope that is in you" (hetoimoi aei pros apologian panti to aitounti hymas logon peri tes en hymin elpidos). The word apologia ("defense") is a legal term — it describes the defense speech given by an accused person in a court of law (Acts 22:1, 25:16; 2 Timothy 4:16; Philippians 1:7, 16). The community should be prepared to explain themselves — not aggressively, not on the attack, but in response to those who ask (aitounti, "asking, requesting"). The word logon ("account, reason, explanation") indicates a rational, articulate explanation, not an emotional outburst. What they are to explain is "the hope that is in you" (tes en hymin elpidos) — the "living hope" of 1:3, the hope "fixed completely on the grace to be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1:13). The community's hope is visibly different from the hopes of the surrounding society, and people notice. The instruction is: when they ask why you live the way you do, why you endure suffering without retaliation, why you are not afraid — be ready to explain.

"But with gentleness and reverence" (alla meta prautetos kai phobou). The word prautetos ("gentleness, meekness") is the noun form of praus ("gentle"), the same quality commended in 3:4 as precious in God's sight. The word phobou ("reverence, fear") is the reverential awareness of God that has governed the letter from 1:17 onward. The defense is to be given with humility, not arrogance; with respect, not contempt. The community's verbal testimony is to match its behavioral testimony — gentle, reverent, non-retaliatory.

"And keep a good conscience" (syneidesein echontes agathen) — the word syneidesis ("conscience, consciousness, inner awareness") appeared in 2:19 ("conscience toward God"). A "good conscience" (agathe syneidesis) is the inner assurance that one's conduct has been right — not self-righteousness but the settled knowledge that one has acted with integrity. This phrase will recur in 3:21 in connection with baptism ("an appeal to God for a good conscience"), tying the ethical instruction here to the baptismal passage at the chapter's end.

"So that in the thing in which you are slandered, those who disparage your good behavior in Christ will be put to shame" (hina en ho katalalelsthe kataischynthosin hoi epereazontes hymon ten agathen en Christo anastrophen). The logic is identical to 2:12 and 2:15: good conduct silences and shames the accusers. The verb katalaleo ("to speak against, to slander") is the same root as the katalalia ("slander") that the community was told to strip off in 2:1. The word epereazein ("to insult, to abuse, to maltreat") describes those who attack the community verbally. The "good behavior in Christ" (agathen en Christo anastrophen) is the conduct shaped by and rooted in Christ — the anastrophe ("behavior, way of life") that has been the letter's constant concern. Those who slander this conduct will be "put to shame" (kataischynthosin) — proved wrong, exposed as false accusers, humiliated by the truth of the community's actual behavior. The word kataischyno echoes 2:6, where the one who believes in the cornerstone "will not be put to shame." The believing community is not shamed; its accusers are.

Verse 17: The Principle Stated

"For it is better, if God should will it so, that you suffer for doing what is right rather than for doing what is wrong" (3:17).

This verse distills the entire argument of the letter's ethical section into a single principle. The word "better" (kreitton) is a comparative: there are two kinds of suffering, and one is better than the other. Suffering for doing right (agathopoiountas, "doing good") is better than suffering for doing wrong (kakopoiountas, "doing evil"). The contrast was made in 2:20: "what credit is there if, when you sin and are harshly treated, you endure it with patience? But if when you do what is right and suffer for it you patiently endure it, this finds favor with God." The same two-category framework applies here.

The phrase "if God should will it so" (ei theloi to thelema tou theou) — literally, "if the will of God should will it" — places suffering under divine sovereignty. Suffering for doing right is not random misfortune; it falls within God's will (thelema). The word thelema ("will") has appeared in 2:15 ("such is the will of God, that by doing right you silence the ignorance of foolish people") and will appear again in 4:2 and 4:19 ("those who suffer according to the will of God shall entrust their souls to a faithful Creator"). The author does not explain why God's will sometimes includes suffering for the righteous. He simply affirms that it does, and that when it does, the suffering is "better" than the alternative. The optative mood (theloi, "should will") indicates possibility, not certainty: God may or may not will suffering in any given case. The community is not promised suffering; it is prepared for it.

Verses 18-19: Christ's Suffering and Proclamation

"For Christ also died for sins once for all, the just for the unjust, so that He might bring us to God, having been put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit; in which also He went and made proclamation to the spirits now in prison" (3:18-19).

This is among the most densely packed and widely discussed passages in the letter. The conjunction "for" (hoti) connects it to verse 17: the principle that suffering for doing right is better than suffering for doing wrong is grounded in Christ's own experience. Christ is the supreme instance of the righteous one who suffered unjustly, and his suffering accomplished its purpose.

"Christ also died for sins once for all" (Christos hapax peri hamartion epathen/apethanen). The word hapax ("once for all, once and for all time") emphasizes the unrepeatable, definitive character of Christ's death. It occurred once and does not need repetition. The preposition peri ("concerning, for, on account of") with "sins" (hamartion) indicates that Christ's death addressed the problem of sins — the same concept expressed in 2:24 ("He Himself brought our sins in His body up on the cross"). The two statements are complementary: in 2:24, Christ bore sins; here, Christ died for sins. The "once for all" language echoes the Day of Atonement imagery from Israel's cult, where the high priest entered the holy of holies once a year (Leviticus 16), but Christ's offering need not be repeated.

"The just for the unjust" (dikaios hyper adikon) — the substitutionary pattern is stated with maximum economy. The word dikaios ("righteous, just") describes Christ; the word adikon ("unrighteous, unjust") describes those for whom he died. The preposition hyper ("on behalf of, for the sake of") indicates substitution, as in 2:21 ("Christ also suffered for you," hyper hymon). Christ's death was not his own punishment but his bearing of the unjust's penalty. The righteous one stood in the place of the unrighteous.

"So that He might bring us to God" (hina hymas prosagago to theo) — the purpose (hina, "in order that") of Christ's suffering is stated: to bring the community to God. The verb prosago ("to bring to, to lead to, to present before") has technical significance. In the LXX, it is used for bringing offerings to God (Leviticus 1:2, 3:7, 8:14) and for bringing people into God's presence (Exodus 29:4, 40:12). It is also the word used for introducing someone into the presence of a king or dignitary. Christ's death creates access — it brings people who were far from God (the "unjust") into God's presence. This is the priestly function: the priest brings the worshiper and the offering before God. Christ, as both the offering (the lamb of 1:19) and the one who offers (the priest who brings the community to God), fulfills the entire sacrificial system in a single act.

"Having been put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit" (thanatotheis men sarki zoopoietheis de pneumati) — the two participles describe two contrasting events. Christ was "put to death" (thanatotheis, passive — he was killed by others) "in the flesh" (sarki) — in his physical, bodily existence. But he was "made alive" (zoopoietheis, passive — God made him alive) "in the spirit" (pneumati). The dative forms sarki and pneumati indicate the sphere or domain: in the sphere of flesh, he died; in the sphere of spirit, he was made alive. This does not mean Christ became a disembodied spirit; rather, it describes the contrast between the mode of his death (physical, fleshly) and the mode of his resurrection (by the power of the Spirit, in the spiritual realm). The passive voice of zoopoietheis ("was made alive") points to God as the agent — the same God "who raised Him from the dead and gave Him glory" (1:21).

"In which also He went and made proclamation to the spirits now in prison" (en ho kai tois en phylake pneumasin poreutheis ekeruxen). This verse has been the subject of extensive discussion since antiquity. The question is: who are these spirits, when did Christ proclaim to them, and what did he proclaim?

The phrase "in which" (en ho) can mean "in which [spirit]" — referring back to the pneuma in which Christ was made alive — or "in which [connection/time]" — meaning "in this state of being made alive." Either reading indicates that the proclamation happened after or in connection with Christ's being made alive in the spirit.

"He went" (poreutheis) indicates movement — Christ traveled to a location. The word "spirits" (pneumasin) without further qualification most naturally refers to supernatural beings, not deceased humans. In the LXX and in Jewish literature of the Second Temple period, "spirits" (pneumata) used absolutely regularly refers to angelic or demonic beings. In 1 Enoch — a text widely known and influential in first-century Judaism — the angels who transgressed in Genesis 6:1-4 are called "spirits" and are described as being imprisoned and awaiting judgment (1 Enoch 10:4-6, 12-13; 12:4; 15:8-10; 18:14-16; 19:1; 21:10). The "prison" (phylake) in which these spirits are held corresponds to the place of confinement described in 1 Enoch for the fallen angels: a place of detention where they are kept until the final judgment. The Book of Jubilees (5:6-10) similarly describes the binding and imprisonment of the angels who transgressed.

"Made proclamation" (ekeruxen) — the verb kerusso means "to herald, to proclaim, to announce publicly." It does not necessarily mean "to preach the gospel" or "to offer salvation." A kerux ("herald") announces a message from a sovereign; the content of the announcement is not specified by the verb itself. The content must be determined from context. Given that these are imprisoned spirits who transgressed, the proclamation is most naturally understood as an announcement of Christ's victory — the triumphant declaration by the risen, vindicated Christ to the rebellious powers that their judgment is confirmed and his authority is established. This reading is consistent with the letter's climactic statement in 3:22: Christ "has gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God, having angels and authorities and powers subjected to him." The proclamation to the imprisoned spirits and the subjection of all powers are two aspects of the same event: Christ's cosmic victory over every opposing force.

Verse 20: The Days of Noah

"Who once were disobedient, when the patience of God kept waiting in the days of Noah, during the construction of the ark, in which a few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through the water" (3:20).

The relative clause identifies the imprisoned spirits more precisely: they are the ones who "were disobedient" (apeithesasin, the same verb used of those who disobey the word in 2:8 and 3:1) "in the days of Noah." This confirms the connection to the Genesis 6 tradition. In Genesis 6:1-4, "the sons of God" (bene ha-elohim, rendered in the LXX as hoi angeloi tou theou in some manuscripts, and hoi huioi tou theou in others) saw the daughters of men and took them as wives. This episode immediately precedes the flood narrative and is presented in Genesis as part of the corruption that provoked God's judgment. The Jewish interpretive tradition represented by 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and other Second Temple texts understood these "sons of God" as angelic beings whose transgression was a major cause of the corruption that led to the flood. The author of 2 Peter (2:4-5) also connects the imprisoned angels with the flood: "God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into chains of darkness, reserving them for judgment; and did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah, a herald of righteousness, with seven others." Jude 6 similarly: "angels who did not keep their own domain, but abandoned their proper abode, He has kept in eternal chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day." The author of 1 Peter draws on this same tradition.

"When the patience of God kept waiting" (hote apexedecheto he tou theou makrothymia) — God's patience (makrothymia, "long-suffering, forbearance, patient endurance") is highlighted. During the days of Noah, God waited before sending judgment. The verb apekdechomai means "to wait eagerly, to wait patiently, to expect." God was not hasty; he gave time. This waiting period corresponded with "the construction of the ark" (kataskeuazomenes kibotou) — while Noah built, God waited. The tradition preserved in 2 Peter 2:5 describes Noah as "a herald of righteousness," suggesting that the period of construction was also a period of warning. God's patience meant that the disobedient had time to repent before judgment came.

"In which a few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through the water" (eis hen oligoi, tout estin okto psychai, diesothesan di hydatos). The word oligoi ("few") is emphatic: out of the entire world, only a few were saved. The number eight (okto) is specified: Noah, his wife, his three sons, and their wives (Genesis 7:13). The verb diesothesan ("were brought safely through") is from diasozo, meaning "to bring safely through, to rescue, to deliver through a dangerous passage." The preposition dia ("through") with hydatos ("water") is significant: they were saved through the water, not from the water. The water was the medium of both judgment (it destroyed the disobedient world) and salvation (it carried the ark and its occupants to safety). The same element — water — functioned in two opposite ways, depending on one's relationship to it. Those in the ark were brought through; those outside the ark were destroyed.

The typological structure is clear. The community addressed in the letter is in the position of Noah and his family: a minority within a hostile world, faithful when the majority is disobedient, brought safely through the medium that judges the world. The "few" who were saved parallels the community's own experience as a small, persecuted minority. The world's disobedience parallels the disobedience of those who reject the word (2:8, 3:1). God's patience parallels the present period in which the community suffers while God withholds final judgment.

Verse 21: Baptism as the Antitype

"Corresponding to that, baptism now saves you — not the removal of dirt from the flesh, but an appeal to God for a good conscience — through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (3:21).

The word "corresponding to that" (ho kai hymas antitypon) introduces a typological relationship. The word antitypon ("antitype, corresponding figure, counterpart") means that baptism stands in relation to the flood as the fulfillment stands to the type. As Noah's family was brought safely through water, so the community is brought safely through baptism. The flood-water saved Noah; baptism saves "you" (hymas).

The statement "baptism now saves you" (baptisma nun soizei) is direct: baptism is a saving event. But the author immediately qualifies what he means and does not mean. "Not the removal of dirt from the flesh" (ou sarkos apothesis rhypou) — baptism is not a bath. The physical washing of the body is not what saves. The word rhypos ("dirt, filth") and the phrase "removal" (apothesis, the same word used metaphorically in 2:1 for "putting aside" vices) describe what baptism is not: a merely external, physical cleansing.

"But an appeal to God for a good conscience" (alla syneideseos agathes eperotema eis theon) — what baptism is. The word eperotema is debated. It can mean "a pledge, a commitment, a vow" (in the sense of a formal answer to a question, as in a contract) or "a request, an appeal, a prayer" (in the sense of asking God for something). In either reading, the direction is the same: baptism involves a conscious, deliberate engagement with God — either a pledge of a good conscience toward God, or a request to God for a good conscience. The word syneideseos agathes ("good conscience") connects to 3:16 ("keep a good conscience") and to 2:19 ("conscience toward God"). The conscience — the inner awareness of right and wrong before God — is central. Baptism is not magic; it is a relational act in which the person being baptized engages God with conscience and commitment.

"Through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (di anastaseos Iesou Christou) — the saving power of baptism operates through Christ's resurrection, not through the water itself. This connects to 1:3, where the community was "born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead." The resurrection is the event that gives baptism its power, just as it is the event that generates the living hope and the new birth. Without the resurrection, baptism would be merely a bath; with the resurrection, it is a passage through judgment-waters into new life.

The typological structure is now complete: as the flood waters were the medium through which Noah's family passed from the old, corrupt world into a new, cleansed world, so baptism is the medium through which the community passes from its former life (the "futile way of life inherited from your forefathers," 1:18) into the new life of hope, holiness, and good conscience. The water is the same element functioning in both events — as judgment on the disobedient and as salvation for the faithful. The "few" who are saved through it (3:20, oligoi) correspond to the community of believers within a hostile world.

Verse 22: Christ's Cosmic Authority

"Who is at the right hand of God, having gone into heaven, after angels and authorities and powers had been subjected to Him" (3:22).

This verse is the climax of the passage that began at 3:18. The entire movement of 3:18-22 traces Christ's journey: he suffered and died (v. 18a), was made alive in the spirit (v. 18b), proclaimed to the imprisoned spirits (v. 19), and now has gone into heaven (v. 22a) and sits at God's right hand (v. 22b) with all powers subjected to him (v. 22c). The movement is from death to life to proclamation to enthronement — a complete trajectory from the lowest point (suffering and death) to the highest point (the right hand of God, supreme over all powers).

"Who is at the right hand of God" (hos estin en dexia theou) — the phrase draws on Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1): "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet.'" This psalm was the most frequently cited Old Testament text in the early community, applied to Christ's exaltation and session at God's right hand (Acts 2:34-35; Romans 8:34; Ephesians 1:20; Colossians 3:1; Hebrews 1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2). The "right hand" is the position of supreme honor, authority, and power — the place from which a king rules.

"Having gone into heaven" (poreutheis eis ouranon) — the verb poreutheis ("having gone") is the same verb used in 3:19 ("He went and made proclamation"). Christ's movement is traced with a single verb: he went (poreutheis) to the imprisoned spirits, and he went (poreutheis) into heaven. The journey encompasses the entire cosmos — from the prison of the rebellious spirits to the throne of God. No location is outside Christ's reach or authority.

"After angels and authorities and powers had been subjected to Him" (hypotagenton auto angelon kai exousion kai dynameon). The verb hypotagenton (from hypotasso, "to subject, to place under, to subordinate") is the same root used for the submission of citizens (2:13), servants (2:18), and wives (3:1). The same word that described the community's voluntary submission to human structures now describes the involuntary subjection of cosmic powers to Christ. The community submits willingly; the powers are subjected by divine authority. The three terms — "angels" (angelon), "authorities" (exousion), and "powers" (dynameon) — represent the full range of supernatural beings and forces. Whether hostile or friendly, whether the imprisoned spirits of verse 19 or any other powers in the cosmos, all are now under Christ's authority.

This verse serves a pastoral purpose that extends beyond theological instruction. The community is suffering at the hands of human authorities and social structures. The author has told them to submit to kings and governors (2:13-14), to masters (2:18), and to follow the pattern of Christ who did not retaliate (2:23). But the authority of these human structures is not ultimate. Christ is above all authorities and powers. The human institutions to which the community submits exist under the overarching rule of the one who sits at God's right hand. The community's submission to earthly authorities is temporary and strategic; Christ's supremacy over all powers is permanent and total. This is the theological ground that makes the community's posture of non-retaliation sustainable: they can afford to be gentle, patient, and non-retaliatory because the final authority belongs to Christ, not to their persecutors.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

The chapter moves through four interconnected stages:

First (3:1-7), the continuation of ordered-conduct instructions within specific relationships. Wives in mixed-faith marriages are instructed to pursue the conversion of disobedient husbands through observable conduct rather than verbal argument, following the precedent of Sarah and the holy women who hoped in God. Their true adornment is the imperishable quality of a gentle and quiet spirit, which God values. Husbands are to live with their wives in understanding, honoring them as co-heirs of the same grace, with the warning that failure to do so obstructs their own prayers. The domestic sphere is not a realm separate from the spiritual life; it is a primary arena in which the community's holiness is practiced and tested.

Second (3:8-12), the gathering of all the specific instructions into a unified communal ethic. The entire community is called to harmony, sympathy, love, compassion, humility, and the refusal to repay evil with evil. Instead, they are to bless — because they were called to inherit a blessing. The lengthy citation of Psalm 34:12-16 grounds this ethic in Israel's wisdom tradition: the path to life runs through controlled speech, active good-doing, and the vigorous pursuit of peace. God's eyes are on the righteous and his ears are open to their prayers; his face is against evildoers. The psalm confirms that the letter's ethical instructions are not arbitrary but are the conditions for a life that stands under God's favor.

Third (3:13-17), the theology of innocent suffering. The author affirms that zealous pursuit of good provides genuine protection, but acknowledges that suffering for righteousness may still occur — and if it does, the sufferer is blessed. The community must not fear human intimidation but must sanctify Christ as Lord in their hearts, always ready to explain their hope with gentleness and reverence, maintaining a good conscience that will put their accusers to shame. The governing principle is stated in verse 17: it is better to suffer for doing right than for doing wrong, if God wills it.

Fourth (3:18-22), the christological and cosmic foundation. Christ himself suffered — the righteous for the unrighteous — to bring the community to God. Put to death in the flesh, he was made alive in the spirit and went to proclaim his victory to the imprisoned spirits who had been disobedient in the days of Noah. The flood narrative provides the type: as Noah's family was brought safely through water, so baptism now saves the community — not as an external washing but as a commitment of good conscience toward God, powered by Christ's resurrection. Christ has now gone into heaven and sits at God's right hand, with every angel, authority, and power subjected to him.

The chapter's unity lies in the relationship between earthly conduct and cosmic reality. The community's gentle, non-retaliatory conduct in the face of suffering (3:1-17) is grounded in the cosmic victory of Christ over all opposing powers (3:18-22). The community can afford to be gentle because Christ is supreme. The community can endure suffering without fear because the one who suffered most unjustly — the righteous for the unrighteous — has been vindicated and enthroned above all powers. The same word (hypotasso) that describes the community's voluntary submission to human structures describes the involuntary subjection of cosmic powers to Christ. The community's posture is not weakness but participation in the pattern of the one who was put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit — who descended to the realm of imprisoned spirits and ascended to the right hand of God.

The flood typology in verses 20-21 reinforces the letter's recurring pattern of minority faithfulness within a hostile majority. Noah's family — eight people out of the entire world — was brought safely through the waters that judged the disobedient majority. The community of faith is in the same position: a few, surrounded by the disobedient, sustained by God's patience, brought through judgment-waters into new life. Baptism is the community's passage through the flood — the moment when they entered the ark of Christ's death and resurrection, passing from the old world into the new. The good conscience they pledged in baptism is the same good conscience they are to maintain under persecution (3:16), and the same good conscience that will shame their accusers when the truth of their conduct becomes evident.
