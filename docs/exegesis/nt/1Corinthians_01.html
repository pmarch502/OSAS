1 Corinthians 1 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

The document's genre and occasion: This is a letter (epistole) from Paul to a community he personally founded. Unlike Romans, where Paul wrote to introduce himself and his gospel to a community he had never visited, this letter addresses people Paul knows firsthand. According to the narrative of Acts 18:1-11, Paul came to Corinth from Athens, worked with Aquila and Priscilla as tentmakers, proclaimed the message in the synagogue every Sabbath, and then — after opposition from some in the synagogue — moved next door to the house of Titius Justus, a God-fearer. The Lord spoke to Paul in a night vision: "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many people in this city" (Acts 18:9-10). Paul stayed in Corinth eighteen months, "teaching the word of God among them" (Acts 18:11). The Corinthian community was Paul's work. He laid the foundation (3:10).

Corinth itself was a Roman colony, refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BCE after lying in ruins for a century following its destruction by Rome in 146 BCE. By Paul's day it was the capital of the province of Achaia, a commercial crossroads connecting the Aegean and Adriatic seas, a city of significant wealth, cultural diversity, and social stratification. It was also a city dominated by Greek cultural values — rhetoric, philosophical debate, competition for honor and status. These characteristics of Corinthian culture will prove essential to understanding the problems Paul addresses.

The occasion for writing is specific. Paul has received information from multiple sources. "Chloe's people" (1:11) have reported quarreling. The community has also apparently written Paul a letter with questions (7:1: "Now concerning the matters about which you wrote"). The letter therefore responds to both reports and inquiries, addressing a range of problems: factionalism (chapters 1-4), sexual immorality (chapter 5), lawsuits among believers (chapter 6), marriage and celibacy (chapter 7), food offered to idols (chapters 8-10), worship practice and head coverings (chapter 11), spiritual gifts and their use in assembly (chapters 12-14), and the reality of bodily resurrection (chapter 15). The letter is not a theological treatise; it is pastoral intervention in a community that Paul founded and that has gone badly wrong in multiple areas.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 2): Paul continues the argument about wisdom and the cross by turning to his own example. He reminds the Corinthians that when he first came to them, he did not come "proclaiming to you the testimony of God with lofty speech or wisdom" (2:1). Instead, "I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (2:2). He came "in weakness and in fear and much trembling" (2:3), and his message was not delivered "in plausible words of wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power" (2:4). The purpose: "so that your faith might not rest in the wisdom of men but in the power of God" (2:5). Paul then introduces a distinction between the wisdom of this age and a hidden wisdom of God that is revealed through the Spirit (2:6-16). The Spirit searches everything, "even the depths of God" (2:10), and the "spiritual person" (pneumatikos) judges all things but is judged by no one (2:15). The chapter closes: "But we have the mind of Christ" (2:16). Chapter 2 thus deepens the argument of chapter 1 by demonstrating that the rejection of worldly wisdom does not mean the rejection of all wisdom. There is a wisdom, but it is God's wisdom, revealed by the Spirit, and it is the wisdom of the cross — not the wisdom of rhetorical performance.

How chapter 1 connects to what follows: Chapter 1 performs three functions that the rest of the letter depends on. First, the opening and thanksgiving (1:1-9) establish the identity of the community: they are "the assembly of God" (ekklesia tou theou), sanctified, called, enriched with gifts, waiting for the Lord's revealing. This identity is defined by God's action, not by human achievement or affiliation. Second, the exposure of the faction problem (1:10-17) diagnoses the disease: the community has divided itself along the lines of human leaders, treating the gospel as a platform for competitive loyalty. This diagnosis governs not just chapters 1-4 but the entire letter, since nearly every problem Paul addresses — lawsuits, worship disorder, the abuse of spiritual gifts — is rooted in the same competitive, status-seeking mentality. Third, the argument about the cross and wisdom (1:18-31) provides the theological foundation for everything that follows. If God's power operates through apparent weakness and God's wisdom operates through apparent foolishness, then every form of human boasting and status competition is exposed as contrary to the gospel. The Corinthians' problems are not merely social failures; they are theological failures — a misunderstanding of what the cross reveals about how God works.

Verses 1-3: The Opening — Sender, Recipients, and Greeting

"Paul, called to be an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, and our brother Sosthenes" (1:1).

Paul identifies himself with the same language he used in Romans 1:1. He is "called" (kletos) — summoned by divine initiative, not self-appointed. The word kletos places Paul in the line of those whom God summoned for specific tasks: Abraham called from Ur (Genesis 12:1), Moses from the bush (Exodus 3:4), Isaiah in the temple (Isaiah 6:8-9), Jeremiah before his birth (Jeremiah 1:5). The calling is "by the will of God" (dia thelematos theou), removing any suggestion that Paul's authority rests on human decision or personal ambition. His apostleship originates in God's will, not his own.

The co-sender is "our brother Sosthenes" (Sosthenes ho adelphos). In Acts 18:17, after Gallio dismissed the charges brought against Paul by the synagogue in Corinth, "they all seized Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, and beat him in front of the tribunal." If this is the same Sosthenes — and the unusual name and Corinthian connection make this plausible — then a man who was once the leader of the Corinthian synagogue is now Paul's associate, addressed as "brother." The Jewish synagogue ruler has become a member of the community he may once have opposed. The Corinthians would recognize the name and the transformation it represents.

"To the church of God that is in Corinth, to those sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints, together with all those who in every place call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, both their Lord and ours" (1:2).

The recipients are described with extraordinary precision, and every element bears weight.

"The church of God" (te ekklesia tou theou) — the word ekklesia in the LXX translates the Hebrew qahal, the formal assembly of Israel gathered before God. Deuteronomy 23:1-8 legislates who may enter "the assembly of the LORD" (ekklesia kyriou). 1 Chronicles 28:8 uses the phrase "the assembly of the LORD" (ekklesia kyriou) for all Israel gathered in the presence of God. Nehemiah 13:1 uses it for the assembly that hears the Torah read. The word carries the weight of Israel's identity as God's assembled people. Paul applies this term to a community of mostly Gentile believers in a Greek commercial city. But the genitive is critical: this is not "the church of Corinth" or "the church of Paul." It is "the church of God" (tou theou). The assembly belongs to God. This possessive will prove to be the first blow against the factionalism Paul is about to address. Those who say "I am of Paul" or "I am of Apollos" have already been told, in the greeting, whose they actually are.

"That is in Corinth" (te ouse en Korintho) — the participle ouse ("being, existing") with the locative "in Corinth" identifies the assembly's location without defining its identity by that location. The assembly of God exists in Corinth, but it is not constituted by Corinth. It belongs to God and happens to be located in this city.

"To those sanctified in Christ Jesus" (hegiasmenois en Christo Iesou) — a perfect passive participle. "Sanctified" (hegiasmenois, from hagiazein) means set apart, consecrated, made holy. The perfect tense indicates a completed action with ongoing results: they have been sanctified and remain in that sanctified state. The passive voice identifies the action as done to them, not by them. The agent of sanctification is God, and the sphere in which it occurred is "in Christ Jesus" (en Christo Iesou). In the LXX, hagiazein describes the consecration of priests (Exodus 28:41, 29:1), the tabernacle (Exodus 29:44), the Sabbath (Genesis 2:3), and Israel itself. Leviticus 20:8: "I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (ego kyrios ho hagiazein hymas). The Corinthians, for all their problems — and the problems are severe, as the letter will reveal — have been consecrated by God's action. This is not a statement about their moral achievement; it is a statement about God's claim on them. They are God's holy possession. The irony that Paul will exploit throughout the letter is the gap between their consecrated status and their actual behavior.

"Called to be saints" (kletois hagiois) — identical to the phrase in Romans 1:7. The word hagioi ("holy ones, saints") in the LXX refers to those who belong to God's sacred sphere. They are "called" (kletois) — summoned — into this status. The repetition of the calling vocabulary (kletos in v. 1, kletois in v. 2) binds Paul and the Corinthians together: both owe their identity to God's summons, not to their own initiative.

"Together with all those who in every place call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (syn pasin tois epikaloumenois to onoma tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou en panti topo) — this phrase explodes the boundaries of the letter. The Corinthian community is not an isolated unit. It exists "together with" (syn) every community everywhere that calls on the name of Jesus. The phrase "call upon the name" (epikaleisthai to onoma) is loaded with scriptural resonance. Joel 2:32 (LXX 3:5): "And it shall come to pass that everyone who calls upon the name of the LORD shall be saved" (pas hos an epikalesetai to onoma kyriou sothesetai). Peter quoted this text at Pentecost (Acts 2:21). In Joel, the name called upon is YHWH. Here, without argument or explanation, the name called upon is "our Lord Jesus Christ." The Joel formula — which defined Israel's relationship to YHWH — is transferred to Jesus as a matter of established practice. The Corinthians are part of a worldwide community defined by this act of invocation.

"Both their Lord and ours" (auton kai hemon) — the same Lord belongs to every community and to this one. The unity of lordship is the foundation of community unity. If the Corinthians share the same Lord with all believers everywhere, the division into competing factions around human leaders is not merely socially destructive; it contradicts the basic structure of their identity.

"Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (1:3).

The standard Pauline greeting, combining the Greek epistolary convention (chairein, "greetings," transformed into charis, "grace") with the Hebrew greeting (shalom, rendered eirene, "peace"). The source of both is identified: "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." God and Jesus are placed side by side as co-source of grace and peace, a pairing that would be extraordinary to anyone trained in Israel's strict monotheism, yet which Paul states without defense, as though the point is already settled.

Verses 4-9: Thanksgiving — God's Gifts and God's Faithfulness

"I give thanks to my God always for you because of the grace of God that was given you in Christ Jesus" (1:4).

Paul's thanksgivings in his letters typically signal the major themes that will follow, and this one is no exception. He thanks God — not the Corinthians themselves — for "the grace of God that was given you" (te chariti tou theou te dotheise hymin). Grace is a gift (dotheise, from didomi, "to give"), and it was given "in Christ Jesus" (en Christo Iesou). Everything the Corinthians possess spiritually is a gift from God received in the sphere of Christ. This is not incidental phrasing; it is the theological foundation Paul will use to dismantle their boasting. If everything is a gift, no one has grounds for self-congratulation. Paul will make this logic explicit in 4:7: "What do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?"

"That in every way you were enriched in him in all speech and all knowledge" (1:5).

"Enriched" (eploutisthete) — aorist passive. They were made rich by God's action. The wealth is specified: "in all speech" (en panti logo) and "in all knowledge" (pase gnosei). Both logos and gnosis will become contested terms in this letter. The Corinthians clearly prized rhetorical ability and knowledge — the very things that fuel the factionalism Paul is about to address. Paul affirms these as genuine gifts of God's grace. He does not deny that the Corinthians have been enriched; he will challenge what they have done with their enrichment. The gifts are real; the problem is how they are being deployed.

"Even as the testimony about Christ was confirmed among you" (1:6).

"The testimony about Christ" (to martyrion tou Christou) — the proclamation concerning Christ. "Confirmed" (ebebaiothe) — the verb bebaioo means to make firm, validate, establish, guarantee. In Greek legal and commercial usage, bebaiosis was the guarantee that a seller provided for a transaction — the assurance that the sale was valid and would hold. The gospel was validated and established among the Corinthians — by the evidence of the Spirit's work, by the gifts that accompanied their reception of the message, by the transformation of their lives. The passive voice attributes the confirming to God.

"So that you are not lacking in any gift, as you wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:7).

"Not lacking in any gift" (me hysterousthai en medeni charismati) — the Corinthians possess the full range of spiritual gifts (charismata). This will be confirmed elaborately in chapters 12-14, where Paul addresses the diversity and proper use of gifts in the assembly. The community is overflowing with divine endowment. The irony is sharp: a community lacking nothing in gifts is tearing itself apart. The problem in Corinth is never the absence of divine provision; it is the misuse and misunderstanding of what has been abundantly given.

"As you wait for" (apekdechomenous) — the present middle participle describes an ongoing posture of expectation. The community exists in a state of anticipation, oriented toward a future event. The object of that expectation is "the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (ten apokalypsin tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou). The word apokalypsis means "unveiling, uncovering, disclosure" — the moment when what is presently hidden becomes visible. The community lives between the gifts already received and the Lord not yet fully manifested. This "already/not yet" structure governs the entire letter: the Corinthians have been sanctified, enriched, and gifted, but the final revelation has not yet occurred. They live in the interval, and how they live in that interval matters.

"Who will also sustain you to the end, guiltless in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:8).

"Who" (hos) — the antecedent is most naturally "our Lord Jesus Christ" from the preceding clause, though the logic connects to God's action throughout the thanksgiving. "Sustain" (bebaiosei) — the same verb as "confirmed" (ebebaiothe) in verse 6. The one who confirmed the testimony among them will also confirm/sustain the believers themselves. The word carries the same legal force: a guarantee, a securing of what has been transacted. "To the end" (heos telous) — until the consummation, until the goal is reached, until the telos arrives.

"Guiltless" (anenkletous) — literally "not called to account, without accusation, unimpeachable." The word is legal: it describes someone against whom no charge can be brought. The intended outcome of God's sustaining action is that the Corinthians will stand on the day of judgment without accusation against them. "The day of our Lord Jesus Christ" (en te hemera tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou) transposes the prophetic concept of "the day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) — which in Amos 5:18-20, Isaiah 2:12, Joel 2:1, and Zephaniah 1:14-18 was a day of reckoning and judgment — onto Jesus Christ. Christ presides over the day of judgment.

Two things must be noted about this verse. First, the sustaining to the end is attributed to God/Christ's action, not to the Corinthians' performance. The ground is God's initiative, not human effort. Second, this statement of divine commitment does not make the rest of the letter superfluous. Paul will go on to warn the Corinthians at length about conduct that endangers their standing: "Do you not know that the unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom of God?" (6:9); "let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (10:12); "if I preach the gospel, that gives me no ground for boasting" (9:16); "I discipline my body and keep it under control, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified" (9:27). The affirmation of God's faithfulness and the urgency of serious warnings coexist in the same letter. A first-century reader would not hear a contradiction but a description of how covenant relationship works: God is faithful to his commitments, and the community is responsible to live within the relationship God has established. God's faithfulness does not eliminate human responsibility; it grounds it. Israel's own story illustrates this pattern: God was faithful to his covenant with Israel while simultaneously holding Israel accountable for covenant violation (Deuteronomy 7:9-12, where God's faithfulness is described alongside the warning "know therefore that the LORD your God is God, the faithful God who keeps covenant... with those who love him and keep his commandments").

"God is faithful, by whom you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord" (1:9).

"God is faithful" (pistos ho theos) — this is the theological ground of everything in verses 4-8. The word pistos in the LXX describes God's essential character. Deuteronomy 7:9: "the faithful God" (ho theos ho pistos) who keeps covenant and steadfast love with those who love him and keep his commandments. Isaiah 49:7: "the LORD, who is faithful" (pistos estin kyrios). God can be relied upon. His faithfulness is not abstract; it is covenantal — he keeps his commitments, he does not abandon what he has begun. Paul will invoke this same principle again in 10:13: "God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your ability."

"By whom you were called" (di hou eklethete) — passive voice with God as agent. The Corinthians did not choose God; God called them. The initiative is entirely on God's side. The verb kaleo ("to call") has appeared three times in two verses (kletos in v. 1, kletois in v. 2, eklethete here). The repetition is deliberate: everything about this community originates in God's summons.

"Into the fellowship of his Son" (eis koinonian tou huiou autou) — the word koinonia is rich and specific. It means partnership, sharing, participation, joint involvement, communion. It is not mere association but active participation in a shared reality. The Corinthians were called into a shared life with Christ — a partnership in which they participate in his story, his death, his resurrection, his ongoing life. Paul will develop this concept in 10:16-17, where koinonia describes participation in the body and blood of Christ through the common meal, and where that participation constitutes the community as "one body." The factionalism that Paul is about to expose is a direct violation of this koinonia. God called them into shared life with his Son; they have divided that shared life into competing parties. The thanksgiving sets up the accusation.

Verses 10-17: The Exposure of the Community's Division

"I appeal to you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you agree, and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same judgment" (1:10).

The transition from thanksgiving to problem is immediate. Paul uses parakalo ("I appeal, I urge, I exhort") — not the language of command (epitasso) but of urgent entreaty. The appeal is grounded "by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (dia tou onomatos tou kyriou hemon Iesou Christou). The name is the one "called upon" by all believers everywhere (v. 2). If one name unites all communities in every place, that same name should unite the factions within a single community.

The appeal has three parts: "that all of you agree" (hina to auto legete pantes — literally "that you all say the same thing"); "that there be no divisions among you" (me e en hymin schismata); and "that you be united in the same mind and the same judgment" (ete de katertismenoi en to auto noi kai en te aute gnome).

The word schismata ("divisions, tears, rents") is used elsewhere for the tearing of cloth (Matthew 9:16, Mark 2:21) and for a split in a crowd (John 7:43, 9:16, 10:19). The community has been torn apart, like a garment ripped along its seams. The verb katertismenoi ("made complete, restored, mended") is the word used for mending fishing nets (Matthew 4:21, Mark 1:19). The community that has been torn needs to be mended. The metaphor is domestic and practical: this is repair work, not abstract theology.

"For it has been reported to me by Chloe's people that there is quarreling among you, my brothers" (1:11).

Paul names his source: "Chloe's people" (ton Chloes — literally "those of Chloe"). Chloe is named without introduction, suggesting she is known to the Corinthians. Her "people" — household members, slaves, commercial associates, or emissaries — have brought Paul the report. Paul is transparent about where his information comes from. He is not trafficking in anonymous rumor. The problem is "quarreling" (erides) — strife, contention, competitive argument.

"What I mean is that each one of you says, 'I follow Paul,' or 'I follow Apollos,' or 'I follow Cephas,' or 'I follow Christ'" (1:12).

Four slogans. The formula "ego eimi Paulou" (I am of Paul) uses the genitive of belonging — "I belong to Paul." The factions are organized around human leaders:

Paul — the community's founder, who spent eighteen months in Corinth, laid the foundation (3:10), and knows the community intimately.

Apollos — described in Acts 18:24-28 as "an eloquent man" (aner logios), "competent in the Scriptures" (dynatos on en tais graphais), who came to Corinth after Paul's departure and "greatly helped those who through grace had believed" (Acts 18:27). In a city that prized rhetorical skill, Apollos's eloquence would have been magnetic. The "I am of Apollos" faction likely admired his oratorical and intellectual gifts — the very sophia logou ("wisdom of word") that Paul will identify as dangerous to the gospel's integrity.

Cephas — the Aramaic name of Peter (Petros), the leading apostle of the Jerusalem community. Whether Peter visited Corinth personally or whether a faction simply claimed his authority as superior to Paul's, the "I am of Cephas" group represents an appeal to Jerusalem-based apostolic prestige.

Christ — the fourth slogan is the most difficult to interpret. It could be Paul's own corrective interjection ("but I belong to Christ!"), or it could be a fourth faction that claims direct access to Christ without apostolic mediation, using the Lord's own name as a party label in the same competitive way others use Paul's or Apollos's. If the latter, the irony is devastating: the name that should unite all is being wielded as one more dividing marker.

The deeper problem is not merely social discord but theological confusion. The Corinthians are treating the gospel as a platform for competing allegiances, evaluating teachers by the standards of rhetorical performance and personal prestige — the very standards that Paul will argue the cross overturns.

"Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?" (1:13).

Three rhetorical questions, each demanding the answer "No."

"Is Christ divided?" (memeristai ho Christos) — the perfect passive: has Christ been divided into portions, distributed among factions, so that the Paul-party gets one piece and the Apollos-party gets another? The question is absurd, and the absurdity is the argument. Christ is indivisible. A divided community implies a divided Christ, which is impossible. Therefore the division must be wrong.

"Was Paul crucified for you?" (me Paulos estaurōthe hyper hymon) — the particle me expects a negative answer. Paul was not crucified for anyone. Only Christ was. The foundation of the community's identity is not which teacher they heard first but which person died for them. Belonging is determined by the cross, not by the classroom.

"Were you baptized in the name of Paul?" (e eis to onoma Paulou ebaptisthete) — baptism is "into the name of" (eis to onoma) the one to whom one gives allegiance, the one under whose authority and ownership one is placed. The Corinthians were baptized into Christ's name, not Paul's. Their baptism constituted them as Christ's possession, not Paul's followers. The faction slogans contradict the logic of their own baptism.

"I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius, so that no one may say that you were baptized in my name" (1:14-15).

Paul expresses relief at having baptized so few Corinthians. If he had baptized the entire community, the "I am of Paul" faction would have an additional claim: "we were baptized by Paul himself." The few he did baptize are named. Crispus is identified in Acts 18:8 as "the ruler of the synagogue" who "believed in the Lord, together with his entire household" — one of Paul's earliest and most significant converts in Corinth, a Jewish community leader who crossed over. Gaius is likely the same Gaius Paul mentions in Romans 16:23 as "host to me and to the whole church" — a man of sufficient means to host the entire community for assembly.

"(I did baptize also the household of Stephanas. Beyond that, I do not know whether I baptized anyone else)" (1:16).

The parenthetical correction is revealing. Paul's memory catches up with his pen: he forgot Stephanas's household. This is the voice of real correspondence, unpolished and authentic. Stephanas will reappear in 16:15 as part of "the firstfruits of Achaia," a household that "devoted themselves to the service of the saints." Paul's uncertainty ("I do not know whether I baptized anyone else") further demonstrates that he is not constructing a careful argument about baptism in the abstract; he is dealing with a concrete social situation and drawing practical conclusions.

"For Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel, and not with words of eloquent wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power" (1:17).

This verse is the pivot of the entire chapter. It connects the faction problem (vv. 10-16) to the theology of the cross (vv. 18-31) through a single hinge: the relationship between wisdom and the cross.

"Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel" (ou gar apesteilen me Christos baptizein alla euangelizesthai) — Paul does not denigrate baptism. He ranks his commission. The verb apesteilen ("sent") connects to apostolos ("one sent") in verse 1. The apostle was sent to proclaim (euangelizesthai), and the baptizing of converts was a secondary activity that others could perform. The priority is the announcement.

"Not with words of eloquent wisdom" (ouk en sophia logou) — literally "not in wisdom of word." This phrase diagnoses the Corinthian disease. The Corinthians live in a culture that prizes rhetorical sophistication. Corinth, like all Greek cities, valued the rhetorician — the person who could persuade, dazzle, and dominate through the power of speech. Traveling speakers (sophists) competed for followers by demonstrating their verbal skill. The Corinthians have imported this cultural value system into the community and are evaluating apostles the way they would evaluate competing rhetoricians: Who speaks best? Who is most impressive? Whose style do I prefer? Paul says he was sent to preach the gospel explicitly not in that mode.

"Lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power" (hina me kenothe ho stauros tou Christou) — the verb kenoō means "to empty, to make void, to drain of content." If the gospel is delivered in the packaging of rhetorical brilliance, the power shifts from the message to the messenger, from the event of the cross to the performance of the speaker, from God's act to human artistry. The cross's power — as Paul will now argue — resides precisely in its apparent weakness and foolishness. To dress it in the trappings of worldly wisdom is to neutralize what makes it effective. The cross is either foolish and powerful, or it is made eloquent and emptied.

Verses 18-25: The Word of the Cross — God's Power Through Apparent Foolishness

"For the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1:18).

"The word of the cross" (ho logos tou staurou) — the message whose content is the crucifixion. The stauros (cross, stake, upright pole for execution) was the instrument of the most degrading form of Roman capital punishment, reserved for slaves, insurrectionists, and the lowest classes of criminals. Roman citizens were exempt from it. Crucifixion was designed not merely to kill but to humiliate — the victim was displayed publicly, stripped, powerless, a spectacle of shame. Cicero called it "the most cruel and disgusting penalty" (crudelissimum taeterrimumque supplicium, In Verrem 2.5.165) and declared that "the very word 'cross' should be far removed not only from the person of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes, and his ears" (Pro Rabirio 16). To first-century ears, the "word of the cross" was the announcement of a message centered on the most shameful event imaginable.

"Folly" (moria) — not merely error but absurdity, manifest stupidity. The crucified man as God's agent of salvation was not just wrong by ancient standards; it was laughable. A graffito from the Palatine Hill in Rome, likely from the late first or early second century, depicts a man with a donkey's head on a cross, with the inscription "Alexamenos worships his god" — a mocking caricature of Christian devotion. The social contempt for the cross was real and pervasive.

"To those who are perishing" (tois apollymenois) — present middle/passive participle. The present tense describes an ongoing process: those who are in the course of being destroyed, those currently on the path of destruction. This is not a fixed, past-tense assignment. Paul does not say "those who were destroyed" or "those who are the destroyed" (as if describing an unchangeable category). He describes a current trajectory.

"To us who are being saved" (tois sozomenois hemin) — likewise a present passive participle. "Being saved" is an ongoing process, not a completed event. The Corinthians are in the course of being saved. Paul includes himself in this group (hemin, "to us"). The parallel between the two participles is precise: as the perishing are in the process of perishing, so the saved are in the process of being saved. Both descriptions indicate a current direction, not a finished destination. Paul will use similar language in 2 Corinthians 2:15, distinguishing between "those who are being saved and those who are perishing" (tois sozomenois kai en tois apollymenois) — again, present participles describing ongoing trajectories.

"It is the power of God" (dynamis theou estin) — the cross, which appears to be the very embodiment of powerlessness, is in reality the operative power of God. The paradox is not rhetorical decoration; it is the argument. What the world sees as weakness is God's chosen instrument of power.

"For it is written, 'I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the discernment of the discerning I will thwart'" (1:19).

Paul quotes Isaiah 29:14 (LXX), drawing on a prophetic context that illuminates his point. In Isaiah 29, God pronounces judgment on Jerusalem ("Ariel, Ariel, the city where David encamped," Isaiah 29:1) because the people offer superficial worship: "this people draw near with their mouth and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me, and their fear of me is a commandment taught by men" (Isaiah 29:13 LXX). God's response to this surface-level piety is a "wonderful and extraordinary" act (Isaiah 29:14 LXX) that will cause "the wisdom of the wise" to perish and "the understanding of the prudent" to be hidden. The pattern in Isaiah is this: when human religion becomes a performance of wisdom and tradition disconnected from genuine devotion, God acts in a way that shatters the entire framework.

Paul sees the cross as the ultimate instance of this pattern. God has acted in a way — through a crucified Messiah — that destroys every system of human wisdom that claims to know how God should operate. The Isaiah quotation is not ornamental; it identifies the cross as the fulfillment of a pattern that runs through Israel's history: God repeatedly acts in ways that confound human expectations and overturn human calculations.

"Where is the one who is wise? Where is the scribe? Where is the debater of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?" (1:20).

Three rhetorical challenges, echoing the prophetic taunt form. Isaiah 33:18 (LXX): "Where are the scribes? Where are the counselors? Where is the one who counts those being nourished?" — directed at the now-vanished threat of Assyria, whose intimidating power proved empty before God's deliverance. Isaiah 19:12: "Where then are your wise men?" — addressed to Egypt, whose sages could not fathom God's purposes. The form itself — "where is...?" — is the language of prophetic triumph: the thing that seemed so formidable has been swept away by God's action.

"The one who is wise" (sophos) represents philosophical and intellectual achievement. "The scribe" (grammateus) represents learned scholarship — the expert in texts and traditions. "The debater of this age" (syzetetees tou aionos toutou) represents the skilled disputant, the one who wins arguments. The phrase "of this age" (tou aionos toutou) is crucial. It identifies the wisdom, learning, and debating skill as belonging to a particular era — "this age" as opposed to the age to come. The framework is eschatological: there is a present age, governed by certain standards, and there is God's coming age, which operates on entirely different principles. The wisdom of "this age" has been rendered obsolete by God's action in the cross.

"Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?" (ouchi emoranen ho theos ten sophian tou kosmou) — the verb morainein means "to make foolish, to show to be foolish, to expose as absurd." God has not merely disagreed with worldly wisdom; he has actively unmasked it as foolishness through his own surprising action. The cross is the demonstration that proves worldly wisdom bankrupt.

"For since, in the wisdom of God, the world did not know God through wisdom, it pleased God through the folly of what we preach to save those who believe" (1:21).

This verse contains a remarkable logical structure. "In the wisdom of God" (en te sophia tou theou) — God's own wisdom determined the outcome. It was part of God's design that the world would fail to reach God through human wisdom. The failure of philosophy and rhetoric to arrive at the knowledge of God was not an accident; it was built into God's plan. "The world did not know God through wisdom" (ouk egno ho kosmos dia tes sophias ton theon) — despite all its intellectual achievements, the world (ho kosmos, the ordered system of human civilization and thought) did not arrive at knowledge of God. The wisdom project failed.

"It pleased God" (eudokesen ho theos) — the verb eudokeo means "to be well-pleased, to determine with delight, to resolve upon." God's choice of an alternative path was not reluctant but deliberate and pleased. "Through the folly of what we preach" (dia tes morias tou kerygmatos) — the foolishness of the proclamation, the absurdity of the announced message. The word kerygma is not the act of preaching (as if the style of delivery were foolish) but the content of the proclamation — what is preached, namely the crucified Messiah. "To save those who believe" (sosai tous pisteuontas) — the present participle pisteuontas ("those who believe, those who are believing") identifies the recipients of salvation by their characteristic activity: ongoing trust, active faith.

"For Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom, but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1:22-23).

Two cultural groups, two demands, one message that offends both.

"Jews demand signs" (Ioudaioi semeia aitousin) — the demand for miraculous authentication was embedded in Jewish messianic expectation. The Messiah was expected to perform signs that would validate his identity and demonstrate God's power. The exodus from Egypt was accompanied by signs; the prophets performed signs; the Messiah should surpass them all. A Messiah who ended on a Roman cross, without miraculous deliverance, without overthrowing the occupying power, met none of these expectations. The demand for signs is the demand for visible, undeniable demonstrations of power.

"Greeks seek wisdom" (Hellenes sophian zetousin) — the Greek intellectual tradition valued rational coherence, philosophical sophistication, and persuasive argument. The sophists and philosophers who shaped Greek education sought truth through logos — reason, argument, discourse. A message centered on the execution of a Jewish artisan from Galilee offered nothing that this tradition would recognize as wisdom.

"But we preach Christ crucified" (hemeis keryssomen Christon estauromenon) — Paul places the proclamation in direct opposition to both demands. The perfect passive participle estauromenon ("having been crucified, in the state of crucifixion") emphasizes the enduring condition. This is not "Christ who was once crucified and has now moved past it" but "Christ as the crucified one" — the crucifixion defines his identity permanently. The Messiah bears the marks of the cross as his permanent characteristic.

"A stumbling block to Jews" (Ioudaiois skandalon) — the word skandalon means the trigger of a trap, the stick that springs the snare. It does not merely disappoint; it actively trips and ensnares. A crucified Messiah was not simply unexpected for Jewish hope; it was offensive, scandalous, a direct contradiction. Deuteronomy 21:22-23 declared: "if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree, his body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God." The Messiah, God's anointed, cannot be under God's curse. A crucified claimant was, by the logic of Deuteronomy, proven to be a false messiah. The cross was not neutral evidence; it was disqualifying evidence.

"Folly to Gentiles" (ethnesin morian) — to the Greco-Roman world, the claim was simply absurd. To worship a crucified provincial was cultural insanity. The cross carried no grandeur, no philosophical sophistication, no rhetorical power. It was the death of slaves and rebels.

"But to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1:24).

"Those who are called" (tois kletois) — the same adjective from verse 1 (Paul is "called apostle") and verse 2 ("called saints"). The "called" are those who have responded to God's summons. Among the called, the division between Jew and Greek is transcended: "both Jews and Greeks" (Ioudaiois te kai Hellesin). The cultural categories that define how the cross is received — skandalon for Jews, moria for Greeks — are overcome within the community of the called.

To these called ones, Christ is simultaneously "the power of God" (theou dynamin) and "the wisdom of God" (theou sophian). The two things the cross appears to lack — power and wisdom — are the two things it actually embodies. The cross is God's power because it accomplishes what human power cannot: the reconciliation of humanity to God, the defeat of the forces that enslave. The cross is God's wisdom because it achieves what human wisdom cannot even conceive: salvation through apparent defeat, victory through apparent shame.

"For the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men" (1:25).

Paul does not concede that God is actually foolish or weak. The construction is comparative and rhetorical: even if you categorize God's action (the cross) as foolishness, that divine "foolishness" exceeds all human wisdom. Even if you call it weakness, that divine "weakness" exceeds all human strength. The comparison is not between entities on the same scale but between entirely different orders of reality. What appears foolish from below is wisdom from above. What appears weak from below is power from above. The categories of human evaluation are inverted.

Verses 26-31: The Evidence in the Community Itself — God Chooses What the World Discards

"For consider your calling, brothers: not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth" (1:26).

"Consider your calling" (blepete ten klesin hymon) — the verb blepete ("look at, observe, examine") directs them to examine the empirical evidence of their own community. The "calling" (klesis) functions simultaneously as the divine act of summoning and as the community that resulted from that summons. The evidence is visible: look at who God actually called.

"Not many wise according to the flesh" (ou polloi sophoi kata sarka) — not according to the world's standards of wisdom. "Not many powerful" (ou polloi dynatoi) — not many with social, political, or economic influence. "Not many of noble birth" (ou polloi eugeneis) — literally "well-born," from eu ("well") and genos ("birth, family"). Not many from the aristocratic families that dominated civic life.

Paul says "not many" (ou polloi), not "none." There were people of standing in the community: Crispus the synagogue ruler (Acts 18:8), Gaius who hosted the entire assembly (Romans 16:23), Erastus the city treasurer (Romans 16:23), Stephanas whose household was among the firstfruits of Achaia (16:15). But the community was primarily composed of people without social capital — workers, freedmen, slaves, the ordinary and undistinguished. This demographic reality is not a failure of the mission; it is evidence of how God works. The composition of the community embodies the logic of the cross.

"But God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong; God chose what is low and despised in the world, even things that are not, to bring to nothing things that are" (1:27-28).

Three parallel statements of God's election, each escalating in intensity:

God chose (exelexato) the foolish things (ta mora tou kosmou) to shame (hina kataischyne) the wise. God chose the weak things (ta asthene tou kosmou) to shame the strong. God chose the low-born (ta agene — literally "without family, of no lineage") and the despised (ta exouthenemena — "treated as nothing, counted worthless") and the things that are not (ta me onta) to bring to nothing (hina ta onta katargese) the things that are (ta onta).

The verb exelexato ("chose") is the LXX's standard term for God's election. Its most foundational use is in Deuteronomy 7:6-7: "The LORD your God has chosen you (exelexato) to be a people for his treasured possession, out of all the peoples who are on the face of the earth. It was not because you were more in number than any other people that the LORD set his love on you and chose you, for you were the fewest of all peoples." God's election of Israel was explicitly not based on Israel's greatness, numerical strength, or merit. It was based on God's love and God's oath to the patriarchs (Deuteronomy 7:8). The logic Paul employs is identical: God chose the foolish, weak, and lowborn not despite their lack of worldly standing but precisely through that lack. God's method of operation consistently bypasses what the world considers valuable.

The final phrase is the most radical: "the things that are not, to bring to nothing the things that are" (ta me onta, hina ta onta katargese). God chose what has no standing, no recognized existence in the world's hierarchy — what literally "is not" by the world's measurement — in order to nullify what does have standing, what "is" by those standards. In Greek philosophical categories, "being" (to on) is the highest term; "non-being" (to me on) is the lowest. God inverts even this most fundamental distinction. The things that count as real in the world's accounting are nullified by God's choice of the things that count as nothing. The verb katargeo ("to render inoperative, to make of no effect, to nullify") is strong: the existing power structures are not merely challenged but abolished by God's selection of the powerless.

"So that no human being might boast in the presence of God" (1:29).

"So that" (hopos) — purpose clause. Everything described in verses 18-28 — God's choice of the cross, God's choice of the foolish and weak, God's inversion of human hierarchies — serves a single unified purpose: the elimination of human boasting before God. The word kauchaomai ("to boast, to glory, to take pride") will become a major theme throughout 1 and 2 Corinthians. "All flesh" (pasa sarx — "every human being" in their creaturely limitation) is deprived of grounds for self-congratulation in God's presence.

This is not arbitrary divine humiliation. The point is not that God delights in shaming the powerful for its own sake. The point is that the nature of the gospel — the cross, grace, divine initiative — is incompatible with human boasting. If God saves through weakness, no one can claim strength as the basis of their standing. If God chooses through foolishness, no one can claim wisdom as their qualification. The elimination of boasting is the natural consequence of a salvation that operates entirely through divine grace and initiative.

"And because of him you are in Christ Jesus, who became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1:30).

"Because of him" (ex autou) — literally "out of him, from him." The Corinthians' existence "in Christ Jesus" originates from God. Their location in Christ is God's doing, not their achievement. The phrase "in Christ Jesus" (en Christo Iesou) — Paul's characteristic expression for the sphere of existence that defines the community — is grounded in divine initiative.

"Who became to us wisdom from God" (hos egenethe sophia hemin apo theou) — Christ is the wisdom that the Greeks sought (v. 22). But this wisdom is "from God" (apo theou), not from human discovery. The sophia that the world sought through philosophy and rhetoric is found, paradoxically and exclusively, in the crucified Messiah.

Three further characterizations follow, joined to "wisdom" as either coordinate terms or as specifications of what that wisdom includes:

"Righteousness" (dikaiosyne) — right standing before God, the condition of being in right relationship with God. The word in the LXX (tsedaqah) encompasses both God's saving action and the status of those who are rightly aligned with God's purposes.

"Sanctification" (hagiasmos) — the process of being made holy, set apart for God. This connects directly back to verse 2, where the Corinthians are described as "sanctified in Christ Jesus" (hegiasmenois en Christo Iesou). What was stated as their identity in the greeting is now grounded in Christ: he is the agent and sphere of their sanctification.

"Redemption" (apolytrosis) — release through the payment of a ransom, liberation from bondage. The word carries deep resonance with Israel's foundational narrative: God "redeemed" Israel from Egypt (Exodus 6:6, Deuteronomy 7:8, 9:26, 13:5, 15:15). The verb lytroo and the noun lytrosis/apolytrosis describe God's liberating action, the act of buying back what belongs to him. In the LXX, God is repeatedly called Israel's "redeemer" (lytrotes, goēl). The Corinthians' redemption is located in Christ, who became for them what God became for Israel in the exodus.

All four — wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, redemption — are located in Christ, not in human achievement. Christ did not merely teach these things or point the way toward them. He "became" (egenethe) them. He is the embodiment of what God provides.

"So that, as it is written, 'Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord'" (1:31).

The quotation is from Jeremiah 9:23-24 (LXX 9:22-23): "Thus says the LORD: 'Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, let not the mighty man boast in his might, let not the rich man boast in his riches, but let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me, that I am the LORD who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth. For in these things I delight, declares the LORD.'"

The Jeremiah context is extraordinarily precise in its application to Paul's argument. Jeremiah explicitly identifies three objects of illegitimate boasting: wisdom (sophia), might (ischys), and riches (ploutos). These correspond directly to the three things Paul has said the Corinthians lack: "not many wise, not many powerful, not many of noble birth" (v. 26). The Jeremiah passage is not a detached proof-text selected at random; it is the scriptural foundation of the entire argument of 1:18-31. Paul has been arguing, in effect, an extended commentary on Jeremiah 9:23-24.

Jeremiah redirects boasting from human achievements to knowledge of God's character: "that he understands and knows me, that I am the LORD who practices steadfast love (chesed), justice (mishpat), and righteousness (tsedaqah) in the earth." The God Jeremiah describes is known by his covenant loyalty, his justice, and his righteousness — and he delights in these things. Paul's abbreviated quotation — "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" — captures the redirection: all boasting belongs to the Lord, not to human agents.

This citation closes the chapter's argument with the same logic that opened it. The community belongs to God (v. 2: "the church of God"). The community was called by God (vv. 1, 2, 9, 24, 26). The community was sanctified by God (v. 2, 30). The community was enriched by God (v. 5). Everything is gift. Everything originates in divine initiative. Therefore, all boasting in human leaders, human wisdom, or human status is excluded. The only legitimate object of boasting is the Lord himself — the God who works through the cross, who chooses the weak, who gives what cannot be earned.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 1 builds a single sustained argument through three movements. The opening (vv. 1-9) establishes the community's identity as God's creation: called, sanctified, enriched, sustained, and summoned into fellowship with God's Son. Every predicate is passive — things done to them by God, not achieved by them. The exposure of factionalism (vv. 10-17) then reveals that the community has contradicted its own identity by dividing along the lines of human leaders and human eloquence. The theology of the cross (vv. 18-31) provides the reason why factionalism is not merely a social problem but a theological error: the gospel operates through apparent foolishness and weakness, God chose the lowly to nullify the powerful, and therefore all human boasting — including boasting in one's preferred teacher — is excluded.

The three movements are inseparable. Without the opening's emphasis on divine initiative, the faction problem would seem like a mere social inconvenience. Without the faction problem, the theology of the cross would seem like abstract speculation. Without the theology of the cross, the appeal for unity would lack any grounding deeper than pragmatism. Paul weaves the three together: you belong to God (opening), therefore stop claiming to belong to human leaders (faction problem), because the entire logic of the gospel — the cross, the choice of the weak, the exclusion of boasting — makes such claims absurd (theology of the cross).

The word "called" (kletos/klesis/kaleo) is the thread that stitches the chapter together. Paul is "called" as an apostle (v. 1). The Corinthians are "called" saints (v. 2). They were "called" into fellowship with God's Son (v. 9). Christ is power and wisdom "to those who are called" (v. 24). Their "calling" is visible in the community's composition (v. 26). The repetition drives a single point: everything in this community originates in God's summons. No human initiative, no human wisdom, no human leader is the source of their identity. God called. They responded. That is the entire basis of their existence. Factionalism around human figures is, therefore, not just unfortunate; it is a repudiation of the calling itself.

The LXX citations anchor the argument in Israel's own scriptures. Isaiah 29:14 (v. 19) establishes that God has always confounded human wisdom when it became self-referential and detached from genuine devotion. Isaiah 33:18 and 19:12 (echoed in v. 20) provide the prophetic taunt form that declares human expertise irrelevant before God's action. Jeremiah 9:23-24 (v. 31) provides the climactic scriptural command: boast only in the Lord. Paul does not innovate; he applies Israel's own prophetic tradition to the community's situation. The Corinthians' problem — competitive attachment to human wisdom and eloquence — is the same problem the prophets diagnosed in Israel: a confusion of human performance with genuine knowledge of God.
