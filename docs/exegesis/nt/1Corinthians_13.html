1 Corinthians 13 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 12): Paul opened a new section of the letter with the formula peri de ("now concerning"), signaling a response to another question from the Corinthians' letter: "Now concerning spiritual gifts, brothers, I do not want you to be uninformed" (12:1). The word pneumatikon can mean either "spiritual gifts" or "spiritual persons" — the ambiguity may be deliberate, since the Corinthians' problem involved both the gifts and the inflated self-regard of those who possessed them. Paul began by contrasting their former pagan life — "when you were pagans you were led astray to mute idols, however you were led" (12:2) — with the criterion for genuine Spirit-speech: no one speaking by God's Spirit says "Jesus is accursed" (anathema Iesous), and no one can say "Jesus is Lord" (kyrios Iesous) except by the Holy Spirit (12:3). The fundamental test of the Spirit's presence is christological confession, not spectacular manifestation.

Paul then developed the diversity-within-unity principle through a three-fold statement: there are varieties of gifts (charismata) but the same Spirit, varieties of service (diakoniai) but the same Lord, varieties of activities (energemata) but the same God who empowers them all in everyone (12:4-6). Each person receives a manifestation of the Spirit pros to sympheron — "for the common good" (12:7). This phrase is critical: the purpose of every gift is the benefit of the community, not the enhancement of the individual. Paul then listed specific gifts — word of wisdom, word of knowledge, faith, gifts of healing, working of miracles, prophecy, distinguishing of spirits, various kinds of tongues, interpretation of tongues (12:8-10) — and attributed all of them to "one and the same Spirit, who apportions to each one individually as he wills" (12:11). The Spirit distributes; the individual does not choose.

The chapter then introduced the body metaphor that dominates its second half: "For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ. For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body — Jews or Greeks, slaves or free — and all were made to drink of one Spirit" (12:12-13). The body metaphor addressed the Corinthian problem from two directions. First, no member can say to another "I have no need of you" (12:21) — the eye cannot dismiss the hand, the head cannot dismiss the feet. Second, no member can say of itself "because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body" (12:15) — the foot is no less a member for not being a hand. Both the superiority complex and the inferiority complex are addressed. God has composed the body so that "there may be no division (schisma) in the body, but that the members may have the same care for one another" (12:25). The word schisma reaches all the way back to 1:10, where Paul first named the Corinthian disease: divisions. The body metaphor is the prescription for the disease that has afflicted the community from the start.

The chapter concluded with a hierarchy among the gifts: "And God has appointed in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers, then miracles, then gifts of healing, helping, administrating, and various kinds of tongues" (12:28). The order is significant: tongues, which the Corinthians appear to have prized above all else, comes last. Paul then asked a series of rhetorical questions — "Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Do all work miracles? Do all possess gifts of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret?" (12:29-30) — each expecting the answer "no." No one gift belongs to all, and no one person possesses all gifts. Then came the transitional statement that leads directly into chapter 13: zeloute de ta charismata ta meizona. kai eti kath' hyperbolen hodon hymin deiknymi — "But earnestly desire the higher gifts. And I will show you a still more excellent way" (12:31). The word hodon ("way, path, road") introduces chapter 13 not as a digression about love in the abstract but as the "way" that surpasses even the most impressive spiritual gifts — the path that makes all other paths intelligible and all other gifts functional.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 14): Paul returns to the practical question of how spiritual gifts should function in the assembly. He opens with a direct instruction: "Pursue love, and earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy" (14:1). The verb diokete ("pursue") applies to love — the subject of chapter 13. The verb zeloute ("earnestly desire") applies to spiritual gifts, echoing 12:31. Love is pursued; gifts are desired. The two commands are not in competition but in sequence: pursue love first, then desire the gifts, particularly prophecy. Chapter 14 then develops an extended comparison between tongues-speaking and prophecy. The one who speaks in tongues "speaks not to men but to God; for no one understands him" (14:2). The one who prophesies "speaks to people for their building up (oikodomen) and encouragement (paraklesin) and consolation (paramythian)" (14:3). The verb oikodomeo ("to build up") reappears as the governing criterion: "The one who speaks in a tongue builds up himself, but the one who prophesies builds up the church" (14:4). Tongues edifies the individual; prophecy edifies the community. Given that chapter 13 has just argued that love "does not seek its own" (13:5) and that everything must serve the common good, the superiority of prophecy over tongues follows directly.

Paul does not forbid tongues — "I thank God that I speak in tongues more than all of you" (14:18) — but he insists that in the assembly, intelligibility governs: "in church I would rather speak five words with my mind in order to instruct others, than ten thousand words in a tongue" (14:19). The word nous ("mind") is central: worship must engage the mind, not bypass it. The argument reaches its practical conclusion in the regulation of worship: "when you come together, each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation. Let all things be done for building up (pros oikodomen)" (14:26). Everything in the assembly is measured by the criterion of community edification. Tongues-speakers must take turns, and only if an interpreter is present; prophets must take turns and be subject to evaluation by others (14:27-29). "For God is not a God of confusion (akatastasias) but of peace" (14:33). The chapter concludes: "all things should be done decently and in order" (14:40).

How chapter 13 connects these: Chapter 13 is not an interruption in the spiritual gifts discussion. It is the foundation on which the practical instruction of chapter 14 rests and the answer to the problem chapter 12 diagnosed. Chapter 12 established that the body has many members with diverse gifts, all distributed by one Spirit for the common good. But the Corinthians have been treating the gifts as occasions for competitive display — particularly the gift of tongues, which they appear to have elevated above all others as the mark of spiritual superiority. The body metaphor corrected the theology (all members are necessary, no gift is dispensable), but it did not yet name the governing principle that should direct the exercise of gifts within the body. Chapter 13 names that principle: agape, love. Chapter 14 then applies it: because love "does not seek its own" (13:5), gifts must be exercised for the community's benefit, not the individual's; because love "builds up" (from 8:1, the thesis that chapter 13 develops), prophecy — which builds up the church — is superior to tongues, which builds up only the speaker.

The transition from 12:31 to 13:1 is seamless. Paul has just said "I will show you a still more excellent way" (12:31b). The word hodon ("way") is not a detour from the gifts discussion but the lens through which all gifts must be evaluated. The "way" is not an alternative to spiritual gifts but the manner in which they must be exercised. Without this way, even the most spectacular gifts are worthless (13:1-3). With this way, the gifts serve their intended purpose: the building up of the body.

The transition from 13:13 to 14:1 is equally seamless. Chapter 13 concludes with "the greatest of these is love" (13:13). Chapter 14 opens with "pursue love" (diokete ten agapen) — the practical command that flows directly from the conclusion. The Corinthians are told to make love their pursuit, and then, within that pursuit, to desire the spiritual gifts, especially prophecy. The architecture is clear: love is the way (chapter 13); prophecy over tongues is the application of that way (chapter 14); the regulation of worship is the practical outworking of both (14:26-40).

Chapter 13 also reaches far back into the letter's earlier arguments. In 8:1, Paul declared: "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up" (he gnosis physioi, he de agape oikodomei). That verse was a compressed thesis statement; chapter 13 is its full development. The word physioo ("to puff up"), which has served as the diagnostic term for the Corinthian disease throughout the letter (4:6, 4:18, 4:19, 5:2, 8:1), reappears in 13:4: love "is not puffed up" (ou physiotai). The word gnosis ("knowledge"), which was the Corinthians' boast in 8:1 and the basis of their claim to freedom, reappears in 13:2 (if I have all knowledge), 13:8 (knowledge will pass away), 13:9 and 13:12 (we know in part). The word teleios ("mature, complete"), which appeared in 2:6 for those to whom Paul speaks wisdom, reappears in 13:10 ("when the complete comes"). The word nepios ("infant"), which Paul used in 3:1 to describe the Corinthians' spiritual immaturity, reappears in 13:11. The vocabulary of chapter 13 is drawn from the letter's own reservoir of key terms, and each reappearance carries the accumulated weight of its prior usage. Chapter 13 is not a self-contained poem inserted into the letter; it is the letter's argument in concentrated form.

Verses 1-3: Without Love, the Most Impressive Gifts and Sacrifices Are Nothing

"If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal" (13:1).

Paul begins with the gift the Corinthians evidently prized most: tongues. The phrase glossais ton anthropon kai ton angelon ("in the tongues of men and of angels") escalates from human languages to angelic speech. The Corinthians may have regarded their tongues-speaking as the language of heaven — a sign that the speaker had ascended to the realm of the angels and was participating in heavenly worship. The phrase "tongues of angels" suggests a tradition in which ecstatic speech was understood as the language spoken in God's throne room. The Testament of Job (48-50), a Jewish text likely circulating in various forms in the first century, describes Job's daughters receiving sashes that enabled them to speak in the language of angels and of the heavenly beings. Whether Paul endorses this understanding or merely grants it for the sake of argument, he accepts the most exalted possible claim for tongues-speaking: suppose you speak not only every human language but the very language of the angels. Even so, if love is absent, the speaker is chalkon echon e kymbalon alalazon — "a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal."

The word chalkos ("bronze, copper, brass") describes the metal itself, and by extension any object made from it. The word kymbalon ("cymbal") is a percussion instrument used in worship and festival. In the LXX, cymbals appear in Israel's worship: Psalm 150:5 (LXX 149:5) commands praise "with sounding cymbals" (en kymbalois euchois) and "with loud clashing cymbals" (en kymbalois alalagmou). In 1 Chronicles 15:16, the Levites are appointed with "instruments of music, harps and lyres and cymbals, sounding aloud" (en kymbalois) for the transport of the ark. Cymbals were legitimate instruments in Israel's worship, but used alone, without melody or harmonic context, they produce noise rather than music. The verb alalazo ("to clang, to clash, to ring, to wail") describes a sound that is loud but meaningless — a crash without content.

In the Greco-Roman context, bronze gongs and cymbals were associated with the ecstatic worship of pagan cults, particularly the rites of Cybele and Dionysus, where loud percussive instruments accompanied frenzied states of possession. For the Corinthians, this comparison would carry a sharp edge: the tongues-speaking they prize, when divorced from love, produces the same meaningless racket as pagan ecstatic worship. It is noise without communication, sound without substance.

The conditional structure ean ("if") with the subjunctive lalo ("I speak") marks this as a hypothetical case. Paul does not deny the gift of tongues. He grants it at its highest possible level — angelic speech — and then evacuates it of all value by removing love. The verb echo ("I have," in the negative me echo, "I do not have") treats love as something one possesses or does not possess, a governing disposition that shapes everything the person does. Without it (agapen de me echo), the person with the most exalted gift is nothing more than noise.

"And if I have prophetic powers, and understand all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing" (13:2).

Paul now moves from tongues to prophecy, which he will argue in chapter 14 is the superior gift. Even prophecy, the gift Paul himself ranks highest, is worthless without love. The word propheteia ("prophecy") describes speaking God's word to the community — the gift that "builds up and encourages and consoles" (14:3). Paul escalates further: not merely prophecy but eido ta mysteria panta kai pasan ten gnosin — "I understand all the mysteries and all the knowledge." The word mysterion ("mystery, secret, hidden plan") appeared in 2:7 for "God's wisdom, secret and hidden, which God decreed before the ages for our glory." The word gnosis ("knowledge") has been a contested term since 8:1, where the Corinthians boasted that "all of us possess knowledge." Paul hypothetically grants the knower total access: all mysteries, all knowledge. The word pasan/panta ("all") is repeated three times in the verse, building toward an absolute: all prophecy, all mysteries, all knowledge.

Then comes all faith: pasan ten pistin hoste ore methistanai — "all faith, so as to remove mountains." The phrase recalls a saying of Jesus preserved in the tradition: "If you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move" (Matthew 17:20; cf. Mark 11:23). Paul invokes the most extreme expression of faith imaginable — mountain-displacing faith. Even this, without love, renders the person ouden eimi — "I am nothing." The verdict is not that the person's work is diminished or their reward reduced. The verdict is ontological: the person is nothing. Without love, the most impressive constellation of spiritual endowments produces a zero.

The word ouden ("nothing") recalls 3:7, where Paul said of himself and Apollos: "neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but only God who gives the growth." There the "nothing" described the relative insignificance of human agents compared to God's action. Here the "nothing" is more severe: the gifted person without love is not merely insignificant in comparison to God but is, in themselves, nothing at all. Their gifts have not produced what gifts are meant to produce — the building up of the body in love — and therefore the person has produced nothing of value.

"And if I give away all my possessions, and if I hand over my body so that I may boast, but have not love, I gain nothing" (13:3).

Paul moves beyond spiritual gifts to acts of extraordinary sacrifice. The verb psomizo ("to feed, to give out in morsels, to distribute bit by bit") describes the breaking up and distribution of one's property to feed others. The phrase panta ta hyparchonta mou ("all my possessions") makes the sacrifice total: every possession, distributed to feed the hungry. In the LXX, feeding the poor is a mark of righteousness. Psalm 112:9 (LXX 111:9): "He has distributed freely; he has given to the poor; his righteousness endures forever." Isaiah 58:7: "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house?" The act Paul describes is the epitome of charitable generosity.

The second clause is more difficult textually. Some manuscripts read ean paradō to soma mou hina kauthēsomai ("if I hand over my body to be burned"), while others read hina kauchēsomai ("so that I may boast"). The difference between kauthēsomai ("I will be burned") and kauchēsomai ("I will boast") is a single letter in Greek. If the reading is "to be burned," Paul envisions the ultimate physical sacrifice — martyrdom by fire. If the reading is "so that I may boast," Paul envisions handing over one's body to some extreme hardship or sacrifice in order to gain honor or renown. Either reading makes Paul's point: even the most dramatic act of self-sacrifice, carried to the point of either death or boasting-worthy suffering, profits nothing (ouden opheloumai) without love. The verb opheleo ("to profit, to benefit, to gain advantage") in the passive means "I am benefited, I gain." The negation is total: ouden — nothing. The person gains zero.

The progression in verses 1-3 is deliberate. Paul moves from the spiritual gift the Corinthians most value (tongues) through the gift he himself most values (prophecy) to the most complete forms of spiritual knowledge (all mysteries, all knowledge) to the most extreme faith (mountain-moving) to the most radical forms of physical generosity (total distribution of possessions) and sacrifice (surrender of the body). At each stage, the result without love is the same: noise, nothing, no gain. The escalation makes the point inescapable. If tongues-speaking without love is worthless, the Corinthian who prizes tongues above all should take notice. But Paul does not stop there. Even prophecy, even comprehensive knowledge, even extraordinary faith, even martyrdom — all worthless without love. No gift, no sacrifice, no achievement of any kind compensates for the absence of love. Love is not a nice addition to the spiritual life; it is the condition without which the spiritual life is empty.

Verses 4-7: The Character of Love

"Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant or rude. It does not insist on its own way; it is not irritable or resentful; it does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth. Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things" (13:4-7).

Paul now describes what love is and what it is not. The description unfolds in fifteen predications — two positive, eight negative, one contrastive pair, and four comprehensive positives. Each predication uses the verb in the third person singular with he agape ("love") as the subject: love itself is the actor. The description is not abstract but behavioral — love is defined by what it does and does not do, not by what it feels.

The first-century hearer would recognize that nearly every characteristic Paul attributes to love is the opposite of a behavior the letter has diagnosed in the Corinthian community. The description functions simultaneously as a portrait of love and as a mirror held up to the community's failures.

"Love is patient" (he agape makrothymei) — the verb makrothymeo ("to be long-tempered, to bear patiently, to wait with endurance") describes restraint under provocation. The word is composed of makros ("long") and thymos ("passion, anger, spirit"). The long-tempered person does not react quickly to offense. In the LXX, makrothymia is a divine attribute. Exodus 34:6 (LXX): kyrios ho theos oiktirmon kai eleemon, makrothymos — "The Lord God, merciful and gracious, long-suffering." Numbers 14:18: makrothymos kyrios — "The Lord is long-suffering." Psalm 86:15 (LXX 85:15): sy, kyrie, ho theos oiktirmon kai eleemon, makrothymos — "You, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious, long-suffering." The patience Paul attributes to love is the patience God exercises with his people — the restraint that delays judgment, that bears with failure, that does not destroy at the first provocation. The Corinthians have shown the opposite: they are quick to quarrel (1:11), quick to take offense, quick to assert their rights at the expense of others.

"Love is kind" (chresteuetai) — the verb chresteuomai ("to be kind, to show oneself useful, to act benevolently") appears only here in the New Testament. It derives from chrestos ("useful, kind, good, gracious"). In the LXX, the adjective chrestos describes God's goodness: Psalm 34:8 (LXX 33:9): geusasthe kai idete hoti chrestos ho kyrios — "Taste and see that the Lord is good." Psalm 100:5 (LXX 99:5): hoti chrestos ho kyrios — "For the Lord is good." Psalm 145:9 (LXX 144:9): chrestos kyrios tois sympasi — "The Lord is good to all." Kindness, like patience, is first a divine quality that love then embodies in human behavior.

"Love does not envy" (ou zeloi) — the verb zeloo ("to be jealous, to envy, to desire ardently") can be positive (to be zealous for something good) or negative (to envy what another has). Here the negative sense governs: love does not burn with resentment at another's gift, position, or recognition. The noun zelos ("jealousy") appeared in 3:3 as the primary evidence that the Corinthians were "still of the flesh": "for while there is jealousy (zelos) and strife (eris) among you, are you not of the flesh and behaving only in a human way?" The jealousy that diagnosed fleshliness in chapter 3 is the exact opposite of love in chapter 13. A community governed by love does not envy; a community consumed by jealousy over who has which gift, who follows which teacher, who holds which status, is a community without love.

"Love does not boast" (ou perpereuetai) — the verb perpereuomai ("to boast, to brag, to vaunt oneself") appears only here in the New Testament. It describes the self-promoting display that calls attention to one's own achievements or superiority. The Corinthians' boasting has been a persistent problem: they boast in their teachers (3:21), in their wisdom (4:7), in their tolerance of immorality (5:2, 5:6). Love does not do this.

"Love is not arrogant" (ou physiotai) — the verb physioō ("to puff up, to inflate, to make arrogant") is the single most diagnostic word in the entire letter for the Corinthian condition. It appeared in 4:6: "that none of you may be puffed up in favor of one against another." In 4:18: "some are puffed up, as though I were not coming to you." In 4:19: "the arrogance (physiosin) of those who are puffed up." In 5:2: "and you are puffed up" — the community's response to the incestuous man was not grief but inflation. In 8:1: "knowledge puffs up, but love builds up" — the thesis statement that chapter 13 develops. Now in 13:4, love ou physiotai — is not puffed up. The Corinthians' defining disease is the precise opposite of love's character. If love is not puffed up, and the Corinthians are puffed up, then the Corinthians do not have love, regardless of what gifts they possess. The word physioō appears seven times in 1 Corinthians — more than in all of Paul's other letters combined. It is the letter's signature diagnosis, and its appearance here, in the description of what love is not, draws the letter's entire argument to a point.

"Love is not rude" (ouk aschemonei) — the verb aschemoneo ("to behave indecently, to act improperly, to do what is unseemly") describes conduct that violates the standards of fitting behavior within the community. The cognate noun aschemosynē ("shameful behavior") and the adjective aschēmōn ("unseemly") appear in the letter's discussion of the body's less presentable parts (12:23). But the behavioral sense is primary here: love does not act in ways that shame or dishonor others. The Corinthians' conduct at the Lord's supper — gorging while others go hungry, humiliating those who have nothing (11:21-22) — is precisely the behavior of a community that is aschemonei.

"Love does not insist on its own way" (ou zetei ta heautes) — literally, "does not seek the things of itself." This is the exact principle Paul stated in 10:24: "Let no one seek his own good, but the good of his neighbor" (medeis to heautou zeteto alla to tou heterou). And in 10:33: Paul does not seek "my own advantage, but that of many, that they may be saved." The phrase ta heautes ("its own things") describes self-interest — the orientation that places personal benefit, personal rights, personal preferences above the well-being of others. The Corinthians have been relentlessly seeking their own: their own preferred teachers (chapters 1-4), their own sexual freedom (chapters 5-6), their own right to eat idol-food (chapters 8-10), their own meal before others arrive (11:21), their own preferred spiritual gift (chapters 12-14). Love does not do this. Love seeks the other's good.

"Love is not irritable" (ou paroxunetai) — the verb paroxuno ("to provoke, to irritate, to sharpen") in the passive means "to be provoked, to become irritated, to be exasperated." In the LXX, the word frequently describes God's anger when provoked by Israel's idolatry. Deuteronomy 9:18: "you had done what was evil in the sight of the Lord your God, provoking him to anger" (paroxunai auton). But when used of human relationships, paroxunetai describes the quick-tempered disposition that flares at every offense, the person who is constantly irritated by others. Love does not react this way.

"Love does not keep a record of wrongs" (ou logizetai to kakon) — the verb logizomai ("to reckon, to count, to calculate, to credit, to take into account") is an accounting term. In commercial and legal usage, it means to enter something into the ledger, to record a debt, to charge an amount to someone's account. In the LXX, the word appears in a crucial text: Psalm 32:2 (LXX 31:2): makarios aner hou ou me logisetai kyrios hamartian — "Blessed is the man against whom the Lord does not reckon sin." God's grace is defined by what he does not count. Love operates the same way: it does not keep a running ledger of wrongs received. It does not accumulate grievances. The person who catalogues every offense committed against them and produces the list at every conflict is operating in the opposite of love. The Corinthians, with their lawsuits against one another (6:1-8) and their competitive grievances, are keeping precisely the kind of record love refuses to maintain.

"Love does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth" (ou chairei epi te adikia, sunchairei de te aletheia) (13:6) — the verb chairo ("to rejoice, to be glad") appears in both its negative and positive forms. Love does not celebrate when injustice occurs (adikia — "unrighteousness, injustice, wrongdoing"). The word adikia echoes 6:1 and 6:9, where Paul addressed the Corinthians' lawsuits: "why not rather be wronged?" (adikeisthe, 6:7). The Corinthians who defraud their brothers (6:8: "you yourselves wrong and defraud — and that even your own brothers") are rejoicing in adikia. Love instead rejoices with (sunchairei, "rejoices together with") the truth (aletheia). The preposition syn ("with, together with") gives the verb a communal quality: love does not merely approve of truth from a distance but participates in truth's triumphs, celebrates when truth prevails.

"Love bears all things" (panta stegei) (13:7) — the verb stego ("to cover, to bear, to endure, to protect") can mean either "to cover over" (as in covering a roof — the related noun stege means "roof") or "to bear, to endure." Both senses may be active: love covers the faults of others (not exposing, not publicizing, not weaponizing another's failures) and love endures hardship without breaking. Paul used the same verb in 9:12: "we endure (stegomen) anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ." Paul's own practice of endurance is the embodiment of what love does.

"Love believes all things" (panta pisteuei) — the verb pisteuo ("to believe, to trust, to have faith in") does not describe gullibility or naivety. In the context of community life, it describes the disposition that extends trust to others rather than suspicion, that gives the benefit of the doubt, that does not assume the worst about a brother or sister. The jealousy and factionalism of the Corinthian community are built on suspicion and competitive evaluation. Love operates from trust.

"Love hopes all things" (panta elpizei) — the verb elpizo ("to hope, to expect, to look forward to") extends the posture of trust into the future. Love does not give up on people. It continues to hope for their growth, their restoration, their maturation. The word elpis ("hope") will reappear in verse 13 as one of the three things that abide.

"Love endures all things" (panta hypomenei) — the verb hypomeno ("to endure, to remain under, to bear up under, to persevere") describes the capacity to sustain pressure without collapsing. The word is composed of hypo ("under") and meno ("to remain") — to remain under the weight, to stay in place when everything pushes you to abandon your post. In the LXX, the word describes the faithful who wait for God under duress. Psalm 27:14 (LXX 26:14): "Wait (hypomeinon) for the Lord; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait (hypomeinon) for the Lord!" Isaiah 40:31: "they who wait (hoi hypomenontes) for the Lord shall renew their strength." The four-fold panta ("all things") — bears all, believes all, hopes all, endures all — gives the description a comprehensive, all-encompassing character. Love is not selective in its patience; it bears everything, trusts in every circumstance, hopes in every situation, endures every trial.

The cumulative effect of the fifteen predications is devastating when read against the Corinthians' behavior. Point by point, the description of love is the description of what the Corinthian community is not. They are impatient with one another (factions, lawsuits). They are not kind (the wealthy humiliate the poor at the Lord's supper). They are jealous (zelos, 3:3). They boast (in their teachers, 3:21; in their tolerance, 5:6). They are puffed up (physioō, throughout the letter). They behave indecently (at the meal, at worship). They seek their own (their own rights, their own preferred gifts). They are irritable (quarreling, erides, 1:11). They keep records of wrongs (lawsuits, 6:1-8). They rejoice at wrongdoing (the case of incest, 5:1-2). They fail to bear, to trust, to hope, to endure. The portrait of love in 13:4-7 is simultaneously a portrait of everything the Corinthians are failing to be. It is not a sentimental poem but a surgical diagnosis.

Verses 8-12: The Permanence of Love and the Temporality of Gifts

"Love never ends. As for prophecies, they will pass away; as for tongues, they will cease; as for knowledge, it will pass away" (13:8).

The verb piptei in the phrase he agape oudepote piptei ("love never falls") means "to fall, to collapse, to fail." The negation oudepote ("never, at no time") is emphatic. Love does not fail — not ever, not under any circumstance. The verb piptō in the LXX describes the falling of walls (Joshua 6:5, 20 — the walls of Jericho fell), the falling of the mighty (2 Samuel 1:19 — "How the mighty have fallen"), and the collapse of what seemed permanent. Love is the thing that does not collapse. Everything else may fall; love does not.

Paul then names three gifts that will pass away, and the three he names are not random. Prophecies (propheteia), tongues (glossai), and knowledge (gnosis) are the very gifts that dominate the Corinthians' spiritual landscape and the gifts Paul has been discussing throughout chapters 12-14.

"Prophecies will be made inoperative" (prophetiai katargethesontai) — the verb katargeo ("to render inactive, to make of no effect, to abolish, to bring to an end") appeared in 1:28 for God's nullification of the things that "are" by choosing the things that "are not." It is a strong word: the gifts will not merely fade; they will be rendered inoperative, abolished, made obsolete. Prophecies will one day serve no function.

"Tongues will cease" (glossai pausontai) — the verb pauo in the middle voice (pausontai) means "to cease, to stop of their own accord." Tongues will simply stop. The middle voice may suggest that tongues will cease on their own, without an external force shutting them down — they will reach their natural endpoint and stop.

"Knowledge will be made inoperative" (gnosis katargethesetai) — the same verb as prophecy. Knowledge too will be abolished. The gnosis the Corinthians boast about — their theological understanding, their insight into the nature of idols and God — is a temporary gift. It will be made obsolete.

The point is not that these gifts are bad but that they are temporary. They serve a function in the present age — the time between Christ's death and his return (cf. 11:26: "you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes"). But they belong to the interval. Love belongs to the destination.

"For we know in part and we prophesy in part, but when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away" (13:9-10).

The phrase ek merous ("in part, partially, incompletely") describes the fragmentary nature of present knowledge and prophecy. The preposition ek with meros ("part, portion, share") indicates that what the community possesses now is a portion of something larger, a fragment of a whole. Present knowledge is not false; it is incomplete. Present prophecy is not fraudulent; it is partial. The community sees truly but not fully.

The word to teleion ("the perfect, the complete, the mature, the whole") stands in direct contrast to ek merous ("in part"). The word teleios has already appeared in this letter. In 2:6, Paul spoke of wisdom "among the mature" (en tois teleiois) — those who have progressed beyond infancy. In 14:20, Paul will urge the Corinthians: "Brothers, do not be children (paidia) in your thinking. Be infants (nepiazete) in evil, but in your thinking be mature (teleioi)." The word describes completeness, wholeness, maturity — the state in which the partial gives way to the full.

What is "the perfect" that is coming? Paul does not specify it by name. The context provides two complementary pointers. First, the eschatological framework of the letter places the community between two events: the death of Christ (11:26: "you proclaim the Lord's death") and the return of Christ (11:26: "until he comes"; 1:7: "as you wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ"; 15:23-28: the sequence of Christ's coming, his reign, and the subjection of all things to God). The "partial" characterizes the present age; the "complete" characterizes the age to come. When the Lord returns and all things are brought to their consummation, partial knowledge will be superseded by full knowledge, and partial prophecy will give way to unmediated reality. Second, verse 12 will specify the content of to teleion in relational terms: "then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known." The perfect is the state of direct, unmediated knowledge of God — face-to-face encounter rather than mirror-reflected glimpses.

The verb katargethesetai ("will be made inoperative") reappears: when the complete arrives, the partial will be abolished. The partial is not destroyed because it was wrong but because it is no longer needed. A partial map becomes unnecessary when you arrive at the destination. A scaffolding is removed when the building is complete. The gifts of prophecy, tongues, and knowledge are the scaffolding of the present age; love is the building itself.

"When I was a child, I spoke like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I gave up childish ways" (13:11).

Paul introduces an analogy from human development. The word nepios ("child, infant") is the same word he used in 3:1 to describe the Corinthians: "I could not address you as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh, as infants (nepiois) in Christ." The connection is pointed: the community's attachment to spiritual gifts as markers of status is itself a form of spiritual infancy. Three verbs describe the child's behavior: elaloun ("I was speaking"), ephronoun ("I was thinking"), elogizomen ("I was reasoning") — all imperfect tense, describing continuous past activity. The child's speech, thought, and reasoning were characteristic of a child's level of development. They were not wrong for a child; they were appropriate to the stage. But hote gegona aner ("when I became a man") — the perfect tense of ginomai, marking a completed transition with lasting results — katergeka ta tou nepiou ("I put away the things of the child"). The same verb katargeo appears for the third time: the things of childhood are abolished, rendered inoperative, set aside as no longer appropriate.

The analogy maps directly onto the gifts discussion. The spiritual gifts the Corinthians prize belong to the community's present stage of development — the "childhood" of the present age, the period of partial knowledge and incomplete understanding. These gifts are not wrong; they are appropriate to the stage. But they are not permanent. When maturity arrives — when to teleion comes — the gifts of the partial era will be set aside as naturally as an adult sets aside childhood toys. The Corinthians' fixation on tongues and other spectacular gifts is, by this analogy, a fixation on childhood things. Love is the mature reality that endures.

The word nepios also connects to 14:20, where Paul will say: "Brothers, do not be children (paidia) in your thinking. Be infants (nepiazete) in evil, but in your thinking be mature (teleioi)." The letter consistently calls the Corinthians to grow up — to move from infancy to maturity, from partial understanding to complete understanding, from fascination with gifts to the governance of love.

"For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (13:12).

The word esoptron ("mirror") describes the ancient bronze or copper mirror — a polished metal surface that produced a reflection, but one that was imperfect, somewhat distorted, indirect. In the LXX, the word appears in Wisdom of Solomon 7:26, where wisdom is described as "a reflection (esoptron) of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God." The first-century mirror was not the clear glass mirror of the modern world. It provided a real image but a mediated, partial one. Looking at a mirror was looking at a representation, not at the thing itself.

The phrase di' esoptrou en ainigmati ("through a mirror in an enigma/riddle") doubles the indirectness. The word ainigma ("riddle, dark saying, puzzle") appears in Numbers 12:8 (LXX), where God distinguishes Moses from other prophets: "With him I speak mouth to mouth (stoma kata stoma), clearly (eidos), and not in riddles (di' ainigmaton), and he beholds the form of the Lord." All prophets receive revelation en ainigmati — in riddles, through enigmatic visions and dark sayings. Moses alone received revelation "mouth to mouth," clearly, face to face. Paul's language in 13:12 draws directly on this distinction. The present mode of knowing God is the mode available to ordinary prophets: through a mirror, in riddles, indirectly. The future mode — tote de prosopon pros prosopon ("but then face to face") — is the mode God granted uniquely to Moses.

The phrase prosopon pros prosopon ("face to face") echoes the LXX of several foundational passages. Genesis 32:30 (LXX 32:31): Jacob named the place Peniel, saying "I have seen God face to face (eidos theou prosopon pros prosopon), and yet my life has been delivered." Exodus 33:11: "The Lord used to speak to Moses face to face (enopios enopio), as a man speaks to his friend." Deuteronomy 34:10: "And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face (prosopon kata prosopon)." In the LXX tradition, face-to-face encounter with God is the highest form of knowledge — unmediated, direct, personal. Paul says this is what awaits: when the partial gives way to the complete, the community will know God not through mirrors and riddles but face to face.

The final clause — arti ginosko ek merous, tote de epignosomai kathos kai epegnosthen — "now I know in part, then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known." The verb epignosomai (future of epiginosko, "to know fully, to recognize completely, to understand thoroughly") is strengthened by the prefix epi-, intensifying the basic verb ginosko. Present knowledge is ek merous — partial, fragmentary. Future knowledge will be epignosis — full recognition, complete understanding. And the standard for that future knowledge is established by the passive: kathos kai epegnosthen — "even as I have been fully known." God's knowledge of the believer is already complete. God already knows fully. The future consummation brings the believer's knowledge into correspondence with God's knowledge — the believer will know God as God already knows the believer.

This language reaches back to 8:2-3, where Paul said: "If anyone thinks he knows something, he does not yet know as he ought to know. But if anyone loves God, he is known by him." The priority established in 8:2-3 was that being known by God matters more than knowing about God. Now in 13:12, that priority is maintained but a future convergence is promised: the one who is already known by God will come to know God fully. The present asymmetry — God knows me fully; I know partially — will be resolved at the consummation. The resolution does not mean the creature becomes equal to the Creator in knowledge; it means the creature's knowledge becomes direct, unmediated, face-to-face, no longer filtered through mirrors and riddles.

Verse 13: The Three That Remain

"So now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; but the greatest of these is love" (13:13).

The word nyni ("now") is temporal, referring to the present period — the time between the ages, the era in which the community lives as it awaits the completion. In this present time, three things menei ("abide, remain, endure"): pistis ("faith"), elpis ("hope"), and agape ("love"). The verb meno ("to remain, to abide, to stay, to endure") is the same verb used in 3:14 for the builder's work that "survives" the fire: "if the work of anyone survives (menei), he will receive a reward." The things that remain are the things that survive the testing. Prophecies are abolished; tongues cease; knowledge is made obsolete. But faith, hope, and love remain.

The triad of faith, hope, and love appears elsewhere in Paul. In 1 Thessalonians 1:3, Paul remembers "your work of faith and labor of love and steadfastness of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ." In 1 Thessalonians 5:8, Paul urges: "putting on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation." The three appear to function as a summary of the Christian life: faith is the posture of trust toward God in the present; hope is the orientation toward God's future action; love is the active commitment to the good of others in the community and beyond.

But the final clause breaks the equality: meizon de touton he agape — "but the greatest of these is love." The comparative meizon ("greater") elevates love above faith and hope. Why is love the greatest?

The argument of the chapter provides the answer. Faith and hope belong to the present age's structure of partial knowledge and anticipation. Faith is the posture of trust in what is not yet seen. When the object of faith is fully present — when the Lord is revealed face to face — faith as trust in the unseen will be transformed into sight. Hope is the orientation toward what has not yet arrived. When the hoped-for consummation occurs, hope will be fulfilled and thus transformed. Faith and hope are oriented toward a future that will one day become present; when it does, they will be changed. Love, by contrast, is not oriented toward something it lacks. Love gives; it does not wait. Love bears, believes, hopes, endures (13:7). Love is the mode of God's own existence — God is patient, God is kind, God does not insist on his own way. When the consummation arrives and faith becomes sight and hope becomes possession, love will not be transformed because love is already the character of the consummated reality. Love is the substance of which faith and hope are the anticipation.

The word meizon also carries resonance from 12:31, where Paul urged the Corinthians to desire ta charismata ta meizona — "the greater gifts." The Corinthians want the meizon gifts. Paul has just shown them the meizon reality: love. The "still more excellent way" (12:31) is not a higher gift alongside the others but the governing principle that makes all gifts valuable. Without love, the meizon gifts are nothing (13:1-3). With love, even the lesser gifts serve the body's edification. The Corinthians have been seeking greatness in the wrong category. They seek the greatest gift; Paul tells them the greatest thing is not a gift at all but the disposition that gives gifts their purpose.

The Chapter's Argument as a Whole

1 Corinthians 13 is the theological center of the three-chapter unit on spiritual gifts (chapters 12-14) and, in many ways, the theological center of the entire letter. It is not a detachable hymn to love inserted into a discussion of spiritual gifts. It is the argument's essential foundation: the principle without which the gifts discussion collapses and the principle that resolves the tensions diagnosed from the letter's opening paragraph.

The chapter performs three interconnected functions.

First (vv. 1-3), it establishes love as the non-negotiable condition for the value of all spiritual gifts and acts of sacrifice. The structure is hypothetical and escalating: if I have tongues — the gift the Corinthians prize — but not love, I am noise. If I have prophecy, knowledge, faith — the gifts Paul himself values most — but not love, I am nothing. If I sacrifice everything I own and even my body — the most extreme acts of devotion imaginable — but without love, I gain nothing. The progression is designed to dismantle every possible basis for self-congratulation except love. No gift, no sacrifice, no knowledge compensates for its absence. The Corinthians who compete for prominence through the display of spectacular gifts are building on nothing if love is absent from their exercise.

Second (vv. 4-7), it describes love's character in terms that function simultaneously as a theological portrait and as a diagnosis of the community's failures. Each predication of what love is or is not corresponds to a specific problem Paul has addressed in the letter. Love is patient — the Corinthians are contentious. Love does not envy — the Corinthians are jealous. Love is not puffed up — the Corinthians are puffed up (the letter's signature diagnosis). Love does not seek its own — the Corinthians insist on their own rights. Love does not keep a record of wrongs — the Corinthians take each other to court. The description is not decorative but diagnostic: hold up this portrait and compare it to your community's behavior, and the gap between what you are and what love requires becomes visible.

Third (vv. 8-13), it establishes love as the only permanent reality among the things the community possesses. Prophecy, tongues, and knowledge belong to the present age — the age of partial understanding, mirror-reflections, and riddles. They serve their function now, but they will be abolished when the complete arrives. Love never falls. Love survives the transition from the partial to the complete because love is the character of the completed reality itself. God's own nature is described throughout the LXX in the terms Paul uses for love: patient, kind, not keeping a record of wrongs (Psalm 32:2, where God does not reckon sin). Love is not waiting for the consummation; love is the substance of the consummation. Faith and hope are oriented toward the future; love is the future already operative in the present.

The chapter's argument reaches back to 8:1 and brings it to full development. "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up" was a compressed thesis; chapter 13 is its exposition. Knowledge — even theological knowledge about the one God and the nothingness of idols — produces inflation without love. Love produces construction — the building up of the body (oikodomeo, the governing verb from 3:9-14 through 14:26). The Corinthians possess knowledge; they lack love. Therefore they are puffed up instead of built up, divided instead of united, competing instead of serving.

The chapter also advances the letter's sustained argument about maturity and immaturity. In 3:1, Paul called the Corinthians nepioi ("infants") because their factionalism proved their spiritual immaturity. In 13:11, Paul uses nepios again: "when I was a child, I spoke like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child." The gifts the Corinthians fixate on — especially tongues — are the speech of childhood. Growing up means putting away childish things and embracing the mature reality: love. The Corinthians' resistance to this message is itself evidence of their infancy. A community that prizes tongues over prophecy, display over edification, and personal status over communal well-being is a community that has not yet grown up.

The chapter's placement between chapters 12 and 14 is structurally essential. Chapter 12 asked: how do the diverse gifts relate to one another within the one body? The answer: all are necessary, none is dispensable, and God distributes them as he wills. Chapter 14 will ask: how should gifts be exercised in the assembly? The answer: for building up, intelligibly, in orderly fashion. Chapter 13 provides the principle that connects the two: love. Love is the reason all members must be valued (because love does not say "I have no need of you"). Love is the reason prophecy is superior to tongues in the assembly (because love seeks the other's good, and prophecy is intelligible while tongues without interpretation is not). Love is the reason worship must be orderly (because love is not rude and does not insist on its own). Without chapter 13, chapter 14's practical instructions would lack their theological foundation. Without chapter 13, chapter 12's body metaphor would lack its animating principle. The body has many members (chapter 12); the body is animated by love (chapter 13); therefore the body's members exercise their gifts for the common good in an orderly assembly (chapter 14).

Several vocabulary threads from earlier in the letter reach their culmination in this chapter.

The verb physioō ("to puff up"), which diagnosed the Corinthian disease in 4:6, 4:18, 4:19, 5:2, and 8:1, appears in 13:4 as the direct opposite of love's character. The diagnosis that has run through the letter now receives its definitive treatment: puffing up is not merely a social problem but the negation of love itself.

The noun gnōsis ("knowledge"), which the Corinthians boasted about in 8:1 and which Paul affirmed as genuine but insufficient without love, appears in 13:2 (all knowledge), 13:8 (knowledge will pass away), 13:9 and 13:12 (we know in part). Knowledge is real and valuable, but it is temporary and partial. Love is permanent and complete.

The verb oikodomeō ("to build up"), which appeared in 3:9-14 for building on the foundation and in 8:1 for what love does, will appear again in 14:4 (prophecy builds up the church) and 14:26 (let all things be done for building up). Chapter 13 does not use the verb itself, but the concept governs the argument: love builds up because love seeks the other's good; gifts without love do not build up because they serve only the individual. The building metaphor that runs from chapter 3 through chapter 14 has love as its operating principle.

The word teleios ("perfect, complete, mature") appeared in 2:6 for those to whom Paul speaks wisdom. Now in 13:10 it describes the consummation that will abolish the partial. The movement from nepios (infant, 3:1) to teleios (mature, 2:6, 13:10, 14:20) is the movement the Corinthians need to make — from the infantile fixation on gifts to the mature governance of love.

The phrase prosopon pros prosopon ("face to face") in 13:12 draws on the LXX's deepest tradition of theophany — the unmediated encounter with God that was granted uniquely to Moses (Numbers 12:8, Deuteronomy 34:10). Paul says this encounter awaits the entire community. The present age offers mirrors and riddles; the consummation offers face-to-face knowledge of God. This eschatological promise grounds the chapter's argument about the temporality of gifts: gifts belong to the mirror-and-riddle age; love belongs to the face-to-face age.

The chapter's final word — meizon de touton he agape, "the greatest of these is love" — answers the question the Corinthians have been asking throughout the letter, though they have been asking it in the wrong form. They asked: which teacher is greatest? (chapters 1-4). They asked: which knowledge is greatest? (chapter 8). They asked: which gift is greatest? (chapters 12-14). Paul's answer, at every turn, has been the same: the question itself is wrongly framed. The greatest thing is not a teacher, not a body of knowledge, not a spiritual gift. The greatest thing is love — the disposition that makes teachers servants instead of celebrities, that makes knowledge constructive instead of inflating, that makes gifts communal instead of competitive. Love is not one more item on the list of things to desire. Love is the reality that gives all other things their meaning, their function, and their value. Without it, the most impressive spiritual resume amounts to a noisy gong and a clanging cymbal. With it, even the humblest member of the body serves the good of the whole, and the community is built up into the dwelling place of God's Spirit.
