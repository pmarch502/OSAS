1 Corinthians 4 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Corinthians 3): Paul opened chapter 3 by applying the argument of chapters 1-2 directly to the Corinthians. Despite everything he taught them about the Spirit's wisdom, they are behaving as "fleshly people" (sarkinois) and "infants in Christ" (nepiois en Christo) rather than as "spiritual people" (pneumatikois) (3:1). The evidence of their immaturity is the factionalism itself: "For when one says, 'I am of Paul,' and another, 'I am of Apollos,' are you not merely human?" (3:4). Paul then corrected their understanding of apostles: "What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you believed, as the Lord assigned to each" (3:5). The word diakonoi ("servants, attendants") strips the teachers of the exalted status the Corinthians have been conferring on them. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth; neither planter nor waterer is anything — only God matters (3:6-7).

Paul then shifted to a construction metaphor. He laid a foundation — "Jesus Christ" (3:11) — and others build on it. The quality of each builder's work will be tested by fire on "the day" (3:13). A builder whose work survives receives a reward; a builder whose work burns up will "suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire" (3:15). Then came a solemn warning: "If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple" (3:17). The pronoun "you" (hymeis) is plural — the temple is the community, not the individual, and the one who destroys it faces God's destruction. The warning applies to anyone whose factional behavior tears apart the community that is God's sacred dwelling.

The chapter closed with a return to the wisdom/foolishness theme. "If anyone among you thinks that he is wise in this age, he must become foolish, so that he may become wise" (3:18). Paul quoted Job 5:13 — "He catches the wise in their craftiness" — and Psalm 94:11 (LXX 93:11) — "The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are futile." Both citations reinforce the argument from 1:19-20: God overturns human wisdom. The climax of chapter 3 inverted the Corinthians' entire framework: "So then, no one is to be boasting in people. For all things belong to you, whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas, or the world or life or death, or things present or things to come; all things belong to you, and you belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God" (3:21-23). The Corinthians have been saying "I belong to Paul" or "I belong to Apollos." Paul says the opposite: Paul belongs to you. Apollos belongs to you. The teachers are your servants, not your patrons. The hierarchy runs not from apostle down to community but from God through Christ to the community, with apostles included among the community's possessions.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Corinthians 5): Paul transitions from the factionalism crisis to a specific case of sexual immorality: "It is actually reported that there is sexual immorality among you, and sexual immorality of such a kind as does not exist even among the Gentiles, namely, that someone has his father's wife" (5:1). The case is extreme — a man cohabiting with his stepmother, a violation of Leviticus 18:8 ("You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's wife") and Deuteronomy 22:30 (LXX 23:1) ("A man shall not take his father's wife"). Even the surrounding Gentile culture, which lacked the Torah's prohibitions, recognized this as transgressive. Roman law prohibited marriage between a stepson and stepmother.

The critical link between chapters 4 and 5 is the word phusioo ("to puff up, to inflate, to make arrogant"). Paul used it in 4:6 ("that no one of you will become arrogant [physiothe] in behalf of one against the other"), 4:18 ("Now some have become arrogant [ephysiothesan]"), and 4:19 ("the words of those who are arrogant [pephysiomenon]"). In 5:2, the same word reappears: "You have become arrogant [pephysiomenoi] and have not mourned instead." The arrogance that Paul diagnosed as the root of factionalism in chapters 1-4 is the same arrogance that now prevents the community from dealing with open sin. They are "puffed up" — inflated with self-importance about their spiritual status, their wisdom, their gifts — and this inflation has made them incapable both of properly evaluating their teachers (the faction problem) and of exercising moral discernment within the community (the immorality problem). The disease is the same; chapter 5 is a new symptom.

Paul's response in chapter 5 also demonstrates exactly what he claimed in chapter 4. In 4:19-21, he warned that he was coming and would test not words but power, and asked whether the Corinthians wanted him to come "with a rod." In 5:3-5, he exercises that authority: "For I, on my part, though absent in body but present in spirit, have already judged him who has so committed this." The apostolic authority Paul asserted in chapter 4 is immediately deployed in chapter 5. The father who warned his children (4:14-15) now disciplines his household.

How chapter 4 connects these: Chapter 4 is the conclusion of the entire argument that began in 1:10. Chapters 1-3 established the theological foundation: the cross overturns human wisdom (ch. 1), the Spirit provides a different kind of wisdom inaccessible to natural faculties (ch. 2), and the Corinthians' factionalism proves they are operating by the world's criteria rather than the Spirit's (ch. 3). Chapter 4 now draws the practical conclusions. It does four things: (1) it defines how the Corinthians should regard apostles — as servants and stewards, not as competing patrons to be ranked (vv. 1-5); (2) it reveals that Paul has been using himself and Apollos as illustrations throughout, and commands the Corinthians not to go "beyond what is written" in their evaluations (vv. 6-7); (3) it deploys devastating irony to expose the Corinthians' pretensions by contrasting their self-satisfied condition with the apostles' actual suffering (vv. 8-13); and (4) it shifts from irony to paternal appeal, asserting Paul's unique relationship to the community as their father and warning the arrogant that he is coming (vv. 14-21). The chapter thus bridges the theological argument (chs. 1-3) and the specific disciplinary action (ch. 5) by establishing Paul's authority and the Corinthians' accountability.

Verses 1-5: How to Regard Apostles — Servants, Stewards, and the Lord's Judgment

"This is how one should regard us: as servants of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God" (4:1).

The opening word houtos ("thus, in this way, so") points backward to everything Paul has argued in chapters 1-3. The Corinthians have been regarding Paul and Apollos as competing wisdom-figures, ranking them by rhetorical skill and attaching themselves to one against another. Paul now prescribes how they should regard the apostles instead. The verb logizomai ("to reckon, to consider, to regard, to calculate") is a term of accounting and assessment — it describes the mental act of placing something in the correct category. The Corinthians have miscategorized the apostles. Paul tells them to recalculate.

The first category is hyperetas Christou ("servants of Christ"). The word hyperetes is distinct from diakonos (the word Paul used in 3:5). A diakonos is a general servant or attendant. A hyperetes was originally an "under-rower" — one who rowed in the lower tier of a galley ship, taking direction from above. In first-century usage, it described an assistant, an attendant who carries out the orders of a superior: in the synagogue, the hyperetes was the attendant who handled the scrolls (Luke 4:20); in the courts, the hyperetes was the officer who carried out the judge's sentences (Matthew 5:25); in Acts 13:5, John Mark served as hyperetes to Paul and Barnabas. The word emphasizes subordination and instrumentality. The apostles are not independent authorities competing for followers; they are under-rowers taking direction from Christ. They have no independent standing that would justify competitive allegiance.

The second category is oikonomous mysterion theou ("stewards of the mysteries of God"). The word oikonomos ("steward, household manager") describes the person entrusted with managing another's household or estate. In the LXX and in first-century Greco-Roman society, the oikonomos was typically a slave or freedman who managed the master's property, distributed provisions to the household, and was accountable to the owner for the management of resources that were never his own. The word appears in Jesus' parables about stewards who must give account (Luke 12:42-48; 16:1-8). The critical feature of the oikonomos is that he manages what belongs to someone else. The mysteries are God's, not Paul's. Paul dispenses them; he does not originate them.

The word mysteria ("mysteries") connects directly to 2:7, where Paul spoke of "God's wisdom in a mystery" (theou sophian en mysterio) — the hidden plan of God, concealed from the rulers of this age, now revealed through the Spirit. The plural "mysteries" may indicate the various aspects of God's revealed plan — the gospel of the crucified Messiah, the inclusion of the Gentiles, the coming resurrection, the nature of the present age and the age to come. Paul and the other apostles are entrusted with dispensing these revealed secrets to the community. They are not the source of wisdom; they are its distributors. Evaluating them by their personal rhetorical skill is like evaluating a steward by his wardrobe rather than by whether he faithfully distributes his master's provisions.

"In this case, moreover, it is required of stewards that one be found trustworthy" (4:2).

The word pistos ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") identifies the single criterion by which a steward is properly evaluated. Not eloquence. Not philosophical sophistication. Not rhetorical impressiveness. Trustworthiness. The verb heurisko ("to find, to discover") in the passive (heurethe, "be found") implies that the steward's faithfulness will be discovered upon examination — when the master returns and inspects the accounts. The only question that matters about a steward is: did he faithfully manage what was entrusted to him? This reframes the entire Corinthian evaluation. The Corinthians have been asking, "Which teacher is most impressive?" Paul says the only relevant question is, "Which steward is faithful to the master's trust?"

"But to me it is an insignificant matter that I would be examined by you, or by any human court; in fact, I do not even examine myself" (4:3).

The verb anakrino ("to examine, to investigate, to judge, to evaluate") is the same word that dominated 2:14-15. There, Paul established two categories: the psychikos person "does not accept the things of the Spirit" and cannot examine (anakrino) them; the pneumatikos person "examines all things but is himself examined by no one" (2:15). Now Paul applies that principle to his own case. The phrase eis elachiston estin ("it is a very small thing, it is insignificant") is emphatic — Paul treats the Corinthians' evaluation of him as a matter of negligible importance.

Three courts of judgment are dismissed. First, "by you" (hyph' hymon) — the Corinthians' own assessment. They have been evaluating Paul and finding him less impressive than Apollos. Paul declares this evaluation irrelevant. Second, "by any human court" (hypo anthropines hemeras) — literally "by a human day," where hemera ("day") functions as a metonym for a day of judicial examination, a court session, a day of reckoning. Any human tribunal, any merely human standard of evaluation, is incapable of rendering a valid verdict on an apostle's faithfulness. Third, and most strikingly, "I do not even examine myself" (all' oude emauton anakrino). Paul declines to be his own judge. This is not a claim of perfection; it is a claim about jurisdiction. The question of whether Paul has been a faithful steward is not one that any human judgment — including his own — is competent to settle.

"For I am not aware of anything against myself; however I am not vindicated by this, but the one who examines me is the Lord" (4:4).

Paul's clean conscience (ouden gar emauto synoida, "I know nothing against myself" — the verb synoida means "to be conscious of, to share knowledge with oneself") does not constitute acquittal. The phrase all' ouk en touto dedikaiomei ("but I am not justified/vindicated by this") uses the perfect passive of dikaioo ("to justify, to declare righteous, to acquit"). Paul's self-examination, even when it finds nothing amiss, does not produce a verdict of acquittal. The reason: ho de anakrinon me kyrios estin — "the one who examines me is the Lord." The Lord is the only competent judge of an apostle's stewardship. Human courts lack the jurisdiction. Personal conscience lacks the reliability. Only the Lord possesses both the authority and the knowledge required for a valid assessment.

The word kyrios ("Lord") here carries its full weight. In the argument of chapters 1-3, the kyrios is the one whose mind the community possesses (2:16), the one who assigns roles to servants (3:5), the one whose building the community is (3:9). The Lord who commissioned the steward is the Lord who will evaluate the steward. No other evaluator is competent.

"Therefore do not go on passing judgment before the time, but wait until the Lord comes, who will both bring to light the things hidden in the darkness and disclose the motives of human hearts; and then praise will come to each person from God" (4:5).

The word hoste ("therefore, so then, consequently") draws a practical conclusion from the preceding argument. The imperative me krinete ("do not judge") with pro kairou ("before the time, before the appointed occasion") does not prohibit all judgment — Paul will exercise fierce judgment in chapter 5. It prohibits premature judgment, specifically the ranking and evaluating of apostles that the factionalism represents. The "time" (kairos) is specified: heos an elthe ho kyrios — "until the Lord comes." The verb erchomai ("to come") describes the Lord's future arrival, his parousia — the event the community is awaiting (1:7, where they "wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ").

What the Lord will do at his coming is described with two verbs. First, photise ta krypta tou skotous — "he will illuminate the hidden things of darkness." The verb photizo ("to illuminate, to bring to light, to make visible") means to shine light on what is concealed in skotos ("darkness"). The hidden things are not necessarily evil; they are the concealed realities of each person's service — the true quality and motivation of their work, which cannot be assessed from external appearances. Second, phanerosei tas boulas ton kardion — "he will make manifest the purposes/deliberations of the hearts." The verb phaneroo ("to make manifest, to reveal, to make visible") and the noun boule ("purpose, intention, plan, counsel") together describe the exposure of interior motivation. The kardiai ("hearts") in Hebrew and LXX usage are the seat of will, intention, and deliberation — not merely emotion. The Lord will reveal what each person was actually trying to accomplish, what motivated their service, what they were building and why.

The result: kai tote ho epainos genesetai hekasto apo tou theou — "and then the praise will come to each from God." The word epainos ("praise, commendation, approval") is striking. Paul does not say "then judgment" or "then punishment" but "then praise." The emphasis is on commendation from God as the only praise that matters. The Corinthians have been distributing praise according to their own preferences — praising Apollos for his eloquence, or Paul for his founding role, or Cephas for his apostolic seniority. Paul says: the only praise that counts will come from God, and it will come at the appointed time, not before.

The implication for the factionalism is direct. The Corinthians' entire enterprise of evaluating and ranking teachers is doubly illegitimate: they lack the competence (only the Lord can judge) and they lack the timing (the judgment belongs to the future coming of the Lord). Their factionalism is premature, unauthorized judgment.

Verses 6-7: The Application Revealed — Not Beyond What Is Written

"Now these things, brothers and sisters, I have figuratively applied to myself and Apollos on your account, so that in us you may learn not to exceed what is written, so that no one of you will become arrogant in behalf of one against the other" (4:6).

This verse is a key disclosure. The verb metaschematizo ("to transfer the form of, to apply figuratively, to transform the outward appearance of") means that Paul has taken a principle and repackaged it in terms of himself and Apollos as concrete examples. The word schema ("form, outward appearance, figure") is what has been transferred. Paul has used himself and Apollos as case studies — not because the problem is primarily about Paul and Apollos personally, but because by using himself and Apollos as examples, he could address the Corinthians' broader pattern of competitive allegiance to human leaders without directly naming those who are causing the most damage. The di' hymas ("on your account, for your benefit") confirms the pedagogical purpose.

The phrase to me hyper ha gegraptai ("not beyond what is written") has generated extensive discussion, but within the context of Paul's argument its force is clear. Throughout chapters 1-3, Paul has repeatedly anchored his argument in Scripture: Isaiah 29:14 (quoted in 1:19), Jeremiah 9:24 (quoted in 1:31), Isaiah 64:4 (adapted in 2:9), Job 5:13 (quoted in 3:19), and Psalm 94:11 (quoted in 3:20). Every one of these scriptures taught the same lesson: God overturns human wisdom, catches the wise in their craftiness, knows that the thoughts of the wise are futile, and is the only legitimate object of boasting. "What is written" refers to these scriptural principles that Paul has been deploying throughout the argument. The command "not to go beyond" (me hyper) means: do not exceed the boundaries that Scripture itself has established for evaluating God's servants and distributing praise. Scripture says "let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1:31, citing Jeremiah 9:24); the Corinthians are boasting in human leaders, going beyond what is written.

The purpose clause hina me heis hyper tou henos physiosthe kata tou heterou ("so that no one of you will be puffed up in favor of one against the other") introduces the verb phusioo ("to inflate, to puff up, to make arrogant") for the first time in the letter. This word will become one of Paul's signature terms for the Corinthian disease. It appears in 4:6, 4:18, 4:19, 5:2, 8:1 ("knowledge puffs up, but love builds up"), and 13:4 ("love is not puffed up"). The image is of inflation — a balloon swollen with air rather than filled with substance. The Corinthians are inflated with self-importance about their preferred teacher, swollen with the honor they derive from their factional affiliation. The preposition hyper ("on behalf of, in favor of") with kata ("against") describes the partisan dynamic: puffed up for one teacher against another.

"For who considers you as superior? What do you have that you did not receive? And if you did receive it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?" (4:7).

Three rapid questions, each escalating in force.

The first question — tis gar se diakrinei ("for who distinguishes you?" or "who considers you as superior?") — challenges the basis of the Corinthians' self-differentiation. The verb diakrino can mean "to distinguish, to differentiate, to set apart, to make a difference." Paul asks: who gave you the right to consider yourselves superior on the basis of your factional allegiance? If everything comes from God, what basis exists for one group to elevate itself over another?

The second question — ti de echeis ho ouk elabes ("what do you have that you did not receive?") — is the devastating center of Paul's argument against boasting, and it connects directly to the thanksgiving of 1:4-7. There Paul affirmed that the Corinthians were "enriched in every way" and "not lacking in any gift." Everything they possess spiritually — their speech, their knowledge, their spiritual gifts, their very existence as a community — is a gift (charisma, from charizomai, "to give freely"). The verb lambano ("to receive") appeared in 2:12: "we have received not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God." The logic is airtight: if everything is received, nothing is earned, and therefore nothing provides grounds for boasting against another.

The third question — ei de kai elabes, ti kauchase hos me labon ("and if you did receive it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?") — exposes the contradiction at the heart of the Corinthian behavior. To boast about a gift as though it were an achievement is to deny its character as gift. The Corinthians are treating their spiritual endowments — including whatever benefits they gained from association with Paul or Apollos — as personal accomplishments that elevate them over other believers. Paul says this is incoherent. You cannot simultaneously acknowledge that something is a gift and boast about it as if you earned it. The act of boasting converts grace into merit and thereby falsifies the entire transaction.

This verse also closes a circle begun in 1:31. There Paul quoted Jeremiah 9:24: "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord." Now he identifies why the Corinthians cannot legitimately boast in anything except the Lord: everything they have was received. The exclusion of boasting is not an arbitrary command; it follows logically from the nature of grace.

Verses 8-13: The Ironic Contrast — The Corinthians' Self-Satisfaction and the Apostles' Suffering

"You are already filled, you have already become rich, you have become kings without us; and indeed, I wish that you had become kings so that we also might reign with you!" (4:8).

The transition is abrupt and the tone shifts to biting irony. Paul employs three words that describe the Corinthians' self-assessment, each in the aorist tense, indicating their presumed completed achievement.

First: ede kekoresmenoi este ("you are already filled, already satiated"). The verb korennymi ("to satiate, to fill to satisfaction, to gorge") describes the condition of someone who has eaten to the point of complete satisfaction. In the LXX, the word appears in contexts of fullness and abundance: Deuteronomy 31:20, "when they have eaten and are satisfied and grown fat"; Psalm 17:14 (LXX 16:14), where the wicked are "satisfied with children." The Corinthians consider themselves spiritually satiated — they need nothing more.

Second: ede eploutesate ("you have already become rich"). The verb plouteo ("to become rich, to be wealthy") echoes the language of 1:5, where Paul said they were "enriched" (eploutisthete) in every way. But there Paul attributed the enrichment to God's grace; here the Corinthians have claimed the wealth as their own status marker.

Third: choris hemon ebasileusate ("without us you have become kings" or "you have reigned"). The verb basileuo ("to reign, to rule as king") is the most pointed term. The Corinthians consider themselves to have already entered into the fullness of the kingdom — they are already reigning. The phrase choris hemon ("without us, apart from us") carries multiple edges. It may mean "without waiting for us," suggesting they have raced ahead of the apostles into a state of spiritual completion; or "without needing us," suggesting they have outgrown their teachers; or both. The irony is devastating: the community considers itself to have arrived at the consummation while the apostles who brought them the gospel are still suffering.

The exclamation kai ophelon ge ebasileusate, hina kai hemeis hymin symbasileusomen ("and I wish that you had indeed become kings, so that we also might reign with you!") contains a wish contrary to fact. The particle ophelon expresses an unfulfilled wish. Paul wishes the Corinthians really had entered the kingdom's fullness — not because their self-assessment is correct, but because if it were true, the apostles would share in that reign. The implication is clear: the apostles are not reigning, and therefore neither are the Corinthians. The kingdom's fullness has not yet arrived. The Corinthians have confused their present spiritual gifts with the consummation. They are living as though the "not yet" has already become "already" — as though the resurrection glory is a present possession rather than a future hope. Paul will address this confusion at length in chapter 15, where he will argue for the bodily resurrection that has not yet occurred. The Corinthians' realized eschatology — their sense that they have already arrived at spiritual completion — is the theological root of many of their problems.

"For I think, God has exhibited us, the apostles, last of all as men condemned to death, because we have become a spectacle to the world, both to angels and to mankind" (4:9).

The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the evidence that contradicts the Corinthians' self-assessment. If the kingdom had fully arrived, the apostles would not be suffering. Paul uses the verb apodeiknymi ("to display, to exhibit, to put on show") — the same root as apodeixis ("demonstration") in 2:4, where Paul's preaching was "in demonstration of the Spirit and of power." Now God's demonstration takes a different form: he displays the apostles as epithanatious ("condemned to death, sentenced to die, doomed to execution"). The word describes someone who has received a death sentence and is being paraded to execution.

The image is drawn from the Roman triumphal procession (pompa triumphalis), in which conquered enemies and condemned prisoners were displayed publicly as they were led to their deaths in the arena. The general rode in glory at the front; the captives stumbled in chains at the rear. Paul says God has placed the apostles "last of all" (eschatous) — at the very end of the procession, in the position of maximum humiliation. The apostles are not the triumphant generals; they are the condemned captives.

The word theatron ("spectacle, theater") confirms the public-display imagery. The apostles have been made a theatrical exhibition — put on stage for the cosmos to watch. The audience is comprehensive: to kosmo kai angelois kai anthropois ("to the world, both to angels and to people"). The scope is universal. The apostles' suffering is visible to every order of being — heavenly and earthly, angelic and human. The world watches the spectacle of men who are condemned, suffering, and dying — and this, Paul says, is how God displays his apostles. The contrast with the Corinthians' self-image as reigning kings could not be sharper.

"We are fools on account of Christ, but you are prudent in Christ! We are weak, but you are strong! You are distinguished, but we are without honor!" (4:10).

Paul constructs three antitheses, each placing the apostles and the Corinthians on opposite sides of the categories that have dominated the argument since chapter 1.

First: hemeis moroi dia Christon, hymeis de phronimoi en Christo — "we are fools for Christ's sake, but you are prudent/wise in Christ." The word moroi ("fools") is the adjective form of moria ("foolishness"), the word Paul used in 1:18 ("the word of the cross is foolishness"), 1:21 ("the foolishness of the proclamation"), 1:23 ("foolishness to Gentiles"), and 1:25 ("the foolishness of God"). The apostles embody the very foolishness that Paul has been arguing is God's chosen instrument. The word phronimoi ("prudent, wise, sensible") is used with a sting — the Corinthians consider themselves sophisticated and discerning, "wise in Christ," while their teachers are treated as fools.

Second: hemeis astheneis, hymeis de ischyroi — "we are weak, but you are strong." The word astheneis ("weak") echoes 1:25 ("the weakness of God is stronger than men"), 1:27 ("God chose what is weak to shame the strong"), and 2:3 ("I was with you in weakness"). The apostles embody the weakness that Paul argued is God's instrument of power. The Corinthians consider themselves ischyroi ("strong, powerful") — robust, secure, lacking nothing.

Third: hymeis endoxoi, hemeis de atimoi — "you are distinguished/honored, but we are without honor/dishonored." The word endoxos ("glorious, distinguished, held in esteem") and atimos ("without honor, despised, dishonored") describe social status. The Corinthians have achieved social respectability in their own eyes; the apostles have none.

The irony cuts in multiple directions. On one level, Paul is simply exposing the absurdity of the Corinthians' position: they claim to be wise, strong, and honored while the apostles who brought them the gospel are fools, weak, and dishonored. On another level, Paul is deploying the categories of chapters 1-2 to show which group more closely resembles the pattern of the cross. The cross works through foolishness, weakness, and dishonor (1:23-25, 27-28). The apostles — fools, weak, dishonored — conform to the cross's pattern. The Corinthians — wise, strong, honored — conform to the world's pattern. The question is left implicit but devastating: whose condition reflects the gospel, and whose contradicts it?

"Up to this present hour we are both hungry and thirsty, and are poorly clothed and roughly treated and homeless; and we labor, working with our own hands" (4:11-12a).

Paul now moves from irony to plain description. The present tense (achri tes arti horas, "up to the present hour") indicates that this is not past history but current reality. The apostles' condition is described in concrete, physical terms: peinomen ("we hunger"), dipsomen ("we thirst"), gymniteumen ("we are poorly clothed, we are exposed"), kolaphizometha ("we are struck, beaten, roughly treated" — the verb kolaphizo means literally "to strike with the fist"), astatoumen ("we are homeless, without a settled dwelling").

The phrase ergazomenoi tais idiais chersin ("working with our own hands") refers to Paul's practice of manual labor — his tentmaking, described in Acts 18:3. In Greco-Roman culture, manual labor was generally despised by the educated classes. Philosophers and rhetoricians were supported by patrons; working with one's hands was the mark of low social status. For a teacher to support himself by manual labor was, in the eyes of Corinthian society, evidence of inferiority. The Corinthians may have been embarrassed by Paul's self-support, preferring the model of Apollos or other teachers who may have accepted patronage. Paul's insistence on manual labor is part of the pattern of apostolic weakness that the Corinthians evaluate by the wrong criteria.

"When we are verbally abused, we bless; when we are persecuted, we endure it; when we are slandered, we reply as friends. We have become as the scum of the world, the dregs of all things, even until now" (4:12b-13).

Three responses to mistreatment, each following the pattern of returning good for evil: loidoroumenoi eulogoumen ("being reviled, we bless"), diokomenoi anechometha ("being persecuted, we endure"), dysphemoumenoi parakaloumen ("being slandered, we appeal/encourage/entreat"). This triad echoes the teaching attributed to Jesus: "Bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you" (Luke 6:28); "Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you" (Luke 6:27). Paul's response to suffering is not retaliation but blessing — the apostles embody the ethic of the cross in their daily lives.

The concluding description is the most extreme: hos perikatharma tou kosmou egenetheemen, panton peripsema heos arti — "we have become as the scum of the world, the dregs of all things, until now." The word perikatharma ("that which is removed by cleansing, scum, filth, offscouring") has a specific background. In Greek usage, perikalharma could refer to a human scapegoat — a person of low social standing who was ritually expelled from a city during times of plague or disaster, theoretically absorbing the community's pollution. The related term peripsema ("what is scraped off, scrapings, refuse, offscouring") carries a similar sense of what is discarded after cleansing. In Tobit 5:19 (some manuscripts), the word appears in the sense of a ransom or substitute. Proverbs 21:18 (LXX) uses perikatharma for the wicked person who serves as a "ransom" for the righteous.

Paul applies both terms to the apostles: they are the refuse of the world, the scrapings of all things. The language of scapegoat and ritual pollution is not accidental. The apostles occupy the position of those cast out for the sake of others — they absorb contempt, suffer deprivation, and are treated as disposable. This is the condition of the people the Corinthians have been evaluating and ranking as though they were competing rhetoricians.

The contrast between 4:8 and 4:11-13 is the heart of Paul's argument in this section. The Corinthians are satiated, rich, and reigning. The apostles are hungry, homeless, and treated as garbage. If the gospel operates through weakness and apparent foolishness (as Paul argued in chapters 1-2), which group's condition reflects the gospel's pattern? The question answers itself. The Corinthians' comfortable self-assessment is not evidence of spiritual maturity; it is evidence that they have adopted the world's values, the very wisdom "of this age" that Paul has been dismantling since 1:18.

Verses 14-17: The Father's Appeal and the Sending of Timothy

"I do not write these things to shame you, but to admonish you as my beloved children" (4:14).

The shift in tone is abrupt. Paul moves from devastating irony to tender affection. The verb entrepo ("to shame, to put to shame, to cause to turn inward in embarrassment") describes what Paul says he is not doing. The verb noutheteo ("to admonish, to warn, to instruct, to put into one's mind") describes what he is doing. The distinction matters: shaming aims to humiliate and diminish; admonishment aims to correct and redirect. Paul's purpose is not to crush the Corinthians but to redirect them. The phrase hos tekna mou agapeta ("as my beloved children") introduces a new relational category. Throughout chapters 1-3, Paul has been arguing about apostles and the community's relationship to them. Now he specifies the nature of his own relationship: he is their father.

"For if you were to have countless tutors in Christ, yet you would not have many fathers, for in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel" (4:15).

The word paidagogous ("tutors, guardians, child-attendants") is specific. A paidagogos in the first-century Greco-Roman world was not a teacher but a household slave who supervised a child's daily life — walking the child to school, overseeing behavior, providing discipline and protection. The paidagogos was a temporary figure in a child's development; the father was permanent. Paul uses the same word in Galatians 3:24-25, where the Torah served as a paidagogos "until Christ came." The paidagogos is useful but temporary, secondary to the parent who generated the child.

Paul's claim is that whatever number of subsequent instructors the Corinthians may have (myrioi, "ten thousand, countless" — the word is hyperbolic), they have only one father. The phrase ego hymas egennesa ("I fathered you, I begot you") uses the verb gennao ("to beget, to give birth to, to generate"). The means of this generation is specified: en Christo Iesou dia tou euangeliou ("in Christ Jesus through the gospel"). Paul did not beget them in his own name or through his own wisdom; he begot them in Christ, through the instrumentality of the gospel message. The paternity is real but derivative — Paul is the human agent; the gospel is the instrument; Christ is the sphere.

This claim carries weight for the factionalism argument. The Corinthians have been treating their teachers as interchangeable competitors — Paul, Apollos, and Cephas as rival brands. Paul says the relationship is not interchangeable. Other teachers are paidagogoi — useful, perhaps numerous, but not constitutive. Paul is the father — the one through whom their communal existence originated. This does not make Paul superior to Apollos in general; it makes his relationship to this particular community unique and non-transferable. The argument is not about hierarchical ranking of apostles but about the specific, unrepeatable relationship of originator to community.

"Therefore I urge you, be imitators of me" (4:16).

The word mimetai ("imitators") from mimeomai ("to imitate, to follow the pattern of") calls the Corinthians to conform their lives to Paul's pattern. Given the preceding context (4:9-13), what Paul is asking them to imitate is clear: the apostolic pattern of weakness, suffering, self-denial, and blessing in the face of abuse. He is not asking them to imitate his personal style or rhetorical approach (he has just spent three chapters arguing that rhetorical style is irrelevant). He is asking them to imitate the pattern of the cross as it is lived out in apostolic existence — the willingness to be fools, weak, dishonored, hungry, and homeless for the sake of Christ. This is the antidote to the Corinthians' self-satisfaction: not more knowledge, not better teacher-selection, but the adoption of a cruciform way of life.

Paul will make this even more explicit in 11:1: "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ." The chain of imitation runs from Christ through Paul to the community. Paul imitates Christ's pattern of self-giving; the Corinthians are to imitate Paul's enactment of that same pattern.

"For this reason I have sent to you Timothy, who is my beloved and faithful child in the Lord, and he will remind you of my ways which are in Christ, just as I teach everywhere in every church" (4:17).

Paul has sent Timothy as his representative. Timothy is described as mou teknon agapeton kai piston en kyrio ("my beloved and faithful child in the Lord") — the same parental language Paul just used for the Corinthians (tekna mou agapeta in v. 14). Timothy is Paul's child in the same sense that the Corinthians are — through the gospel. But Timothy is also pistos ("faithful") — the very quality that Paul identified as the requirement for stewards in verse 2. Timothy is a faithful child; the question is whether the Corinthians are.

Timothy's assignment is to hypomnese ("remind" — from hypomimnesko, "to remind, to call to mind, to bring to remembrance") the Corinthians of tas hodous mou tas en Christo ("my ways which are in Christ"). The word hodoi ("ways, paths, manner of life") describes Paul's pattern of conduct — his practical way of living the gospel. The same word appears in Acts 9:2, where the early movement is called "the Way" (he hodos). The phrase kathos pantachou en pase ekklesia didasko ("just as I teach everywhere in every church") indicates that Paul's ways are not peculiar to his relationship with Corinth; they are universal. What he teaches the Corinthians is what he teaches everywhere. The Corinthians are not a special case requiring unique treatment; they are a community that has deviated from the universal standard.

Verses 18-21: Warning to the Arrogant — Words versus Power

"Now some have become arrogant, as though I were not coming to you" (4:18).

The verb phusioo ("to puff up, to inflate") reappears — ephysiothesan tines ("some have been puffed up"). The "some" (tines) are unnamed individuals within the community who have become inflated with self-importance, presumably the leaders or instigators of the factions. The clause hos me erchomenou mou pros hymas ("as though I were not coming to you") reveals their assumption: they believe Paul will not come in person and that they can therefore behave with impunity. The sending of Timothy (v. 17) may have emboldened this assumption — they may have concluded that Timothy was Paul's substitute, not his advance guard. Paul's words are a warning: Timothy's arrival does not mean Paul's absence is permanent.

"But I will come to you soon, if the Lord wills, and I shall find out, not the words of those who are arrogant, but their power" (4:19).

Paul asserts his intention to come (eleusomai de tacheoos pros hymas, "I will come to you soon"), qualified by ean ho kyrios thelese ("if the Lord wills") — the same Lord who is the only competent judge (v. 4) and who will come at the appointed time (v. 5). Paul does not claim autonomous control over his movements; the Lord determines the timing.

When Paul arrives, he will gnosomai ("know, find out, learn") not ton logon ton pephysiomenon ("the word/talk of those who are puffed up") but ten dynamin ("the power"). The contrast between logos ("word, speech, talk") and dynamis ("power") reaches back to the foundations of the entire argument. In 1:17, the cross was emptied by sophia logou ("wisdom of speech"); in 1:18, the word of the cross is dynamis theou ("the power of God"); in 2:4, Paul's proclamation was "not in persuasive words of wisdom but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power (dynamis)"; in 2:5, faith should rest on dynamis theou, not sophia anthropon. Throughout, Paul has distinguished between impressive speech (which the Corinthians value) and actual power (which God provides through the cross and Spirit).

The arrogant Corinthians may have impressive logoi — eloquent speech, sophisticated arguments, displays of knowledge. Paul says he will ignore all of that and test their dynamis — the actual power of God at work in their lives. If the kingdom operates through power, not words, then the test of any person's standing is not what they say but what God is doing through them.

"For the kingdom of God is not in words, but in power" (4:20).

This compressed statement — ou gar en logo he basileia tou theou all' en dynamei — is the principle that underlies Paul's entire evaluation. The phrase basileia tou theou ("kingdom of God") appears here for the first time in 1 Corinthians (it will appear again in 6:9-10, 15:24, and 15:50). The kingdom of God — God's reign, God's sovereign activity, the sphere where God's will is operative — does not consist in logos ("speech, words, rhetorical performance") but in dynamis ("power, effective force, operative energy"). The Corinthians have been evaluating everything — their teachers, their spiritual status, their factional allegiances — by words. Paul says the kingdom is elsewhere. It is in the power that transforms lives, that sustains apostles through suffering, that forms communities out of nobodies, that works through the crucified Messiah. Words without power are empty performance. The arrogant Corinthians may have the words; Paul will come to test whether they have the power.

"What do you desire? That I come to you with a rod, or with love and a spirit of gentleness?" (4:21).

The closing question offers two alternatives. The word rhabdos ("rod, staff") is an instrument of discipline. In the LXX, the rod appears as a tool of correction: Proverbs 13:24, "He who spares his rod hates his son, but he who loves him is diligent to discipline him"; Proverbs 22:15, "Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child; the rod of discipline will remove it far from him"; Proverbs 23:13-14, "Do not withhold discipline from a child; if you strike him with the rod, he will not die." The rod is a father's instrument. Paul has just identified himself as the Corinthians' father (v. 15). The question is therefore parental, not tyrannical: a father may come with correction or with tenderness, depending on the child's behavior.

The alternative is en agape pneumati te praytetos ("with love and a spirit of gentleness"). The word praiotes ("gentleness, meekness, mildness") describes a disposition of restraint and kindness. Paul's preference is clearly the second option. But the choice belongs to the Corinthians: their behavior between now and his arrival will determine which mode of fatherly presence they experience.

The question also transitions to chapter 5. Paul will, in fact, exercise the rod — not against the Corinthians as a whole, but against the specific case of the man living with his father's wife. The father who offered gentleness will be compelled by the severity of the case to act with authority. Chapter 5 thus answers the question of 4:21: the Corinthians' failure to deal with the immoral man forces Paul to come, in effect, with the rod.

Summary of the Argument's Movement

Chapter 4 concludes the four-chapter unit (1 Corinthians 1-4) that addresses the Corinthian factionalism, and it does so by moving through four stages:

First (vv. 1-5): Paul establishes how apostles should be evaluated — not by rhetorical skill or social prestige but solely by faithfulness to their role as servants and stewards. He then removes the evaluation from human hands entirely. No human court, not even the apostle's own conscience, is competent to render a verdict. Only the Lord, at his coming, will judge with the necessary knowledge of hidden motives. The Corinthians' premature ranking of teachers is therefore doubly illegitimate: wrong criteria and wrong timing.

Second (vv. 6-7): Paul reveals that he has been using himself and Apollos as pedagogical illustrations, and commands the Corinthians not to go beyond the scriptural principles he has cited throughout the argument. The devastating question of verse 7 — "What do you have that you did not receive?" — eliminates all grounds for competitive boasting. Everything is gift. Boasting about gifts as though they were achievements contradicts the nature of grace itself.

Third (vv. 8-13): Paul deploys sustained irony to expose the gap between the Corinthians' self-image and the apostles' actual condition. The Corinthians are satiated, rich, and reigning; the apostles are hungry, homeless, and treated as refuse. The irony is not mere mockery; it forces the question of which condition reflects the pattern of the cross. If God's power operates through weakness, foolishness, and dishonor (as Paul argued in chapters 1-2), then the apostles' suffering confirms the gospel's pattern while the Corinthians' comfort-seeking confirms the world's pattern. The Corinthians have adopted a realized eschatology — acting as though the kingdom has already fully arrived — while the apostles live in the reality of the "not yet," bearing in their bodies the marks of the present age's hostility to the gospel.

Fourth (vv. 14-21): Paul shifts from irony to paternal appeal, claiming a unique relationship to the community as their father through the gospel. He sends Timothy to remind them of his ways and warns the arrogant that he is coming personally. The final contrast — "the kingdom of God is not in words but in power" — recapitulates the entire argument of chapters 1-4 in a single sentence. The Corinthians have been operating in the realm of words — evaluating by eloquence, boasting in human leaders, inflated with self-assessed wisdom. The kingdom operates in the realm of power — the power of the cross, the power of the Spirit, the power that sustains apostles through suffering and forms communities out of the world's refuse. Paul's closing question — rod or gentleness? — places the responsibility on the Corinthians and transitions to the disciplinary action of chapter 5.

The word phusioo ("puffed up") serves as the chapter's diagnostic thread. It appears three times (vv. 6, 18, 19) and will reappear in 5:2. This inflation — self-importance derived from factional allegiance, spiritual gifts, and worldly wisdom — is the disease that produces both the factionalism of chapters 1-4 and the moral failure of chapter 5. The cure Paul prescribes is the opposite of inflation: the pattern of the cross, embodied in apostolic suffering, which deflates all human pretension and grounds the community's life in the power of God rather than the words of human beings.
