Mark 4 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Mark 3): After the death conspiracy in 3:1-6 concluded the five-part confrontation sequence begun in chapter 2, the author presented Jesus withdrawing to the sea with enormous crowds from every direction — Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, beyond the Jordan, Tyre and Sidon (3:7-8). The scope of response has expanded dramatically beyond Capernaum. Jesus appointed twelve to be "with him" and to be sent out with authority to proclaim and cast out demons (3:14-15). The chapter then pivoted to two scenes that together define the crisis the author is building toward. First, Jesus's own family attempted to seize him, believing he had lost his mind (3:21). Second, scribes from Jerusalem accused him of casting out demons by the power of Beelzebul, the ruler of demons (3:22). Jesus responded to the scribes with the argument that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand and warned that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit — attributing the Spirit's work to an unclean spirit — constitutes an "eternal sin" with no forgiveness (3:28-30). The chapter ended with Jesus redefining family: when told his mother and brothers are outside asking for him, he looked at those sitting around him and declared, "Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, this person is my brother, and sister, and mother" (3:34-35). The author has now drawn a sharp line between two groups: those sitting around Jesus who do God's will ("insiders"), and those standing outside — including both the Jerusalem scribes who attribute his work to Satan and his own biological family who think him deranged.

What the author argues immediately after (Mark 5): The crossing of the sea that concludes chapter 4 delivers Jesus and his disciples to "the other side," the country of the Gerasenes (5:1). There Jesus encounters a man possessed by a legion of unclean spirits — the most extreme case of demonic oppression in the document. After the exorcism, the Gerasene townspeople beg Jesus to leave their region, while the healed man wants to follow Jesus. Jesus refuses and instead sends him to tell "how much the Lord has done for you" (5:19). The man goes and proclaims "how much Jesus had done for him" (5:20) — the author equates what the Lord has done with what Jesus has done. Chapter 5 continues with Jairus's daughter and the woman with the hemorrhage, further demonstrating Jesus's authority over death and disease. The pattern of chapter 5 is the enactment, on the far side of the sea, of everything chapter 4 taught about: the word going out, encountering various soils, and producing results in receptive hearers.

How chapter 4 connects them: Mark 3 ended with a crisis of reception. The religious authorities have rejected Jesus so thoroughly that they attribute his Spirit-empowered work to Satan. His own family stands outside, unable to comprehend him. Meanwhile, a circle of those "sitting around him" receive his teaching as insiders. Chapter 4 takes this division — insiders who hear and outsiders who do not — and makes it the explicit subject of Jesus's teaching. The parable that opens the chapter is about the varied responses that occur when the word goes out. The explanation of why Jesus teaches in parables addresses directly the insider/outsider divide established in 3:31-35. The chapter then closes with a demonstration at sea: the disciples, who are the insiders given the mystery of the kingdom, are tested and found lacking in faith, asking "Who then is this?" The author is showing that being on the inside does not automatically mean understanding. Chapter 4 is the author's sustained meditation on the word, its reception, its concealment, its power, and the question of who truly hears — a question that will be tested in the actions of chapter 5.

Verses 1-2: The Setting by the Sea

"He began to teach again by the sea. And such a very large crowd gathered to Him that He got into a boat on the sea and sat down; and the whole crowd was by the sea on the land. And He was teaching them many things in parables, and was saying to them in His teaching..."

The author returns Jesus to the sea (thalassa), the same location as 2:13 and 3:7. This is the third time the author has placed Jesus teaching by the sea. The crowd is so large that Jesus must get into a boat and teach from the water. The spatial arrangement is important: Jesus is in the boat on the sea; the crowd is on the land (epi tes ges). This physical separation — Jesus on the water, the crowd on the shore — creates a visual image of the distinction the chapter will draw between those who are near and those who are at a distance.

The author introduces the method: "He was teaching them many things in parables" (en parabolais). This is the first sustained parabolic discourse in the document. The word parabole in the LXX renders the Hebrew mashal, which covers a range of forms: proverbs, riddles, allegories, comparative stories. In Ezekiel, God frequently instructs the prophet to "set forth a parable" (parabolen) to Israel (Ezekiel 17:2; 24:3). The mashal in Israel's tradition is not merely an illustration to make a point clearer; it is a form that can conceal as much as it reveals, that requires perception to penetrate, and that functions as a test of the hearer's receptivity. Ezekiel 20:49 records the people's complaint: "He is just telling parables" (parabolai) — the people heard but did not understand. The author's use of this method is deliberate and connects to the explanation Jesus will give in verses 10-12.

Verses 3-9: The Parable of the Sower, the Seed, and the Soils

"Listen! Behold, the sower went out to sow; and as he was sowing, some seed fell beside the road, and the birds came and ate it up. Other seed fell on the rocky ground where it did not have much soil; and immediately it sprang up because it had no depth of soil. And when the sun had risen, it was scorched; and because it had no root, it withered away. Other seed fell among the thorns, and the thorns came up and choked it, and it yielded no crop. Other seeds fell into the good soil, and as they grew up and increased, they yielded a crop and produced thirty, sixty, and a hundredfold."

"And He was saying, 'He who has ears to hear, let him hear.'"

The opening command — "Listen!" (akouete) — is emphatic. The parable begins and ends with hearing: "Listen" at the start and "he who has ears to hear, let him hear" at the close. The verb akoun (to hear) will dominate this entire chapter. Hearing is not presented as a passive reception but as an activity that some perform and others do not. The framing commands signal that what follows is a test of the listener, not merely a story.

The parable itself describes a single sower performing a single action — sowing seed — with four different outcomes determined entirely by where the seed lands. The sower's action is uniform; the variable is the soil. The structure is three failures and one success:

1. Seed on the road (para ten hodon) — birds eat it immediately. It never enters the soil at all. 2. Seed on rocky ground (to petrdes) — it springs up quickly because the soil is shallow, but the sun scorches it and it withers because it has no root. 3. Seed among thorns (eis tas akanthas) — it grows but is choked by thorns and produces no crop (karpon ouk edken). 4. Seed in good soil (eis ten gen ten kalen) — it grows, increases, and produces a crop: thirty, sixty, a hundredfold.

Several details require attention within the first-century agricultural context. In Galilean farming practice, sowing preceded plowing. The sower broadcast seed across an unplowed field, and the field was then plowed to work the seed into the soil. This explains why seed falls on paths, rocky ground, and thorny patches — these conditions are not yet visible or have not yet been addressed. The sower is not being careless; he is following standard practice. The parable's focus is not on the sower's technique but on the response of the ground.

The yields described — thirty, sixty, a hundredfold — are extraordinary. Typical yields in ancient Palestine were estimated at seven to tenfold. A thirtyfold return would be remarkable; a hundredfold would be astonishing. Genesis 26:12 records that Isaac "sowed in that land and reaped in the same year a hundredfold, and the LORD blessed him." The exceptional yield is attributed to divine blessing. The parable's conclusion implies that where the word finds receptive ground, the result is not merely adequate but superabundant — a harvest that exceeds normal expectation and signals God's action.

The closing formula — "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (hos echei ta akouein akouet) — separates the audience. It implies that not everyone who physically hears the parable will actually hear it. The statement presupposes that hearing in the true sense is not automatic. This is the pivot point of the chapter. The parable has been told publicly. The question is who will hear it and who will not.

Verses 10-12: The Purpose of Parabolic Teaching

"As soon as He was alone, His followers, along with the twelve, began asking Him about the parables. And He was saying to them, 'To you has been given the mystery of the kingdom of God, but to those who are outside, everything comes in parables, so that while seeing, they may see and not perceive, and while hearing, they may hear and not understand, otherwise they might return and be forgiven.'"

This passage is among the most important in the document for understanding the author's argument about the reception of Jesus's teaching. It must be read as a direct continuation of the insider/outsider distinction established in 3:31-35.

The setting: Jesus is now "alone" (kata monas) with "His followers, along with the twelve" (hoi peri auton sun tois ddeka). The phrase "those around him" (hoi peri auton) recalls 3:34, where Jesus looked at "those sitting around him in a circle" and identified them as his true family. These are the same people. The twelve are named as a subset of a larger group of followers. The spatial arrangement mirrors the relational one: those who have moved close to Jesus receive further explanation; those who remain at a distance do not.

"The mystery of the kingdom of God" (to mysterion tes basileias tou theou). The word mysterion requires careful first-century contextualization. In the LXX of Daniel, mysterion translates the Aramaic raz and refers to a divine secret — God's plan hidden from human perception until God chooses to reveal it. Daniel 2:28-29: "There is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries (mysteria), and He has made known to King Nebuchadnezzar what will take place in the latter days." The mystery is not esoteric knowledge for an elite; it is God's operative plan that has been hidden and is now being disclosed. In the context of Mark, the "mystery of the kingdom of God" is the reality that the kingdom Jesus announced in 1:15 ("The kingdom of God has come near") is arriving in the very events the reader has been witnessing: healings, exorcisms, the calling of sinners, the confrontation with religious authorities, the redefinition of who belongs to God's family. The mystery is that the kingdom of God is present and active in Jesus's own person and activity — not in the form expected (military deliverance, political restoration), but through a teacher from Nazareth who eats with tax collectors, redefines the Sabbath, and will be "taken away" (2:20).

The verb "has been given" (dedotai) is a divine passive — the subject performing the giving is God. God has given the mystery to those around Jesus. This is not a reward for their merit; it is a grant. They receive the mystery because they are positioned around Jesus — they have come close, they have stayed, they are asking.

"But to those who are outside" (ekeinois de tois ex). The word "outside" (ex) connects directly to 3:31-32, where Jesus's mother and brothers stood "outside" (ex) while the crowd sat around him inside. The author is using the same spatial language to describe the same relational reality. Those "outside" are not simply people who happened not to hear the parable; they are people who have positioned themselves at a distance from Jesus — the scribes who attribute his power to Beelzebul (3:22), the family who think he is out of his mind (3:21), and by extension anyone who refuses to enter the circle around Jesus.

"So that" (hina). The conjunction hina followed by a subjunctive verb normally expresses purpose in Koine Greek: "in order that." This is the most natural reading. The author presents the parables as serving a purpose that includes concealment from those outside. This is difficult for modern readers but fits the scriptural precedent Jesus is citing.

The Isaiah citation. Jesus quotes Isaiah 6:9-10, the passage in which God commissions Isaiah and tells him that his prophetic ministry will result in the people hearing but not understanding, seeing but not perceiving, so that they will not turn and be healed. The original context is crucial: Isaiah 6 comes after five chapters of oracles against Israel's sin, and the commission is given in the same vision where Isaiah sees the Lord on his throne and receives the cleansing coal. God is not expressing a desire that Israel remain blind; he is declaring the result that Isaiah's ministry will produce given the condition of the people. The people have already hardened themselves — through the injustice, idolatry, and covenant violation described in Isaiah 1-5 — and Isaiah's preaching will confirm rather than reverse that hardening. The hardening is not arbitrary; it is the consequence of prior rebellion.

The author of Mark applies this same logic to Jesus's situation. The scribes from Jerusalem have already rejected Jesus — not from ignorance but from a settled opposition so deep that they attribute the Holy Spirit's work to Satan (3:22-30). Jesus's parables function like Isaiah's preaching: they reveal truth to those who are receptive and confirm the blindness of those who have already closed themselves off. The concealment is not the cause of their rejection; their rejection is the reason the concealment applies to them.

The final clause — "otherwise they might return and be forgiven" (mepote epistrepssin kai aphethe autois) — echoes the Isaiah text and raises a sharp question. In Isaiah's context, the hardening has a limit: Isaiah asks "How long, Lord?" and receives the answer that it will continue "until cities are devastated" (Isaiah 6:11) — but 6:13 concludes with "the holy seed is its stump," a remnant that survives. The hardening is severe but not final; it serves a larger purpose within God's plan. The author of Mark does not quote Isaiah 6:13 here, but the attentive first-century reader who knows the full passage would recognize that the hardening in Isaiah was not God's last word on Israel. Within Mark's narrative, even the disciples — who are insiders — will repeatedly fail to understand (4:13, 4:40, 6:52, 8:17-21), suggesting that the line between hearing and not hearing is not fixed but fluid.

Verses 13-20: The Explanation of the Seed and the Soils

"And He said to them, 'Do you not understand this parable? How will you understand all the parables?'"

Before explaining the parable, Jesus addresses the disciples with a question that carries a note of rebuke. They are the insiders who have been given the mystery, yet they do not understand. The question "How will you understand all the parables?" implies that this particular parable is foundational — it is the key to the rest. If they cannot grasp this one, the others will remain opaque. The author is signaling that even the insiders are not exempt from the struggle to hear. Being given the mystery does not mean automatic comprehension.

"'The sower sows the word. These are the ones who are beside the road where the word is sown; and when they hear, immediately Satan comes and takes away the word which has been sown in them.'"

The identification is explicit: the seed is "the word" (ton logon). This is the same term the author has used consistently. In 1:45, the healed leper spread "the word." In 2:2, Jesus was "speaking the word" in the crowded house at Capernaum. The sowing of the seed is the proclamation of the word — the announcement of the kingdom of God that Jesus began in 1:14-15. The parable is not about agricultural technique; it is about the range of responses that occur when the kingdom is proclaimed.

The first soil — the road: the word is sown, but Satan (ho Satanas) immediately removes it. The word never penetrates at all. "Satan" has appeared in the document only twice before: in 1:13, where Jesus was tested by Satan in the wilderness, and in 3:23-26, where Jesus argued that Satan's kingdom cannot be divided against itself. The birds of the parable are identified with Satan's activity. The author presents opposition to the word as ultimately originating from a personal adversary, not merely from human inattention.

"'And in a similar way, these are the ones on whom seed was sown on the rocky places, who, when they hear the word, immediately receive it with joy; and they have no firm root in themselves, but are only temporary; then, when affliction or persecution arises because of the word, they immediately fall away.'"

The second soil — the rocky ground: these hear and receive the word "with joy" (meta charas) and respond "immediately" (euthys). The initial response is enthusiastic and rapid. But they have "no root in themselves" (ouk echousin rhizan en heautois) and are "temporary" (proskairoi — literally "for a season"). The word proskairos indicates something that lasts only for a limited time. When "affliction" (thlipsis) or "persecution" (digmos) comes "because of the word" (dia ton logon), they "fall away" (skandalizontai — literally "are scandalized," "are caused to stumble"). The verb skandalizn carries the force of being tripped up, of encountering an obstacle that causes a fall.

Two elements are critical. First, the initial reception is genuine — they hear, they receive, they rejoice. The author does not present the rocky-ground hearers as insincere. Their problem is not the quality of their initial response but the absence of depth and root. Second, the cause of falling away is specified: it is affliction or persecution that comes "because of the word." The word itself generates opposition, and it is this opposition that tests whether the hearer has root. Receiving the word without developing root — without the deep internal formation that sustains the hearer through opposition — leads to apostasy. The author describes a real reception that does not endure.

"'And others are the ones on whom seed was sown among the thorns; these are the ones who have heard the word, but the worries of the world, and the deceitfulness of wealth, and the desires for other things enter in and choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful.'"

The third soil — the thorns: these also hear the word, but three categories of distraction render it unfruitful (akarpon). "The worries of the age" (hai merimnai tou ainos) — the anxieties generated by life in the present age. "The deceitfulness of wealth" (he apate tou ploutou) — riches that promise security but choke the word's growth. "The desires for other things" (hai peri ta loipa epithymiai) — a catch-all category for any craving that competes with the word. The word grows alongside these competitors but is gradually overpowered. The result is not sudden collapse (as with the rocky ground) but slow suffocation. The word is present but produces nothing.

The verb "choke" (sympnigousin) is vivid. The thorns do not destroy the seed; they surround and strangle the plant that grows from it. The word is not lost through dramatic failure but through gradual displacement by competing concerns. The author presents this as a distinct category of failed hearing — distinct from outright rejection (the road) and from enthusiastic-but-rootless reception (the rocky ground). This is the long, slow death of the word in a hearer who never formally rejects it but never allows it to produce fruit.

"'And those are the ones on whom seed was sown on the good soil; and they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirty, sixty, and a hundredfold.'"

The fourth soil — good ground: these hear, accept (paradechontai — receive, welcome), and bear fruit. The verb paradechomai implies active, willing reception — not merely hearing but taking the word in. The result is fruit, and the fruit is abundant: thirty, sixty, a hundredfold. The author places no upper limit on the yield. The varying amounts (thirty, sixty, a hundred) suggest that even among the good-soil hearers, the degree of fruitfulness varies, but all produce.

The overall structure of the explanation reveals a pattern: in each of the four soils, the word is sown — the same word, from the same sower. The variable is entirely in the reception. Three of the four responses fail to produce lasting fruit. Only one does. The author is presenting the kingdom's proclamation as a venture with a high rate of apparent failure but with a harvest so abundant that it justifies the sowing. The parable addresses the question that the author's narrative has already raised: if Jesus is the one whose way was prepared (1:2-3), who was endorsed by the heavenly voice (1:11), who teaches with unique authority (1:22, 27), who forgives sins (2:5, 10), who is Lord of the Sabbath (2:28) — then why is he rejected by the scribes, accused of working for Satan, and misunderstood even by his own family? The parable answers: because this is how the word works. It goes out broadly, and its reception depends on the condition of the soil it encounters. Rejection does not disprove the word's authority; it confirms the pattern described in Isaiah 6.

Verses 21-25: The Lamp and the Measure

"And He was saying to them, 'A lamp is not brought to be put under a basket, is it, or under a bed? Is it not brought to be put on the lampstand? For nothing is hidden, except to be revealed; nor has anything been secret, but that it would come to light. If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear.'"

The author places these sayings in continuity with the parable explanation. The connecting phrase "and He was saying to them" (kai elegen autois) maintains the discourse. The lamp saying appears to address a tension the reader might feel from verses 10-12: if the parables are meant to conceal the mystery from outsiders, is the kingdom permanently hidden?

The answer is no. A lamp exists to give light, not to be concealed under a basket (modion — a measuring container of about one peck) or under a bed. Its purpose is the lampstand. The concealment described in verses 11-12 is temporary and purposeful, not permanent. The mystery is hidden now in order to be revealed later. The word "except" (ei me) is critical: "nothing is hidden except to be revealed" — the hiding serves the revealing. The secret exists for the purpose of being brought to light.

This corresponds to the structure of Daniel's mysteries, where what God conceals is concealed precisely so that its revelation will be all the more striking when it comes (Daniel 2:22: "He reveals deep and hidden things; He knows what is in the darkness, and the light dwells with Him"). The author is assuring the insiders that the current obscurity of the kingdom — its presence in parables, its operation through a Galilean teacher rather than through military triumph — is not its final form. What is hidden will be disclosed.

The refrain "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear" recurs (cf. v. 9), reinforcing the division between those who hear and those who do not.

"And He was saying to them, 'Take care what you listen to. By your standard of measure it will be measured to you; and more will be given you besides. For whoever has, to him more shall be given; and whoever does not have, even what he has shall be taken away from him.'"

"Take care what you listen to" (blepete ti akouete) — literally, "see what you hear." The command unites sight and hearing, both senses that were at issue in the Isaiah 6 quotation (seeing but not perceiving, hearing but not understanding). The warning is addressed to the insiders: receiving the mystery brings responsibility. How they listen determines what they receive.

"By your standard of measure it will be measured to you" — this saying uses commercial language. The "measure" (metron) is a measuring container used in the marketplace. The principle is reciprocal: the measure of attention and receptivity you bring to the word determines the measure of understanding you receive back. Those who bring much receive much; those who bring little receive little.

"For whoever has, to him more shall be given; and whoever does not have, even what he has shall be taken away from him." This saying sharpens the reciprocity into a principle of accumulation and loss. The one who already "has" (echei) — who has received the word, attended to it, allowed it to take root — will receive more. The one who does "not have" (ouk echei) — who has not truly received, who has heard without hearing — will lose even what he seemed to possess. The saying describes a dynamic process, not a static situation. Reception of the word generates more reception; failure to receive generates progressive loss.

This connects directly to the four soils. The good-soil hearer who produces thirty, sixty, a hundredfold is one to whom "more is given." The road-soil hearer, from whom Satan immediately takes the word, is one from whom "even what he has is taken away." The author presents hearing as a trajectory: it moves in one direction or the other, toward abundance or toward emptiness. There is no stable middle ground.

Verses 26-29: The Seed That Grows by Itself

"And He was saying, 'The kingdom of God is like a man who casts seed upon the soil; and he goes to bed at night and gets up by day, and the seed sprouts and grows — how, he himself does not know. The soil produces crops by itself; first the stalk, then the head, then the mature grain in the head. But when the crop permits, he immediately puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come.'"

This parable appears only in Mark; neither Matthew nor Luke includes it. The author places it between the sower parable and the mustard seed, forming a sequence of three seed parables. Each addresses a different aspect of the kingdom's arrival.

The parable's focus is on what happens between sowing and harvest — and specifically on the fact that the sower does nothing during this interval. He sleeps, he rises, and the seed grows "by itself" (automate — from which the English "automatic" derives). The farmer does not understand the process ("how, he himself does not know"). The growth occurs without his knowledge or effort. The earth "produces crops by itself" (automate he ge karpophorei). The agent of growth is the earth itself — or, more precisely, the process God has built into the creation.

The sequence — "first the stalk, then the head, then the mature grain in the head" (prton chorton, eita stachyn, eita pleres siton en t stachyi) — describes a gradual, ordered, and inevitable development. The kingdom of God, the author is arguing, operates by a process analogous to agricultural growth: once the seed is sown, the result unfolds according to its own internal logic, on its own timetable, without human engineering.

The conclusion — "when the crop permits, he immediately puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come" — introduces harvest language that carries heavy prophetic weight. Joel 3:13 (4:13 LXX) declares: "Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe. Come, tread, for the wine press is full." In Joel, the harvest is a metaphor for divine judgment. The author's allusion connects the kingdom's growth to an eventual culmination that involves judgment and gathering. The farmer waits, but when the time comes, he acts decisively. The verb "puts in" (apostellei — literally "sends forth") the sickle uses the same root as "apostle" (apostolos, one sent forth), though the connection here is to Joel's harvest imagery, not to the twelve.

The parable addresses a particular anxiety that the author's narrative has generated: if the kingdom has been announced (1:15), if the word is being sown, why is the visible result so modest? Where is the triumph? The parable answers: the kingdom grows on its own terms and timetable. The sower's job is to sow; the harvest will come when the crop is ready. Human inability to perceive the growth does not mean it is not happening.

Verses 30-32: The Mustard Seed

"And He said, 'How shall we picture the kingdom of God, or by what parable shall we present it? It is like a mustard seed, which, when sown upon the soil, though it is the smallest of all the seeds that are upon the soil, yet when it is sown, it grows up and becomes larger than all the garden plants, and forms large branches; so that the birds of the sky can nest under its shade.'"

The author introduces this parable with a deliberative question: "How shall we picture the kingdom of God?" The "we" (pothmen) is notable — Jesus invites the hearers into the process of comparison, as if the question of how to describe the kingdom is a shared project.

The mustard seed (kokk sinapes) was proverbially small in first-century Palestine. The author describes it as "the smallest of all the seeds upon the soil" — a comparison relative to seeds commonly sown in that agricultural context, not a botanical absolute. The mustard plant (sinapis nigra) could grow to eight or ten feet in the Jordan Valley, large enough to be called "larger than all the garden plants" (meizon pantn tn lachanon). The contrast between the seed's insignificance and the plant's size is the parable's engine.

The concluding image — "the birds of the sky can nest under its shade" (ta peteina tou ouranou kataskeinoun hypo ten skian autou) — carries significant scriptural resonance. In Ezekiel 17:22-24, God promises to take a sprig from the top of a cedar and plant it on a high mountain, where it will "put out branches and bear fruit and become a magnificent cedar. Birds of every kind will nest under it; they will nest in the shade of its branches." The context in Ezekiel is the restoration of the Davidic monarchy — God will plant a tree under which the nations will find shelter. Daniel 4:12 (4:9 LXX) uses nearly identical language for Nebuchadnezzar's kingdom: a tree "whose foliage was beautiful and its fruit abundant... under which the beasts of the field found shade, and in whose branches the birds of the sky nested." In both prophetic texts, a great tree with birds nesting in its branches is a kingdom that shelters the nations.

The author's choice of the mustard seed rather than the cedar is deliberate and subversive. Ezekiel's cedar is majestic, royal, and impressive from the start. A mustard seed is tiny, common, and associated with kitchen gardens. The kingdom of God, the author argues, arrives not as Ezekiel's cedar — not in immediately recognizable grandeur — but as a mustard seed: the smallest, most easily overlooked beginning, which grows into something that provides shelter for all.

The "birds of the sky" (ta peteina tou ouranou) create an interesting tension within the chapter. In verse 4, the birds ate the seed sown on the road — and in verse 15, the birds were identified with Satan taking away the word. Now in verse 32, birds find shelter in the grown plant. The same element that threatens the seed at its most vulnerable stage finds its home in the kingdom at its mature stage. Whether the author intends a deliberate contrast or is simply using conventional prophetic imagery, the effect is to present the kingdom's final state as one of universal shelter — even for those who once seemed opposed.

Verses 33-34: The Author's Summary of Jesus's Parabolic Teaching

"With many such parables He was speaking the word to them, so far as they were able to hear it; and He did not speak to them without a parable; but He was explaining everything privately to His own disciples."

The author provides an editorial summary that makes several things explicit.

First, Jesus spoke "the word" (ton logon) to the crowds through parables. The author again uses the term that has been consistent throughout: the word is what Jesus proclaims, and it is the same word as the seed in the parable. The proclamation of the kingdom of God — "the word" — takes parabolic form when addressed to the general audience.

Second, the parables were calibrated to the audience's capacity: "so far as they were able to hear it" (kathos edynanto akouein). This is not arbitrary concealment but pedagogical accommodation. The word comes in the form the hearers can receive, even if that form does not yet convey full understanding. The author presents Jesus as a sower who adjusts his method to the soil without changing the seed.

Third, the private explanation is reserved for "His own disciples" (tois idiois mathetais). The word "own" (idiois) is emphatic — these are the ones who belong to Jesus, who have entered the inner circle, who sit around him (3:34). The public/private distinction — parables for the crowd, explanation for the disciples — is the structural expression of the insider/outsider dynamic that runs through the chapter.

The effect of this summary is to frame everything in chapter 4 as an instance of the very process the parables describe. The sower sows the word; some hear, some do not. Jesus sows the word through parables; some receive the explanation, others do not. The chapter is self-referential: it is simultaneously about the word's reception and an instance of it.

Verses 35-41: The Crossing and the Storm

"On that day, when evening came, He said to them, 'Let us go over to the other side.' Leaving the crowd, they took Him along in the boat, just as He was; and other boats were with Him. And a fierce gale of wind developed, and the waves were breaking over the boat so much that the boat was already filling up. Jesus Himself was in the stern, asleep on the cushion; and they woke Him and said to Him, 'Teacher, do You not care that we are perishing?'"

The transition is seamless: "on that day" (en ekeine te hemera) connects the storm to the parabolic teaching. The author presents this event as occurring on the same day as the teaching, suggesting a deliberate sequence. The teaching about the word, its reception, and the kingdom's hidden growth is followed immediately by an event that tests whether the disciples have heard what they were taught.

Jesus initiates the crossing: "Let us go over to the other side" (dielthmen eis to peran). The destination is the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee — Gentile territory (the country of the Gerasenes, 5:1). The phrase "just as He was" (hs en) — still in the boat from the teaching session — emphasizes continuity between the discourse and what follows.

The storm is described in terms that evoke Israel's scriptural tradition. The word for "fierce gale" is lailaps megale — a sudden, violent squall. The Sea of Galilee, set in a basin surrounded by hills, is known for such storms. But the author's description carries echoes beyond meteorology. In Jonah 1:4, the Lord "hurled a great wind on the sea and there was a great storm (klydon megas) on the sea so that the ship was about to break up." In Psalm 107:23-29 (106:23-29 LXX), sailors go down to the sea, and "He spoke and raised up a stormy wind, which lifted up the waves of the sea. They rose up to the heavens, they went down to the depths... Then they cried out to the LORD in their trouble, and He brought them out of their distresses. He caused the storm to be still, so that the waves of the sea were hushed."

Jesus is asleep in the stern (en te prymne epi to proskephalaion katheuddn). The detail is specific — on the cushion, in the stern. In Jonah 1:5, while the storm rages, "Jonah had gone below into the hold of the ship, lain down, and fallen sound asleep." The parallel is unmistakable: in both scenes, the one who has authority over the situation is asleep while the others panic. Jonah's sleep was a sign of his flight from God; Jesus's sleep, in the author's presentation, is a sign of his sovereign unconcern — he knows who he is.

The disciples' cry — "Teacher, do You not care that we are perishing?" (didaskale, ou melei soi hoti apollymetha?) — is a question about Jesus's character, not just a request for help. "Do You not care?" challenges his concern for them. The verb "perishing" (apollymetha) is the same word used for destruction and death. The disciples face what they believe is mortal danger and interpret Jesus's sleep as indifference.

"And He got up and rebuked the wind and said to the sea, 'Hush, be still.' And the wind died down and it became perfectly calm."

Jesus responds by "rebuking" (epetimesen) the wind. This is the same verb used for his authority over unclean spirits in 1:25 ("Be quiet, and come out of him!"). The command to the sea — "Hush, be still" (Sipa, pephims) — combines two words: sipa (be silent, hush) and pephims (perfect passive imperative of phimo — be muzzled, be silenced). The second word is identical to 1:25, where Jesus told the unclean spirit "Be quiet" (phimtheti). The author uses the same vocabulary for Jesus's authority over the demonic realm and over the natural world. Whether the author intends the reader to understand the storm as demonically driven or simply governed by the same authority, the linguistic connection is deliberate.

The result is immediate: "the wind died down and it became perfectly calm" (egalene megale). The phrase galene megale (great calm) mirrors lailaps megale (great storm). The transformation is total — from great storm to great calm through a spoken command.

The connection to Israel's scriptures is direct and specific. Psalm 89:9 (88:10 LXX): "You rule the swelling of the sea; when its waves rise, You still them." Psalm 65:7 (64:8 LXX): "Who stills the roaring of the seas, the roaring of their waves." Psalm 107:29 (106:29 LXX): "He caused the storm to be still, so that the waves of the sea were hushed." In every case, the one who calms the sea is YHWH. This is not a power attributed to prophets, kings, or angels in Israel's scriptures. Control over the sea belongs to God alone. Job 38:8-11 presents it as the defining act of creation: "Who enclosed the sea with doors... and said, 'Thus far you shall come, but no farther; and here shall your proud waves stop'?"

"And He said to them, 'Why are you afraid? Do you still have no faith?' They became very much afraid and said to one another, 'Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?'"

Jesus's response to the disciples contains two questions. "Why are you afraid?" (ti deiloi este) — the word deilos means cowardly or timid, not merely frightened. It is a rebuke. "Do you still have no faith?" (oup echete pistin) — the word "still" (oup) implies that by this point they should have faith. They have been with him through the healings, the exorcisms, the teachings. They have been given the mystery of the kingdom. Yet in the face of the storm, they have no faith. The author presents faith not as a feeling but as a settled trust in Jesus's identity and authority that should have been produced by everything they have witnessed.

The disciples' response is paradoxical: having been rescued from the storm, they become more afraid, not less. The verb "became very much afraid" (ephobethesan phobon megan — literally "feared a great fear") uses a Hebraic construction for intensification. Their fear of the storm has been replaced by a deeper fear — the fear that comes from proximity to divine power. Their question — "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?" (tis ara houtos estin hoti kai ho anemos kai he thalassa hypakouei aut?) — is the question the entire chapter has been building toward.

The verb "obey" (hypakouei) means literally "to listen under" — to hear and submit. The wind and sea "hear" Jesus. In a chapter dominated by the theme of hearing — who hears the word, who does not, what happens when the word is heard — the climactic statement is that even the wind and sea hear and obey. The disciples, who have been given the mystery but do not understand (v. 13), who have been warned to take care what they hear (v. 24), now witness the wind and sea doing what they themselves struggle to do: hearing and obeying immediately.

The question "Who then is this?" is left unanswered in the text. The author does not provide a response. But the scriptural context supplies one: the one who commands the sea and the storm is YHWH. The reader, who was told in 1:1 that this is "Jesus Christ, Son of God," and who heard the heavenly voice declare "You are My beloved Son" (1:11), has the answer. The disciples do not — yet. The gap between what the reader knows and what the disciples understand is one of the author's primary narrative techniques, and it reaches its sharpest expression here.

The Structure and Argument of Mark 4

The chapter is organized around a single theme — hearing — and develops it through three movements:

Movement 1 (vv. 1-25): The word and its reception. The seed parable describes the four possible responses to the word. The mystery passage (vv. 10-12) explains why some hear and others do not, grounding the explanation in Isaiah 6. The lamp and measure sayings (vv. 21-25) establish that the concealment is temporary and that hearing is a dynamic process that moves toward abundance or loss.

Movement 2 (vv. 26-34): The kingdom's hidden growth. The two seed parables — the seed growing by itself and the mustard seed — address the apparent insignificance of the kingdom's beginning. The kingdom grows by its own power, on its own timetable, from the smallest start to a sheltering greatness. The author's summary (vv. 33-34) frames all of this as the word being spoken to the crowds through parables.

Movement 3 (vv. 35-41): The test of hearing. The storm scene functions as an enacted parable. The disciples have received the teaching about the word, the kingdom, and the importance of hearing. Now they are tested. They fail. They have no faith. They do not recognize who is in the boat with them. Yet the chapter ends not with their failure but with the right question: "Who then is this?" The question that should lead to faith has been posed, even if it has not yet been answered by those who ask it.

Key Themes and Connections within the Document

"The word" (ho logos) as a continuous thread. The author has been building this term since 1:45, through 2:2, and now gives it its fullest treatment. The word is what Jesus proclaims; it is the announcement of the kingdom's arrival; it is the seed sown in the parable; and it is what produces varied responses in its hearers. From this point forward in the document, any reference to "the word" carries the weight of the entire parable discourse.

The insider/outsider dynamic. Chapter 3 established the division between those around Jesus and those outside. Chapter 4 explores this division and reveals its complexity. The insiders have been given the mystery, but they do not understand the foundational parable (v. 13) and have no faith in the storm (v. 40). Being inside the circle is necessary but not sufficient. The insiders must still hear — actively, attentively, with the measure of openness that produces more understanding rather than less.

The kingdom of God as present and growing. The three seed parables together present the kingdom as something that has already been sown (1:15), that grows by a power beyond human control, that begins in apparent insignificance, and that will reach a harvest of overwhelming abundance. The kingdom is not waiting to arrive; it is growing now, in the soil of human response to the word.

Jesus's identity as the chapter's unresolved question. The parable discourse presents Jesus as the sower of the word. The storm scene presents him as the one whom wind and sea obey — a role that Israel's scriptures reserve for YHWH. The disciples' concluding question — "Who then is this?" — is the question the author wants the reader to carry forward into the rest of the narrative. The answer has been provided (1:1, 1:11) but has not yet been grasped by those within the story.

Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 4

Mark 4 presents the author's most sustained discourse on the nature of the kingdom's arrival and the varied responses it produces. The chapter argues:

1. The kingdom of God arrives through the proclamation of the word, and the word produces four categories of response: immediate rejection, enthusiastic but rootless reception that collapses under pressure, gradual suffocation by competing concerns, and fruitful reception that yields an extraordinary harvest. The variable is not the word but the hearer.

2. The concealment of the kingdom in parabolic form is not arbitrary but serves a purpose rooted in Isaiah's prophetic pattern: those who have already rejected the word are confirmed in their blindness, while those who draw near to Jesus receive understanding. The concealment is temporary — what is hidden exists to be revealed.

3. The kingdom grows by its own power, on its own timetable, from the smallest beginning to a greatness that shelters all. The sower's task is to sow; the growth belongs to God.

4. Hearing is not a one-time event but a dynamic trajectory. Those who hear well receive more; those who do not hear lose even what they have. The insiders are warned that their position brings responsibility, not security.

5. The one who teaches about the kingdom in parables demonstrates authority over the natural world by commanding the wind and sea — an act that Israel's scriptures attribute exclusively to YHWH. The disciples' question "Who then is this?" is the question the entire chapter has been preparing, and the answer, though available to the reader from the document's opening verse, remains unresolved within the narrative itself.
