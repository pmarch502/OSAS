1 Timothy 6 — First-Century Reading

Context and Placement

The document's genre and occasion: This is a mandate letter from Paul to Timothy, his delegate in Ephesus, authorizing and instructing him to manage a community in crisis. The crisis, identified in chapter 1, is false teaching — certain persons promote myths, genealogies, and speculative elaborations of the Torah rather than the stewardship from God that operates through faith (1:3-7). The letter functions simultaneously as personal correspondence and public authorization: it will be read before the Ephesian assembly, and its instructions carry apostolic authority. The letter's stated purpose appears in 3:14-15: "I am writing these things to you so that, if I delay, you may know how one ought to behave in the household of God, which is the church of the living God, a pillar and buttress of the truth." Chapter 6 is the letter's final chapter. It completes the practical instructions, returns explicitly to the false teaching crisis, delivers the final personal charge to Timothy, and closes the ring opened in chapter 1.

What the author was arguing immediately before (1 Timothy 5): Chapter 5 was the practical core of the mandate letter — the detailed working-out of how one manages the household of God. It opened with the household framework for Timothy's conduct toward different age groups (5:1-2): older men as fathers, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters. It devoted extensive attention to widows (5:3-16), distinguishing "truly widows" who have no family and whose lives are defined by prayer from those who have children or grandchildren who must care for them. Paul declared that anyone who does not provide for his own family "has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (5:8). Qualifications for enrolled widows were specified (5:9-10), and younger widows were directed to marry, bear children, and manage their households (5:14) — directly countering the false teachers who forbid marriage (4:3). The chapter noted that "some have already strayed after Satan" (5:15). Instructions about elders followed: those who rule well deserve double honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching (5:17-18), accusations require two or three witnesses (5:19), and those who persist in sin are to be rebuked publicly (5:20). Timothy was charged before God, Christ Jesus, and the elect angels to act without prejudgment or partiality (5:21). He was warned against hasty ordination (5:22), told to keep himself pure (5:22), given practical advice about his health (5:23), and assured that both sins and good works eventually come to light (5:24-25).

How chapter 6 connects to what came before: The transition from chapter 5 to chapter 6 is seamless. Chapter 5 concluded with instructions about the visibility of character — sins and good works that are conspicuous or eventually revealed (5:24-25). This provided the rationale for patience and thoroughness in evaluating leaders. Chapter 6 opens with the last category of persons in the household who require instruction: slaves under the yoke. Having addressed older and younger men and women (5:1-2), widows (5:3-16), elders (5:17-25), and Timothy himself (5:22-25), Paul now turns to slaves and their masters (6:1-2a). The subject shifts but the governing framework remains the same: the household of God contains people in every social position, and each must know how to conduct themselves "so that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled" (6:1).

After the brief instruction about slaves, Paul returns to the false teaching crisis that opened the letter (6:2b-5), identifies the proper alternative in godliness with contentment (6:6-8), warns about the love of money as the mechanism of departure from the faith (6:9-10), delivers a final personal charge to Timothy (6:11-16), gives instructions about the rich (6:17-19), and closes with the command to guard the deposit and a final warning about those who have swerved from the faith (6:20-21). The chapter's movement mirrors the letter's overall structure: from practical household management, through the false teaching problem, to the final charge and closing. The letter ends where it began — with the contrast between sound teaching and the speculations of those who have departed from it.

Verses 1-2a: Slaves and Masters — Honor and the Name of God

"Let all who are under a yoke as bondservants regard their own masters as worthy of all honor, so that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled. Those who have believing masters must not be disrespectful on the ground that they are brothers; rather they must serve all the better since those who benefit by their good service are believers and beloved."

"Let all who are under a yoke as bondservants" (hosoi eisin hypo zygon douloi). The relative pronoun hosoi ("as many as, all who") with the present tense eisin ("are") identifies a present social reality within the Ephesian community: some members are slaves. The noun zygon ("yoke") is a metaphor drawn from agriculture — the wooden frame placed on the necks of draft animals to harness them to a plow or cart. Applied to slavery, it describes the condition of subjection, of bearing a load not of one's choosing. The word doulos ("bondservant, slave") denotes a person who is the legal property of another, without personal autonomy in the economic and social sphere. In the first-century Roman Empire, slavery was pervasive: estimates suggest that between a quarter and a third of the population of major cities were enslaved persons. Slaves in Ephesus would have served in households, workshops, commercial establishments, and agricultural operations.

The phrase "under a yoke" is not merely descriptive but evaluative — it acknowledges the weight and burden of the condition. The LXX uses zygon extensively for subjection and servitude. Leviticus 26:13: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, that you should not be their slaves. I have broken the bars of your yoke (zygou) and made you walk erect." Deuteronomy 28:48: "He will put a yoke of iron (zygon sidēroun) upon your neck until he has destroyed you." 1 Kings 12:4 (LXX 3 Kingdoms 12:4): the people plead with Rehoboam, "Your father made our yoke (zygon) heavy." Isaiah 9:4 (LXX 9:3): "For the yoke (zygon) of his burden, and the staff for his shoulder, the rod of his oppressor, you have broken as on the day of Midian." The yoke is consistently associated with oppression, bondage, and the heavy burden of servitude. Paul's use of it here acknowledges that slavery is a burden, not a blessing.

"Regard their own masters as worthy of all honor" (tous idious despotas pasēs timēs axious hēgeisthōsan). The verb hēgeisthai ("to consider, to regard, to count, to reckon") commands a mental act: the slave is to consider, to form a judgment about, his master. The noun despotēs ("master, lord, owner") is the standard term for a slaveholder — the one who exercises absolute authority over the slave's person and labor. The word carries connotations of total power: despotēs is stronger than kyrios ("lord, sir"), which can be used as a polite address, while despotēs implies ownership and command. The instruction is that the slave should regard the master as worthy of pasēs timēs ("all honor, every kind of honor"). The noun timē ("honor, respect, value, price") appeared in 5:3 for honoring widows and in 5:17 for the "double honor" due to elders who rule well. In both cases, timē included practical action, not merely internal sentiment. Here, the "all" (pasēs) makes the honor comprehensive — every form of respect and dutiful service the master's position requires.

The command is striking because it does not condition honor on the master's character. The text does not say "regard your good masters as worthy of honor" or "honor your masters if they treat you well." It says "all who are under a yoke" — every slave, regardless of circumstance — should regard "their own masters" — whatever kind of masters they have — as worthy of all honor.

"So that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled" (hina to onoma tou theou kai hē didaskalia mē blasphēmētai). The conjunction hina ("so that, in order that") introduces the purpose: the slaves' conduct toward their masters serves a larger goal. The subject that must not be reviled is dual: to onoma tou theou ("the name of God") and hē didaskalia ("the teaching, the doctrine"). The verb blasphēmein ("to blaspheme, to revile, to speak injuriously of, to defame") means to bring the name of God into disrepute, to cause outsiders to speak evil of God because of the behavior of God's people.

The logic is practical and contextual. In the first-century Roman world, the social order depended on the subordination of slaves to masters. A slave who became a believer and then began to act insubordinately toward his master — on the grounds that the gospel had made them equal — would provoke a backlash not against the individual slave but against the community and its God. The master, and Roman society generally, would conclude that the teaching produces disorder, that the God worshiped by these people undermines the social fabric. The "name of God" would be blasphemed — spoken of contemptuously, associated with social disruption — and "the teaching" (hē didaskalia, the same word used throughout the letter for sound doctrine) would be discredited.

This concern has deep roots in Israel's prophetic literature. Ezekiel 36:20-23 describes how Israel's exile caused God's name to be profaned among the nations: "When they came to the nations, wherever they came, they profaned my holy name, in that people said of them, 'These are the people of the LORD, and yet they had to go out of his land.' But I had concern for my holy name, which the house of Israel had profaned among the nations." Isaiah 52:5 (LXX): "My name is blasphemed (blasphēmeitai) continually all day long among the nations." Romans 2:24 cites this same tradition: "The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you." The conduct of God's people either honors or disgraces God's name before the watching world. Paul's instruction to slaves operates within this framework: their behavior is not a private matter but a public testimony.

"Those who have believing masters must not be disrespectful on the ground that they are brothers" (hoi de pistous echontes despotas mē kataphroneitōsan, hoti adelphoi eisin). The conjunction de ("but, and") introduces a second scenario: slaves whose masters are also believers. The verb kataphronein ("to despise, to look down on, to think little of, to treat with contempt") — the same verb used in 4:12 ("let no one despise your youth") — describes the danger: a slave who discovers that his master shares his faith may begin to treat the master's authority with contempt, reasoning that since they are adelphoi ("brothers") in the faith, the social distinction of master and slave no longer applies.

The reasoning would have seemed logical: if in Christ "there is neither slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28), then why should a slave continue to defer to a believing master? The distinction between the social institution (slavery) and the spiritual reality (brotherhood) creates tension. Paul does not resolve this tension by eliminating either term; he addresses the practical behavior it produces. The slave must not use the brotherhood as a justification for disrespect.

"Rather they must serve all the better since those who benefit by their good service are believers and beloved" (alla mallon douleuetōsan, hoti pistoi eisin kai agapētoi hoi tēs euergesias antilambanomenoi). The conjunction alla ("but, rather, on the contrary") introduces the correct response. The verb douleuein ("to serve, to be a slave, to perform the duties of a slave") in the imperative (douleuetōsan, "let them serve") commands continued and even increased service. The adverb mallon ("more, rather, all the more") intensifies: they should serve better than before, not worse.

The reason is stated in a hoti clause: "because those who benefit by their good service are believers and beloved" (hoti pistoi eisin kai agapētoi hoi tēs euergesias antilambanomenoi). The participle antilambanomenoi (from antilambanesthai, "to take hold of in return, to benefit from, to receive, to lay claim to") with euergesias ("good service, beneficence, benefit") describes the masters as recipients of the slaves' service. They are pistoi ("believers, faithful ones") and agapētoi ("beloved, dear ones"). The logic reverses the expected argument: instead of "your master is your brother, so stop serving," Paul argues "your master is your brother, so serve even better" — because the person benefiting from your work is someone beloved, a fellow member of God's household. The quality of service should increase, not decrease, when the relationship is between believers.

This instruction closes the letter's survey of the various groups within the household of God. From older men to younger women (5:1-2), from widows to elders (5:3-25), and now to slaves and masters (6:1-2a), every social category within the community has received instruction on how to conduct themselves in God's household.

Verses 2b-5: The Return to False Teaching — Different Doctrine and Its Fruits

"Teach and urge these things. If anyone teaches a different doctrine and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that accords with godliness, he is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing. He has an unhealthy craving for controversy and for quarrels about words, which produce envy, dissension, slander, evil suspicions, and constant friction among people who are depraved in mind and deprived of the truth, imagining that godliness is a means of gain."

"Teach and urge these things" (tauta didaske kai parakalei). Two imperatives direct Timothy. The verb didaskein ("to teach, to instruct") commands formal doctrinal instruction. The verb parakalein ("to exhort, to urge, to encourage, to appeal") commands persuasive application. The demonstrative tauta ("these things") refers to the practical instructions just given — about slaves and masters, about the conduct of all groups within the household. These are not optional suggestions but content that Timothy must actively teach and press upon the community.

This brief transitional sentence (6:2b) functions as a hinge. It concludes the practical instruction section (5:1-6:2a) and opens the return to the false teaching theme that dominates the remainder of the chapter. The phrase "these things" looks backward; the conditional "if anyone teaches a different doctrine" looks forward. Paul is about to contrast what Timothy is to teach with what the false teachers promote.

"If anyone teaches a different doctrine" (ei tis heterodidaskalei). The verb heterodidaskalein ("to teach different doctrine, to teach what is other") appeared at the letter's very opening in 1:3: "charge certain persons not to teach different doctrine" (mē heterodidaskalein). The repetition is deliberate. The letter began with the problem of different doctrine (1:3) and now returns to it (6:3), creating a frame around the entire body of instructions. Everything between 1:3 and 6:3 is the content of the sound teaching that replaces the different doctrine. The verb heterodidaskalein is rare — it appears only in these two locations in all surviving Greek literature of this period — and its recurrence signals that the letter's argument is circling back to its starting point.

"And does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ" (kai mē proserchetai hygiainousin logois tois tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou). The verb proserchesthai ("to approach, to come to, to agree with, to assent to, to attach oneself to") describes the act of drawing near and giving one's agreement. The one who teaches differently "does not come to" or "does not agree with" the sound words. The adjective hygiainousin (present active participle of hygiainein, "to be healthy, to be sound, to be in good condition") is the medical metaphor that characterizes teaching throughout this letter: sound teaching is healthy teaching (1:10, "sound teaching"; 2 Timothy 1:13, "the pattern of sound words"; 4:3, "sound teaching"; Titus 1:9, "sound doctrine"; 2:1, "sound doctrine"). Unsound teaching is diseased — it produces sickness in the community.

The words are identified as tois tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou ("those of our Lord Jesus Christ") — the words that belong to, originate from, or accord with the Lord Jesus Christ. Whether this means the actual sayings of Jesus (the teaching tradition from his ministry), the apostolic teaching about Jesus (the gospel proclamation), or both, the standard is clear: sound teaching is measured against the words of the Lord.

"And the teaching that accords with godliness" (kai tē kat' eusebeian didaskalia). The preposition kata with the accusative ("according to, in accordance with") identifies the teaching as one that corresponds to eusebeia ("godliness, piety, right reverence toward God"). The word eusebeia is one of the letter's central terms (2:2; 3:16; 4:7, 8; 6:3, 5, 6, 11). The "teaching that accords with godliness" is teaching that produces godly living — reverent, ordered, faithful conduct. Any teaching that does not produce this fruit fails the test. The false teaching, by contrast, produces the litany of vices that follows.

"He is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing" (tetyphōtai, mēden epistamenos). The verb typhoō ("to wrap in smoke, to puff up, to blind with conceit, to make proud") in the perfect passive (tetyphōtai) describes a settled condition: the false teacher has been puffed up and remains in that state. The same verb appeared in 3:6, where the danger for a neophyte overseer was that he might become "puffed up with conceit" (typhōtheis) and fall into the condemnation of the devil. The word's etymology — from typhos ("smoke, vapor") — suggests someone surrounded by a cloud of self-importance, unable to see clearly through the haze of their own ego. The irony is pointed: the false teacher is inflated with self-perceived knowledge while in reality epistamenos mēden — "understanding nothing." The verb epistasthai ("to know, to understand, to be acquainted with") is negated absolutely by mēden ("nothing"). This echoes 1:7, where the false teachers were described as "not understanding either what they are saying or the things about which they make confident assertions." Maximum confidence, zero comprehension.

"He has an unhealthy craving for controversy and for quarrels about words" (nosōn peri zētēseis kai logomachias). The verb nosein ("to be sick, to be diseased, to have an unhealthy craving") extends the medical metaphor. The false teacher's relationship to controversies is described as a sickness — a morbid obsession, an unhealthy fixation. The word is chosen deliberately to contrast with hygiainein ("to be healthy"): the sound words are healthy; the false teacher's craving is sick. The dative objects are zētēseis ("controversies, investigations, speculative questions") — the same word used in 1:4 for the "speculations" that myths and genealogies promote — and logomachias ("quarrels about words, verbal battles"). The compound noun logomachia (logos, "word" + machē, "fight, battle") appears only here in the New Testament. It describes combat conducted with words rather than weapons — not productive debate but destructive verbal warfare.

"Which produce envy, dissension, slander, evil suspicions, and constant friction" (ex hōn ginetai phthonos, eris, blasphēmiai, hyponoiai ponērai, diaparatribai). The relative pronoun hōn ("from which") identifies the controversies and word-battles as the source from which five evils are generated:

(1) Phthonos ("envy, jealousy") — the resentment of another's advantage. In the LXX, phthonos is associated with the corruption of relationships: Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 states that "through the devil's envy (phthonō diabolou) death entered the world." The word describes the disposition that cannot tolerate another person's success, position, or recognition.

(2) Eris ("strife, discord, contention, rivalry") — the active conflict that results from envy. Romans 1:29 lists eris among the fruits of a debased mind. The word describes open antagonism between persons or factions within the community.

(3) Blasphēmiai ("slanders, abusive speech, defamations") — the plural indicates repeated instances of injurious speech. The same word appeared in its verb form in 1:13 (Paul formerly a "blasphemer") and in 1:20 (Hymenaeus and Alexander handed to Satan to learn "not to blaspheme"). Here it describes the community-destroying speech that false teaching produces: people begin to speak injuriously about one another.

(4) Hyponoiai ponērai ("evil suspicions, malicious surmises") — the noun hyponoia ("suspicion, surmise, conjecture") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of hypo ("under") + nous ("mind") — what is under the mind, beneath the surface, the hidden assumption. The adjective ponērai ("evil, wicked, malicious") characterizes these suspicions as harmful. The community begins to interpret one another's motives maliciously, assuming the worst, reading bad intent behind innocent actions.

(5) Diaparatribai ("constant friction, mutual irritation, incessant wrangling") — the noun diaparatribē appears only here in the New Testament and is extremely rare in Greek literature generally. It is composed of dia ("through, thoroughly") + para ("beside, alongside") + tribein ("to rub, to wear away"). The image is of continuous rubbing — the friction that wears surfaces raw through repeated contact. The community's relationships have been abraded to the point of constant irritation. There is no peace, no rest, no resolution — only unending mutual provocation.

"Among people who are depraved in mind and deprived of the truth" (diephtharmenōn ton noun kai apesterēmenōn tēs alētheias). Two perfect passive participles describe the condition of those who produce these evils:

(1) Diephtharmenōn ton noun ("corrupted in mind, depraved in understanding") — the verb diaphtheirein ("to destroy utterly, to corrupt completely, to ruin") in the perfect passive with ton noun ("the mind") describes a mind that has been thoroughly corrupted and remains in that state. The prefix dia- intensifies: this is not partial corruption but thorough ruin of the faculty of understanding. The same concept appeared in different words at 4:2, where the false teachers' consciences were "seared" (kekautēriasmenōn) — the interior faculty that should guide them has been destroyed.

(2) Apesterēmenōn tēs alētheias ("deprived of the truth, robbed of the truth") — the verb aposterein ("to deprive, to rob, to defraud") in the perfect passive indicates that these persons have been stripped of the truth and remain without it. The same verb appears in 1 Corinthians 6:7-8, where Paul accuses the Corinthians of defrauding one another. The truth (hē alētheia) was once available to them — they are not people who never encountered truth but people who have been deprived of it, whether by their own corruption or by the influence of deceiving spirits (4:1). The knowledge of the truth that God desires all people to reach (2:4) has been taken from these persons.

"Imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (nomizontōn porismon einai tēn eusebeian). The verb nomizein ("to think, to suppose, to consider, to imagine") introduces a false calculation. The noun porismos ("gain, means of gain, source of profit") appears only here and in verse 6 in the New Testament. The word describes a revenue stream, a way of making money. The false teachers treat eusebeia ("godliness, piety") as a commercial enterprise — a product to be marketed, a performance to be monetized. Their teaching is not motivated by love of truth but by love of profit. They have turned the community's spiritual life into their personal income source.

This accusation connects to several threads in the letter. The overseer must be "not a lover of money" (aphilargyron, 3:3). The deacon must be "not greedy for dishonest gain" (mē aischrokerdē, 3:8). Titus 1:11 describes false teachers in Crete who teach "for shameful gain" (aischrou kerdous charin). The commercialization of religious teaching was not unique to Ephesus; wandering philosophers and religious teachers who charged fees for their instruction were a well-known feature of the first-century Mediterranean world. Paul distinguished himself from such persons: "we are not, like so many, peddlers of God's word" (2 Corinthians 2:17); "we never came with words of flattery, as you know, nor with a pretext for greed" (1 Thessalonians 2:5).

The portrait of the false teacher in 6:3-5 is now complete, and it recapitulates the portrait from chapter 1 while adding new details. In chapter 1, the false teachers swerved from love, good conscience, and sincere faith (1:5-6), wandered into empty talk (1:6), desired to be Torah teachers without understanding (1:7). Here the same teachers are puffed up (6:4), understand nothing (6:4), are sick with controversy (6:4), produce a catalogue of relational destruction (6:4-5), are corrupted in mind and deprived of truth (6:5), and treat godliness as a business (6:5). The letter frames the false teaching problem at both ends with the same diagnosis: these people are not merely wrong; they are corrupted, and their corruption produces observable damage in the community.

Verses 6-8: Godliness with Contentment — The True Gain

"But godliness with contentment is great gain, for we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world. But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content."

"But godliness with contentment is great gain" (estin de porismos megas hē eusebeia meta autarkeias). The conjunction de ("but") marks the contrast with verse 5. The false teachers imagine that godliness is a means of gain; Paul responds that godliness with contentment is indeed great gain — but not the kind they imagine. The word porismos ("gain, source of profit") is the same word from verse 5, now redeployed. The adjective megas ("great, large") amplifies: this is not small or marginal gain but great gain.

The critical qualifier is meta autarkeias ("with contentment, accompanied by self-sufficiency"). The noun autarkeia ("contentment, self-sufficiency, independence from external circumstances") is composed of autos ("self") + arkein ("to be sufficient, to be enough"). The word was a central concept in Stoic and Cynic philosophical traditions, where it described the wise person's freedom from dependence on external goods. Epictetus taught that happiness consists in wanting only what one has. Seneca wrote extensively about the sufficiency of virtue alone for the good life. But the word also has Jewish and Pauline roots that give it a different foundation. In 2 Corinthians 9:8, Paul writes: "God is able to make all grace abound to you, so that having all sufficiency (autarkeian) in all things at all times, you may abound in every good work." In Philippians 4:11-12, Paul says: "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content (autarkēs). I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need." For Paul, autarkeia is not Stoic self-reliance but trust-based contentment — the ability to be at peace in any circumstance because one's security rests in God, not in possessions.

The combination of eusebeia and autarkeia redefines "gain" entirely. The false teachers seek material profit through religious performance; the genuine believer already possesses the greatest gain available — a right relationship with God (eusebeia) and freedom from the tyranny of wanting more (autarkeia). This gain cannot be lost to market fluctuations, theft, or death. It is the only gain that is truly megas ("great").

"For we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world" (ouden gar eisēnenkamen eis ton kosmon, hoti oude exenenkein ti dynametha). The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the rationale. The argument is from the boundaries of human existence: birth and death. At birth, we arrive with nothing (ouden eisēnenkamen, "we brought nothing in"). At death, we leave with nothing (oude exenenkein ti dynametha, "neither can we carry anything out"). Everything between is temporary possession, not permanent ownership. The argument strips material wealth of its pretension to permanence.

The reasoning echoes Job 1:21 (LXX): "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." Ecclesiastes 5:15 (LXX 5:14): "As he came from his mother's womb, naked shall he go, returning as he came; nothing from his toil shall he carry in his hand." Psalm 49:17 (LXX 48:18): "For when he dies he will carry nothing away (ou gar en tō apothnēskein auton lēmpsetai ta panta); his glory will not go down after him." The wisdom tradition of Israel consistently taught that wealth is temporary, that death strips all accumulation, and that the only thing that survives death is one's relationship with God and the deeds that flowed from it.

"But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content" (echontes de diatrophas kai skepasmata, toutois arkesthēsometha). The participle echontes ("having") is conditional: if we have these things. The noun diatrophē ("food, sustenance, nourishment") appears only here in the New Testament. The noun skepasma ("covering, clothing, shelter") also appears only here in the New Testament; it derives from skepazein ("to cover, to shelter") and can include both clothing and shelter — whatever covers and protects the body. Together, diatrophai kai skepasmata represent the minimum necessary for physical survival: something to eat and something to cover oneself with.

The verb arkesthēsometha (future passive of arkein, "to be sufficient, to be enough") means "we will be satisfied, we will be content, we will regard these as sufficient." The standard Paul sets is deliberately minimal: not luxury, not comfort, not abundance, but bare sufficiency. This does not mean that having more is sinful — Paul will address the wealthy in verses 17-19 without commanding them to divest everything — but it means that the baseline for contentment is low. If you have food and covering, you have enough. Everything beyond that is extra, not essential.

The instruction directly opposes the false teachers' orientation. They treat godliness as a means to acquire more; Paul teaches that godliness means needing less. They measure success by accumulation; Paul measures it by contentment. Their gain is external and transient; Paul's gain is internal and permanent.

Verses 9-10: The Love of Money — The Mechanism of Departure

"But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils. It is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs."

"But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires" (hoi de boulomenoi ploutein empiptousin eis peirasmon kai pagida kai epithymias pollas anoētous kai blaberas). The conjunction de ("but") contrasts the contentment of verses 6-8 with the opposite disposition. The verb boulesthai ("to desire, to wish, to intend, to will") describes deliberate aspiration — not a passing thought but a settled purpose. The object is ploutein ("to be rich, to become wealthy") — the infinitive describes the goal toward which their will is directed. The key word is boulomenoi, not echontes — those who desire to be rich, not those who are rich. The diagnosis is about aspiration and craving, not about the fact of possessing wealth. (Paul will address those who are already wealthy in vv. 17-19 with different instructions.)

The verb empiptein ("to fall into") describes an unintended descent — the person who desires wealth does not intend to fall into temptation; the fall is the consequence of the desire. Three things are fallen into:

(1) Peirasmon ("temptation, testing, trial") — the condition of being tested, of facing inducements to sin. The word can mean either a test imposed by God (for growth) or a temptation from evil (for destruction). Here the context indicates the latter: the desire for wealth creates conditions in which one is pulled toward sin.

(2) Pagida ("a snare, a trap") — the same word used in 3:7 for "the snare of the devil" that catches leaders with bad outside reputations. The image is of a hunting trap: the person walks into it unawares, and once caught, cannot easily escape. The desire for wealth is itself the bait; the trap closes around those who pursue it.

(3) Epithymias pollas anoētous kai blaberas ("many senseless and harmful desires") — the noun epithymia ("desire, longing, craving") is qualified by two adjectives: anoētos ("senseless, foolish, unintelligent" — from the alpha-privative + noein, "to understand") and blaberos ("harmful, injurious, hurtful" — from blaptein, "to hurt, to harm"). The desires that accompany the pursuit of wealth are both stupid and destructive — they make no sense and they cause damage.

"That plunge people into ruin and destruction" (haitines bythizousin tous anthrōpous eis olethron kai apōleian). The relative pronoun haitines ("which are of such a character that") introduces the result. The verb bythizein ("to plunge, to sink, to submerge") is a nautical term — it describes a ship going down, being dragged beneath the water. The same verb appears in Luke 5:7, where the boats were "sinking" (bythizesthai) under the weight of the catch. The image is of descent into deep water — total submersion, drowning. The destinations are stated as a pair:

Olethros ("ruin, destruction, devastation") — the word describes the destruction of what once existed. In 1 Thessalonians 5:3, Paul warns: "When people say, 'There is peace and security,' then sudden destruction (olethros) will come upon them." In 2 Thessalonians 1:9, "they will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction (olethron aiōnion)." In 1 Corinthians 5:5, the man in sexual sin is "delivered to Satan for the destruction (olethron) of the flesh."

Apōleia ("destruction, perdition, loss, waste") — a stronger and more final word. In the LXX, apōleia describes total loss: Deuteronomy 32:35, "the day of their destruction (apōleias) is at hand." In the broader New Testament, apōleia appears as the opposite of salvation: Matthew 7:13, "the gate is wide and the way is easy that leads to destruction (apōleian)"; Philippians 1:28, "a sign to them of their destruction (apōleias), but of your salvation"; Philippians 3:19, "their end is destruction (apōleia)."

The double noun — olethron kai apōleian — is emphatic: ruin and total loss, devastation and final destruction. The desire for wealth does not merely hinder spiritual growth; it drowns the person in comprehensive destruction. The nautical metaphor (bythizousin, "plunge/sink") connects to the shipwreck image of 1:19, where those who rejected conscience "made shipwreck of their faith." The water imagery runs through the letter: the faith can be wrecked at sea, and the desire for wealth can plunge a person beneath the surface.

"For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (rhiza gar pantōn tōn kakōn estin hē philargyria). The conjunction gar ("for") introduces the underlying principle. The noun philargyria ("love of money, avarice, covetousness") is composed of philos ("loving") + argyros ("silver, money"). The word appears only here in the New Testament, though the cognate adjective philargyros ("money-loving") appeared in 3:3 (negated: aphilargyron, "not a lover of money," for the overseer) and in Luke 16:14, where the Pharisees are described as philargyroi ("lovers of money").

The statement is that philargyria is rhiza ("root") — not "the root" (the Greek lacks the definite article before rhiza) but "a root." The genitive pantōn tōn kakōn means "of all the evils" or "of all kinds of evils." The text does not claim that every evil in the world originates from the love of money; it claims that the love of money is a root from which every kind of evil can grow. The metaphor is botanical: as a root produces a plant, so the love of money produces evils. The evils are not accidental; they grow naturally from the root of avarice.

The concept has deep roots in Israel's wisdom tradition. Ecclesiastes 5:10: "He who loves money will not be satisfied with money, nor he who loves wealth with his income." Proverbs 15:27: "Whoever is greedy for unjust gain troubles his own household." Proverbs 28:22: "A stingy man hastens after wealth and does not know that poverty will come upon him." The Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs (Testament of Judah 19:1): "My children, the love of money leads to idolatry, because when led astray by money, men designate as gods those who are not gods." In the broader Greco-Roman moral tradition, Diogenes Laertius attributes to Diogenes the Cynic the saying that "the love of money is the mother-city (mētropolis) of all evils."

"It is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs" (hēs tines oregomenoi apeplanthēsan apo tēs pisteōs kai heautous periepeiran odynais pollais). The relative pronoun hēs ("which") refers back to philargyria. The verb oregesthai ("to reach out for, to stretch toward, to aspire to, to crave") — the same verb used in 3:1 for the positive aspiration to the office of overseer — here describes reaching toward money. The same gesture of reaching that is noble when directed at oversight becomes destructive when directed at wealth.

The verb apoplanasthai ("to wander away from, to be led astray from, to stray from") is composed of apo ("away from") + planasthai ("to wander, to go astray, to err"). The perfect tense (apeplanthēsan) indicates a completed action with lasting results: they have wandered away and remain astray. The object from which they have wandered is apo tēs pisteōs ("from the faith") — the definite article marks this as "the faith," the community's shared confession and trust, the body of belief and practice that defines the community's identity.

This is the fourth explicit statement in the letter that people have departed from the faith:

  • 1:6: "Certain persons, by swerving from these, have wandered away into vain discussion."
  • 1:19-20: "By rejecting this, some have made shipwreck of their faith, among whom are Hymenaeus and Alexander."
  • 4:1: "The Spirit expressly says that in later times some will depart from the faith."
  • 5:15: "Some have already strayed after Satan."

Now in 6:10: "some have wandered away from the faith." The verb changes each time (astochein, nauagein, aphistēmi, ektrepesthai, apoplanasthai) but the reality is the same: real people within the community have actually departed from the faith. This is not a theoretical possibility but a reported fact. The mechanism in this case is specific: the love of money. The craving for wealth is not merely a moral failing; it is a vector of apostasy, a path by which people leave the faith.

"And pierced themselves with many pangs" (kai heautous periepeiran odynais pollais). The verb peripeirein ("to pierce through, to impale, to run through") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of peri ("around, through") + peirein ("to pierce"). The reflexive pronoun heautous ("themselves") is emphatic: they have pierced themselves. No one did this to them. The self-inflicted nature of the damage is stressed. The noun odynē ("pain, grief, anguish, pang") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Romans 9:2, where Paul describes the "great sorrow and unceasing anguish" (odynēn) he feels over Israel's unbelief. The adjective pollais ("many") multiplies the pain: not one pang but many pangs, an accumulation of self-inflicted wounds.

The image is vivid and physical: the person who reaches for money ends up impaled on the object of their desire. The very thing they pursued has become the instrument of their suffering. They wandered from the faith and pierced themselves — the departure and the self-destruction are aspects of the same movement.

Verses 11-12: The Final Charge to Timothy — Flee, Pursue, Fight, Take Hold

"But as for you, O man of God, flee these things. Pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness. Fight the good fight of the faith. Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called and about which you made the good confession in the presence of many witnesses."

"But as for you, O man of God" (sy de, ō anthrōpe theou). The emphatic pronoun sy ("you") with the adversative conjunction de ("but") marks a sharp contrast: those people wander from the faith and pierce themselves; you, Timothy, must do the opposite. The vocative ō anthrōpe theou ("O man of God") is a title with deep resonance in Israel's history. The phrase "man of God" (anthrōpos theou / ish elohim) was a designation for prophets and leaders who served as God's authorized representatives:

  • Moses: Deuteronomy 33:1, "Moses the man of God"; Psalm 90:1 (LXX 89:1), "A prayer of Moses, the man of God."
  • Samuel: 1 Samuel 9:6-10, the young Saul is directed to consult "a man of God" in the city.
  • Elijah: 1 Kings 17:18, 24, the widow of Zarephath calls Elijah "man of God."
  • Elisha: 2 Kings 4:7, 9, 16, 21, 25, 27, 40, 42; 5:8, 14, 15, 20; 6:6, 9, 10, 12, 15; 7:2, 17, 18, 19; 8:4, 7, 8, 11; 13:19 — Elisha is called "man of God" more than any other figure in scripture.
  • David: Nehemiah 12:24, 36, "David the man of God."
  • Various unnamed prophets: 1 Kings 13:1; 2 Chronicles 25:7, 9.

By calling Timothy "man of God," Paul places him in the prophetic succession — he is not merely a church administrator but a person who stands in the tradition of Moses, Elijah, and Elisha as God's representative to God's people. The title confers both dignity and responsibility: the man of God speaks for God, acts for God, and is accountable to God.

"Flee these things" (tauta pheuge). The verb pheugein ("to flee, to run away from, to escape") is a present imperative commanding ongoing flight — not a one-time departure but a continuous running away from the things just described: the love of money, the desire to be rich, the craving that leads to wandering from the faith. The same verb appears in 2 Timothy 2:22: "flee youthful passions." The command is not to resist these things or to manage them but to flee them — to put distance between oneself and the danger, as one would flee a burning building or a pursuing enemy. The language is urgent and physical: run.

"Pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness" (diōke de dikaiosynēn, eusebeian, pistin, agapēn, hypomonēn, praypathian). The verb diōkein ("to pursue, to chase, to run after, to persecute") is the opposite of pheugein: flee the vices, pursue the virtues. The same word is used for persecution (Paul "pursued" the church, diōkōn, Philippians 3:6) and for the active pursuit of what is good (Romans 12:13, "pursue hospitality"; Romans 14:19, "pursue what makes for peace"). Timothy is to run toward six virtues with the same energy and determination with which others pursue wealth:

(1) Dikaiosynē ("righteousness, justice, right standing, right conduct") — the comprehensive quality of being right before God and acting rightly toward others. In the LXX, tsedaqah/dikaiosynē describes the character of God (Psalm 7:17 [LXX 7:18]; 11:7 [LXX 10:7]; 145:17 [LXX 144:17]) and the required conduct of God's people (Genesis 15:6; Deuteronomy 6:25; Isaiah 1:27).

(2) Eusebeia ("godliness, piety, right reverence") — the letter's signature virtue, appearing for the sixth time (2:2; 3:16; 4:7, 8; 6:3, 5, 6, 11). The training for godliness that Paul commanded in 4:7 is now made specific: godliness is something to be pursued actively, not merely admired from a distance.

(3) Pistis ("faith, faithfulness, trust") — the foundational posture of the believer toward God. Faith in this letter is both the content believed (the faith) and the act of trusting (faith in God). Timothy is to run toward deeper faith, not merely maintain the faith he already has.

(4) Agapē ("love") — the aim of the charge stated in 1:5: "love that issues from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith." Love is not merely the starting point of the letter's argument; it is the ongoing goal that must be actively pursued.

(5) Hypomonē ("steadfastness, endurance, patient perseverance") — the quality of bearing up under pressure without breaking. The noun is composed of hypo ("under") + menein ("to remain") — remaining under the weight, holding position. In the LXX, hypomonē is associated with waiting on God: Psalm 9:18 (LXX 9:19), "the hope of the poor shall not perish forever"; Psalm 27:14 (LXX 26:14), "Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait for the LORD." In the context of the Ephesian crisis, where Timothy faces opposition from false teachers, the temptation of his youth (4:12), and the demands of managing a troubled community, hypomonē — the capacity to endure without quitting — is essential.

(6) Praypathia ("gentleness, meekness, forbearance") — the word appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of prays ("gentle, meek") + pathos ("feeling, suffering, experience"). The compound describes a gentle disposition toward suffering — the ability to endure difficulty without harshness, to absorb provocation without retaliation. In 2 Timothy 2:25, Paul instructs Timothy to correct opponents "with gentleness" (en praytēti). The quality is not weakness but strength held in check — the power to strike back that chooses instead to bear.

The six virtues together compose a comprehensive portrait of the character Timothy must cultivate: right conduct, right worship, right trust, right love, right endurance, and right temperament. They stand as the direct opposite of the false teachers' character: where the false teachers are puffed up (6:4), Timothy must be gentle; where they are sick with controversy (6:4), he must pursue peace; where they love money (6:5, 10), he must pursue righteousness; where they have wandered from the faith (6:10), he must pursue faith.

"Fight the good fight of the faith" (agōnizou ton kalon agōna tēs pisteōs). The verb agōnizesthai ("to contend, to fight, to struggle, to compete") — the same verb used in 4:10 ("we toil and strive") — is an athletic and military metaphor. The noun agōn ("contest, fight, struggle, competition") is the arena in which the struggle takes place. The adjective kalon ("good, noble, beautiful") — the same word used for the "noble task" (kalon ergon) of the overseer in 3:1 and the "good warfare" (kalēn strateian) of 1:18 — marks the fight as worthy and honorable. The genitive tēs pisteōs ("of the faith") identifies the domain: this is the fight that belongs to the faith, the contest for the faith, the struggle to preserve and advance the faith against those who corrupt it. The command closes the ring with 1:18, where Paul charged Timothy to "wage the good warfare" (strateue... tēn kalēn strateian). The letter began with warfare and ends with fighting.

"Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called" (epilabou tēs aiōniou zōēs, eis hēn eklēthēs). The verb epilambanesthai ("to take hold of, to seize, to grasp, to lay hold of") is a strong word — it describes reaching out and gripping something firmly. The same verb appears in Hebrews 2:16: Christ "takes hold of" (epilambanetai) the offspring of Abraham. The aorist imperative (epilabou) commands a decisive action: seize it, grip it, do not let it slip from your hands.

The object is tēs aiōniou zōēs ("the eternal life"). In 1:16, Paul described believers as "those who were to believe in him for eternal life" (eis zōēn aiōnion). Now Timothy is told to take hold of this same eternal life — not to earn it but to grasp it, to lay hold of what has been offered to him. The eternal life is not merely future; it is a present reality that must be actively seized and held.

The relative clause eis hēn eklēthēs ("to which you were called") identifies a divine initiative: Timothy did not discover eternal life on his own; he was called (eklēthēs, aorist passive of kalein, "to call") — summoned by God, invited into this life by divine action. The calling precedes the grasping: God called, and Timothy must now take hold of what God called him to.

"And about which you made the good confession in the presence of many witnesses" (kai hōmologēsas tēn kalēn homologian enōpion pollōn martyrōn). The verb homologein ("to confess, to profess, to acknowledge publicly, to declare") describes a public declaration — the making of a formal statement before witnesses. The noun homologia ("confession, profession, public declaration") is the content of what was declared. The adjective kalēn ("good, noble, beautiful") characterizes the confession as worthy. The phrase enōpion pollōn martyrōn ("in the presence of many witnesses") establishes the public and binding nature of the event: this was not a private sentiment but a formal declaration witnessed by many.

The occasion of Timothy's confession is not specified in the text. The most natural candidates are his baptism (when new believers publicly confessed their faith) or his ordination (when the council of elders laid hands on him, 4:14, and prophecies were spoken over him, 1:18). In either case, Timothy made a public, witnessed declaration of his faith — and that declaration constitutes a commitment that he is now charged to honor by fighting the fight and taking hold of the life to which he was called.

Verses 13-16: The Solemn Charge Before God — Keep the Commandment Until the Appearing

"I charge you in the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession, to keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ, which he will display at the proper time — he who is the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone has immortality, who dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see. To him be honor and eternal dominion. Amen."

"I charge you in the presence of God, who gives life to all things" (parangellō soi enōpion tou theou tou zōogonountos ta panta). The verb parangellein ("to charge, to command, to give orders") — the same military and official language from 1:3, 1:18, 4:11, 5:7 — delivers the letter's final and most solemn charge. The preposition enōpion ("in the presence of, before the face of") places the charge under divine observation: God is the witness to this command. The participle zōogonountos (from zōogonein, "to give life, to make alive, to preserve alive") with ta panta ("all things") identifies God as the one who gives life to everything. The verb zōogonein in the LXX carries the sense of preserving life: in Exodus 1:17-18, the midwives "let the male children live" (ezōogonoun, they preserved them alive) against Pharaoh's command. In 1 Samuel 2:6, "The LORD kills and brings to life (zōopoiei)." The designation grounds the charge in God's power over life itself: the God before whom Timothy stands is the God who holds life and death in his hands. The charge is not merely apostolic but divine.

"And of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession" (kai Christou Iēsou tou martyrēsantos epi Pontiou Pilatou tēn kalēn homologian). Christ Jesus is the second witness to the charge. The participle martyrēsantos (from martyrein, "to bear witness, to testify") describes Jesus' act of witnessing before (epi, "before, in the time of, in the presence of") Pontius Pilate. The noun homologia ("confession") and the adjective kalēn ("good, noble") are the same words used for Timothy's confession in verse 12. The parallel is deliberate and structurally important: Timothy made the good confession before many witnesses (v. 12); Jesus made the good confession before Pontius Pilate (v. 13). Timothy is now charged to keep the commandment in light of both confessions — his own and Jesus'.

What was Jesus' "good confession" before Pilate? The Gospel accounts record Jesus affirming his identity when questioned by Pilate. In John 18:36-37, Jesus declares: "My kingdom is not of this world... For this purpose I was born and for this purpose I have come into the world — to bear witness to the truth." In Mark 15:2 (and parallels), when Pilate asks "Are you the King of the Jews?" Jesus responds "You have said so" (sy legeis). The confession before Pilate was Jesus' acknowledgment of who he was and what he came to do, spoken at the cost of his life. The good confession is a confession that costs something — Jesus confessed before Pilate and was executed; Timothy confessed before many witnesses and is now charged to live in accordance with that confession. The parallel implies that Timothy's faithfulness may also cost him something.

"To keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach" (tērēsai se tēn entolēn aspilon anepilēmpton). The verb tērein ("to keep, to guard, to observe, to maintain") commands careful preservation. The noun entolē ("commandment, instruction, order") — what commandment? In context, "the commandment" most likely refers to the entire body of instruction Paul has given Timothy throughout the letter: the charge to combat false teaching, to maintain sound doctrine, to manage the household of God, to watch his life and teaching, to pursue righteousness and godliness. The singular "commandment" gathers all these instructions into one unified mandate.

Two adjectives qualify how the commandment must be kept:

(1) Aspilon ("unstained, spotless, without blemish") — from the alpha-privative + spilos ("stain, spot, blemish"). The word describes something free from contamination, like a garment without a stain. In James 1:27, "pure and undefiled religion" includes keeping oneself "unstained (aspilon) from the world." In 2 Peter 3:14, believers are urged to "be found by him without spot (aspiloi) or blemish." The commandment must be kept pure — not diluted by compromise with false teaching, not contaminated by the love of money, not stained by partiality or prejudice.

(2) Anepilēmpton ("free from reproach, beyond accusation, not able to be seized upon") — the same word used for the overseer's required character in 3:2 ("above reproach") and for the families and widows in 5:7 ("without reproach"). Timothy must keep the commandment in a way that gives no opening for legitimate criticism.

"Until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (mechri tēs epiphaneias tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou). The preposition mechri ("until, up to") establishes a temporal horizon: Timothy's fidelity must extend from the present moment to the appearing. The noun epiphaneia ("appearing, manifestation, advent") derives from epiphainein ("to show forth, to appear, to manifest"). In the LXX, epiphaneia describes God's dramatic, visible interventions in history — his appearances for judgment and deliverance. 2 Samuel 7:23 (LXX 2 Kingdoms 7:23): God acted with "greatness and epiphaneia" for Israel. 2 Maccabees 2:21: God's "manifestations" (epiphaneias) on behalf of Israel. 2 Maccabees 3:24: "the Sovereign of spirits and of all authority made a great manifestation (epiphaneian megalēn)." The word carries the connotation of dramatic, visible, unmistakable divine action — not a quiet arrival but a manifestation that cannot be missed or denied. In the Pastoral Epistles, epiphaneia is the standard term for both Christ's first and second appearing: 2 Timothy 1:10, the grace "now made manifest through the appearing (epiphaneias) of our Savior Christ Jesus"; Titus 2:13, "the appearing (epiphaneian) of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ." Here it refers to the future appearing — the return of Christ that will conclude the present age and bring all things to judgment and completion.

The charge to keep the commandment "until the appearing" defines the duration of Timothy's obligation: it extends to the end. There is no point at which he can stop, no moment when the commandment expires. Faithfulness is required for the entire distance, from now until Christ appears.

"Which he will display at the proper time" (hēn kairois idiois deixei). The verb deiknynai ("to show, to display, to reveal") places the initiative with God: God will display the appearing at the time he determines. The phrase kairois idiois ("at the proper times, at his own appointed times") echoes 2:6, where Christ's self-giving was "the testimony given at the proper time" (kairois idiois). God has appointed times for his actions, and the appearing of Christ will occur at the time God has set, not at a time humans choose or predict. The community must be faithful until then, without knowing when "then" will be.

"He who is the blessed and only Sovereign" (ho makarios kai monos dynastēs). The pronoun ho ("he who") introduces a doxological description of God. The adjective makarios ("blessed, happy, fortunate") appeared in 1:11 for "the blessed God" (tou makariou theou). Here it returns as the opening attribute of the final doxology, creating a frame with the first chapter. The adjective monos ("only, sole, alone") emphasizes exclusivity — no other being shares this status. The noun dynastēs ("sovereign, ruler, potentate, one who holds power") appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Luke 1:52 ("he has brought down the mighty [dynastas] from their thrones") and Acts 8:27 (the Ethiopian "court official" [dynastēs] of the queen). The word denotes absolute political power — the ruler who holds authority by inherent right, not by delegation or election.

"The King of kings and Lord of lords" (ho basileus tōn basileuontōn kai kyrios tōn kyrieuontōn). The double title uses participial forms: basileuontōn (from basileuein, "to reign, to rule as king") and kyrieuontōn (from kyrieuein, "to exercise lordship, to be lord of"). The construction "King of those who reign" and "Lord of those who exercise lordship" places God above every earthly ruler and every power that claims authority. The title has direct precedent in the Hebrew scriptures: Deuteronomy 10:17, "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords (kyrios tōn kyriōn), the great, the mighty, and the awesome God." Daniel 2:47, "Your God is God of gods and Lord of kings (kyrios tōn basileōn)." Psalm 136:3 (LXX 135:3), "Give thanks to the Lord of lords (tō kyriō tōn kyriōn)." The title will reappear in Revelation 17:14 and 19:16, applied to Christ: "King of kings and Lord of lords." Here in 1 Timothy it is applied to God the Father. In a letter written to a community in the Roman Empire, where the emperor claimed supreme authority, the title is a direct theological assertion: the true King of kings is not Caesar but God. Every human sovereign rules under the authority of the one Sovereign who alone holds ultimate power.

"Who alone has immortality" (ho monos echōn athanasian). The adjective monos ("alone, only") restricts this attribute: immortality is not a shared quality but belongs exclusively to God. The noun athanasia ("immortality, deathlessness") is composed of the alpha-privative + thanatos ("death") — freedom from death, the inability to die. In the LXX, Wisdom of Solomon 3:4 uses the word: "their hope is full of immortality (athanasias)." But that is a gift to the righteous; here, God alone possesses immortality by nature. All other beings who live do so because God gives them life (v. 13, "who gives life to all things"). Immortality is not an inherent property of the human soul or of angels; it belongs to God alone and is given by God to those he chooses to give it to. This connects to the "eternal life" Timothy is told to take hold of (v. 12): eternal life is not self-generated but received from the one who alone possesses it.

"Who dwells in unapproachable light" (phōs oikōn aprositon). The noun phōs ("light") with the verb oikein ("to dwell, to inhabit, to live in") describes God's habitation: he lives in light. The adjective aprositon ("unapproachable, inaccessible") — from the alpha-privative + prosienai ("to come near, to approach") — means that no created being can approach this light by their own power. The image draws on the theophanic traditions of Israel: Exodus 33:20, God tells Moses, "You cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live." Exodus 24:17, "The appearance of the glory of the LORD was like a devouring fire on the top of the mountain in the sight of the people of Israel." Psalm 104:2 (LXX 103:2), God "wraps himself in light as with a garment." 1 Kings 8:12 (LXX 3 Kingdoms 8:12), "The LORD has said that he would dwell in thick darkness" — even the cloud of God's presence at the temple was a covering for a glory too intense to be seen. The "unapproachable light" describes not merely brightness but holiness — the utter otherness of God's being that makes direct access impossible for mortals.

"Whom no one has ever seen or can see" (hon eiden oudeis anthrōpōn oude idein dynatai). The verb horaō ("to see") in two forms: eiden (aorist, "has seen") and idein (infinitive, "to see"). No human being has ever seen God, and no human being has the capacity to see God. This echoes John 1:18: "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." And Colossians 1:15, where Christ is "the image of the invisible (aoratou) God." The doxology's assertion that God cannot be seen reinforces the earlier attribution in 1:17, where God was described as aoratos ("invisible"). The God who charges Timothy through Paul is a God who transcends all human perception, who cannot be captured in an image or comprehended by sight, who is known only through his self-revelation in his word and his Son.

"To him be honor and eternal dominion. Amen." (hō timē kai kratos aiōnion, amēn). The dative hō ("to him") directs the ascription. Two things are attributed: timē ("honor, value, respect, worth") — the same word used for the honor due to widows (5:3) and elders (5:17), now directed to God himself — and kratos aiōnion ("eternal dominion, everlasting might, power without end"). The noun kratos ("power, might, dominion, strength") describes active ruling force. The adjective aiōnion ("eternal, everlasting, pertaining to the age to come") removes any temporal limit: God's dominion has no expiration. The Hebrew amēn ("truly, so be it, it is established") seals the doxology as communal affirmation.

The doxology of 6:15-16 parallels and surpasses the doxology of 1:17: "To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen." The two doxologies frame the letter: the first responds to Paul's experience of mercy (1:12-16); the second accompanies the final charge to Timothy (6:13-14). Both ascribe to God the attributes of sovereignty, immortality, invisibility, and exclusivity. Together they establish the theological context of the letter: the instructions Paul gives are not human preferences but mandates from the eternal, invisible, immortal, sole Sovereign of the universe. The weight of the charge is commensurate with the weight of the God who stands behind it.

Verses 17-19: Instructions to the Rich — Right Use of Wealth

"As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share, thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is truly life."

"As for the rich in this present age" (tois plousiois en tō nyn aiōni). The dative tois plousiois ("the rich, the wealthy") identifies a group within the community. These are people who already possess wealth — unlike the "those who desire to be rich" in verse 9, who are pursuing wealth they do not yet have. Paul does not command the rich to give away everything; he instructs them on how to relate to their wealth rightly. The phrase en tō nyn aiōni ("in the present age, in this current era") places wealth in temporal context: the "present age" is contrasted with the "future" (to mellon, v. 19) and with "the appearing" (tēn epiphaneian, v. 14). Wealth belongs to the present order, which is passing. The rich are rich now; the question is what their wealth means in light of what is coming.

"Charge them not to be haughty" (parangelle mē hypsēlophronein). The verb parangellein ("to charge, to command") — Timothy is to issue an authoritative directive, not merely offer friendly advice. The verb hypsēlophronein ("to be high-minded, to think lofty thoughts about oneself, to be arrogant") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of hypsēlos ("high, lofty, exalted") + phronein ("to think, to set one's mind on"). The rich are warned against allowing their wealth to inflate their self-estimation. Wealth in the ancient world — as in every world — conferred social status, public honor, and the deference of others. The danger is that the wealthy begin to believe that their social standing reflects their actual standing before God, that having more makes them more. The charge corrects this: do not let money make you think you are above others.

"Nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches" (mēde ēlpikenai epi ploutou adēlotēti). The verb elpizein ("to hope, to set one's hope on, to trust") in the perfect infinitive (ēlpikenai) describes a settled posture of trust. The negative command is: do not set your settled trust on wealth. The reason is identified by the noun adēlotēs ("uncertainty, unreliability, instability") — wealth is adēlos ("uncertain, unseen, unclear, unreliable"). It can disappear. Fortunes are lost, economies collapse, theft occurs, catastrophes destroy. Proverbs 23:5: "When your eyes light on it, it is gone, for suddenly it sprouts wings, flying like an eagle toward heaven." Proverbs 27:24: "For riches do not last forever; and does a crown endure to all generations?" The uncertainty of wealth makes it an unstable foundation for hope. To set one's hope on something uncertain is to build on sand.

"But on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (all' epi theō tō parechonti hēmin panta plousiōs eis apolausin). The contrast is sharp: not on wealth but on God. The verb parechein ("to provide, to furnish, to present, to supply") with hēmin ("to us") identifies God as the source of provision. The adverb plousiōs ("richly, abundantly, generously") — related to plousios ("rich") — contains a wordplay: do not hope in riches but in God who richly provides. God is the truly rich one; his provision is abundant. The phrase eis apolausin ("for enjoyment, for the purpose of pleasure") is striking: God provides not merely for survival but for enjoyment. The noun apolausis ("enjoyment, pleasure, delight") appears only here and in Hebrews 11:25 in the New Testament. God's gifts — including material gifts — are given to be enjoyed, not merely endured. This is consistent with the letter's earlier affirmation that "everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (4:4). The ascetic false teachers reject God's gifts; the proper response is to receive them with gratitude and enjoy them — while not setting one's hope on them.

"They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share" (agathoergein, plousiois einai en ergois kalois, eumetadotous einai, koinōnikous). Four infinitives describe what the rich should do:

(1) Agathoergein ("to do good, to do beneficial deeds") — a compound verb appearing only here in the New Testament. The rich should use their resources to benefit others.

(2) Plousiois einai en ergois kalois ("to be rich in good works") — another wordplay: they are materially rich, but they should also be rich in a different currency — good works. The adjective plousios now modifies their moral portfolio, not their bank account.

(3) Eumetadotous einai ("to be generous, to be ready to give, to be liberal in sharing") — the adjective eumetadotos (eu, "well, easily" + metadidonai, "to share, to impart, to give a share of") appears only here in the New Testament. It describes someone who gives readily and easily, without grudging or calculating.

(4) Koinōnikous ("ready to share, willing to participate in fellowship through giving, generous") — from koinōnia ("fellowship, sharing, participation"). The adjective describes the disposition to treat one's possessions as shared resources, available for the community's benefit. In Acts 2:44-45, the early Jerusalem community "had all things in common (koina), and they were selling their possessions and belongings and distributing the proceeds to all, as any had need." The koinōnikos person embodies this spirit: what I have is available for others.

"Thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future" (apothēsaurizontas heautois themelion kalon eis to mellon). The verb apothēsaurizein ("to store up, to treasure away, to lay up") appears only here in the New Testament. It is composed of apo ("away") + thēsaurizein ("to store up treasure, to accumulate wealth"). The irony is deliberate: by giving generously, the rich are actually storing up treasure — but treasure of a different kind. The noun themelios ("foundation, base") describes what is laid at the bottom of a structure to support everything built above it. The adjective kalon ("good, noble") qualifies the foundation as sound. The phrase eis to mellon ("for the future, for what is coming") places this foundation in eschatological context: the future includes the appearing of Christ (v. 14) and the eternal life that Timothy was told to take hold of (v. 12). The wealth used generously in the present becomes the foundation on which the future is built.

The language echoes Jesus' instruction in Matthew 6:19-20: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven." And Luke 12:33: "Sell your possessions and give to the needy. Provide yourselves with moneybags that do not grow old, with a treasure in the heavens that does not fail." The connection between earthly generosity and heavenly reward is consistent across the tradition: the right use of wealth in the present secures benefit in the age to come.

"So that they may take hold of that which is truly life" (hina epilabōntai tēs ontōs zōēs). The conjunction hina ("so that") introduces the purpose. The verb epilambanesthai ("to take hold of, to seize, to grasp") is the same verb used in verse 12, where Timothy was told to "take hold of the eternal life" (epilabou tēs aiōniou zōēs). Now the same verb is applied to the rich: through generosity, they take hold of tēs ontōs zōēs — "the life that is truly life" or "that which is really life." The adverb ontōs ("truly, really, in fact") — the same word used in 5:3, 5, 16 for "truly" widows — distinguishes genuine life from what merely appears to be life. The wealthy life of accumulation and self-indulgence may look like the good life, but it is not ontōs zōē — not life in the truest sense. The life that is truly life is the eternal life that comes through generosity, godliness, and hope set on God rather than on wealth.

The instruction to the rich thus recapitulates the chapter's argument in miniature: do not hope in uncertain wealth (v. 17) but in God (v. 17); do not accumulate for self but give generously to others (v. 18); the result is a foundation for the future (v. 19) and the experience of genuine life (v. 19). The love of money leads away from the faith (v. 10); the generous use of money leads toward true life (v. 19). The difference is not between having and not having but between hoarding and sharing, between self-serving and God-trusting.

Verses 20-21: The Final Exhortation — Guard the Deposit

"O Timothy, guard the deposit entrusted to you. Avoid the irreverent babble and contradictions of what is falsely called 'knowledge,' for by professing it some have swerved from the faith. Grace be with you."

"O Timothy, guard the deposit entrusted to you" (ō Timothee, tēn parathēkēn phylaxon). The vocative ō Timothee ("O Timothy") — the personal address, using his name, conveys both intimacy and gravity. This is the father speaking to the child (1:2, 1:18), the apostle charging his delegate for the final time. The verb phylassein ("to guard, to keep watch over, to protect, to preserve") in the aorist imperative (phylaxon) commands decisive action: guard it. The noun parathēkē ("deposit, trust, something entrusted to another's keeping") is a legal and banking term. In the ancient world, a parathēkē was an item given to someone for safekeeping — money, documents, or valuables deposited with a trusted custodian. The custodian's obligation was to preserve the deposit intact and return it unchanged to its owner. The same word appears in 2 Timothy 1:12 ("I am convinced that he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me") and 2 Timothy 1:14 ("By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you").

The "deposit" is the gospel, the sound teaching, the mystery of the faith, the body of apostolic instruction that Paul has transmitted to Timothy throughout this letter and throughout their relationship. It is not Timothy's creation; it was given to him. It is not his to modify, dilute, or augment with speculation; it is his to guard and transmit faithfully. The language of deposit and guardianship runs through the entire letter: Paul was "entrusted" (episteuthēn) with the gospel (1:11); the charge was "entrusted" (paratithemai) to Timothy (1:18); Timothy must "guard" (phylaxon) the deposit (6:20). The letter began with entrustment and ends with the command to guard what was entrusted.

"Avoid the irreverent babble and contradictions of what is falsely called 'knowledge'" (tas bebēlous kenophōnias kai antitheseis tēs pseudōnymou gnōseōs). The verb ektrepesthai (implied from the participial construction, "turning away from, avoiding") commands avoidance — Timothy is to turn aside from these things, to give them no place in his attention.

The objects to avoid are described in two phrases:

(1) Bebēlous kenophōnias ("irreverent babble, profane empty speech") — the adjective bebēlos ("profane, unholy, common") appeared in 1:9 (for the "unholy and profane") and in 4:7 (for "irreverent, silly myths"). The noun kenophōnia ("empty speech, vain babble") is composed of kenos ("empty, hollow, without content") + phōnē ("voice, sound, speech"). The false teachers' speech is characterized as both profane (lacking any sacred quality) and empty (devoid of content). It sounds impressive but communicates nothing.

(2) Antitheseis tēs pseudōnymou gnōseōs ("contradictions of what is falsely called knowledge") — the noun antithesis ("contradiction, opposition, objection") refers to the opposing arguments or counter-claims that the false teachers advance against sound teaching. The adjective pseudōnymos ("falsely named, bearing a false name") is composed of pseudos ("falsehood") + onyma ("name"). The thing that bears a false name is gnōsis ("knowledge"). The false teachers claim to possess gnōsis — special knowledge, deeper understanding, advanced insight — but their knowledge is pseudōnymos: falsely named, not genuine. It calls itself knowledge but is not.

The phrase tēs pseudōnymou gnōseōs is significant. By the mid-to-late first century, various forms of speculative teaching that claimed special "knowledge" (gnōsis) about the divine realm, the origin of the world, and the nature of spiritual reality were circulating in the Mediterranean world. These teachings often involved elaborate genealogies of spiritual beings (connecting to the "genealogies" of 1:4), ascetic practices (connecting to the prohibition of marriage and food in 4:3), and claims to deeper understanding that surpassed ordinary faith. Paul's characterization of this "knowledge" as pseudōnymos — falsely named — is a direct rebuttal: what they call knowledge is not knowledge at all. It is a counterfeit, a false label on an empty container.

"For by professing it some have swerved from the faith" (hēn tines epangellomenoi peri tēn pistin ēstochēsan). The relative pronoun hēn ("which") refers to the falsely-named knowledge. The verb epangellesthai ("to profess, to promise, to declare, to claim") describes the public adoption of this knowledge — these people have declared themselves adherents of the false gnōsis. The result is stated with the verb astochein ("to miss the mark, to swerve, to deviate") — the same verb used in 1:6, where certain persons "by swerving from these have wandered away into vain discussion." The verb also appears in 2 Timothy 2:18, where Hymenaeus and Philetus "have swerved from the truth." The preposition peri with the accusative (peri tēn pistin, "concerning the faith, with regard to the faith") identifies the domain of the swerving: they have deviated concerning the faith. Their profession of the false knowledge has caused them to miss the target of the faith entirely.

This is the letter's fifth and final statement about departure from the faith:

  • 1:6: Some have "swerved" (astochēsantes) from the aim of the charge.
  • 1:19: Some have "made shipwreck" (enauagēsan) of their faith.
  • 4:1: Some will "depart" (apostēsontai) from the faith.
  • 5:15: Some have already "strayed after Satan" (exetrapēsan opisō tou Satana).
  • 6:10: Some have "wandered away" (apeplanthēsan) from the faith.
  • 6:21: Some have "swerved" (ēstochēsan) from the faith.

The repetition is relentless and deliberate. The letter opens with departure (1:6) and closes with departure (6:21). Between the first and the last, the departures accumulate: shipwreck, predicted apostasy, straying after Satan, wandering away through love of money, and swerving through false knowledge. Every chapter of the letter reports or warns about the same reality: people who were within the community of faith have left it. They were not outsiders who never believed; they were insiders who swerved, shipwrecked, departed, strayed, wandered, and deviated. The mechanisms vary — rejected conscience (1:19), deceiving spirits (4:1), love of money (6:10), false knowledge (6:21) — but the result is the same: they are no longer in the faith.

The letter exists because this is happening. Timothy is stationed in Ephesus because this is happening. Every instruction in the letter — about prayer, about conduct, about leadership qualifications, about widows, about elders, about slaves, about the rich, about Timothy's own life and teaching — is given in the context of this ongoing reality. The faith can be left. People are leaving it. Timothy must fight to prevent more departures and to guard the deposit that makes the faith possible.

"Grace be with you" (hē charis meth' hymōn). The closing benediction is plural: hymōn ("you," plural), not sou ("you," singular). Although the letter is addressed to Timothy, the closing extends to the community. The letter that will be read before the Ephesian assembly closes with a blessing on the assembly: "grace be with you all." The noun charis ("grace, favor, gracious gift") — the first word of the greeting (1:2, "Grace, mercy, and peace") — is the last substantive word of the letter. The letter begins with grace and ends with grace. Everything between — the charges, the warnings, the instructions, the doxologies, the lists of qualifications, the catalogues of vices, the reports of departure — exists within the framework of grace. The God who gives grace to sinners (1:14, "the grace of our Lord overflowed") is the same God who gives grace to the community for the task of faithfulness. Grace is both the gift that saves and the power that sustains.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1 Timothy 6 is the letter's final chapter, and it functions simultaneously as a conclusion to the practical instructions, a return to the false teaching crisis, and a final charge to Timothy that closes the ring opened in chapter 1.

(1) Verses 1-2a complete the household survey. The last group to receive instruction is slaves and their masters. The governing concern is the same as throughout the letter: conduct that honors God's name and does not bring reproach on the teaching. Slaves with believing masters are not to use the brotherhood as an excuse for disrespect but should serve even better, since the beneficiaries of their service are beloved brothers in the faith.

(2) Verses 2b-5 return explicitly to the false teaching crisis. The verb heterodidaskalein ("to teach different doctrine") from 1:3 returns in 6:3, framing the entire body of the letter between these two occurrences. The false teacher is diagnosed as puffed up, understanding nothing, sick with controversy, productive of envy, strife, slander, suspicion, and friction, corrupted in mind, deprived of truth, and treating godliness as a business. The portrait recapitulates and intensifies the diagnosis of chapter 1.

(3) Verses 6-8 present the true alternative: godliness with contentment. The false teachers treat godliness as a means of financial gain; Paul redefines gain as the combination of eusebeia and autarkeia. The argument is grounded in the boundaries of human existence: we arrive with nothing, we leave with nothing, and therefore food and covering are sufficient for contentment. The teaching counters the acquisitive orientation of the false teachers with the sufficiency orientation of the gospel.

(4) Verses 9-10 expose the love of money as a mechanism of departure from the faith. Those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, snare, and destructive desires that plunge them into ruin. The love of money is a root of all kinds of evils, and through it some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs. This section provides the fourth explicit report of departure from the faith in the letter, identifying the specific mechanism: the craving for wealth.

(5) Verses 11-12 charge Timothy to flee the dangers and pursue the virtues. The title "man of God" places Timothy in the prophetic succession. The command structure is fourfold: flee the evils, pursue the virtues (righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness), fight the good fight of the faith, and take hold of the eternal life to which he was called. The reference to Timothy's "good confession in the presence of many witnesses" links his personal history to his present obligation.

(6) Verses 13-16 deliver the letter's most solemn charge, witnessed by God and Christ Jesus. The charge is to keep the commandment unstained and above reproach until the appearing of Christ. Christ's own "good confession" before Pontius Pilate provides the model for Timothy's faithfulness. The doxology that follows (vv. 15-16) ascribes to God the attributes of sole sovereignty, supreme kingship, exclusive immortality, unapproachable light, and invisibility — paralleling and surpassing the doxology of 1:17. The two doxologies frame the letter, establishing the theological gravity of its contents.

(7) Verses 17-19 instruct the rich. Unlike the "those who desire to be rich" (v. 9), who are warned of ruin, the rich who already possess wealth are given positive instructions: do not be arrogant, do not trust in uncertain wealth but in God, do good, be rich in good works, be generous and ready to share. The result is a good foundation for the future and the experience of "that which is truly life." The passage redeems wealth without endorsing the love of money: wealth is dangerous as an object of hope but useful as a tool for generosity.

(8) Verses 20-21 close the letter with the command to guard the deposit and a final warning. Timothy is to protect the entrusted teaching from the profane babble and false knowledge of the false teachers. Some who have professed this false knowledge have "swerved from the faith" — the letter's final report of departure, using the same verb (astochein) that appeared in the letter's opening (1:6). The inclusio is complete: the letter began with swerving from the faith and ends with swerving from the faith. Grace is the final word.

(9) The chapter's relationship to the letter's controlling themes is comprehensive. The false teaching that chapter 1 introduced in general terms (myths, genealogies, speculation) and chapter 4 specified (prohibition of marriage, food abstinence) is now given its economic dimension (treating godliness as gain, 6:5) and its intellectual pretension (falsely called "knowledge," 6:20). The love of money that was prohibited for overseers (3:3) and that the false teachers exemplify (6:5) is now identified as a root of all kinds of evils and a mechanism of apostasy (6:10). The godliness (eusebeia) that was the letter's signature virtue from 2:2 through 6:11 reaches its final definition here: godliness with contentment is great gain (6:6), and the pursuit of godliness is part of the man of God's essential calling (6:11). The faith that can be shipwrecked (1:19), departed from (4:1), and wandered from (6:10) is the same faith whose fight must be fought (6:12) and whose deposit must be guarded (6:20). And the eternal life that was the goal of believing in Christ (1:16) is now something Timothy must actively take hold of (6:12) and that the rich may grasp through generosity (6:19).

(10) The chapter sustains the letter's consistent presentation of faith as a reality that can be lost. The departures reported and warned about in chapter 6 — wandering from the faith through love of money (6:10), swerving from the faith through false knowledge (6:21) — are not theoretical possibilities but observed events. The tense is past: apeplanthēsan ("they have wandered away"), ēstochēsan ("they have swerved"). The letter does not treat faith as an irrevocable possession. It treats faith as a deposit to be guarded (6:20), a fight to be fought (6:12), a life to be seized and held (6:12), and a path from which one can deviate (6:10, 21). Timothy's entire mission — the reason Paul left him in Ephesus, the reason this letter was written, the reason every instruction was given — presupposes that the community's faith is genuinely at risk. If departure were impossible, the letter would be unnecessary. The letter exists because departure is real, because it has already happened to named and unnamed individuals, and because Timothy must do everything in his power to prevent more of it while guarding the deposit of sound teaching for the next generation.
