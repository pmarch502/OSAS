Luke 22 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 21): Luke 21 opens with the widow who gives her two small coins — "all the livelihood she had" — contrasted with the wealthy who give out of their surplus (21:1-4). Jesus then predicts the destruction of the temple: "the days will come in which there will not be left one stone upon another which will not be torn down" (21:6). The disciples ask when these things will happen, and Jesus responds with a discourse covering false messiahs, wars, persecution, the encirclement and destruction of Jerusalem ("when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then recognize that her desolation is near," 21:20), signs in the heavens, the coming of the Son of Man in a cloud with power and great glory (21:27), and the parable of the fig tree as a sign that "the kingdom of God is near" (21:31). He warns that "this generation will not pass away until all things take place" (21:32). The chapter closes with an instruction to watchfulness and a summary: "Now during the day He was teaching in the temple, but at evening He would go out and spend the night on the mount that is called Olivet. And all the people would get up early in the morning to come to Him in the temple to listen to Him" (21:37-38). This summary establishes the pattern of daily temple teaching and nightly withdrawal to the Mount of Olives — the very pattern that the conspiracy of chapter 22 will exploit.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 23): Luke 23 opens with the entire assembly rising to bring Jesus before Pilate, where the charges are explicitly political: "We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding to pay taxes to Caesar, and saying that He Himself is Christ, a King" (23:2). Pilate finds no guilt, sends Jesus to Herod Antipas, who mocks him and sends him back. Pilate again declares Jesus innocent but yields to the crowd's demand for crucifixion. On the way to the cross, Jesus warns the weeping women of Jerusalem about what is coming upon them and their children (continuing the 19:41-44 and 21:20-24 themes). At the cross, one criminal reviles Jesus while the other asks to be remembered "when You come in Your kingdom" (23:42) — and Jesus responds: "Today you shall be with Me in Paradise" (23:43), a "today" (semeron) echo of 2:11, 4:21, and 19:9. Jesus dies, the temple curtain is torn, and a centurion declares "Certainly this man was innocent" (23:47). Joseph of Arimathea, a council member who "had not consented to their plan and action" (23:51), buries the body.

How chapter 22 connects these: Chapter 21 ended with Jesus controlling the temple — teaching daily, the people flocking to him each morning, the authorities unable to act. Chapter 23 opens with Jesus bound and delivered to Pilate, the authorities having prevailed. Chapter 22 is the hinge between these two positions: it traces every mechanism by which the transfer occurs. The conspiracy finds its instrument through Satan entering Judas (the return of the adversary from 4:13). The Passover meal reinterprets Israel's founding deliverance around Jesus's own impending death, establishing a new covenant. The prediction of Peter's failure and the two swords passage prepare the reader for the collapse of the disciple community. The Mount of Olives prayer — at the very location of Jesus's nightly withdrawal (21:37) — becomes the site of his arrest. Peter's denial fulfills the prediction. The council hearing produces the formal charges that chapter 23 will carry to Pilate. Every transition in this chapter moves from the space where Jesus exercised authority (temple, table, garden) to the space where his enemies hold power (courtyard, council chamber). And the author frames this entire reversal not as political accident but as the convergence of divine necessity ("it has been determined," "this which is written must be fulfilled in Me") and satanic agency ("Satan entered into Judas," "the power of darkness").

Verses 1-6: The Conspiracy and the Return of Satan

"Now the Feast of Unleavened Bread, which is called the Passover, was approaching. The chief priests and the scribes were seeking how they might put Him to death; for they were afraid of the people" (22:1-2).

The author opens by anchoring the narrative in the festival calendar. The Feast of Unleavened Bread (he heorte ton azumon) and Passover (to pascha) are identified together. Technically, Passover was the evening meal of Nisan 14 and Unleavened Bread was the seven-day festival beginning Nisan 15 (Leviticus 23:5-6), but by the first century the terms were used interchangeably for the entire festival period (Josephus, Antiquities 14.21; War 2.10). The Passover commemorated the Exodus — YHWH's deliverance of Israel from slavery in Egypt through the blood of the lamb smeared on the doorposts (Exodus 12). Every element of this chapter will be shaped by this Passover context: the lamb, the blood, the covenant, the deliverance, and the judgment that falls on those who are not covered.

The chief priests and scribes have been seeking to destroy Jesus since 19:47: "the chief priests and the scribes and the leading men among the people were trying to destroy Him." Their inability to act has been a function of popular support: "all the people were hanging on His words" (19:48). Now they need a method that circumvents the crowd. The verb ezetoun ("were seeking") is imperfect tense — ongoing, continuous searching. They have the desire but lack the opportunity.

"And Satan entered into Judas who was called Iscariot, belonging to the number of the twelve" (22:3).

This is the single most important structural marker in the chapter. In Luke 4:13, after the wilderness testing, the author wrote: "When the devil had finished every temptation, he left Him until an opportune time" (achri kairou). That phrase — "until an opportune time" — has hung over the entire narrative since chapter 4. Through the Galilean ministry, through the journey to Jerusalem, through the temple confrontations, Satan has been absent from the narrative. Now he returns. The kairos has arrived.

The author does not say that Satan tempted Judas or influenced Judas. He says Satan "entered into" (eiselthen eis) Judas. The language is that of invasion and occupation, the same language used for demons entering people throughout the Gospel (8:30, 33; 11:26). Judas is identified by two markers: his surname Iscariot and his membership "in the number of the twelve" (ek tou arithmou ton dodeka). The phrase is precise and devastating. This is not an outsider. This is one of the inner circle, specifically numbered among the twelve whom Jesus chose (6:13-16, where "Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor" appears). The author introduced him as a traitor at the moment of selection; now the narrative fulfills that designation.

"And he went away and discussed with the chief priests and officers how he might betray Him to them. They were glad and agreed to give him money. So he consented, and began seeking a good opportunity to betray Him to them apart from the crowd" (22:4-6).

The "officers" (strategois) are the temple police commanders — the strategoi tou hierou who appear in Acts 4:1 and 5:24. Judas provides what the authorities lacked: a way to arrest Jesus "apart from the crowd" (ater ochlou). The verb exomologesan means he "consented, agreed fully." The word eukairian ("good opportunity") echoes the kairos language from 4:13 — Satan waited for the opportune time, and now Judas seeks the opportune moment. The authorities' gladness (echarēsan) is a bitter parody of the joy (chara) that properly accompanies encounters with Jesus in this Gospel (1:14, 2:10, 10:17, 15:7, 19:6).

Verses 7-13: Preparation of the Passover

"Then came the first day of Unleavened Bread on which the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed. And Jesus sent Peter and John, saying, 'Go and prepare the Passover for us, so that we may eat it'" (22:7-8).

The verb edei ("had to," "it was necessary") is the Lukan term for divine necessity — the same word used throughout the Gospel for what must happen according to God's purposes (2:49, 4:43, 9:22, 13:33, 17:25, 24:7, 26, 44). Here it applies to the Passover lamb: the lamb had to be sacrificed. Within the narrative frame that the author is constructing — where Jesus is about to reinterpret the Passover around his own body and blood — the divine necessity of the lamb's sacrifice points beyond the ritual to what the author understands it to foreshadow.

Jesus sends specifically Peter and John. These two appear together repeatedly as the core of the inner circle: they were present at the raising of Jairus's daughter (8:51), the transfiguration (9:28), and will appear together again in Acts (3:1-11, 4:13, 8:14). Their role in preparing the Passover gives them a specific association with the meal that is about to be reinterpreted.

"And He said to them, 'When you have entered the city, a man will meet you carrying a pitcher of water; follow him into the house that he enters. And you shall say to the owner of the house, "The Teacher says to you, 'Where is the guest room in which I may eat the Passover with My disciples?'"' And he will show you a large, furnished upper room; prepare it there.' And they left and found everything just as He had told them; and they prepared the Passover" (22:10-13).

The arrangement parallels the colt instructions of 19:29-34 precisely: Jesus gives specific advance instructions, provides a verbal signal, and the disciples find everything exactly as he said. The pattern demonstrates Jesus's sovereign foreknowledge and orchestration even as the conspiracy closes around him. In 19:30-34, Jesus arranged his entry as king; here he arranges the meal at which he will interpret his death. The man carrying a water pitcher would be distinctive in a culture where water-carrying was typically women's work. Whether this reflects a prearranged signal or prophetic knowledge, the author's point is the same: Jesus is not being overtaken by events. He is directing them.

The word katalyma ("guest room") is the same word used in 2:7, where there was "no room in the katalyma" at Jesus's birth. At the beginning of the narrative, there was no space for him; now, at the approach of his death, a large furnished upper room (anagaion mega estromenon) is ready.

Verses 14-20: The Passover Meal Reinterpreted

"When the hour had come, He reclined at the table, and the apostles with Him" (22:14).

"The hour" (he hora) echoes the language of appointed time that has been building through the Gospel. In 12:12, "the Holy Spirit will teach you in that very hour what you ought to say." In 12:40, "the Son of Man is coming at an hour that you do not expect." And in 22:53, Jesus will identify the moment of arrest as "your hour" (he hora hymon). This is the appointed hour. The word "apostles" (apostoloi) rather than "disciples" (mathetai) is deliberate; it emphasizes their commissioned status — these are not merely followers but those sent with authority (6:13, 9:10).

"And He said to them, 'I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer; for I say to you, I shall never again eat it until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God'" (22:15-16).

The construction epithymia epethymesa ("with desire I have desired," i.e., "I have earnestly desired") is a Hebraism — an intensifying construction that uses the cognate noun with the verb (as in Genesis 31:30 LXX, epithymia epethymesas). It conveys the depth of Jesus's longing. This is not a routine observance. He identifies it as the final Passover he will eat before suffering (pro tou me pathein). The word pathein ("to suffer") connects to every passion prediction in the Gospel: the Son of Man "must suffer many things" (9:22), "must suffer many things" (17:25).

The phrase "until it is fulfilled (plerothe) in the kingdom of God" is significant. The Passover is not simply repeated each year; Jesus claims it awaits a fulfillment. The original Passover commemorated deliverance from Egypt. Jesus positions his coming suffering as the event in which what the Passover pointed toward will be completed. The kingdom of God is the context of that completion.

"And when He had taken a cup and given thanks, He said, 'Take this and share it among yourselves; for I say to you, I will not drink of the fruit of the vine from now on until the kingdom of God comes'" (22:17-18).

Luke's account has two cups — this one before the bread (vv. 17-18) and another after the meal (v. 20). The first cup carries a vow of abstinence: Jesus will not drink wine again until the kingdom of God comes. This is an oath-form familiar from Jewish practice — a self-imposed restriction that signals the gravity of the moment and the expectation of a decisive future event. The phrase "the fruit of the vine" (to genema tes ampelou) is the standard blessing formula used at Passover.

"And when He had taken some bread and given thanks, He broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is My body which is given for you; do this in remembrance of Me.' And in the same way He took the cup after they had eaten, saying, 'This cup which is poured out for you is the new covenant in My blood'" (22:19-20).

The bread and cup words constitute the center of the chapter's theological argument. Several elements require attention.

First, the bread. "This is My body which is given for you" (touto estin to soma mou to hyper hymon didomenon). The present participle didomenon ("being given") indicates an ongoing act, not a completed one. Jesus is in the process of giving his body. The phrase hyper hymon ("for you," "on your behalf") identifies the beneficiaries: the twelve reclining with him.

Second, the instruction "do this in remembrance of Me" (touto poieite eis ten emen anamnesin). The word anamnesis ("remembrance") is significant in the LXX context of Passover. The Passover itself was a commanded remembrance (Exodus 12:14: "this day will be a memorial [LXX: mnemosynon] to you, and you shall celebrate it as a feast to the LORD"). Jesus now redirects the act of remembrance from the Exodus event to himself. What Israel was commanded to remember about YHWH's deliverance at the original Passover, the apostles are now told to remember about Jesus.

Third, the cup. "This cup which is poured out for you is the new covenant in My blood" (touto to poterion he kaine diatheke en to haimati mou to hyper hymon ekchynnomenon). Two texts converge here. The first is Exodus 24:8, where Moses sprinkled the blood of the covenant sacrifice on the people, saying: "Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words." Blood ratified that covenant at Sinai; blood ratifies this one. The second is Jeremiah 31:31-34 (LXX: Jeremiah 38:31-34): "Behold, days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant (diatheke kaine) with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah, not like the covenant which I made with their fathers in the day I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt." Jeremiah's new covenant was promised specifically because Israel broke the Sinai covenant; it would be characterized by the law written on the heart, universal knowledge of YHWH, and the forgiveness of sin. Jesus claims that this long-promised new covenant is now being inaugurated, and that his blood — the blood about to be shed — is the means of its ratification.

The word ekchynnomenon ("being poured out") is the same word used in the LXX for the pouring out of blood in sacrifice (Leviticus 4:7, 12, 18, 25, 30, 34) and for the pouring out of wrath (Hosea 5:10, Nahum 1:6). Jesus's blood is simultaneously the covenant-ratifying sacrifice and the cup of suffering he accepts.

Verses 21-23: The Betrayer at the Table

"But behold, the hand of the one betraying Me is with Mine on the table. For indeed, the Son of Man is going as it has been determined; but woe to that man by whom He is betrayed!" (22:21-22).

The conjunction plen ("but," "nevertheless") creates a sharp contrast. Jesus has just spoken of his body given and blood poured out; now he identifies the betrayer as present at the very table where this covenant is being enacted. The phrase "his hand is with Mine on the table" (he cheir tou paradidontos me met' emou epi tes trapezes) is not merely a spatial description but an indictment: the betrayer shares the covenant meal while plotting to hand over the covenant-maker. In the Psalms, this is the ultimate betrayal: "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted up his heel against me" (Psalm 41:9, LXX 40:10).

The Son of Man goes "as it has been determined" (kata to horismenon). The verb horizō means "to fix, appoint, determine." It is used in Acts 2:23: "this Man, delivered over by the predetermined plan and foreknowledge of God." The divine determination does not exonerate the betrayer: "woe to that man by whom He is betrayed" (ouai to anthropo ekeino di' hou paradidotai). Divine necessity and human moral responsibility coexist without the author attempting to resolve the tension. The Son of Man's path is determined; the betrayer's choice is still his own, and the consequence is woe.

"And they began to discuss among themselves which one of them it might be who was going to do this thing" (22:23).

The verb syzētein ("to discuss, to debate") indicates active argument. Notably, no one knows who the betrayer is — not even the inner circle can identify him. The secrecy of Judas's arrangement (v. 6, "apart from the crowd") extends even to the table.

Verses 24-30: The Dispute about Greatness and the Covenant Grant of a Kingdom

"And there arose also a dispute among them as to which one of them was regarded to be greatest" (22:24).

The word philoneikia ("dispute, contention" — literally "love of striving") appears only here in the New Testament. The placement is devastating. Jesus has just offered his body and blood, identified his betrayer, spoken of divine determination and woe — and the apostles' response is to argue about their own ranking. The word kai ("also") links this dispute to the discussion of the betrayer: from "which of us would betray?" they slide into "which of us is greatest?" Both questions center on self.

"And He said to them, 'The kings of the Gentiles lord it over them; and those who have authority over them are called "Benefactors." But it is not this way with you, but the one who is the greatest among you must become like the youngest, and the leader like the servant'" (22:25-26).

Jesus identifies two features of Gentile power structures. First, they "lord it over" their subjects (kyrieuousin) — the exercise of dominion as domination. Second, those in power receive the honorific title "Benefactor" (Euergetai). The title Euergetes was a formal Hellenistic royal designation; Ptolemy III of Egypt (246-222 BCE) and Ptolemy VIII (170-116 BCE) both bore the title "Euergetes." First-century rulers and wealthy patrons used the patronage system to acquire honor through benefaction — giving in order to receive public praise and social power. Jesus rejects both: the domination model and the benefaction-as-self-aggrandizement model.

The alternative: "the greatest among you must become like the youngest" (ho meizōn ... ginomai hōs ho neōteros). In first-century culture, the youngest (neoteros) had the lowest status in the household — the newest, least experienced, last in precedence. "The leader like the servant" (ho hegoumenos hōs ho diakonōn) uses the verb diakonō, which denotes table service — waiting on others at meals.

"For who is greater, the one who reclines at the table or the one who serves? Is it not the one who reclines at the table? But I am among you as the one who serves" (22:27).

The rhetorical question has an obvious social answer: the one reclining is greater. But Jesus identifies himself as the one who serves (ho diakonōn). He has just enacted this: he is the host of the meal, yet he is giving his body and pouring out his blood for them. The one reclining at the table is the recipient; the one serving is Jesus. The Passover meal they are eating is the concrete demonstration of the principle he is teaching.

"You are those who have stood by Me in My trials; and just as My Father has granted Me a kingdom, I grant you that you may eat and drink at My table in My kingdom, and you will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (22:28-30).

Three elements converge. First, Jesus acknowledges the apostles' faithfulness: "you have stood by Me in My trials" (en tois peirasmois mou). The word peirasmois ("trials, tests, temptations") is the same root as peirasmos ("temptation") that will appear in vv. 40 and 46, and the same root as the devil's testing in chapter 4. The apostles have endured with Jesus through the period of his public testing — not perfectly (the dispute they are currently having demonstrates that), but they have remained. They did not leave when the Pharisees opposed him, when the crowds turned away, when the journey to Jerusalem became dangerous.

Second, the verb diatithemai ("I grant, I covenant") appears twice: "My Father has granted Me a kingdom" and "I grant you." This verb is the cognate of diatheke ("covenant"), the word Jesus used for the cup in verse 20. The kingdom is not simply given; it is covenantally bestowed. The Father covenants a kingdom to Jesus; Jesus covenants a share in that kingdom to those who have stood by him. The chain of covenant grants runs from the Father through the Son to the apostles.

Third, the content of the grant: eating and drinking at Jesus's table in his kingdom, and sitting on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel. The thrones and the judging of the twelve tribes echo Daniel 7:9-14, 22, 27, where "the court sat" and "judgment was given to the saints of the Most High." The twelve apostles on twelve thrones governing twelve tribes represents the reconstitution of Israel under new leadership — a restoration theme that runs throughout Luke-Acts.

Verses 31-34: Satan's Demand and Peter's Coming Failure

"Simon, Simon, behold, Satan has demanded permission to sift you like wheat; but I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned again, strengthen your brothers" (22:31-32).

The double address "Simon, Simon" parallels other moments of intense divine address in Israel's tradition: "Abraham, Abraham" (Genesis 22:11), "Moses, Moses" (Exodus 3:4), "Samuel, Samuel" (1 Samuel 3:10). The use of the birth name Simon rather than the given name Peter may be significant — Jesus addresses the man as he was before his calling.

The pronoun shift is critical and must not be missed. "Satan has demanded permission to sift you (hymas, plural) like wheat" — all of the apostles are targeted. The demand (exetesato) uses a verb that implies a formal request for permission, suggesting a scene analogous to Job 1:6-12 and 2:1-6, where the satan requests and receives divine permission to test Job. Satan has asked to test the entire group. The image of sifting wheat (siniazo) comes from the agricultural process of shaking grain in a sieve to separate the usable kernel from the chaff. Amos 9:9 uses similar language: "I will shake the house of Israel among all nations as grain is shaken in a sieve." The sifting will be violent, and its purpose is to reveal what is genuinely wheat and what is chaff.

"But I have prayed for you (sou, singular) that your faith may not fail" — Jesus has prayed specifically for Simon, not for the group. The verb ekleipo ("fail, give out, be eclipsed") is strong: it means to fail entirely, to be utterly extinguished. Jesus does not pray that Peter will avoid falling but that Peter's faith (pistis) will not be completely destroyed by the fall. The implication is clear: Peter will fall. The sifting will happen. But the faith will survive.

"And you (sy, singular), when once you have turned again (epistrepsas), strengthen (sterison) your brothers." The verb epistrepho ("to turn, to return, to convert") is the standard word for repentance and return to God in the LXX (Deuteronomy 30:2, 1 Kings 8:33, Isaiah 6:10, Hosea 14:1-2). Jesus assumes Peter will need to turn back — that he will deviate and then return. The command is then to use the experience of failure and recovery to strengthen (sterizō — to make firm, to establish) the other apostles. Peter's restoration is not simply for his own sake; it has a communal purpose. The one who fell and returned is uniquely equipped to stabilize others who were sifted by the same trial.

"But he said to Him, 'Lord, with You I am ready to go both to prison and to death!' And He said, 'I say to you, Peter, the rooster will not crow today until you have denied three times that you know Me'" (22:33-34).

Peter's declaration is emphatic: he is ready (hetoimos) for prison and death "with You" (meta sou). He addresses Jesus as "Lord" (Kyrie). Jesus responds by switching to the name Peter — the rock-name, the identity Peter has been given — and predicts the precise manner and timing of his failure. Not prison, not death, but denial. Three times. Before the rooster crows today. Peter claims readiness for the greatest sacrifice; Jesus says he will fail at the most basic loyalty: acknowledging that he knows Jesus. The contrast between the claim and the prediction could not be more stark.

Verses 35-38: The Changed Era and the Fulfillment of Isaiah 53

"And He said to them, 'When I sent you out without money belt and bag and sandals, you did not lack anything, did you?' They said, 'No, nothing'" (22:35).

Jesus shifts from Peter to the entire group. He recalls the earlier missions: the sending of the twelve in 9:3 ("Take nothing for your journey, neither a staff, nor a bag, nor bread, nor money") and the sending of the seventy-two in 10:4 ("Carry no money belt, no bag, no shoes"). During those missions, they lacked nothing. The point is not that they were self-sufficient but that they were providentially protected and supplied. The mission conditions reflected a time of welcome and provision.

"And He said to them, 'But now, whoever has a money belt is to take it along, likewise also a bag, and whoever has no sword is to sell his coat and buy one'" (22:36).

"But now" (alla nun) marks a decisive shift. The conditions that prevailed during the earlier missions no longer apply. The era of welcome, provision, and protection is ending. The instruction about the sword has generated much interpretation, but the author provides his own explanation in the very next verse:

"For I tell you that this which is written must be fulfilled in Me, 'And He was numbered with transgressors'; for that which refers to Me has its fulfillment" (22:37).

The word dei ("must") is again the language of divine necessity. The quotation is from Isaiah 53:12 (LXX: kai en tois anomois elogisthe — "and he was reckoned among the lawless"). This is the only direct quotation of Isaiah 53 in Luke's Gospel, though the servant figure has been alluded to elsewhere (the servant language of the baptism in 3:22 echoes Isaiah 42:1). Jesus explicitly identifies himself as the subject of Isaiah 53: "that which refers to Me" (to peri emou) — literally, "the things concerning Me." The "must be fulfilled in Me" (dei telesthēnai) uses teleo, meaning "to complete, to bring to an end, to accomplish."

The logic, then, is this: Jesus is about to be counted among transgressors — classified and treated as a criminal. The instruction about swords is not a call to armed resistance (as the arrest scene will immediately demonstrate). Rather, it establishes the surface conditions under which Jesus will be "numbered with transgressors." Armed followers create the appearance of a band of outlaws. The sword instruction serves the Isaiah 53:12 prophecy: Jesus will look like a transgressor because he is surrounded by armed men.

"They said, 'Lord, look, here are two swords.' And He said to them, 'It is enough'" (22:38).

The apostles produce two swords — already present among the group, not newly acquired. Two swords would be absurdly insufficient for armed resistance against the temple police and Roman garrison. Jesus's response, hikanon estin ("it is enough"), is best read as a termination of the discussion. The disciples have taken his words with wooden literalism, missing the point about Isaiah 53. Jesus ends the conversation. The reading is confirmed by the arrest scene: when a disciple actually uses a sword (v. 50), Jesus immediately commands "Stop! No more of this" and heals the damage done (v. 51).

Verses 39-46: Prayer on the Mount of Olives

"And He came out and proceeded as was His custom to the Mount of Olives; and the disciples also followed Him" (22:39).

The phrase "as was His custom" (kata to ethos) connects to the summary of 21:37: "at evening He would go out and spend the night on the mount that is called Olivet." Jesus follows his known pattern — the very pattern that Judas is exploiting to find him "apart from the crowd" (v. 6). The Mount of Olives (to Oros ton Elaion) carries the significance established in 19:37 (the site of the royal acclamation) and the Zechariah 14:4 association with YHWH's eschatological coming. Notably, Luke does not use the name "Gethsemane." He identifies the location only as the Mount of Olives.

"When He arrived at the place, He said to them, 'Pray that you may not enter into temptation'" (22:40).

The instruction frames what follows. The word peirasmon ("temptation, testing, trial") is the same root as peirasmois in verse 28 ("you have stood by Me in My trials") and the same root as the devil's testing in 4:1-13 (peirazomenos). The sifting Satan has demanded (v. 31) is about to begin. Jesus instructs them to pray against entering into it — not that the testing would not come, but that they would not be drawn into it, would not be overcome by it. The same petition appears in the prayer Jesus taught in 11:4: "do not lead us into temptation" (me eisenegkes hemas eis peirasmon). What Jesus taught them to pray for daily is now urgently needed.

"And He withdrew from them about a stone's throw, and He knelt down and began to pray, saying, 'Father, if You are willing, remove this cup from Me; yet not My will, but Yours be done'" (22:41-42).

Jesus addresses God as "Father" (Pater), the same address used in 10:21 ("I praise You, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth") and 11:2 (the prayer model: "Father, hallowed be Your name"). The intimacy of the address stands in contrast to the agony of the request.

"This cup" (to poterion touto) recalls the cup of verses 17 and 20 — the cup of the new covenant in his blood. But the cup metaphor has deep roots in the prophetic tradition. In the LXX, the cup is a recurring image of divine judgment: the cup of YHWH's wrath (Isaiah 51:17, 22; Jeremiah 25:15-17, 28-29; Ezekiel 23:31-34; Habakkuk 2:16; Psalm 75:8). The cup Jesus asks to have removed is the cup of suffering and death — the cup he has just identified with his own blood poured out for the new covenant.

The prayer "not My will, but Yours be done" (plen me to thelema mou alla to son ginestho) echoes the Lord's Prayer of 11:2: "Your will be done" (genesthe to thelema sou — in some manuscripts). Jesus practices what he taught. The submission is not resignation but active alignment: he has a will (he does not want to drink the cup), and he subordinates it to the Father's will. The wilderness testing of chapter 4 was a test of Jesus's obedience as Son; this is the final and most severe form of the same test.

"Now an angel from heaven appeared to Him, strengthening Him. And being in agony He was praying very fervently; and His sweat became like drops of blood, falling down upon the ground" (22:43-44).

An angel appears and strengthens (enischyōn) Jesus. The precedent in Israel's history is Elijah, who was strengthened by an angel's provision during his flight to Horeb (1 Kings 19:5-8). In both cases, the servant of God is at the point of exhaustion and despair, and angelic intervention enables the continuation of the mission.

The word agonia appears only here in the New Testament. In Greek usage, it denoted the anxiety and tension of an athletic contest — the inner struggle of supreme exertion. Jesus prays ektenesteron ("more fervently, more earnestly" — a comparative adverb from the root ektenes, "stretched out, strained"). His sweat became "like" (hosei) drops of blood falling to the ground. The simile describes the density and profusion of the sweat, not necessarily literal blood. The physiological phenomenon of hematidrosis (sweat tinged with blood under extreme stress) is attested in ancient medical literature, but the author may simply be describing sweat so heavy that it fell in thick, visible drops like blood dripping from a wound.

"When He rose from prayer, He came to the disciples and found them sleeping from sorrow, and said to them, 'Why are you sleeping? Get up and pray that you may not enter into temptation'" (22:45-46).

Luke softens the disciples' failure slightly compared to the other accounts by adding the explanatory phrase "from sorrow" (apo tes lypes) — grief has overwhelmed them into sleep. But the judgment still stands: at the moment they most needed to pray against entering temptation (as Jesus explicitly told them to do in v. 40), they slept instead. Jesus repeats the identical instruction with which he began: "Pray that you may not enter into temptation." The repetition brackets the prayer scene and establishes the disciples' failure to pray as the context for their subsequent failures — Peter's denial, the group's scattering.

Verses 47-53: The Arrest and the Hour of Darkness

"While He was still speaking, behold, a crowd came, and the one called Judas, one of the twelve, was preceding them; and he approached Jesus to kiss Him. But Jesus said to him, 'Judas, are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?'" (22:47-48).

The author again emphasizes Judas's identity: "one of the twelve" (ho legomenos Ioudas heis ton dodeka). The repetition of this identification (from v. 3) drives the point: the betrayer is not an outsider. The kiss (phileo) was a customary greeting between teacher and disciple, a sign of affection and respect. To use it as the signal for arrest is to weaponize intimacy. Jesus's question — "Are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?" — does not ask for information. It confronts Judas with the meaning of his act: the sign of love becomes the instrument of betrayal, and the one betrayed is not merely a man but the Son of Man, the figure of Daniel 7:13-14 who receives dominion and glory.

"When those who were around Him saw what was going to happen, they said, 'Lord, shall we strike with the sword?' And one of them struck the slave of the high priest and cut off his right ear. But Jesus answered and said, 'Stop! No more of this.' And He touched his ear and healed him" (22:49-51).

The disciples ask whether to use the swords (the very swords of v. 38). One of them does not wait for the answer and strikes. The healing of the ear is unique to Luke. It is the final healing miracle in the Gospel, and its placement is remarkable: at the moment of his arrest, Jesus heals a wound inflicted by his own follower on a servant of his enemy. The act repudiates violence on his behalf and demonstrates the character of the king whose body is about to be given. The command "Stop! No more of this" (eate heos toutou — literally, "permit/allow up to this point") signals that this is the boundary: no further resistance.

"Then Jesus said to the chief priests and officers of the temple and elders who had come against Him, 'Have you come out with swords and clubs as you would against a robber? While I was with you daily in the temple, you did not lay hands on Me; but this hour and the power of darkness are yours'" (22:52-53).

Jesus addresses the leadership directly — chief priests, temple officers (strategoi), and elders. Their presence at an arrest operation is unusual; these are not the agents who would normally be dispatched. Jesus calls out the absurdity: they come armed against a man who was publicly available in the temple every day (the summary of 21:37). They could have seized him at any time but feared the people (v. 2, 19:48, 20:19).

The final phrase is the chapter's most sweeping theological statement: "this hour and the power of darkness are yours" (haute estin hymon he hora kai he exousia tou skotous). The hora ("hour") is the appointed time. The exousia tou skotous ("the authority/power of darkness") identifies the spiritual reality behind the arrest. The word exousia means "authority, right, power." Darkness (skotos) in Luke's usage carries the weight of the prophetic tradition: darkness as the realm opposed to God's light (1:79, "those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death"; Acts 26:18, "to turn them from darkness to light and from the authority of Satan to God"). This is Satan's hour — the return promised in 4:13, the sifting demanded in 22:31. The human agents (chief priests, officers, elders) are exercising the authority of darkness. Jesus does not resist because this hour has been determined (v. 22, kata to horismenon), even though the human agents bear responsibility for their choices within it.

Verses 54-62: Peter's Denial

"Having arrested Him, they led Him away and brought Him to the house of the high priest; but Peter was following at a distance" (22:54).

The verb ekolouthei ("was following") is the same word used throughout the Gospel for discipleship (5:11, 27-28; 9:23, 49, 57, 59, 61; 18:22, 28, 43). Peter is still following — but apo makrothen ("from a distance"). The discipleship language is intact; the proximity is not. This is a portrait of faith under extreme pressure: not yet abandoned, but no longer close.

"After they had kindled a fire in the middle of the courtyard and had sat down together, Peter was sitting among them. And a servant-girl, seeing him as he sat in the firelight and looking intently at him, said, 'This man was with Him too.' But he denied it, saying, 'Woman, I do not know Him'" (22:55-57).

The fire in the courtyard places Peter in the light — visible, exposed. The verb atenisasa ("looking intently, staring fixedly") indicates a sustained, examining gaze. The servant-girl's observation is accurate: "This man was with Him too" (houtos syn auto en). The word syn ("with") is the same preposition Peter used in his boast: "with You (meta sou) I am ready to go to prison and to death" (v. 33). Peter denies: "I do not know Him" (ouk oida auton). Hours earlier, Jesus predicted Peter would deny three times "that you know Me" (v. 34). The first denial matches the prediction exactly.

"A little later, another saw him and said, 'You are one of them too!' But Peter said, 'Man, I am not!'" (22:58).

The second challenge shifts from association with Jesus to group identity: "You are one of them" (kai sy ex auton ei). Peter denies not only knowing Jesus but belonging to the disciple community. Each denial moves further from the center.

"After about an hour had passed, another man began to insist, saying, 'Certainly this man also was with Him, for he is a Galilean too.' But Peter said, 'Man, I do not know what you are talking about'" (22:59-60a).

The third challenge comes after an hour's interval and is the most insistent (diischyrizeto — "kept insisting, affirming strongly"). The evidence is Peter's Galilean identity — his accent or manner betrays his origin, which connects him to the Galilean teacher inside. Peter's third denial is the most evasive: "I do not know what you are talking about" — he refuses even to engage with the claim.

"Immediately, while he was still speaking, a rooster crowed. The Lord turned and looked at Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord, how He had told him, 'Before a rooster crows today, you will deny Me three times.' And he went out and wept bitterly" (22:60b-62).

The fulfillment is precise: the rooster crows while Peter's third denial is still on his lips. Then comes the detail unique to Luke: "The Lord turned and looked at Peter" (ho Kyrios eneblepsen to Petro). The verb emblepo means "to look at, to gaze at" — not a passing glance but a direct, intentional look. This requires physical proximity: Jesus, being led through or held in the courtyard area, turns and meets Peter's eyes. No words. The look itself is the communication.

Peter "remembered" (hypomnesthen) the word of the Lord. The word is the same root as anamnesis ("remembrance") from verse 19, where Jesus said "do this in remembrance of Me." At the table, Peter was told to remember Jesus through the bread and cup. Now, in the courtyard, Peter remembers Jesus through the word of prediction fulfilled. Both forms of remembrance connect to the same Lord.

Peter went out (exelthon) and wept bitterly (eklausen pikros). The verb klaiō ("to weep") is the same word used of Jesus weeping over Jerusalem in 19:41. The adverb pikros ("bitterly") indicates not just sorrow but the sharp pain of self-knowledge — the recognition that Jesus's prediction was accurate and Peter's boast was hollow. This is the beginning of the epistrepsas ("turning back") that Jesus predicted in verse 32. Peter's bitter weeping is the first movement of the return.

Verses 63-65: Mocking and Beating

"Now the men who were holding Jesus in custody were mocking Him and beating Him, and they blindfolded Him and were asking Him, saying, 'Prophesy, who is the one who hit You?' And they were saying many other things against Him, blaspheming" (22:63-65).

The mocking takes a specific form: they blindfold Jesus and demand that he "prophesy" (propheteuson) by identifying who struck him. The irony, invisible to the mockers but clear to the reader, is that Jesus has just exercised prophetic knowledge with perfect accuracy: he predicted Judas's betrayal, Peter's triple denial, and the precise timing of the rooster's crow. Every prophecy in this chapter has been fulfilled within hours. The men demanding that Jesus prove his prophetic ability are themselves the evidence that his prophecy is true.

The author calls their words "blasphemy" (blasphemountes). In the LXX and first-century Jewish usage, blasphemy involved speaking against God or treating what is sacred with contempt. By labeling the guards' mockery as blasphemy, the author signals that what they are abusing is not merely a man but one who stands in a unique relationship to God — a claim the council scene is about to make explicit.

Verses 66-71: Before the Council

"When it was day, the Council of elders of the people assembled, both chief priests and scribes, and they led Him away to their council chamber" (22:66).

Luke places the formal council hearing in the morning ("when it was day," hemera egeneto), distinguishing it from the nighttime abuse. The "Council of elders" (to presbyterion tou laou) is the Sanhedrin — the supreme governing body of Jewish civic and religious life. The composition is specified: chief priests (archiereis) and scribes (grammateis). They lead Jesus into their synedrion ("council chamber"). The formal, institutional character of the proceeding is emphasized.

"'If You are the Christ, tell us.' But He said to them, 'If I tell you, you will not believe; and if I ask a question, you will not answer'" (22:67-68).

The first question concerns messianic identity: "Are you the Christ?" (ei sy ei ho Christos). The word Christos (the Greek translation of the Hebrew Mashiach, "anointed one") has appeared throughout Luke with specific content: the angel told the shepherds that "a Savior has been born to you who is Christ the Lord" (2:11); Simeon was told he would not die before seeing "the Lord's Christ" (2:26); Peter confessed Jesus as "the Christ of God" (9:20); Jesus warned the disciples to "tell this to no one" (9:21).

Jesus's response is not a denial but a statement about the impossibility of the conversation: "If I tell you, you will not believe; and if I ask a question, you will not answer." This echoes the dynamic of 20:1-8, where the chief priests and scribes asked Jesus by what authority he acted, and Jesus replied with a question about John's baptism that they refused to answer ("If we say, 'From heaven,' He will say, 'Why did you not believe him?' But if we say, 'From men,' all the people will stone us"). The pattern has been established: the authorities will not engage honestly. Any answer Jesus gives will be used as evidence, not received as truth.

"But from now on the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God" (22:69).

Instead of answering their question on their terms, Jesus makes a declaration. "From now on" (apo tou nun) is a distinctly Lukan phrase that marks decisive turning points (1:48, 5:10, 12:52). What Jesus declares is a composite of two texts. "The Son of Man" comes from Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a Son of Man" comes on the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and receives "dominion, glory, and a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations, and men of every language might serve Him." "Seated at the right hand" comes from Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1): "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet.'" This is the same psalm Jesus cited in 20:42-43 to demonstrate that the Messiah is not merely David's son but David's Lord.

The combination of Daniel 7 and Psalm 110 asserts two things simultaneously: the Son of Man receives universal authority (Daniel 7), and he occupies the position of supreme honor and power at God's right hand (Psalm 110). The phrase "the power of God" (tes dynameōs tou theou) is a reverential circumlocution for God himself. And the temporal marker "from now on" is startling: Jesus claims that this enthronement is not a distant future event but begins now — from this moment, from the point of his apparent defeat before the council.

"And they all said, 'Are You the Son of God, then?' And He said to them, 'Yes, I am'" (22:70).

The council draws the implication: if the Son of Man sits at God's right hand, sharing in divine power, then he is claiming to be "the Son of God" (ho huios tou theou). Jesus's response — hymeis legete hoti ego eimi, literally "You say that I am" or "You yourselves say that I am" — affirms the title while placing the confession in their mouths. They have said it. The reader recalls the declaration from heaven: "You are My beloved Son" (3:22). What the heavenly voice declared to Jesus at the baptism, the earthly council now extracts from him at his trial.

"Then they said, 'What further need do we have of testimony? For we have heard it ourselves from His own mouth'" (22:71).

The council treats Jesus's response as self-incriminating testimony. They need no further witnesses because the defendant has spoken. The irony is layered: they asked him to tell them, he told them, and they condemn him for what he said. His response in verse 67 — "if I tell you, you will not believe" — is proved correct. They received the truth and used it as grounds for condemnation rather than belief.

Summary: The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. The return of Satan completes the narrative bracket from 4:13. The devil departed after the wilderness testing "until an opportune time." That time arrives in chapter 22. Satan enters Judas (v. 3), demands to sift the apostles (v. 31), and exercises "the power of darkness" through the arrest (v. 53). The entire chapter is framed as a confrontation between Jesus and the adversary who has been waiting for this moment since chapter 4.

2. The Passover is reinterpreted around Jesus's death. The lamb "had to be" sacrificed (v. 7, dei). Jesus gives his body "for you" and identifies his blood as the ratification of the "new covenant" promised in Jeremiah 31. The Passover is not abolished but fulfilled: what the original deliverance from Egypt pointed toward — the definitive act of liberation through the blood of the lamb — is accomplished in Jesus.

3. The dispute about greatness and the kingdom grant define the nature of messianic authority. Immediately after giving his body and blood, Jesus corrects the apostles' pursuit of rank by identifying himself as the servant at the table. The kingdom is then covenantally granted to those who stood by him — the same verb (diatithemai) as the covenant (diatheke) of the cup. Authority in Jesus's kingdom flows from service, not domination.

4. Peter's predicted failure illustrates the difference between satanic sifting and the total destruction of faith. Satan demands to sift all the apostles. Jesus prays specifically for Peter that his faith would not be utterly extinguished. Peter will fall — the triple denial is predicted and fulfilled with precise accuracy — but the fall is not the end. The turning back (epistrepsas) is built into the prediction before the failure even occurs (v. 32). Peter's bitter weeping (v. 62) is the first evidence that the prayer was effective: the faith survived the sifting.

5. The Isaiah 53 quotation (v. 37) is the only direct citation of Isaiah's servant song in Luke's Gospel. Jesus explicitly identifies himself as the subject of Isaiah 53:12: "He was numbered with transgressors." The changed circumstances of vv. 35-36 ("but now") signal that the era of protection during the mission phase has ended. Jesus enters the final phase of his mission: identification with transgressors, suffering, and death.

6. The Mount of Olives prayer is the final and most severe form of the testing that began in chapter 4. In the wilderness, Jesus was tested about what kind of Son he would be. On the Mount of Olives, the same question reaches its ultimate form: will he drink the cup the Father has given? The answer — "not My will, but Yours be done" — is the climactic expression of the obedience that defined him in chapter 4.

7. The council hearing produces the christological declaration that governs everything that follows. Jesus claims to be the Son of Man who sits at God's right hand (Daniel 7 + Psalm 110), and the council correctly infers that this makes him the Son of God. His response to their question simultaneously confirms the claim and fulfills his own prediction that they would not believe. The trial is the moment where what heaven declared at the baptism ("You are My beloved Son") is confessed — by Jesus himself, before his enemies, at the cost of his life.

8. The chapter traces the collapse and reconstitution of the disciple community. At the table, the apostles are recipients of the new covenant and the kingdom grant. By the arrest, they wield unauthorized violence and scatter. Peter follows at a distance, denies three times, and weeps bitterly. Yet Jesus has already spoken the word of restoration (v. 32) before the failure occurred. The community does not survive by its own strength but by Jesus's intercession: "I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail."
