# Mark 7 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Mark 6):** Chapter 6 developed the theme of reception and rejection through a series of escalating episodes. It opened with Jesus returning to his hometown of Nazareth, where his own people "took offense at him" and their unbelief was so deep that "he could not do any miracle there" (6:3-5). Jesus then sent out the twelve in pairs with authority over unclean spirits (6:7), extending his mission through delegates. The narrative turned to Herod's troubled conscience -- hearing reports of Jesus, Herod concluded "John, whom I beheaded, has risen" (6:16) -- and the author inserted the account of the Baptist's execution, the only extended narrative in the document where Jesus is absent, showing what happens to God's messengers at the hands of political power. The returning apostles and the pressing crowds led to the feeding of the five thousand (6:30-44), where Jesus acted as shepherd over Israel (6:34, echoing Ezekiel 34 and Numbers 27:17) and provided bread in the wilderness. The disciples were then sent across the sea while Jesus prayed on the mountain; he came to them walking on the water, and the author noted that "they had not understood about the loaves, because their heart was hardened" (6:52). The chapter closed at Gennesaret, where people recognized Jesus, brought their sick from the entire surrounding region, and "as many as touched the fringe of his cloak were being healed" (6:56). The people are desperate for contact with Jesus; they understand that his touch -- even the touch of his garment -- restores.

**What the author argues immediately after (Mark 8:1-26):** The feeding of the four thousand (8:1-10) in Gentile territory mirrors the feeding of the five thousand in Jewish territory, completing the pattern of provision for both Israel and the nations that Mark 7 initiates. The Pharisees then demand a sign from heaven (8:11-12), and Jesus refuses, sighing deeply -- the same response category as the Jerusalem authorities in chapter 7 who test him by focusing on externals. The leaven discourse (8:14-21) sees Jesus warn the disciples against the leaven of the Pharisees and of Herod, and when the disciples misunderstand (thinking he means literal bread), Jesus delivers his most sustained rebuke: "Do you not yet understand or comprehend? Do you have a hardened heart? Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear?" (8:17-18). The language echoes both Isaiah 6 (quoted in 4:12) and the healing of the deaf man that closes chapter 7. The healing of the blind man at Bethsaida (8:22-26) -- accomplished in two stages, with the man first seeing "people like trees walking" and then seeing clearly -- serves as a physical enactment of the disciples' gradual, incomplete understanding. The progression from Mark 7 into Mark 8 moves from the articulation of what true defilement is, through demonstrations of Jesus's power among Gentiles, to the question of whether those closest to Jesus can actually perceive what is happening.

**How chapter 7 connects them:** Mark 6 closed with a scene of popular reception -- crowds bringing their sick, touching the fringe of Jesus's cloak, and being healed. The final image is of contact producing restoration. Mark 7 opens with Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem who object not to the healing but to the fact that Jesus's disciples eat with unwashed hands. The juxtaposition is the author's argument in miniature: the people reach out and are healed through touch; the religious authorities from Jerusalem fixate on whether hands have been ritually washed before eating. The question of what constitutes defilement -- external contact or internal condition -- becomes the explicit subject of the chapter. Jesus's answer overturns the purity framework that has governed Israel's relationship with the nations and prepares the ground for the two Gentile encounters that follow: the Syrophoenician woman and the deaf man in the Decapolis. The chapter thus functions as the theological hinge between the ministry primarily directed toward Israel (chapters 1-6) and the expansion of that ministry to include Gentiles that accelerates from this point forward. What was demonstrated in action throughout chapters 1-5 -- Jesus's touch overcoming uncleanness rather than being contaminated by it -- now receives its explicit articulation: "There is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him, but the things that come out of a person are what defile him" (7:15).

## Verses 1-5: The Challenge from Jerusalem

"The Pharisees and some of the scribes who had come from Jerusalem gathered to Him, and saw that some of His disciples were eating their bread with impure hands, that is, unwashed."

The author specifies that these are Pharisees and scribes "from Jerusalem" (apo Hierosolymon). This is the second delegation from Jerusalem in the document. The first appeared in 3:22, when scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" accused Jesus of casting out demons by Beelzebul. That encounter ended with Jesus's warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (3:28-30). The Jerusalem authorities in the author's presentation function as a centralized opposition: they come from the seat of religious power to scrutinize Jesus in Galilee. The reader who remembers 3:22 approaches this encounter with the expectation of confrontation.

The charge concerns the disciples, not Jesus directly: "some of His disciples were eating their bread with impure hands, that is, unwashed" (koinais chersin, tout' estin aniptois). The word koinos (common, shared) in this usage means ritually defiled -- not unclean by Torah definition but in a state that fails to meet the standards of purity beyond Torah that the Pharisaic tradition maintained. The author translates the term for readers unfamiliar with this usage: "that is, unwashed" (tout' estin aniptois). This editorial gloss, like others in the chapter (vv. 3-4, 11, 19), indicates an audience that does not share the customs being described.

### The Author's Explanatory Parenthesis (vv. 3-4)

"(For the Pharisees and all the Jews do not eat unless they carefully wash their hands, thereby holding to the tradition of the elders; and when they come from the marketplace, they do not eat unless they bathe themselves; and there are many other things which they have received as traditions to follow, such as the washing of cups, pitchers, and copper pots.)"

The author interrupts the narrative with an extended explanation addressed to readers who do not know Jewish purity customs. Several features are significant.

First, the phrase "the tradition of the elders" (ten paradosin ton presbyteron). This is the governing category for the entire dispute. The hand-washing requirement the Pharisees enforce is not a commandment of Torah. Exodus, Leviticus, and Deuteronomy prescribe washing for priests before tabernacle service (Exodus 30:19-21) and for purification after specific forms of contamination (Leviticus 15, Numbers 19), but they do not require ordinary hand-washing before meals. The Pharisaic practice extended priestly standards of purity to everyday life -- an expansion motivated by the desire to maintain holiness, but one that rested on oral tradition rather than written Torah. The author carefully distinguishes between "the tradition of the elders" and "the commandment of God" (v. 8), and this distinction will become the axis of Jesus's response.

Second, the scope: "the Pharisees and all the Jews" (hoi Pharisaioi kai pantes hoi Ioudaioi). The phrase "all the Jews" is a generalization that reflects the Pharisees' growing influence over common practice in the first century, particularly after the destruction of the Second Temple when Pharisaic Judaism became the dominant stream. Whether the author is describing the situation during Jesus's ministry or the situation his audience knows, the effect is to present the practice as widespread and normative.

Third, the list of additional practices -- washing from the marketplace, washing cups, pitchers, copper pots -- demonstrates that the tradition extends well beyond hand-washing. The marketplace (agora) was a place where contact with Gentiles or with ritually impure persons was likely. The concern is that such contact produces contamination that must be removed before eating. The entire system operates on the assumption that defilement travels inward through external contact -- an assumption Jesus will directly challenge.

### The Accusation (v. 5)

"The Pharisees and the scribes asked Him, 'Why do Your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders, but eat their bread with impure hands?'"

The question is directed at Jesus about his disciples' behavior, implying that as their teacher he is responsible for their conduct. The verb "walk" (peripatousin) is the standard Jewish metaphor for conduct and manner of life -- halakhah, from the Hebrew halakh (to walk), denotes the way one is to live according to Torah and tradition. The charge is that Jesus's disciples are not following the established path. The issue is not hygiene but halakhah -- not cleanliness but compliance with the tradition of the elders.

## Verses 6-13: Jesus's Response -- Tradition Against Commandment

### The Isaiah Citation (vv. 6-7)

"And He said to them, 'Rightly did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites, as it is written: "This people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far away from Me. And in vain do they worship Me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men."'"

Jesus's response bypasses the specific question about hand-washing and addresses the underlying issue. He reaches for Isaiah 29:13, and his application is sharp: "rightly did Isaiah prophesy of you" -- the word "you" (hymon) makes the identification direct. The Pharisees and scribes are not merely people who happen to resemble the audience Isaiah addressed; they are, in Jesus's assessment, the contemporary instantiation of the pattern Isaiah described.

The quotation follows the LXX text of Isaiah 29:13 closely. The Hebrew Masoretic text reads differently in the final clause -- "their fear of Me is a commandment of men, a lesson learned" -- but the LXX renders it as "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (didaskontes didaskalias entalmata anthropon), which is the form Jesus uses. The LXX version sharpens the point: human commandments are being taught as though they were God's requirements. The charge of "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" will be unpacked in what follows.

The word "hypocrites" (hypokritai) in its first-century Greek usage means an actor, one who plays a part on stage. In the LXX and in Jewish usage by this period, it had acquired the moral sense of one whose external performance does not match their internal reality. Jesus identifies the gap: "this people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far away from Me." The honor is real in its outward form -- they do perform the rituals, they do teach the traditions -- but the heart (kardia), the seat of will and intention in Hebrew anthropology, is distant from God. The entire purity system the Pharisees maintain is, in Jesus's reading, an elaborate lip-service that masks a heart-problem.

The reference to "vain" worship (maten de sebontai me) is devastating. Maten means "in vain, to no purpose, futilely." Their worship is not merely imperfect; it is empty. The reason given: they teach human commandments as divine doctrine. The content of their teaching -- the tradition of the elders -- has displaced the commandment of God and has been elevated to the status of doctrine (didaskalia). What was received as human tradition is being transmitted as divine requirement.

### The Principle Stated (vv. 8-9)

"'Neglecting the commandment of God, you hold to the tradition of men.' He was also saying to them, 'You are experts at setting aside the commandment of God in order to establish your tradition.'"

Jesus states the principle twice, with the second formulation sharper than the first. The first is descriptive: "you neglect (aphentes) the commandment of God and hold to (krateite) the tradition of men." The verb aphiemi (to leave, abandon, neglect) implies a deliberate turning away. The verb krateo (to hold, grasp, seize) implies tenacious adherence. The irony is that what they hold tightly is human tradition, while what they release is God's command.

The second formulation escalates: "You are experts at setting aside (atheteite) the commandment of God in order to establish (stesite) your tradition." The verb atheteo means to nullify, invalidate, or set aside something that has authority. The word is used in legal contexts for voiding a contract or abrogating a law. The author presents Jesus as charging the Pharisees not merely with neglect but with active nullification of God's commands -- and the purpose (hina) of that nullification is to establish their own tradition in its place. The tradition has become not a supplement to Torah but a replacement for it.

### The Corban Example (vv. 10-13)

"'For Moses said, "Honor your father and your mother"; and, "The one who speaks evil of father or mother, is certainly to be put to death"; but you say, "If a man says to his father or his mother, whatever I have that would help you is Corban (that is to say, given to God)," you no longer permit him to do anything for his father or his mother; thereby invalidating the word of God by your tradition which you have handed down; and you do many things such as that.'"

Jesus provides a concrete instance of the principle he has articulated. The example moves the argument from abstraction to demonstration.

**The commandment cited:** Jesus quotes two related texts from Torah. The first is the fifth commandment: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16). The verb "honor" (tima) in the LXX of Exodus 20:12 encompasses material provision -- to honor parents includes supporting them in their need. The second is the penalty clause: "The one who speaks evil of father or mother, is certainly to be put to death" (Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9). Jesus attributes both to "Moses" (Mouses eipen), grounding the commandments in the highest legislative authority Israel recognizes.

**The tradition cited:** "But you say" (hymeis de legete) -- the contrast is explicit. Moses said one thing; you say another. The tradition in question involves "Corban" (Korban), which the author again translates for his readers: "that is to say, given to God" (ho estin, doron). The Hebrew/Aramaic word qorban means an offering or gift dedicated to God, deriving from the root q-r-b (to draw near, to bring near). In Leviticus 1:2 and throughout the sacrificial legislation, qorban is the standard term for an offering brought to YHWH.

The practice Jesus describes works as follows: a person declares that the resources that might have been used to support aging parents are "Corban" -- dedicated to God, and therefore unavailable for parental support. Whether the resources were actually given to the temple or merely placed under a vow that prevented their use for family obligation, the effect was the same: the fifth commandment was nullified by a tradition about religious dedication. The person could avoid the obligation to provide for parents by claiming that the provision had been consecrated.

Jesus's verdict: "You no longer permit him to do anything for his father or his mother" (ouketi aphiete auton ouden poiesai to patri e te metri). The tradition does not merely allow the evasion of the commandment; it actively prevents its fulfillment. A son who has made the Corban declaration is bound by it and is no longer permitted to use those resources for his parents, even if he wished to. The tradition has created a mechanism by which the commandment of God is rendered inoperative.

"Thereby invalidating the word of God by your tradition which you have handed down" (akyrountes ton logon tou theou te paradosei hymon he paredokate). The verb akyroo means to make void, to deprive of authority -- a legal term for the annulment of a binding agreement. The "word of God" (ton logon tou theou) -- the author uses logos, the same word that has been central to the document since the parable discourse of chapter 4. The word of God, which is the seed the sower sows, which is the announcement of the kingdom, is here equated with the commandments of Moses. The tradition "handed down" (paredokate, from paradidomi -- to hand over, transmit) is described using the same root as paradosis (tradition). What the elders handed down has become the instrument by which what God handed down is annulled.

The concluding clause -- "and you do many things such as that" (kai paromoia toiauta polla poieite) -- indicates that the Corban example is illustrative, not exhaustive. The pattern of tradition overriding commandment extends to "many things."

## Verses 14-23: What Defiles a Person

### The Public Declaration (vv. 14-16)

"After He called the crowd to Him again, He began saying to them, 'Listen to Me, all of you, and understand: there is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him; but the things that come out of a person are what defile him. [If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear.]'"

The author marks a shift in audience. Jesus has been addressing the Pharisees and scribes (vv. 6-13). Now he summons the crowd (ton ochlon) and addresses them directly. The opening command -- "Listen to Me, all of you, and understand" (akousate mou pantes kai syniete) -- echoes the parable discourse of chapter 4. The verbs akouo (hear) and syniemi (understand) are the same pair that appeared in the Isaiah 6 quotation (4:12) and that will reappear in Jesus's rebuke of the disciples in 8:17-18. The author frames what follows as a teaching that requires the same kind of active hearing the parables demanded.

The declaration is a single principle with two halves:

1. "There is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him" (ouden estin exothen tou anthropou eisporeuomenon eis auton ho dynatai koinosai auton). Nothing external -- no food, no contact, no unwashed hands -- produces defilement by entering a person.

2. "The things that come out of a person are what defile him" (ta ek tou anthropou ekporeuomena estin ta koinounta ton anthropon). Defilement originates from within and moves outward.

The word koinos (common/defiled) and its verb form koinoo (to make common/defile) are the same terms used in verse 2 for the disciples' "impure hands." Jesus takes the Pharisees' own category -- koinoo, ritual defilement -- and reverses its direction. For the Pharisaic tradition, defilement enters from outside through contact with the unclean. For Jesus, defilement exits from inside through the actions and intentions of the heart.

This declaration has profound implications for the entire purity system and, within the author's larger narrative, for the question of Gentile inclusion. If nothing entering from outside defiles, then contact with Gentiles, with unclean foods, with the ritually impure -- the boundaries that separated Israel from the nations -- cannot produce the kind of defilement the tradition feared. The author has been demonstrating this in practice since chapter 1: Jesus touched the leper (1:41), the hemorrhaging woman touched Jesus (5:27), Jesus took the hand of a corpse (5:41). In every case, the expected direction of contamination was reversed -- holiness overpowered defilement rather than defilement overpowering holiness. Now Jesus states the principle explicitly.

### The Private Explanation (vv. 17-23)

"When He left the crowd and entered the house, His disciples asked Him about the parable. And He said to them, 'Are you so lacking in understanding as well? Do you not understand that whatever goes into a person from outside cannot defile him, because it does not go into his heart, but into his stomach, and is eliminated?' (Thus He declared all foods clean.)"

The pattern matches chapter 4 exactly: public teaching in parabolic form to the crowd, followed by private explanation to the disciples in the house. Jesus calls the public declaration a "parable" (parabolen) -- the same form he used for the seed parables. The word parabole here carries its LXX/mashal sense: a compressed, provocative statement that requires penetration to understand.

Jesus's rebuke -- "Are you so lacking in understanding as well?" (houtos kai hymeis asynetoi este) -- echoes 4:13 ("Do you not understand this parable?"). The disciples remain in the same position they occupied in chapter 4: insiders who have been given the mystery but struggle to grasp it. The word asynetoi (without understanding) is the adjectival form of syniemi (understand), the same verb Jesus used in the public address ("Listen to Me... and understand," v. 14). The disciples are being told they have failed at precisely what Jesus commanded.

The explanation is physiological: food enters the stomach (koilia), not the heart (kardia), and passes through the body. The author's logic is that defilement is a matter of the heart, and food does not reach the heart. The anatomical route -- mouth, stomach, elimination -- bypasses the heart entirely. Therefore food cannot defile.

**The authorial comment:** "Thus He declared all foods clean" (katharizn panta ta brmata). This clause is the author's own interpretive gloss, not Jesus's spoken words. The participle katharizn (cleansing, declaring clean) is grammatically linked to "He said" (legei) in verse 18, making Jesus the subject: he, by saying what he said, effectively declared all foods clean. The author draws out the implication that Jesus himself may not have stated in so many words. If nothing entering from outside can defile, then the food laws of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 -- which classified animals as clean or unclean based on external characteristics -- are rendered inoperative as markers of defilement. The author presents this as the logical consequence of Jesus's principle.

For a first-century audience that included Gentile believers -- the audience for whom the author provides translations and explains Jewish customs -- this authorial comment addresses one of the most contested questions of the early community: whether Gentile followers of Jesus must observe Jewish dietary laws. The author resolves the question by appeal to Jesus's own teaching: the principle that defilement comes from within, not from without, renders the clean/unclean food distinction obsolete. This is not the author imposing a later idea on Jesus's words; it is the author stating what he understands Jesus's words to mean when carried to their conclusion.

### The Catalogue of What Defiles (vv. 20-23)

"And He was saying, 'That which comes out of a person, that is what defiles the person. For from within, out of the hearts of people, come the evil thoughts, acts of sexual immorality, thefts, murders, acts of adultery, deeds of greed, acts of wickedness, deceit, indecent behavior, envy, slander, pride, and foolishness. All these evil things come from within and defile a person.'"

The source is identified: "from within, out of the hearts of people" (esothen gar ek tes kardias ton anthropon). The heart (kardia) is the seat of the problem. In the Hebrew scriptures, the heart is not primarily the center of emotion but the center of thought, will, and moral direction. "The heart is more deceitful than all else and is desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). "The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great on the earth, and that every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" (Genesis 6:5). Jesus's teaching here stands in continuity with this prophetic tradition: the human problem is internal, not external.

The list that follows contains twelve items -- a number that may echo Israel's twelve tribes, though the author does not draw the connection explicitly. The list moves from internal dispositions to external actions:

- **Evil thoughts** (hoi dialogismoi hoi kakoi) -- the plural dialogismoi means reasonings, deliberations, calculations. Evil begins in the mind's activity.
- **Acts of sexual immorality** (porneiai) -- the plural indicates various forms; the word covers the range of sexual transgression forbidden in Torah (Leviticus 18).
- **Thefts** (klopai) -- violations of the eighth commandment (Exodus 20:15).
- **Murders** (phonoi) -- violations of the sixth commandment (Exodus 20:13).
- **Acts of adultery** (moicheiai) -- violations of the seventh commandment (Exodus 20:14).
- **Deeds of greed** (pleonexiai) -- the desire for more, covetousness; related to the tenth commandment (Exodus 20:17).
- **Acts of wickedness** (poneriai) -- a general term for active malice and harmful conduct.
- **Deceit** (dolos) -- cunning, treachery, the manipulation of others through dishonesty.
- **Indecent behavior** (aselgeia) -- wantonness, shameless conduct, behavior that disregards all moral restraint.
- **Envy** (ophthalmos poneros) -- literally "the evil eye," a Semitic idiom for jealousy and resentment at another's good fortune. The phrase appears in Deuteronomy 15:9 (LXX) in connection with the refusal to give generously to the poor.
- **Slander** (blasphemia) -- the word that appeared in 3:28-29 in connection with "blasphemy against the Holy Spirit." Here it covers abusive speech directed at others.
- **Pride** (hyperephania) -- arrogance, the exaltation of self above others and above God.
- **Foolishness** (aphrosyne) -- not intellectual limitation but moral obtuseness, the failure to perceive and respond to God's reality. In the LXX wisdom literature, the "fool" (aphron) is not unintelligent but morally perverse: "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1; 53:1 LXX).

The list encompasses violations of virtually every category of the Decalogue. What is striking is that none of these items has anything to do with food, hand-washing, or external contact. Every entry is an action or disposition that originates "from within." Jesus has shifted the entire axis of the purity discussion. The Pharisees came with a concern about unwashed hands -- external contact producing external defilement requiring external remedy. Jesus responds with a catalogue of internal corruptions that no amount of washing can address. The real defilement is moral, not ritual; internal, not external; a matter of the heart, not the hands.

"All these evil things come from within and defile a person" (panta tauta ta ponera esothen ekporeuetai kai koinoi ton anthropon). The concluding statement returns to the language of verse 15 and completes the reversal. The word koinoi (defiles) is the same word the Pharisees used for the disciples' unwashed hands. Jesus has redefined their own term: what makes a person koinos -- ritually defiled, common, unclean -- is not unwashed hands or unclean food but the catalogue of sins that proceed from the human heart.

## Verses 24-30: The Syrophoenician Woman -- Bread, Children, and Dogs

### The Setting (v. 24)

"And from there He got up and went away to the region of Tyre. And when He had entered a house, He wanted no one to know about it; yet He could not escape notice."

Jesus leaves Jewish territory entirely and enters "the region of Tyre" (ta horia Tyrou). Tyre was a Phoenician coastal city northwest of Galilee, a center of pagan worship and commerce that appears frequently in Israel's prophetic literature as a symbol of Gentile pride and wealth (Isaiah 23; Ezekiel 26-28). The geographic movement is deliberate: immediately after declaring that nothing external defiles and that all foods are clean, Jesus crosses into the most emphatically Gentile territory he has yet entered. The theological principle (nothing from outside defiles) is followed by its practical enactment (entering Gentile territory).

The note that "He wanted no one to know about it; yet He could not escape notice" (ouk edynathe lathein) fits the author's secrecy pattern in Jewish territory (1:44; 5:43) and may suggest that Jesus did not intend a public mission among the Gentiles but was seeking withdrawal. Nevertheless, the author's "could not escape notice" signals that what follows is not accidental but driven by a need that finds Jesus despite his attempt at concealment.

### The Woman's Approach (vv. 25-26)

"But after hearing about Him, a woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit immediately came and fell at His feet. Now the woman was a Gentile, of Syrophoenician descent. And she was asking Him to cast the demon out of her daughter."

The author stacks identifying details. She is "a Gentile" (Hellenis -- literally "a Greek," meaning a non-Jew, a person of the wider Hellenistic world) and specifically "of Syrophoenician descent" (Syrophoinikissa to genei), distinguishing the Phoenicians of the Syrian coast from those of Libya (Libyphoenicians). She falls at Jesus's feet -- the same posture of supplication adopted by Jairus (5:22) and the Gerasene demoniac (5:6). Her daughter has "an unclean spirit" (pneuma akatharton), the same phrase used throughout the document for demonic possession (1:23, 26; 3:11, 30; 5:2, 8, 13).

The situation echoes 5:1-20 (the Gerasene demoniac in Gentile territory) but with a critical difference. There, Jesus crossed into Gentile territory and was immediately confronted by the possessed man. Here, Jesus is in Gentile territory and a Gentile woman seeks him out for her daughter. The initiative has shifted: the Gentiles are now coming to Jesus, having heard about him.

### The Exchange About Children and Dogs (vv. 27-28)

"And He was saying to her, 'Let the children be satisfied first, for it is not good to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs.'"

This saying requires careful attention to its language and context.

**"The children" (ta tekna).** Within the author's narrative and the broader first-century Jewish framework, "the children" refers to Israel -- the people of God, the covenant people. Israel is God's "firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22); the Israelites are "sons of the living God" (Hosea 1:10); the relationship between God and Israel is consistently described in familial terms.

**"First" (proton).** The word is critical. Jesus does not say "only the children" but "the children first." The adverb proton implies a sequence: the children are fed first, but the feeding does not end with them. There is an "after" implied by "first." The priority of Israel in receiving the message and provision of the kingdom is a matter of sequence, not exclusion. This aligns with the pattern the author has been building: Jesus's ministry begins in Jewish territory and among Jewish people, but it has already extended to Gentile territory (5:1-20, the Gerasene demoniac; the present journey to Tyre) and will continue to do so.

**"The dogs" (tois kynariois).** The word is the diminutive kynaria -- not the wild street dogs (kynes) that roamed in packs and were used as a term of contempt (cf. 1 Samuel 17:43, "Am I a dog?"; Psalm 22:16, "dogs surround me"), but the diminutive form: small dogs, house dogs, the dogs that lived under the family table. The distinction matters. Jesus is not using the harshest available term; the diminutive softens the image toward household animals that are part of the domestic scene, even if subordinate to the children.

Nevertheless, the saying draws a distinction between Israel ("children") and Gentiles ("dogs"), and assigns priority to Israel. The metaphor of bread -- "the children's bread" (ton arton ton teknon) -- resonates with the feeding of the five thousand (6:30-44), where Jesus provided bread to Jewish crowds in Jewish territory. The bread is the provision of the kingdom, the healing and restoration Jesus brings.

**The woman's response:** "But she answered and said to Him, 'Yes, Lord, but even the dogs under the table feed on the children's crumbs.'"

The woman does not challenge Jesus's framework. She accepts the distinction between children and dogs, accepts the priority of the children, and then argues from within the metaphor. Her argument is precise: even within the household order Jesus has described, the dogs under the table eat what falls from the children's meal. The crumbs are not taken from the children; they are the surplus, the overflow, what falls naturally as the children eat. She is not asking Jesus to redirect his mission from Israel to the Gentiles. She is asking for what spills over -- the excess of provision that the children cannot contain.

The word "Lord" (kyrie) is noteworthy. In the author's document, no one else addresses Jesus as kyrios in this way. The title carries the resonance of the LXX, where kyrios renders the divine name. Whether the woman intends the full theological weight of the term or is simply using a respectful address, the author records it without comment.

Her response demonstrates the kind of faith the author has been tracking throughout the document. Like the hemorrhaging woman who reasoned "If I just touch His garments, I will be well" (5:28), this woman works within the constraints of her situation and finds a way to reach Jesus's power. She does not demand equality; she asks for adequacy. Even the crumbs of this provision would be sufficient for her daughter's need.

### The Healing at a Distance (vv. 29-30)

"And He said to her, 'Because of this answer, go; the demon has gone out of your daughter.' And going back to her home, she found the child lying on the bed, the demon having gone out."

Jesus responds to the quality of her argument: "Because of this answer" (dia touton ton logon -- literally "because of this word/saying"). The word logos appears again. The woman's logos -- her word, her response -- is what elicits Jesus's action. This is the only exorcism in the document performed at a distance, without Jesus being physically present, without a command spoken to the demon, and without any physical contact. The healing occurs through the exchange of words alone. The author presents this as a demonstration of Jesus's authority that transcends proximity and requires no ritual or physical medium.

The woman returns home and finds her daughter lying on the bed (epi ten klinen), the demon having departed. The confirmation is quiet and domestic -- no dramatic expulsion, no destructive transfer (as with the pigs in 5:13), no crowd reaction. The girl is simply at rest, restored. The contrast with the Gerasene episode is marked: there, the exorcism was public, violent, and spectacular; here, it is private, gentle, and almost silent. The author presents both as equally valid demonstrations of Jesus's authority over unclean spirits.

### The Episode's Place in the Author's Argument

This encounter is the first time in the document that Jesus directly engages in a dialogue about the relationship between Israel and the Gentiles in the purposes of God. The theological groundwork was laid in vv. 1-23: if nothing external defiles, then the purity boundary between Jew and Gentile has lost its force. Now the narrative tests the principle. A Gentile woman approaches Jesus with a need; Jesus articulates the priority of Israel ("the children first"); the woman accepts the priority but presses for the overflow; Jesus responds to her faith. The pattern mirrors the author's narrative arc: the kingdom is announced first to Israel, but its provision overflows to the Gentiles -- not as an afterthought but as the natural consequence of its abundance. The children's bread is so plentiful that the crumbs are sufficient for those outside.

## Verses 31-37: The Deaf Man with the Speech Impediment in the Decapolis

### The Geography (v. 31)

"Again He went out from the region of Tyre, and came through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, within the region of Decapolis."

The route described is geographically circuitous. From Tyre, Jesus goes north to Sidon (farther into Gentile territory, not closer to Galilee), then somehow arrives at the Sea of Galilee "within the region of Decapolis" -- the eastern, predominantly Gentile side of the sea. The route would take Jesus through Gentile territory for the entire journey. Whether the author intends a precise itinerary or is indicating a general movement through non-Jewish regions, the effect is clear: Jesus remains in Gentile territory. The Decapolis is the region where the healed Gerasene demoniac was sent to proclaim "what great things Jesus had done for him" (5:20). Jesus now arrives in the territory where his herald has been at work.

### The Healing (vv. 32-35)

"And they brought to Him one who was deaf and had difficulty speaking, and they begged Him to lay His hand on him."

The people bring the man to Jesus and request the laying on of hands -- the same gesture Jairus requested for his daughter (5:23) and that the people of Gennesaret sought (6:56). The man is described as "deaf" (kophon) and having "difficulty speaking" (mogilalon). The word mogilalos is extremely rare -- it appears only here in the entire New Testament and only once in the LXX, in Isaiah 35:6: "Then the tongue of the mogilalos (the one with difficulty speaking) will shout for joy." The author's use of this precise word is a signal to any reader who knows Isaiah 35, and its significance will become fully apparent in the crowd's response at the end of the episode.

"And Jesus took him aside from the crowd, by himself, and put His fingers in his ears, and after spitting, He touched his tongue with the saliva; and looking up to heaven with a deep sigh, He said to him, 'Ephphatha!' (that is, 'Be opened!')."

The healing is described with unusual physical detail. Jesus takes the man aside privately (kat' idian) -- separating him from the crowd, as he separated the inner three at Jairus's house (5:37, 40). The actions that follow are tactile and intimate:

1. **Fingers in the ears** -- Jesus physically engages with the organ of deafness. The gesture communicates to a man who cannot hear: I am addressing your ears.

2. **Spitting and touching the tongue** -- Jesus uses his own saliva to touch the man's tongue. In the ancient world, saliva was widely believed to have therapeutic properties. The gesture is visceral -- Jesus's own body produces the medium of healing. The action communicates to the man: I am addressing your tongue.

3. **Looking up to heaven** -- Jesus directs his gaze upward, acknowledging the source of the power he exercises. This is the only healing in the document where Jesus is described as looking heavenward before acting (though he does so at the feeding of the five thousand, 6:41).

4. **A deep sigh** (stenazo) -- the verb means to groan, to sigh deeply from within. In Romans 8:23 and 26, Paul will use related words (stenazomen, stenagmois) for the groaning of creation and the Spirit's intercession. The author records this sigh without explanation. It may express the weight of the man's suffering, compassion, the effort of spiritual engagement, or some combination. The detail humanizes the scene: Jesus is not performing a clinical procedure but engaging with a person's affliction at a cost.

5. **"Ephphatha"** -- the author preserves the Aramaic word and translates it: "Be opened" (dianoichtheti). This is the third Aramaic expression the author has retained (after "Boanerges" in 3:17 and "Talitha kum" in 5:41), with others still to come ("Abba" in 14:36 and "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani" in 15:34). Each preserved Aramaic word marks a moment of particular intensity. The command "be opened" addresses the man's entire condition -- ears sealed to sound, tongue bound from speech. The single word encompasses both afflictions.

"And his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was removed, and he began speaking plainly."

The result is immediate and complete. The word for "impediment" is desmos -- literally "bond, chain, fetter." The man's tongue was bound; Jesus loosed it. The language of binding and loosing connects to the Gerasene demoniac, whom no one could bind with chains (5:3-4). There, the man was physically unrestrained but spiritually bound; here, the man's tongue is physically bound and is released. In both cases, Jesus's word breaks the chain.

The man "began speaking plainly" (elalei orthos) -- the adverb orthos means correctly, rightly, properly. His speech is not merely restored but made right. What was distorted is straightened.

### The Command and Its Violation (v. 36)

"And He ordered them not to tell anyone; but the more He ordered them, the more widely they continued to proclaim it."

The secrecy command appears again, but here in Gentile territory it creates an apparent tension. In 5:19, Jesus told the Gerasene demoniac to go and tell; here, in the same Decapolis region, he commands silence. The author records the futility of the command: "the more He ordered them, the more widely they continued to proclaim it." The comparative construction (hoson... mallon perissoteron) conveys escalation -- the prohibition produces the opposite of its intent. The word "proclaim" (ekeryssan) is the same verb used for the public proclamation of the kingdom (1:14, 38, 39, 45; 3:14; 6:12). The people perform the action of heralds -- kerusso, the technical term -- without authorization. Their proclamation is irrepressible because what they have witnessed is, in their judgment, too significant to contain.

### The Crowd's Response (v. 37)

"And they were utterly astonished, saying, 'He has done all things well; He makes even the deaf to hear and the mute to speak.'"

The response of the Gentile crowd carries extraordinary weight within the author's narrative.

**"He has done all things well" (kalos panta pepoieken).** The phrase echoes Genesis 1:31 (LXX): "And God saw all that He had made, and behold, it was very good" (kai eiden ho theos ta panta hosa epoiesen, kai idou kala lian). The verb poieo (to do, to make) and the adjective kalos (good, well, beautiful) connect the crowd's assessment of Jesus's work to God's assessment of creation. The crowd, perhaps without knowing the full weight of what they are saying, uses language that positions Jesus's healing activity as an act of re-creation -- the restoration of what was broken to its intended wholeness.

**"He makes even the deaf to hear and the mute to speak."** This is a direct allusion to Isaiah 35:5-6: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped. Then the lame will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will shout for joy." Isaiah 35 describes the conditions that will accompany the Lord's return to Zion, the restoration of Israel, and the transformation of the wilderness into a garden. The signs listed -- sight for the blind, hearing for the deaf, mobility for the lame, speech for the mute -- are the markers of the age of salvation. The crowd's words identify Jesus's actions as the fulfillment of Isaiah's vision of restoration.

The author's choice of the rare word mogilalos in verse 32, drawing from Isaiah 35:6 (LXX), now reveals its purpose. The author embedded the Isaiah connection in the description of the man's condition so that the reader would recognize the crowd's response as a scriptural identification: this is the restoration Isaiah promised.

What is remarkable is where this recognition occurs. The crowd that articulates the connection between Jesus's works and God's creative/restorative action is a Gentile crowd in the Decapolis. In Jewish territory, the Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem fixated on unwashed hands. In Gentile territory, the people declare, "He has done all things well." The author has structured the chapter so that the contrast is inescapable.

## The Structure and Argument of Mark 7

The chapter divides into two movements that mirror and reinforce each other:

**Movement 1 (vv. 1-23): The overturning of the external purity framework.** The Pharisees challenge Jesus about unwashed hands. Jesus responds by exposing the contradiction between their tradition and God's commandment, then declares the principle that defilement originates from within, not from without. The author adds the interpretive conclusion that Jesus thereby declared all foods clean.

**Movement 2 (vv. 24-37): The practical outworking in Gentile territory.** With the purity barrier theologically dismantled, Jesus moves into Gentile territory and enacts what his teaching implies. A Gentile woman receives the overflow of Israel's provision for her daughter. A deaf man in the Decapolis is healed in a way that fulfills Isaiah's vision of restoration. The Gentile crowd recognizes what the Jerusalem authorities missed.

The two movements are inseparable. Without the theological argument of vv. 1-23, the Gentile encounters of vv. 24-37 would lack their interpretive framework. Without the Gentile encounters of vv. 24-37, the theological argument of vv. 1-23 would lack its narrative demonstration.

---

## Key Connections Within the Document

**The reversal of contamination, from practice to principle.** Since chapter 1, the author has shown Jesus making contact with sources of ritual defilement -- lepers (1:41), the demon-possessed (5:1-20), the hemorrhaging woman (5:25-34), a corpse (5:41) -- and in every case holiness overpowered uncleanness rather than the reverse. In chapter 7, the author presents Jesus articulating the principle that underlies the pattern: nothing external defiles; defilement comes from within. The practice preceded the explanation.

**The word "heart" (kardia).** In 6:52, the author noted that the disciples "had not understood about the loaves, because their heart was hardened" (en he kardia auton pepormene). In 7:6, Jesus quotes Isaiah: "their heart is far from Me." In 7:19, Jesus explains that food does not enter the heart. In 7:21, the evil things that defile come "from within, out of the hearts of people." The heart is the site of both the problem (hardening, distance from God, evil intentions) and the solution (understanding, nearness to God, the reception of the word). The Pharisees' error is not merely intellectual but cardiac: their hearts are far from God even while their lips honor him.

**The hearing/understanding theme.** "Listen to Me, all of you, and understand" (v. 14) continues the demand that has been central since chapter 4. The deaf man who cannot hear (v. 32) is healed by the command "Be opened" (v. 34). The disciples who can physically hear are rebuked for not understanding (v. 18). In 8:17-18, Jesus will combine all these threads: "Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear?" The physical healing of deafness in the Decapolis becomes a sign of the spiritual hearing Jesus is seeking from his own disciples -- and failing to find.

**The Gentile mission.** The Gerasene demoniac was commissioned to proclaim in the Decapolis (5:19-20). The Syrophoenician woman received healing for her daughter through the overflow of Israel's provision (7:24-30). The deaf man was healed in the Decapolis and the crowd proclaimed Jesus's works despite his prohibition (7:31-37). The author is building a narrative in which the Gentile mission does not begin suddenly but emerges through a series of encounters that increasingly reveal the scope of Jesus's work. The theological ground for this mission is laid in 7:1-23: if external contact cannot defile, then the wall separating Jew from Gentile has been rendered theologically obsolete.

---

## Summary of the Author's Argument in Chapter 7

Mark 7 presents the author's most explicit treatment of the purity question and uses it to advance the narrative toward Gentile inclusion. The chapter argues:

1. The tradition of the elders, however well-intentioned, has become a mechanism for nullifying the commandment of God. The Corban example demonstrates how a religious practice can actively prevent obedience to Torah. Lip-honor without heart-commitment is vain worship.

2. Defilement is internal, not external. Nothing entering a person from outside can defile; what defiles comes from within, from the heart. The evil thoughts, actions, and dispositions that proceed from the human heart -- not unwashed hands, not unclean food, not contact with Gentiles -- are what make a person unclean.

3. This principle, when carried to its conclusion, removes the barrier between Jew and Gentile that the purity system maintained. The author's editorial note ("thus He declared all foods clean") draws out this implication explicitly.

4. The two Gentile encounters that follow demonstrate the principle in action. The Syrophoenician woman receives healing for her daughter as the overflow of Israel's provision -- the crumbs that fall from the children's table are sufficient. The deaf man in the Decapolis is healed in fulfillment of Isaiah 35's vision of restoration, and the Gentile crowd declares what the Jerusalem authorities could not see: "He has done all things well."

5. The chapter's deepest irony lies in the contrast between the two audiences. The Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem, guardians of Israel's purity, stand at a distance from God despite their meticulous observance -- their hearts are far from him. The Gentiles of Tyre and the Decapolis, who have no claim on Israel's covenant, recognize in Jesus's works the activity of the God who made all things and is now restoring them. The ones who should see are blind; the ones who had no access to the tradition see clearly. This pattern will reach its climax in the centurion at the cross, who will be the first human being in the document to declare: "Truly this man was the Son of God" (15:39).
