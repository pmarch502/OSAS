Luke 7 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 6): Luke 6 opens with two Sabbath controversies: the disciples plucking grain on the Sabbath and Jesus healing a man with a withered hand on the Sabbath. Both episodes demonstrate Jesus's authority over the Sabbath and provoke intense hostility from the scribes and Pharisees, who "were filled with rage, and discussed together what they might do to Jesus" (6:11). Jesus then withdraws to a mountain to pray all night and in the morning selects twelve from among his disciples, whom he names "apostles" — sent ones. He descends with them and, standing on a level place before a great crowd, delivers an extended discourse. This discourse begins with blessings ("Blessed are you who are poor") and woes ("Woe to you who are rich"), establishing that God's kingdom reverses the expected order of status and fortune. It proceeds to radical ethical instruction: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, lend expecting nothing in return, do not judge, forgive, and give generously. The discourse closes with the parable of two builders — one who builds on rock by hearing and doing Jesus's words, the other who builds on sand by hearing without doing. Chapter 6 thus establishes Jesus as the authoritative teacher whose words must be obeyed and whose kingdom inverts human expectations about who is blessed and who is not.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 8): Luke 8 opens with a summary statement about Jesus traveling through cities and villages, proclaiming the good news of the kingdom of God, accompanied by the twelve and by certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and sicknesses — including Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna — who were supporting the ministry from their own resources. The chapter then moves to the parable of the sower and soils, which addresses the varied responses to the word of God: some hear and fall away, some hear and are choked by the cares and riches of life, and some hear and bear fruit with perseverance. This is followed by the saying about the lamp that is not hidden but placed on a stand, and the declaration that Jesus's true family consists of "those who hear the word of God and do it" (8:21). The remainder of the chapter presents four demonstrations of Jesus's authority: over nature (stilling the storm), over the demonic realm (the Gerasene demoniac), over chronic illness (the woman with a flow of blood), and over death itself (raising Jairus's daughter). Chapter 8 thus shifts from the question of who recognizes Jesus's identity (the focus of chapter 7) to the question of how people respond to the word and whether that response endures.

How chapter 7 connects these: Luke 6 ended with Jesus's authoritative teaching — words that demanded a response. Luke 8 will address the varied quality of that response through the parable of the soils and will continue demonstrating Jesus's power. Chapter 7 functions as the bridge between teaching and response by presenting a series of episodes that explore who actually recognizes what God is doing through Jesus and who does not. The chapter repeatedly overturns expectations about who "gets it." A Roman centurion — a Gentile military officer — displays faith greater than anything Jesus has found in Israel. A dead man is raised in a scene that causes the people to identify Jesus as a great prophet and to declare that God has "visited His people." John the Baptist himself, languishing in prison, sends disciples to ask whether Jesus is really the one who was to come. The crowds who witnessed both John and Jesus are divided: some acknowledged God's righteousness, while the Pharisees and lawyers rejected God's purpose for themselves. Finally, a woman known as a sinner demonstrates extravagant devotion while a Pharisee host cannot see past her reputation to recognize what is happening. Chapter 7 is thus organized around the theme of recognition and response: who sees what God is doing, who is capable of faith, and how the expected categories of insider and outsider, righteous and sinner, are being dismantled by Jesus's ministry.

Verses 1-5: Setting Up the Centurion's Request

"When He had completed all His discourse in the hearing of the people, He went to Capernaum. And a centurion's slave, who was highly regarded by him, was sick and about to die. When he heard about Jesus, he sent some Jewish elders to Him, asking Him to come and save the life of his slave. When they came to Jesus, they earnestly implored Him, saying, 'He is worthy for You to grant this to him; for he loves our nation and it was he who built us our synagogue.'"

The opening phrase ties chapter 7 directly to the discourse of chapter 6: "when He had completed all His discourse in the hearing of the people" (epeidē eplērōsen panta ta rhēmata autou eis tas akoas tou laou). The word plēroō ("to complete, to fill up") signals that the teaching is finished and the narrative now returns to action. The transition is from words to deeds — from instruction about what the kingdom demands to demonstrations of what the kingdom looks like in practice.

The first episode takes place in Capernaum, Jesus's base of operations in Galilee. A centurion (hekatontarchos) — a Roman military officer commanding approximately one hundred soldiers — has a slave (doulos) who is sick and about to die. Luke specifies that the slave "was highly regarded" (entimos) by the centurion. The word entimos means "valued, honored, precious." This detail is not incidental: it establishes that the centurion's request is motivated by genuine care for this person, not merely by the economic loss of losing a slave. The relationship between this centurion and his slave defies the norm of Roman slave-owning culture, where slaves were commonly treated as disposable property.

The centurion does not approach Jesus directly. He sends "Jewish elders" (presbyterous tōn Ioudaiōn) as intermediaries. This is significant in several ways. First, it shows the centurion's awareness of social boundaries: as a Gentile and a representative of the occupying power, he apparently considers it inappropriate to approach a Jewish teacher directly. Second, it shows that he has a relationship with the Jewish community — close enough that the elders are willing to advocate on his behalf. Third, it establishes a pattern of mediation that will continue in the next set of messengers (vv. 6-8).

The Jewish elders' plea on the centurion's behalf is remarkable: "He is worthy (axios) for You to grant this to him." They base his worthiness on two facts: "he loves our nation" (agapā gar to ethnos hēmōn) and "it was he who built us our synagogue" (tēn synagōgēn autos ōkodomēsen hēmin). The centurion is what later Jewish sources would call a "God-fearer" — a Gentile who was attracted to Israel's God and supported the Jewish community without undergoing full conversion (circumcision). Archaeological evidence confirms that Gentile benefactors did build synagogues in this period. The elders' argument is that the centurion has earned access to Israel's God through his deeds of benevolence toward Israel.

This argument about worthiness will be dramatically undercut by the centurion's own words in what follows.

Verses 6-10: The Centurion's Faith

"Now Jesus started on His way with them; and when He was not far from the house, the centurion sent friends, saying to Him, 'Lord, do not trouble Yourself further, for I am not worthy for You to come under my roof; for this reason I did not even consider myself worthy to come to You, but just say the word, and my servant will be healed. For I also am a man placed under authority, with soldiers under me; and I say to this one, "Go!" and he goes, and to another, "Come!" and he comes, and to my slave, "Do this!" and he does it.' Now when Jesus heard this, He marveled at him, and turned and said to the crowd that was following Him, 'I say to you, not even in Israel have I found such great faith.' When those who had been sent returned to the house, they found the slave in good health."

The centurion sends a second delegation — this time "friends" (philous), not Jewish elders — with a message that directly contradicts the elders' assessment. The elders said "he is worthy" (axios estin). The centurion says "I am not worthy" (ou gar hikanos eimi). The word hikanos means "sufficient, adequate, fit." The centurion denies his own fitness for Jesus to enter his house, and he explains that this same sense of unworthiness prevented him from coming to Jesus in person. The contrast between the elders' assessment of the centurion and the centurion's assessment of himself is the first layer of the episode's meaning: those who claim worthiness on his behalf do not understand him as well as he understands himself.

But the centurion's message is not primarily about unworthiness. It is about authority. "Just say the word, and my servant will be healed" (alla eipe logō, kai iathētō ho pais mou). The centurion shifts from doulos ("slave") to pais, a word that can mean "child," "young person," or "servant" — possibly indicating a closer, more affectionate relationship. His confidence is based on an analogy from his own experience: "I also am a man placed under authority" (kai gar egō anthrōpos eimi hypo exousian tassomenos). The word tassomenos ("placed, appointed, ordered") indicates that the centurion occupies a specific position within a chain of command. He has soldiers under him; he gives orders and they are obeyed. The word "also" (kai) is the crucial hinge: just as I operate within a structure of authority where commands are effective, so you operate within a structure of authority where your word is effective.

The centurion's reasoning is sophisticated. He is not comparing himself to Jesus as an equal. He is drawing an analogy between two systems of authority. In the Roman military, when a centurion speaks, the command is executed because behind his word stands the authority of the legion, the governor, and ultimately the emperor. The centurion perceives that when Jesus speaks, the command is executed because behind his word stands an authority of a different order — the authority of God. The centurion does not need to understand the mechanics of healing. He understands authority. He understands that a word spoken from a position of legitimate authority accomplishes what it commands.

Jesus's response is striking: "He marveled at him" (ethaumasen auton). The verb thaumazō indicates astonishment, wonder. In Luke's narrative, it is usually the crowds who marvel at Jesus (4:22, 8:25, 9:43, 11:14). Here Jesus himself marvels at the centurion. What provokes this marvel is not the centurion's piety, his synagogue-building, or his love for Israel — the qualities the elders emphasized — but his faith (pistin). "Not even in Israel have I found such great faith" (oude en tō Israēl tosautēn pistin heuron).

This statement is devastating in its implications. The greatest faith Jesus has encountered is not in the synagogue, not among the disciples, not among the scribes and Pharisees, not among any of the children of Abraham. It is in a Roman military officer — a Gentile, an agent of the occupying empire. This directly continues the pattern established in the Nazareth synagogue scene (4:25-27), where Jesus cited Elijah's mission to the Sidonian widow and Elisha's healing of Naaman the Syrian as precedents for God's saving action going to Gentiles when Israel proved unresponsive. The centurion is the Naaman of Luke's Gospel: a Gentile military figure who recognizes the authority of Israel's God more clearly than Israel itself does.

The healing is reported in a single sentence: "When those who had been sent returned to the house, they found the slave in good health" (hygiainonta). Jesus never enters the house, never sees the slave, never touches him. The word alone was sufficient — exactly as the centurion believed. The healing confirms the centurion's understanding of authority: a word spoken from legitimate authority accomplishes its purpose without the speaker needing to be physically present.

Verses 11-12: The Widow at Nain

"Soon afterward He went to a city called Nain; and His disciples were going along with Him, accompanied by a large crowd. Now as He approached the gate of the city, a dead man was being carried out, the only son of his mother, and she was a widow; and a sizeable crowd from the city was with her."

The author transitions to a new scene with three details that together create maximum pathos. First, the dead person is "the only son" (monogenēs) of his mother. The word monogenēs means "one of a kind, only-begotten" — the same word used of Isaac in Hebrews 11:17 (drawing on the LXX of Genesis 22:2, where Isaac is called the agapētos, "beloved"). The loss of an only son is the most extreme form of bereavement in any culture, but in first-century Jewish society it carries an additional dimension.

Second, the mother is a widow (chēra). She has already lost her husband, and now she has lost her only child. In the social structure of first-century Palestine, a widow without a son had no male protector, no legal advocate, no source of economic support, and no heir to carry forward the family name. She faced destitution and social marginalization. The Torah repeatedly identifies widows as among the most vulnerable members of society, alongside orphans and resident aliens, and commands their protection (Deuteronomy 10:18, 24:17-21, 27:19). The prophets condemned Israel precisely for failing to protect widows (Isaiah 1:17, 23; 10:2; Jeremiah 7:6; Ezekiel 22:7; Zechariah 7:10; Malachi 3:5).

Third, the procession is leaving the city gate. Jewish burial took place outside the city walls. The two crowds — Jesus's followers coming toward the gate, and the funeral procession moving outward — converge at the entrance. This is a chance encounter in narrative terms; Jesus did not seek out this woman or know of her situation in advance.

The combination of widow, only son, and death activates a very specific scriptural memory that the author is about to exploit.

Verses 13-17: Raising the Dead and the People's Response

"When the Lord saw her, He felt compassion for her, and said to her, 'Do not weep.' And He came up and touched the coffin; and the bearers came to a halt. And He said, 'Young man, I say to you, arise!' The dead man sat up and began to speak. And Jesus gave him back to his mother. Fear gripped them all, and they began glorifying God, saying, 'A great prophet has arisen among us!' and, 'God has visited His people!' This report concerning Him went out all over Judea and in all the surrounding district."

The author refers to Jesus as "the Lord" (ho kyrios). This is notable because Luke uses this designation for Jesus in narrative (not just in dialogue) more frequently than the other Synoptic authors. In the LXX, kyrios is the standard translation of both the divine name YHWH and the title Adonai. When Luke the narrator calls Jesus "the Lord" in the same passage where the crowd will declare that "God has visited His people," the overlap of terminology is not accidental.

Jesus "felt compassion for her" (esplanchnisthē ep' autē). The verb splanchnizomai refers to a visceral, gut-level response — the term is derived from splanchna, the internal organs. In the LXX and related literature, this kind of deep compassion is characteristically attributed to God (Psalm 103:13 [102:13 LXX]; Isaiah 49:15; Jeremiah 31:20 [38:20 LXX]). When applied to Jesus, it carries the resonance of divine compassion enacted in a human encounter. The compassion is not solicited: no one asks Jesus to act. He sees, he is moved, and he acts.

His first words are to the mother: "Do not weep" (mē klaie). This command would be absurd — even cruel — unless what follows will remove the cause of her weeping. The command only makes sense as the prelude to an action that will undo the death.

Jesus then "touched the coffin" (hēpsato tēs sorou). The word soros refers to an open bier or coffin used to carry the body to burial. Under the purity regulations of Numbers 19:11-16, touching a corpse or anything associated with burial rendered a person ritually unclean for seven days. Jesus's touch of the bier is a deliberate crossing of the boundary between the living and the dead, between the clean and the unclean. As with his touching of the leper in 5:13, Jesus does not become contaminated by contact with impurity; instead, his touch reverses the condition that produced the impurity. The bearers halt — the funeral procession is stopped.

Jesus addresses the dead man directly: "Young man, I say to you, arise!" (neaniske, soi legō, egerthēti). He speaks to a corpse as though it can hear. The command egerthēti is the same verb (egeirō) used throughout the New Testament writings for resurrection. The dead man "sat up and began to speak" (anekathisen ho nekros kai ērxato lalein) — both actions demonstrating that life has fully returned. The detail that he "began to speak" is not merely proof of life but proof of restored personhood: the man is not a revived body but a living person.

Then comes a phrase loaded with scriptural resonance: "and Jesus gave him back to his mother" (kai edōken auton tē mētri autou). This exact phrase appears in the LXX of 1 Kings 17:23, where Elijah raises the son of the widow of Zarephath: "and Elijah gave him to his mother" (kai edōken auton tē mētri autou). The verbal correspondence is too precise to be coincidental. The author is deliberately presenting Jesus as acting in the pattern of Elijah — the same Elijah that Jesus cited in the Nazareth synagogue (4:25-26) as the prophet whom God sent to a widow outside Israel.

The parallels between the two scenes are extensive:

  • Elijah encountered a widow whose only son had died (1 Kings 17:17-24). Jesus encounters a widow whose only son has died.
  • Elijah was moved to act on behalf of the widow. Jesus is moved by compassion.
  • Elijah restored the son to life. Jesus raises the young man.
  • Elijah "gave him to his mother." Jesus "gave him back to his mother."
  • After the raising, the widow of Zarephath declared: "Now I know that you are a man of God, and that the word of the LORD in your mouth is truth" (1 Kings 17:24). After this raising, the crowd declares: "A great prophet has arisen among us!"

There is also a parallel with Elisha, who raised the son of the Shunammite woman (2 Kings 4:32-37) — the second prophetic precedent Jesus cited at Nazareth. The author is showing that Jesus does not merely cite the Elijah-Elisha pattern; he enacts it.

The crowd's response contains two declarations. First: "A great prophet has arisen among us" (prophētēs megas ēgerthē en hēmin). The verb ēgerthē ("has arisen") uses the same root as Jesus's command to the dead man (egerthēti). The people see in this act the mark of prophetic identity. The phrase "great prophet" (prophētēs megas) echoes the description of Moses in Deuteronomy 34:10 ("there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses") and the promise of Deuteronomy 18:15 ("The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers"). Whether the crowd has the Mosaic promise specifically in mind or is speaking more generally, they recognize that the prophetic line — long dormant — is active again.

Second: "God has visited His people" (epeskepsato ho theos ton laon autou). The verb episkeptomai ("to visit, to look upon, to attend to") is the same word Zechariah used in the Benedictus: "Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for He has visited and accomplished redemption for His people" (1:68). It is the word used of God's intervention on Israel's behalf at the Exodus (Genesis 50:24-25; Exodus 3:16; 4:31) and at the return from exile (Ruth 1:6; Jeremiah 29:10 LXX). When the crowd says "God has visited His people," they are interpreting this event not as a human wonder but as a divine act — God Himself is intervening in Israel's story through this prophet.

The report goes out "all over Judea and in all the surrounding district." As with the use of "Judea" in 4:44, this likely means the land of the Jews broadly, not the southern province specifically.

Verses 18-20: John's Question from Prison

"The disciples of John reported to him about all these things. Summoning two of his disciples, John sent them to the Lord, saying, 'Are You the Expected One, or do we look for someone else?'"

The transition is critical. John's disciples report to him "about all these things" (peri pantōn toutōn) — the healings, the centurion's faith, the raising at Nain, the crowds' declaration that God has visited His people. John is in prison (3:19-20 established his imprisonment by Herod Antipas). He has heard what Jesus is doing, and his response is not confident affirmation but a question: "Are You the Expected One (ho erchomenos), or do we look for someone else?"

The phrase ho erchomenos ("the coming one") is the title John himself used in 3:16: "One is coming who is mightier than I" (erchetai de ho ischyroteros mou). John had announced someone whose ministry would involve baptism "with the Holy Spirit and fire" (3:16) and who would carry a "winnowing fork" to "thoroughly clear His threshing floor" — gathering wheat into the barn and burning chaff with "unquenchable fire" (3:17). John expected a figure of judgment, a figure who would execute the divine separation between righteous and wicked.

What John is hearing about Jesus does not match this expectation. Jesus is healing the sick, raising the dead, proclaiming good news to the poor, and showing compassion to widows. He is not wielding the winnowing fork. He is not baptizing with fire. He is not executing judgment on the wicked. There is no sign of the axe laid at the root of the trees (3:9). The question from prison is not a failure of faith but a genuine theological puzzle: if you are the one I announced, why does your ministry look like mercy rather than judgment?

This connects directly to the Nazareth synagogue scene where Jesus read Isaiah 61:1-2a and stopped before "the day of vengeance of our God" (4:18-19). Jesus is enacting the favorable year of the Lord without the day of vengeance, and this is precisely what confuses John, whose preaching focused on the judgment side of the equation.

Verses 21-23: Jesus's Answer

"At that very time He cured many people of diseases and afflictions and evil spirits; and He gave sight to many who were blind. And He answered and said to them, 'Go and report to John what you have seen and heard: the blind receive sight, the lame walk, those with leprosy are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, the poor have the good news proclaimed to them. Blessed is the one who does not take offense at Me.'"

Jesus does not answer John's question with a direct yes or no. Instead, he performs a series of healings in the presence of John's messengers and then sends them back with a description of what they have witnessed. The description is not random; it is a carefully composed echo of Isaiah's prophecies:

  • "The blind receive sight" — Isaiah 35:5: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened."
  • "The lame walk" — Isaiah 35:6: "Then the lame will leap like a deer."
  • "Those with leprosy are cleansed" — Isaiah 53:4 (the servant bearing sicknesses) and the Levitical legislation that only God could cure leprosy (the priest could only declare clean what God had already healed).
  • "The deaf hear" — Isaiah 35:5: "And the ears of the deaf will be unstopped."
  • "The dead are raised up" — Isaiah 26:19: "Your dead will live; their corpses will rise."
  • "The poor have the good news proclaimed to them" — Isaiah 61:1: "He has anointed Me to bring good news to the poor."

The list draws from Isaiah 35 and Isaiah 61 — passages about God's eschatological restoration of Israel. Jesus's answer to John is: look at what is happening through the lens of Isaiah. The Coming One was supposed to do what Isaiah described, and what Isaiah described is exactly what is happening. Jesus is not denying that judgment will come; he is asserting that his present ministry fulfills the restoration promises. John expected the winnowing fork first; Jesus is saying that the healing comes first.

The final sentence is the sharpest element: "Blessed is the one who does not take offense at Me" (makarios estin hos ean mē skandalisthē en emoi). The word skandalizō means to stumble, to be tripped up, to find cause for rejection. The "offense" is the gap between expectation and reality. Jesus pronounces a blessing on anyone who can accept a messiah who heals rather than judges, who proclaims the favorable year without immediately executing the day of vengeance. This is directed at John, but it applies to anyone whose expectations about how God should act are being confounded by what God is actually doing.

Verses 24-28: Jesus's Testimony about John

"When the messengers of John had left, He began to speak to the crowds about John: 'What did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken by the wind? But what did you go out to see? A man dressed in soft clothing? Those who are splendidly clothed and live in luxury are found in royal palaces! But what did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and one who is more than a prophet. This is the one about whom it is written: "Behold, I send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You." I say to you, among those born of women there is no one greater than John; yet the one who is least in the kingdom of God is greater than he.'"

Jesus waits until John's messengers have left before speaking about John. He addresses the crowds, not John's disciples. His testimony unfolds through three rhetorical questions, each one escalating.

First question: "A reed shaken by the wind?" — The answer is implicitly no. John is not an unstable figure, bending with every breeze of popular opinion. The image of a reed swaying in the wind connotes weakness, pliability, lack of backbone. Some scholars note that Herod Antipas used the image of a reed on his coins minted at Tiberias; if so, the contrast is pointed — John is not like Herod, who bends to political expediency.

Second question: "A man dressed in soft clothing?" — Again, no. Those who wear luxurious garments and live in comfort are in royal palaces (en tois basileiois). John is in Herod's prison, not Herod's court. The irony is sharp: John is indeed in a palace — but as a prisoner, not as a courtier. He suffers precisely because he refused to wear the soft clothing of accommodation and told Herod the truth about his marriage (3:19).

Third question: "A prophet?" — Yes, and more than a prophet. Jesus then cites Malachi 3:1: "Behold, I send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You." In Malachi, the passage reads: "Behold, I am going to send My messenger, and he will clear the way before Me." The original context in Malachi describes a messenger who prepares the way for God Himself to come to His temple. The shift from "before Me" to "before You" in Jesus's citation transforms the text into a statement about the relationship between John (the messenger) and Jesus (the one whose way is prepared). This carries an extraordinary implication: if John is the messenger who prepares the way, and if in Malachi's original the way is being prepared for YHWH's coming, then Jesus is occupying the position that Malachi assigned to God Himself.

Jesus's concluding statement about John is paradoxical: "Among those born of women there is no one greater than John" — John is the greatest figure in all of human history up to this point. "Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of God is greater than he" — the person with the lowest status within the kingdom of God exceeds even John. This is not a demotion of John but a statement about the magnitude of the transition that is occurring. The kingdom of God represents such a radical new reality that even its least member participates in something greater than the greatest figure of the old order. John stands at the threshold: he is the culmination of everything that came before, but the era he announces surpasses everything he himself represents.

Verses 29-30: Two Responses to God's Purpose

"When all the people and the tax collectors heard this, they acknowledged God's justice, having been baptized with the baptism of John. But the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected God's purpose for themselves, not having been baptized by John."

These two verses are among the most important editorial comments in Luke's Gospel. The author steps back from narrating events to describe two sharply contrasting responses to God's work through John.

"All the people and the tax collectors" (ho laos hapas kai hoi telōnai) — the general populace and the most socially despised class — "acknowledged God's justice" (edikaiōsan ton theon), literally "justified God" or "declared God righteous." They acknowledged that God was right in what He was doing through John. The evidence of their acknowledgment was that they had submitted to John's baptism — they had accepted his call to repentance.

"The Pharisees and the lawyers" (hoi Pharisaioi kai hoi nomikoi) — the religious experts, the guardians of Torah — "rejected God's purpose for themselves" (tēn boulēn tou theou ēthetēsan eis heautous). The word boulē means "counsel, purpose, plan." The verb atheteō means "to set aside, to nullify, to reject." By refusing John's baptism, they rejected what God intended for them. The phrase "for themselves" (eis heautous) is devastating: God's purpose was directed toward them, available to them, and they set it aside. Their rejection did not thwart God's overall plan, but it excluded them from participating in it.

The division is the same one that runs throughout Luke: the people on the margins — tax collectors, sinners, the poor, the sick — receive what God is doing, while the religious establishment resists it. This is not because marginalized people are morally superior but because they have less investment in the existing order and less reason to resist the disruption that repentance requires.

Verses 31-35: The Parable of Children in the Marketplace

"'To what then shall I compare the people of this generation, and what are they like? They are like children who sit in the marketplace and call to one another, and they say, "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a song of mourning, and you did not weep." For John the Baptist has come eating no bread and drinking no wine, and you say, "He has a demon!" The Son of Man has come eating and drinking, and you say, "Look, a gluttonous man and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" Yet wisdom is vindicated by all her children.'"

Jesus draws a parable from everyday life: children in the marketplace complaining that the other children will not play along. One group played happy music (the flute), but the others would not dance. They played funeral music (a dirge), but the others would not mourn. The point is that nothing satisfies "the people of this generation" (hoi anthrōpoi tēs geneas tautēs). Whatever God does, they find reason to reject it.

The application is explicit. John came with an ascetic lifestyle — "eating no bread and drinking no wine" — the marks of a Nazirite-like consecration, a life of radical self-denial and prophetic intensity. The response: "He has a demon." His severity was attributed to demonic possession. Jesus came with the opposite approach — "eating and drinking," participating in the normal social life of meals and fellowship. The response: "a gluttonous man and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners." His sociability was attributed to moral laxity.

The phrase "the Son of Man" (ho huios tou anthrōpou) appears here for the first time in this chapter. In Daniel 7:13-14, "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven and receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom from the Ancient of Days. In Aramaic bar enash simply means "a human being," but Daniel's vision invested the phrase with a specific referent: a figure who receives God's authority. Jesus's use of the title in this context — "the Son of Man has come eating and drinking" — associates the Daniel 7 figure with the humble, accessible ministry that the religious critics are rejecting.

The final statement — "wisdom is vindicated by all her children" (edikaiōthē hē sophia apo pantōn tōn teknōn autēs) — personifies wisdom (sophia) as a mother whose children prove she was right. "Vindicated" (edikaiōthē) is the same verb used in verse 29 for the people who "justified God." The children of wisdom are those who responded rightly to both John and Jesus — who danced when the flute played and wept when the dirge sounded, who accepted the call to repentance and welcomed the ministry of mercy. Their lives are the proof that God's strategy — sending both the ascetic prophet and the compassionate healer — was wise.

Verses 36-39: The Setting at the Pharisee's House

"Now one of the Pharisees was requesting Him to eat with him, and He entered the Pharisee's house and reclined at the table. And there was a woman in the city who was a sinner; and when she learned that He was reclining at the table in the Pharisee's house, she brought an alabaster vial of perfume, and standing behind Him at His feet, weeping, she began to wet His feet with her tears, and she kept wiping them with the hair of her head, and kissing His feet and anointing them with the perfume. Now when the Pharisee who had invited Him saw this, he said to himself, 'If this man were a prophet, He would know who and what sort of person this woman is who is touching Him, that she is a sinner.'"

A Pharisee invites Jesus to eat at his house. Jesus accepts — he is not uniformly hostile to Pharisees, and he has just been described as one who "eats and drinks" rather than following John's ascetic practice. The meal is a formal setting: Jesus "reclines at the table" (kateklithē), the standard posture for a formal dinner in the Greco-Roman world, where diners lay on couches with their feet extended behind them.

A woman enters. The author identifies her only as "a woman in the city who was a sinner" (gynē hētis ēn en tē polei hamartōlos). The word hamartōlos ("sinner") in this context probably indicates a woman known for sexual immorality, given the social stigma and the Pharisee's later reaction, though the text does not specify the nature of her sin. The author does not name her. She is not identified as Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, or any other named figure — those identifications are later tradition, not the text's own claim.

Her actions are extravagant and socially transgressive. She stands behind Jesus at his feet (possible because of the reclining posture), weeps, wets his feet with tears, wipes them with her hair (a shocking act — respectable women did not unbind their hair in public), kisses his feet repeatedly (the imperfect tense katephilei indicates continuous action), and anoints them with perfume from an alabaster flask. Every element of her behavior crosses social boundaries: a woman of known sinful reputation touching a teacher, unbinding her hair, performing acts of intimate devotion in a public setting.

The Pharisee — named Simon in verse 40, though the author does not introduce the name until Jesus addresses him — reacts internally: "If this man were a prophet, He would know who and what sort of person this woman is who is touching Him, that she is a sinner." Simon's reasoning has two premises: (1) a true prophet would have supernatural knowledge of people's character, and (2) a true prophet, knowing this woman is a sinner, would refuse her touch to avoid contamination. Both premises reveal Simon's categories: prophets maintain purity by keeping distance from sinners. The irony is that Jesus does know exactly who she is — as the next verses will demonstrate — and his response to that knowledge is the opposite of what Simon expects.

Verses 40-43: The Parable of Two Debtors

"And Jesus responded and said to him, 'Simon, I have something to say to you.' And he replied, 'Say it, Teacher.' 'A moneylender had two debtors: one owed five hundred denarii, and the other fifty. When they were unable to repay, he graciously forgave them both. So which of them will love him more?' Simon answered and said, 'I suppose the one for whom he forgave more.' And He said to him, 'You have judged correctly.'"

Jesus addresses Simon by name, demonstrating the very prophetic knowledge Simon doubted he possessed. He tells a parable — a brief analogy from everyday economic life. A creditor (daneistēs, "moneylender") has two debtors. One owes five hundred denarii and the other fifty. A denarius was approximately a day's wage for a laborer, so the debts represent roughly a year and a half of wages versus a month and a half — both significant, but one ten times larger than the other.

The key action: "when they were unable to repay, he graciously forgave them both" (mē echontōn autōn apodounai amphoterois echarisato). The verb charizomai means "to give freely, to forgive as an act of grace." The root is charis — grace, gift, favor. Neither debtor could pay; both were forgiven entirely. The forgiveness is unearned in both cases.

Jesus's question is about the response to forgiveness: "which of them will love him more?" Simon gives the obvious answer: "the one for whom he forgave more." Jesus confirms: "You have judged correctly" (orthōs ekrinas). Simon has provided the interpretive key to the scene he is witnessing, though he does not yet realize it.

Verses 44-47: Application to the Woman and Simon

"Turning toward the woman, He said to Simon, 'Do you see this woman? I entered your house; you gave Me no water for My feet, but she has wet My feet with her tears and wiped them with her hair. You gave Me no kiss; but she, since the time I came in, has not ceased to kiss My feet. You did not anoint My head with oil; but she anointed My feet with perfume. For this reason I say to you, her sins, which are many, have been forgiven, for she loved much; but the one who is forgiven little, loves little.'"

Jesus turns toward the woman but speaks to Simon. The physical gesture is significant: he directs Simon's attention to the person Simon has been dismissing. "Do you see this woman?" is not a question about visual perception — Simon has obviously seen her. It is a question about recognition: can Simon see what this woman actually represents?

Jesus then draws three contrasts between Simon's behavior and the woman's:

1. Water for feet: Simon did not provide water for Jesus's feet — a basic act of hospitality in a culture where guests walked dusty roads in sandals (Genesis 18:4; 19:2; 24:32; 43:24; Judges 19:21). The woman has washed his feet with her tears and dried them with her hair.

2. A kiss: Simon did not greet Jesus with a kiss — the standard greeting between host and honored guest (Genesis 29:13; 33:4; 2 Samuel 19:39). The woman has not stopped kissing his feet since he arrived.

3. Oil for the head: Simon did not anoint Jesus's head with oil — another mark of honor for a guest (Psalm 23:5; 141:5). The woman has anointed his feet with expensive perfume.

The contrasts establish that the Pharisee has failed in basic hospitality while the sinful woman has exceeded every convention of honor and devotion. The host has treated Jesus with indifference; the uninvited sinner has treated him with overwhelming love.

Then comes the interpretive statement: "Her sins, which are many, have been forgiven, for she loved much" (apheōntai hai hamartiai autēs hai pollai, hoti ēgapēsen poly). This sentence has been read two ways, and the grammar supports both: (a) her sins are forgiven because she loved much (love as the cause of forgiveness), or (b) her sins have been forgiven, which is why she loved much (forgiveness as the cause of love). The parable of the two debtors settles the question decisively in favor of reading (b). In the parable, forgiveness comes first, and love is the response to forgiveness. The debtor who was forgiven more loved more — not the other way around. The woman's extravagant acts of devotion are not the basis of her forgiveness but the evidence of it. She loves much because she has been forgiven much. She knows the size of her debt, and she knows it has been cancelled.

The counterpart confirms this: "the one who is forgiven little, loves little." This is directed at Simon, who considers himself a minor debtor — a basically righteous person with small spiritual debts. Because he perceives his own need for forgiveness as small, his response to Jesus is correspondingly small: no water, no kiss, no oil. Simon's failure of hospitality is not mere rudeness; it is the outward sign of a man who does not believe he has been forgiven much, and therefore does not love much. Whether his actual sin-debt is truly smaller than the woman's is not the point. The point is his perception of it.

Verses 48-50: Forgiveness Declared and the Question of Authority

"Then He said to her, 'Your sins have been forgiven.' Those who were reclining at the table with Him began to say to themselves, 'Who is this man who even forgives sins?' And He said to the woman, 'Your faith has saved you; go in peace.'"

Jesus now addresses the woman directly: "Your sins have been forgiven" (apheōntai sou hai hamartiai). The perfect passive tense (apheōntai) indicates a completed action with continuing effect. Whether Jesus is declaring a forgiveness that has already occurred (consistent with the parable, where her love is the response to a prior forgiveness) or pronouncing forgiveness in this moment, the effect within the narrative is the same: Jesus speaks with the authority to declare the state of a person's sins before God.

The other dinner guests immediately recognize the magnitude of this claim: "Who is this man who even forgives sins?" (tis houtos estin hos kai hamartias aphiēsin). This is the same reaction the scribes and Pharisees had in 5:21 when Jesus told the paralytic, "Your sins are forgiven": "Who is this man who speaks blasphemies? Who can forgive sins, but God alone?" The question is exactly right. In Israel's understanding, the forgiveness of sins was God's prerogative (Exodus 34:6-7; Psalm 103:3; Isaiah 43:25; 44:22; Jeremiah 31:34). No prophet, no priest, no king had the authority to forgive sins on his own authority. Priests administered the sacrificial system through which God granted atonement, but they did not themselves forgive. The claim to forgive sins is a claim to exercise a divine function.

Jesus's final words to the woman are: "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (hē pistis sou sesōken se; poreuou eis eirēnēn). Several elements are critical.

First, "your faith" (hē pistis sou). After the extended discussion of love and forgiveness, Jesus identifies faith — not love, not her tears, not the anointing — as the operative reality. Faith is what connects a person to forgiveness. Love is the fruit of forgiveness, but faith is the means by which forgiveness is received. The woman's extravagant acts were expressions of faith — she came to Jesus believing that he could and would receive her despite her known sinfulness. Her faith was the trust that brought her into the room despite every social reason to stay away.

Second, "has saved you" (sesōken se). The verb sōzō means "to save, to rescue, to deliver, to heal." The perfect tense (sesōken) indicates a completed action with ongoing effect. Jesus used the same phrase for the woman with the flow of blood (8:48) and for the grateful Samaritan leper (17:19). In each case, sōzō encompasses more than physical healing; it describes a comprehensive rescue that includes restoration of relationship with God.

Third, "go in peace" (poreuou eis eirēnēn). This is the standard Hebraic blessing of dismissal (Judges 18:6; 1 Samuel 1:17; 20:42; 2 Samuel 15:9). The Hebrew shalom and Greek eirēnē carry the sense of wholeness, completeness, well-being — not merely the absence of conflict but the presence of everything needed for flourishing. The woman is sent out with her debt cancelled, her relationship with God restored, and a new wholeness that her former life did not possess.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 7 is organized around a single sustained question: who recognizes what God is doing through Jesus, and who does not? The answer systematically overturns the expected categories.

The centurion episode (vv. 1-10) presents a Gentile military officer whose understanding of Jesus's authority surpasses anything Jesus has found in Israel. The Jewish elders argue that the centurion is "worthy" because of his deeds; the centurion himself denies his worthiness and relies entirely on faith in Jesus's authoritative word. Faith, not ethnic identity or meritorious works, is what Jesus commends. This continues the Elijah-Elisha pattern from the Nazareth sermon: God's saving work reaches those outside Israel's boundaries when they respond with trust.

The raising at Nain (vv. 11-17) demonstrates that Jesus acts in the prophetic pattern of Elijah — not merely citing the precedent (as at Nazareth) but reenacting it. The verbal echo of 1 Kings 17:23 ("he gave him to his mother") makes the connection unmistakable. The crowd's response — "God has visited His people" — echoes Zechariah's Benedictus and identifies this event as a divine act within Israel's history of redemption.

John's question and Jesus's answer (vv. 18-35) address the gap between expectation and reality. John expected a figure of judgment; Jesus's ministry is characterized by mercy. Jesus's answer, composed entirely of Isaiah's restoration prophecies, redefines what "the Coming One" looks like: not the winnowing fork first but healing, restoration, and good news to the poor. The blessing on those who are not offended by this redefinition is the chapter's central challenge. The parable of the children in the marketplace exposes the irrationality of those who reject both John's asceticism and Jesus's accessibility — nothing God does satisfies them.

The editorial comment on two responses (vv. 29-30) states the chapter's thesis most directly: tax collectors and the common people accepted God's purpose by submitting to John's baptism; Pharisees and lawyers rejected God's purpose for themselves by refusing it. The division is not between good people and bad people but between those willing to acknowledge their need for repentance and those who are not.

The anointing at Simon's house (vv. 36-50) dramatizes this division in a single scene. The sinful woman, who knows the enormity of her debt and has experienced its cancellation, responds with extravagant love. The Pharisee, who considers his debt small, responds with minimal hospitality and internal critique. The parable of the two debtors establishes the logic: forgiveness precedes love, and the degree of love corresponds to the awareness of forgiveness received. Jesus's declaration that the woman's faith has saved her names what has been operating throughout the chapter — faith is the posture of those who recognize what God is doing and respond accordingly, whether they are Gentile centurions, weeping sinners, or common people accepting a prophet's baptism.

The chapter thus builds a coherent argument that links directly to what precedes and what follows. Luke 6 established Jesus's authoritative teaching about the kingdom's inverted values (blessed are the poor, woe to the rich). Luke 7 shows those inverted values at work in real encounters: the outsiders and sinners who should be furthest from God are closest, while the insiders and righteous who should be closest are furthest away. Luke 8 will then explore, through the parable of the soils, why responses to the word vary — some hear and endure, some hear and fall away. The logic flows from teaching (chapter 6) to demonstration (chapter 7) to explanation (chapter 8).
