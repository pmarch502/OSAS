Matthew 15

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 14): After the third major discourse (the parables discourse in chapter 13, which explained the division between those who hear and those who refuse to hear), the author presented a series of events that demonstrate both the power and the rejection that attend Jesus's ministry. Herod the tetrarch hears of Jesus and identifies him as John the Baptist risen from the dead — the report of John's execution follows (14:1-12). Jesus withdraws and feeds five thousand men (besides women and children) in a deserted place with five loaves and two fish, producing twelve baskets of fragments (14:13-21). The twelve baskets correspond to the twelve tribes — the provision is for Israel. Jesus then walks on the sea, and Peter, who attempts to come to him on the water, begins to sink when he sees the wind and is afraid, prompting Jesus's rebuke: "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (14:31). Those in the boat worship him, saying, "Truly you are the Son of God" (14:33). The chapter closes at Gennesaret, where the sick touch the fringe of his garment and are made whole (14:34-36). Chapter 14 establishes two themes that carry directly into chapter 15: the question of who Jesus is (the Baptist risen? the Son of God?) and the image of Jesus as the provider who feeds his people in the wilderness.

What comes after (Matthew 16): The Pharisees and Sadducees — united for the first time in the document — approach Jesus demanding "a sign from heaven" (16:1). Jesus refuses, calling them a "wicked and adulterous generation" and pointing again to "the sign of Jonah" (16:4; compare 12:39). He then warns his disciples to beware of "the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:6), which the disciples misunderstand as being about bread. Jesus reminds them of both feedings — the five loaves for the five thousand (with twelve baskets remaining) and the seven loaves for the four thousand (with seven baskets remaining) — and asks, "How is it that you fail to understand?" (16:9-11). The disciples finally grasp that "leaven" refers to the teaching (didache) of the Pharisees and Sadducees (16:12). Peter then confesses Jesus as "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16), and Jesus begins to reveal his coming death and resurrection (16:21). The chapter completes what chapter 15 initiates: the exposure of the Pharisees' teaching as corrupt leaven, the contrast between their blindness and the Gentile woman's sight, and the significance of the two feedings.

How chapter 15 connects them: Chapter 14 showed Jesus providing for Israel in the wilderness (five thousand fed, twelve baskets) and being recognized as the Son of God by those in the boat. Chapter 15 opens with a challenge from Jerusalem's religious authorities about the tradition of the elders, which Jesus turns into a confrontation about their displacement of God's commandments with human tradition. The chapter then redefines defilement as a matter of the heart, not of external contact — a principle that the author immediately demonstrates by sending Jesus into Gentile territory, where a Canaanite woman displays faith that surpasses what the Pharisees have shown. Jesus returns to heal crowds who "glorified the God of Israel" and feeds four thousand with seven loaves and seven baskets remaining. Chapter 16 then draws the threads together: the Pharisees demand a sign despite having been shown everything, and Jesus warns against their leaven. The movement across the three chapters is from Israel's feeding (ch. 14) through the exposure of Israel's leaders and the demonstration of Gentile faith (ch. 15) to the explicit contrast between the leaders' failed understanding and the disciples' emerging confession (ch. 16). Chapter 15 is the pivot — the chapter where the author shows that the purity boundaries maintained by the Pharisees are exactly what the kingdom is overflowing.

Jerusalem's Challenge: Human Tradition Against Divine Commandment (vv. 1-9)

"Then Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem (apo Ierosolymon), saying, 'Why do your disciples transgress the tradition of the elders (ten paradosin ton presbuteron)? For they do not wash their hands when they eat.'"

The delegation's origin is significant: "from Jerusalem." Throughout the document, Jerusalem functions as the center of institutional religious authority. These are not local Galilean Pharisees encountered in passing; they are representatives dispatched from the capital. This suggests a formal investigation. The conflict that began with local disputes (9:3, 9:11, 9:34, 12:2, 12:14, 12:24) has now attracted the attention of the Jerusalem establishment.

The charge concerns "the tradition of the elders" (he paradosis ton presbuteron). This phrase refers to the oral traditions that supplemented the written Torah — interpretive rulings and customs attributed to earlier generations of teachers, later codified in the Mishnah. Hand-washing before eating was not a commandment of the Torah itself; it was a practice originating in priestly purity regulations (the priests washed before eating consecrated food, per Leviticus 22:1-9) that some Pharisaic teachers extended to all meals as a standard of piety. The assumption behind the charge is that these oral traditions carry divine authority equivalent to the written Torah. The Pharisees treat the disciples' failure to observe this tradition as a "transgression" (parabainousin) — the same verb used for violating actual commandments.

Jesus does not defend his disciples' behavior. Instead, he counterattacks: "Why do you also transgress the commandment of God (ten entolen tou theou) by your tradition (dia ten paradosin hymon)?"

The structure of his response is precise. He matches their question with a parallel question, but changes the stakes. They accused his disciples of transgressing the tradition of the elders. He accuses them of transgressing the commandment of God — and identifies their tradition as the instrument of the transgression. The word "also" (kai) is pointed: you accuse my disciples, but you also transgress — and your transgression is against a higher authority.

The specific case: the commandment to honor parents (vv. 4-6). "For God commanded (ho gar theos eipen), saying, 'Honor your father and your mother,' and, 'Whoever reviles father or mother, let him surely die (thanato teleutato).'"

Jesus cites two texts together. The first is the fifth commandment of the Decalogue (Exodus 20:12, Deuteronomy 5:16). The second is from Exodus 21:17 (or Leviticus 20:9), prescribing the death penalty for cursing one's parents. By pairing these, Jesus establishes the seriousness of the obligation: honoring parents is not advisory but mandatory, backed by the severest sanction. The introductory formula — "God commanded" (ho theos eipen) — identifies the source as God himself, not Moses, not the elders, not tradition. This sets up the contrast with what the Pharisees say.

"But you say (hymeis de legete): 'If anyone tells his father or his mother, "What you would have gained from me is given to God" (doron, a gift/offering), he need not honor his father.' So for the sake of your tradition you have made void the word of God (ekyrosaie ton logon tou theou dia ten paradosin hymon)."

The practice Jesus describes is the corban declaration (the parallel in Mark 7:11 uses the word korban explicitly). A person could declare that the resources that would have been used to support aging parents were instead dedicated to God — an offering (doron). Once the declaration was made, the resources were formally pledged to the Temple, and the person was no longer obligated to use them for parental support. Whether the resources were actually given to the Temple is debatable; some scholars suggest the declaration functioned as a legal maneuver that released the person from family obligation without necessarily transferring the funds. In either case, the practical result was the same: parents went unsupported, and the obligation imposed by the fifth commandment was nullified through a religious technicality.

Jesus's assessment is devastating: "you have made void the word of God" (ekyrosaie ton logon tou theou). The verb akyroo means to invalidate, to deprive of authority, to annul. The Pharisees are not merely bending the commandment; they have canceled it. And the instrument of cancellation is their own tradition (dia ten paradosin hymon). The pronoun "your" (hymon) is emphatic — this is not God's tradition but theirs, a human construction that has usurped divine authority.

The underlying logic mirrors the Hosea 6:6 principle already established at 9:13 and 12:7: "I desire mercy and not sacrifice." The Pharisees have prioritized a form of religious devotion (the doron/offering) over the actual relational obligation (caring for parents). The sacrificial system is being used to evade the moral command. This is precisely the pattern Hosea condemned: religious performance that displaces covenant loyalty.

The Isaiah citation (vv. 7-9). "You hypocrites (hypokritai)! Well did Isaiah prophesy of you, when he said: 'This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me (he de kardia auton porro apechei ap' emou); in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men (didaskontes didaskalias entalmata anthropon).'"

This is Isaiah 29:13. The quotation follows the LXX more closely than the Hebrew text. In the Hebrew of Isaiah 29:13, the phrase reads "their fear of me is a commandment of men learned by rote" (mitsvat anashim melumadah); the LXX renders this as "teaching the commandments of men as doctrines" (didaskontes entalmata anthropon kai didaskalias). The author's rendering sharpens the application: the Pharisees' traditions are human commandments masquerading as divine teaching.

The word "hypocrites" (hypokritai) has appeared before in the document. At 6:2, 6:5, and 6:16, it described those who perform piety for human audience. At 7:5, it was applied to those who judge others while ignoring their own faults. Here, the term identifies those whose external religious observance conceals a heart that is far from God. The anatomy of hypocrisy as the author presents it is a gap between lips and heart — the mouth produces the right religious language, the hands perform the right religious actions, but the inner orientation (the kardia, the heart) is distant. This is not mere inconsistency; it is a structural inversion, where religious form actively serves as cover for disobedience to God's actual requirements.

The phrase "in vain they worship me" (maten de sebontai me) is particularly severe. Their worship is not merely imperfect or incomplete; it is maten — futile, empty, pointless. Worship that elevates human tradition over God's commandment is not worship at all. It accomplishes nothing before God. The Pharisees believe they are serving God more devotedly than anyone; Jesus declares their service void.

What Defiles: External and Internal (vv. 10-20)

"And he called the people to him and said to them, 'Hear and understand (akouete kai syniete): not what goes into the mouth defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth — this defiles a person.'"

Jesus now turns from the Pharisees to the crowd (ton ochlon). The shift in audience is deliberate: the dispute with the Pharisees was a particular confrontation, but the principle he draws from it is for everyone. The imperative "hear and understand" (akouete kai syniete) echoes the parables discourse of chapter 13, where the divide between hearing and not hearing was the central theme (13:9, "He who has ears, let him hear"; 13:13-15, citing Isaiah 6:9-10 on hearing without understanding). Now Jesus calls the crowd to hear what the Pharisees cannot.

The principle itself is radical in its first-century context. The purity system of the Torah — Leviticus 11 through 15, with its careful delineation of clean and unclean foods, bodily emissions, skin conditions, and household contamination — operated on the premise that certain external contacts and consumptions rendered a person ritually impure. The Pharisees' hand-washing tradition was an extension of this system. Jesus does not directly name the Levitical food laws here, but his statement that "not what goes into the mouth defiles" stands in tension with the entire framework of dietary purity. The first-century hearer would recognize the force of this claim.

The word "defiles" (koinoi) means to make common, to render impure, to pollute. In Jewish usage, koinoo is the opposite of hagiazein (to sanctify, to set apart). To defile something is to remove it from the sphere of the holy. Jesus is relocating the source of defilement from the external (food entering the body) to the internal (what comes out of the person from the heart).

The Pharisees' reaction and Jesus's response (vv. 12-14). "Then the disciples came and said to him, 'Do you know that the Pharisees were offended (eskandalisthesan) when they heard this saying?'"

The verb skandalizo (to cause to stumble, to offend, to trip) is important in the document. At 5:29-30, it described what leads a person to sin. At 11:6, Jesus pronounced a blessing on those who are "not offended" (skandalisthe) by him. At 13:21, it described the response of those who fall away under pressure. At 13:57, Jesus's own townspeople "were offended" (eskandalizonto) by him. The Pharisees being "offended" places them in the category of those who stumble over what Jesus says and does — those who reject rather than receive.

The disciples' question implies concern: they may fear the consequences of antagonizing the Pharisees. Jesus's response addresses both the Pharisees' status and the disciples' anxiety.

"He answered, 'Every plant which my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted up (ekrizothesatai).'"

The image of planting and uprooting draws on a rich scriptural heritage. In Isaiah 60:21, Israel is called "the branch of my planting, the work of my hands, that I might be glorified." In Isaiah 61:3, the righteous are "oaks of righteousness, the planting of the LORD." In Jeremiah 45:4, God says, "What I have built I am breaking down, and what I have planted I am plucking up." The Psalms of Solomon 14:3-4 (a first-century Jewish text) speaks of the faithful as trees planted by God: "The paradise of the Lord, the trees of life, are his devout ones. Their planting is rooted forever; they shall not be uprooted all the days of heaven." The image consistently identifies God's people as God's planting, and uprooting as divine judgment on what does not belong.

By calling the Pharisees plants that "my heavenly Father has not planted," Jesus makes a claim about their origin and destiny. Their traditions, their authority, their entire interpretive framework — these are not from God. They were not planted by the Father. And what God has not planted, God will uproot. The passive voice (ekrizothesatai, "will be rooted up") identifies God as the agent of removal. This is not a threat from Jesus but a description of what will inevitably happen to religious systems that claim divine authority without divine origin.

"Let them alone; they are blind guides of the blind (hodegoi eisin typhloi typhlon). And if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit (eis bothynon pesountai)."

The command "let them alone" (aphete autous) tells the disciples to disengage — not to confront the Pharisees, not to be concerned about their offense, but to leave them to their trajectory. The image of blind guides was already anticipated by the author's use of blindness as a metaphor for spiritual non-perception (13:13-15, citing Isaiah 6). The Pharisees claim to be Israel's guides — teachers of the Torah, interpreters of God's will. Jesus declares them blind. The result is catastrophic not only for the guides but for those who follow them: both fall into the pit. The word bothynos (pit, ditch) carries overtones of the cisterns and traps of the prophetic literature (Jeremiah 18:20, 22; Psalm 7:15). Those who follow blind leaders share their fate.

This saying has direct continuity with the author's argument. If the Pharisees' tradition nullifies God's word (vv. 3-6), if their worship is vain (vv. 7-9), and if they are not God's planting (v. 13), then to follow them is to follow guides who cannot see where they are going. The disciples must understand this in order to detach from the Pharisees' authority without anxiety.

Peter's request and the explanation (vv. 15-20). "But Peter answered, 'Explain this parable to us (phrason hemin ten parabolen tauten).'"

Peter calls Jesus's statement a "parable" (parabole). The same dynamic occurred at 13:36, where the disciples asked for explanation of the wheat-and-weeds parable. The pattern shows the disciples repeatedly needing private instruction to grasp what Jesus teaches publicly — a motif the author uses to distinguish between hearing and understanding.

"And he said, 'Are you also still without understanding (akmen kai hymeis asynetoi este)?' The word "also" (kai) is a mild rebuke — the crowds may not understand, the Pharisees certainly do not, but the disciples should be further along than they are. The word "still" (akmen) indicates that their lack of comprehension is ongoing despite everything they have witnessed.

"Do you not see that whatever goes into the mouth passes into the stomach and is expelled (eis aphedrona ekballetai)? But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart (ek tes kardias exerchetai), and this defiles a person."

The argument is physiological and then theological. Food enters the body, passes through the digestive system, and is eliminated. It follows a path (mouth to stomach to elimination) that does not touch the essential person. The language is deliberately earthy — aphedron is a latrine, a privy. The author is demystifying the purity concern: food is material that passes through the body; it does not alter the person's standing before God.

But what comes out of the mouth has a different origin. It comes from the heart (kardia). The heart, as the author has consistently established, is the center of the person — the seat of will, intention, and fundamental orientation (5:8, 5:28, 6:21, 9:4, 12:34-35, 13:15, 13:19). In 12:34, Jesus told the Pharisees, "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks." The same principle is repeated here in expanded form. Speech reveals the heart, and the heart is where defilement originates.

"For out of the heart come evil thoughts (dialogismoi poneroi), murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander (blasphemiai). These are what defile a person. But to eat with unwashed hands does not defile anyone."

The list of evils proceeding from the heart closely tracks the Decalogue. "Murder" corresponds to the sixth commandment (Exodus 20:13). "Adultery" (moicheiai) and "sexual immorality" (porneiai) correspond to the seventh commandment (Exodus 20:14). "Theft" (klopai) corresponds to the eighth commandment (Exodus 20:15). "False witness" (pseudomartyriai) corresponds to the ninth commandment (Exodus 20:16). "Slander" (blasphemiai) — speaking harm against another, whether human or divine — covers the range of the third commandment (taking God's name in vain) and the broader prohibition on destructive speech. The opening item, "evil thoughts" (dialogismoi poneroi), names the internal origin that generates all the others — the interior deliberation from which external acts emerge.

The effect of this list is to reground defilement in the Torah's own moral commandments. The Pharisees are concerned with washing hands before eating; Jesus points to murder, adultery, theft, and lying. The irony is that the Pharisees' tradition, which claims to protect holiness, has distracted attention from the Torah's actual moral demands. True defilement is not a matter of unwashed hands but of a heart that produces evil thoughts and their corresponding actions.

The concluding statement — "to eat with unwashed hands does not defile anyone" — returns to the original question from verse 2 and answers it directly. The Pharisees asked why the disciples eat without washing; Jesus replies that this practice has nothing to do with defilement. The inclusio (vv. 2 and 20 both mentioning unwashed hands) marks the entire section as a single argument: from the Pharisees' accusation, through the exposure of their tradition as nullifying God's word, to the redefinition of defilement as an internal matter.

The Canaanite Woman: Faith Beyond the Boundary (vv. 21-28)

"And Jesus went away from there and withdrew (anechoresen) to the district of Tyre and Sidon."

The verb anechoresen (withdrew) has appeared at critical junctures in the document: Joseph withdrew to Egypt (2:14), Jesus withdrew to Galilee after John's arrest (4:12), Jesus withdrew after the Pharisees' murder plot (12:15). Each withdrawal follows a confrontation or threat. Here it follows the intense dispute about tradition and defilement. The geographical destination is significant: Tyre and Sidon are Gentile cities on the Phoenician coast, outside the land of Israel. Jesus has just declared that defilement is internal, not external; now the author shows him crossing the boundary that the purity system was designed to maintain — the boundary between Israel and the nations. The teaching and the action are inseparable.

Tyre and Sidon have already appeared in the document. At 11:21-22, Jesus pronounced woes on Chorazin and Bethsaida, declaring: "If the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes." Tyre and Sidon were held up as cities that would have been more responsive than Israel's own towns. Now Jesus enters their territory, and what follows will dramatize this very contrast.

"And behold, a Canaanite woman (gyne Chananaía) from that region came out and was crying, 'Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David (eleeson me, kyrie, huios Dauid); my daughter is severely oppressed by a demon.'"

The author's identification of this woman as "Canaanite" (Chananaía) is a deliberate choice. The parallel account in Mark 7:26 calls her "a Syrophoenician woman" — a contemporary ethnic designation. The author of this document uses the archaic term, which reaches back into the deepest strata of Israel's history. The Canaanites were the inhabitants of the land before Israel's conquest, the nations whom Israel was commanded to dispossess (Deuteronomy 7:1-2). They represented the quintessential outsider — the people who were religiously and ethnically other. By calling her "Canaanite," the author maximizes the distance between this woman and the covenant community of Israel. She is not merely a non-Jew; she is the ancient enemy, the antithesis of everything the purity system was built to separate Israel from.

Yet this Canaanite woman addresses Jesus with the two most significant titles in the document. "Lord" (kyrie) is the term used by the disciples and those who approach Jesus in faith (8:2, 8:6, 8:25, 14:28, 14:30). "Son of David" is the messianic title first spoken by the blind men at 9:27 and rooted in the promise of 2 Samuel 7:12-16. A Canaanite woman recognizes and confesses what the Pharisees from Jerusalem have refused to see. The irony, given what has just preceded in vv. 1-20, is severe: Israel's authorized teachers nullify God's word while a Canaanite woman calls on Israel's Messiah by his proper name.

Her request — "have mercy on me" (eleeson me) — uses the same verb (eleeo) from the same root as the eleos (mercy) of Hosea 6:6, the text that has governed the author's presentation of Jesus's disputes with the Pharisees (9:13, 12:7). She asks for the very thing God desires.

"But he did not answer her a word (ho de ouk apekrithe aute logon). And his disciples came and begged him, saying, 'Send her away (apolyson auten), for she is crying out after us.'"

Jesus's silence is startling. Throughout the document he has responded immediately to those who approach him in need — the leper (8:3), the centurion (8:7), the paralytic (9:2), the hemorrhaging woman (9:22). Here, for the only time in the narrative, he says nothing. The silence creates a test — not a rejection but a space in which the woman's persistence can be displayed.

The disciples' request — "send her away" — is ambiguous. It could mean "grant her request so she stops following us" or "dismiss her without help." In either case, their concern is pragmatic: she is making a scene and they want her gone. Their response recalls the feeding of the five thousand, where the disciples told Jesus to "send the crowds away" (14:15, using the same verb apolyson) so they could find food. In both cases, the disciples want to send people away, and Jesus provides instead.

"He answered, 'I was not sent (ouk apestalen) except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel (eis ta probata ta apolola oikou Israel).'"

This statement echoes 10:6 exactly, where Jesus commissioned the twelve: "Go nowhere among the Gentiles and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." The phrase "lost sheep" (probata apolola) recalls the shepherd imagery of 9:36 and its roots in Ezekiel 34, where God indicts Israel's shepherds for scattering and losing the flock and promises to seek the lost himself (Ezekiel 34:11-16). The verb "sent" (apestalen) is passive — Jesus identifies himself as one who has been sent by another (the Father) for a specific mission. That mission, at this stage, is directed to Israel.

The statement functions within the narrative not as a final refusal but as a statement of priority. The author has already shown Jesus responding positively to Gentiles within Israel's territory — the centurion's servant was healed at 8:5-13, and Jesus declared that "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven" (8:11). The mission is to Israel first, but not to Israel exclusively. What follows tests whether this woman's faith can cross the boundary that priority establishes.

"But she came and knelt before him (prosekynei auto), saying, 'Lord, help me (kyrie, boethei moi).'"

The verb proskyneo (to kneel, to worship, to do obeisance) is used in the document for worship of God and of Jesus. The magi did it (2:2, 11). The disciples did it after the sea-walking (14:33). A leper did it before requesting healing (8:2). The woman's posture is one of worship and supplication combined. Her address is stripped to essentials: "Lord, help me." No theological argument, no elaboration — just the bare cry.

"He answered, 'It is not right to take the children's bread (ton arton ton teknon) and throw it to the dogs (tois kynariois).'"

The metaphor is harsh. "Children" (tekna) refers to Israel. "Dogs" (kynaria, literally "little dogs" or "puppies") refers to Gentiles. The diminutive kynaria (rather than the more hostile kynes, "dogs") may soften the image slightly — these are household dogs, not the scavenging street dogs that the word "dog" typically evoked as a term of contempt for Gentiles in Jewish usage. Still, the image places the Canaanite woman in the position of a dog relative to the children of the household. "Bread" (artos) carries the resonance of the feeding miracle just narrated in chapter 14, where Jesus provided bread for Israel in the wilderness. The bread of the kingdom is for the children first.

"She said, 'Yes, Lord (nai, kyrie), yet even the dogs eat the crumbs (ton psichion) that fall from their masters' (ton kyrion auton) table.'"

The woman's response is remarkable for several reasons. First, she does not contest the metaphor or argue for equal standing. She accepts Jesus's framework — "Yes, Lord." She does not claim to be a child of the household. She accepts the designation and works within it. Second, she extends the metaphor: if the children are eating at the master's table, then there are crumbs. The abundance of the master's provision is such that even what falls from the table is sufficient for those not seated at it. She is arguing not from her own worthiness but from the superabundance of Jesus's provision. Third, the word "masters" (kyrion) echoes her address to Jesus as "Lord" (kyrie). He is the master (kyrios) of the household; she is asking for what falls from his table.

The logic is the same as the feeding of the five thousand: twelve baskets were left over after everyone had eaten (14:20). There was more than enough. The woman is claiming the surplus — the overflow of a provision so abundant that Israel cannot exhaust it. Her argument presupposes not scarcity but excess.

"Then Jesus answered her, 'O woman, great is your faith (megale sou he pistis)! Be it done for you as you desire (hos theleis).' And her daughter was healed instantly (apo tes horas ekeines)."

The phrase "great is your faith" (megale sou he pistis) stands in the document's gallery of faith commendations. The centurion received: "With no one in Israel have I found such faith" (8:10). The hemorrhaging woman received: "Your faith has made you well" (9:22). Peter received: "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (14:31). This Canaanite woman receives the most direct and emphatic commendation: "great is your faith." The adjective megale (great) appears nowhere else in the document as a modifier of faith. Two Gentiles — the centurion and this woman — receive the highest praise for faith in the entire narrative. The pattern is structurally devastating to any claim that Israel's leaders represent faithful response to God.

The phrase "be it done for you as you desire" (hos theleis) grants the woman's own will as the measure of the gift. Compare 8:13, where the centurion was told, "Go; let it be done for you as you have believed" (hos episteusas). In both cases, the scope of the gift matches the scope of the faith.

"From that very hour" (apo tes horas ekeines) — the same temporal marker used for the hemorrhaging woman's healing at 9:22. The healing is immediate and complete.

The placement of this episode immediately after the redefinition of defilement (vv. 1-20) is the author's interpretive key. Jesus has just declared that defilement comes from the heart, not from external contact. The Pharisees, who maintain external purity, have hearts full of tradition that nullifies God's word. This Canaanite woman, who is the epitome of external impurity in the Jewish system — a Gentile, a Canaanite, a woman — has a heart full of faith. The author's argument is enacted, not merely stated: the one whom the purity system excludes is the one who displays the faith that the purity system's guardians lack.

Healings on the Mountain and the Glorification of Israel's God (vv. 29-31)

"Jesus went on from there and walked beside the Sea of Galilee. And he went up on the mountain (eis to oros) and sat down there."

The geographical movement brings Jesus back from Gentile territory toward Galilee. The setting — a mountain beside the sea — echoes the posture of the mountain discourse (5:1, "he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his disciples came to him"). The mountain in the document consistently serves as a place of authoritative action and revelation. Sitting down (ekatheto) is the posture of a teacher or a king. The author sets the scene for a display of power.

"And great crowds came to him, bringing with them the lame, the blind, the mute, the crippled, and many others, and they put them at his feet, and he healed them."

The catalogue of afflictions — lame (cholous), blind (typhlous), mute (kophous), crippled (kyllous) — is not random. This list echoes Isaiah 35:5-6: "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy." Jesus cited this very text through allusion when he answered John the Baptist's question at 11:4-5: "The blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them." The healings are the sign of the messianic age that Isaiah prophesied — the restoration of Israel under the coming kingdom. The author presents these healings not as individual acts of compassion (though they are that) but as fulfillment of prophetic expectation.

"So that the crowd wondered (thaumadzein), when they saw the mute speaking, the crippled healthy, the lame walking, and the blind seeing. And they glorified the God of Israel (edoxasan ton theon tou Israel)."

The phrase "the God of Israel" (ton theon tou Israel) is striking. This designation appears nowhere else in the document. In Jewish speech, one would normally say "God" or "the Lord" or "the God of our fathers." The phrase "the God of Israel" is a way Gentiles or those outside Israel identify Israel's deity — it specifies which god is being praised (compare 1 Kings 8:15, 2 Chronicles 6:4, Ezra 1:3, where non-Israelites or formal contexts use the full designation). The author's use of this phrase here suggests that the crowd includes Gentiles, or at minimum is a mixed crowd in which those present stand at some distance from the covenant community. Having just returned from Tyre and Sidon, Jesus is in a border region where such a mixed crowd is plausible. The Gentile or semi-Gentile crowd sees the healings and glorifies Israel's God — not their own gods, not a generic deity, but the specific God of Israel. What the Pharisees from Jerusalem refused to see, this border-region crowd sees clearly: the God of Israel is acting through Jesus.

This is the Isaiah 35 vision being realized: the desert blooms, the blind see, the lame walk, and the nations witness it. The location — a mountain near the Sea of Galilee, on the border between Israel and the nations — is itself a statement. The kingdom's blessings are spilling over the boundaries, just as the Canaanite woman argued they would.

Feeding in the Wilderness: The Four Thousand (vv. 32-39)

"Then Jesus called his disciples to him and said, 'I have compassion (splagchnizomai) on the crowd because they have been with me now three days and have nothing to eat. And I am unwilling to send them away hungry, lest they faint on the way.'"

The verb splagchnizomai (to be moved with compassion) appeared at 9:36 (compassion on the harassed and helpless crowds) and at 14:14 (compassion on the sick before the feeding of the five thousand). Each occurrence precedes a major act of provision. The author uses this word to mark the moments when Jesus's internal response to human need generates external action. The compassion is visceral — from the splagchna, the bowels, the deepest seat of feeling.

The detail that the crowd has been with Jesus "three days" (hemerai treis) is significant. They have persisted in his presence, enduring hunger, for three days. Their persistence mirrors the Canaanite woman's persistence — both the woman and this crowd refuse to leave Jesus despite obstacles. "I am unwilling to send them away hungry" (ou thelo apolusai autous nesteis) uses the same verb (apolyo, to send away, to dismiss) that the disciples used when they told Jesus to send the Canaanite woman away (v. 23) and that appeared at 14:15 when the disciples urged him to dismiss the five thousand. Jesus's consistent position is that he will not send the needy away empty. Sending away is the disciples' instinct; providing is Jesus's.

"And the disciples said to him, 'Where are we to get enough bread in such a desolate place (en eremía) to feed so great a crowd?'"

The disciples' question is nearly identical to their response before the feeding of the five thousand (14:15-17). They have already witnessed one wilderness feeding, yet they ask the same question. The author does not comment on this failure of memory directly (that will come in 16:9-10, where Jesus rebukes them for forgetting), but records it as evidence of the disciples' persistent struggle to understand. The word eremia (wilderness, desolate place) connects to the wilderness traditions of Israel — the manna provision of Exodus 16, where God fed the entire nation in the desert. The disciples stand in the wilderness asking where bread will come from, having already seen God provide bread in the wilderness.

"And Jesus said to them, 'How many loaves do you have?' They said, 'Seven, and a few small fish.'"

The number seven carries significance throughout Israel's scripture. Seven is the number of completeness and covenant — the Sabbath is the seventh day, the land rests in the seventh year, there are seven lamps on the menorah, seven days of consecration for the priests (Leviticus 8:33), seven sprinklings of blood (Leviticus 4:6). Whether the seven loaves carry symbolic weight beyond their literal number is debated, but the author will treat the number as meaningful in 16:10, where Jesus asks the disciples to recall how many baskets remained from the seven loaves of the four thousand. The numbers matter.

"And directing the crowd to sit down on the ground, he took the seven loaves and the fish, and having given thanks (eucharistesas) he broke them and gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the crowds."

The sequence — taking, giving thanks, breaking, distributing through the disciples — is identical in structure to the feeding of the five thousand (14:19). The verb there was eulogesen (he blessed); here it is eucharistesas (he gave thanks). Both verbs are appropriate for the Jewish berakhah (blessing/thanksgiving) said before a meal. The consistency of the pattern underscores that both feedings are the same kind of act: the Lord of the table providing for his people through his appointed mediators (the disciples).

"And they all ate and were satisfied (echortasthesan). And they took up seven baskets full (hepta spyridas pleis) of the broken pieces left over."

The verb chortazo (to fill, to satisfy, to feed to the full) appeared at 5:6 in the beatitude: "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied (chortasthesontai)." The same verb is used for physical feeding at 14:20. Satisfaction — having enough, being filled — is a mark of God's provision in both its physical and spiritual dimensions.

The seven baskets (spyridas) contrast with the twelve baskets (kophinous) of the previous feeding. The words are different (spyris versus kophinos), and the numbers differ. In 16:9-10, Jesus will distinguish the two feedings precisely by these details: "Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many baskets (kophinous) you gathered? Or the seven loaves for the four thousand, and how many baskets (spyridas) you gathered?" The author treats the two feedings as distinct events with distinct significance.

The twelve baskets of the first feeding corresponded naturally to the twelve tribes of Israel. Seven baskets in the second feeding may correspond to the number of completeness or universality. Some interpreters have noted that Deuteronomy 7:1 lists seven nations inhabiting Canaan before Israel's arrival, and Acts 6:1-6 records seven men appointed to serve the Greek-speaking widows in the early community. The connection is speculative but suggestive: if the twelve baskets represented provision for Israel, the seven baskets may represent provision for the nations. This reading is supported by the surrounding context — Jesus has just healed a Canaanite woman's daughter, healed crowds who "glorified the God of Israel" (a phrase suggesting Gentile observers), and is feeding this multitude in what appears to be a mixed or border-region setting.

"Those who ate were four thousand men, besides women and children."

The number four thousand, like five thousand, includes only men (andres); women and children are in addition. The total crowd is substantially larger than four thousand. The author records both feedings with the same formula ("besides women and children," choris gynaikon kai paidion), ensuring the reader recognizes the parallel.

"And after sending away the crowds, he got into the boat and went to the region of Magadan."

The departure to Magadan (some manuscripts read Magdala) transitions to chapter 16, where the Pharisees and Sadducees will immediately demand a sign — despite the fact that Jesus has just performed one of the most extraordinary signs imaginable: feeding thousands from almost nothing, in a setting that deliberately replays the manna tradition of the exodus. The irony, which the author will exploit in 16:1-4, is that the demand for a sign follows a wilderness feeding. Those who demand signs are standing in the overflow of the sign they cannot see.

The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. It exposes the fundamental conflict as one between human tradition and divine commandment. The Pharisees' authority rests on "the tradition of the elders." Jesus demonstrates that this tradition does not supplement the Torah but actively nullifies it. The case of the corban declaration shows religious devotion being used to evade moral obligation — the very pattern that Hosea 6:6 condemns. The Isaiah 29:13 citation identifies the deeper problem: the heart is far from God, and human commandments have been substituted for divine teaching. The author's argument is not that tradition is inherently wrong but that these particular traditions have displaced what God actually requires.

2. It relocates defilement from the external to the internal. The principle that "what comes out of the mouth defiles" fundamentally reorients the purity framework. The list of heart-generated evils (evil thoughts, murder, adultery, theft, false witness, slander) maps onto the Decalogue's moral commandments. The author presents Jesus not as abolishing the Torah's moral requirements but as identifying their true location: the heart. External observances that ignore the heart's condition accomplish nothing. This principle, stated as teaching in vv. 10-20, is then enacted in the narrative of vv. 21-28, where the externally "impure" Canaanite woman proves to have the heart condition (faith) that the externally "pure" Pharisees lack.

3. It dramatizes the overflow of the kingdom to the Gentiles. The Canaanite woman episode is the most sustained engagement with a Gentile in the document. The woman's argument — that even the dogs eat the crumbs from the master's table — articulates the theology of overflow: Israel's Messiah brings provision so abundant that it cannot be contained within Israel's boundaries. The author pairs this with the feeding of the four thousand in a context where the crowd "glorifies the God of Israel," suggesting Gentile presence. The movement from the five thousand (twelve baskets, Israel) to the four thousand (seven baskets, potentially universal) enacts the expansion that the prophets foretold and that the document will culminate in at 28:19 ("Go and make disciples of all nations").

4. It continues the pattern in which Gentiles display superior faith. The centurion at 8:5-13 received the commendation, "With no one in Israel have I found such faith." The Canaanite woman receives, "Great is your faith." In both cases, the Gentile's faith is explicitly contrasted with what Jesus has encountered within Israel. Meanwhile, the disciples are "still without understanding" (v. 16) and the Pharisees' hearts are "far from God" (v. 8). The author is not arguing that Gentiles are inherently superior to Jews; he is arguing that faith — active, persistent trust in Jesus's authority and provision — is what the kingdom requires, and that this faith can be found where the purity system least expects it.

5. It pairs two feedings that together represent the scope of Jesus's provision. The five thousand (chapter 14) and the four thousand (chapter 15) are not redundant repetitions. The author treats them as distinct events with distinct audiences and distinct symbolic residues (twelve baskets and seven baskets). Jesus will explicitly reference both in 16:9-10 and expect the disciples to derive meaning from the contrast. The two feedings together declare that Jesus's provision extends to Israel and beyond Israel — to the full scope of those who come to him.

6. It advances the disciples' characterization as those who are learning but have not yet understood. Peter needs the parable explained (v. 15). The disciples want to send the Canaanite woman away (v. 23). The disciples cannot imagine where bread will come from despite having witnessed the feeding of the five thousand (v. 33). The author presents the disciples not as exemplars of faith but as works in progress — contrasted repeatedly with the Canaanite woman's instant and tenacious faith. The disciples' trajectory is toward understanding (Peter will confess Jesus as Christ in 16:16), but they have not arrived yet. The chapter places them between the Pharisees (who refuse to understand) and the Canaanite woman (who understands immediately).
