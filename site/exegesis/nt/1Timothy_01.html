1 Timothy 1 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

The document's genre and occasion: This is a letter from Paul to Timothy, his co-worker in the gospel, who has been left behind in Ephesus to address a specific problem: certain persons are teaching different doctrine and promoting speculative elaborations of the Torah. The letter is simultaneously personal correspondence and an instrument of authorization. Paul writes to Timothy not as a stranger but as a father to a child (v. 2), yet the letter also functions as a public document: it conveys Paul's apostolic authority to Timothy for the exercise of his mission in Ephesus. A private note between friends would not need the formal assertion of apostleship "according to the command of God" (v. 1); that language exists because others in Ephesus will need to recognize Timothy's mandate.

The setting is recoverable from the letter itself. Paul has departed for Macedonia (v. 3) and has left Timothy in Ephesus to manage a crisis of teaching. The nature of the crisis emerges across chapter 1: certain persons desire to be teachers of the law (v. 7) but have deviated into empty speculation (v. 6), promoting myths and endless genealogies (v. 4) rather than the divine stewardship that operates through faith. At least two individuals — Hymenaeus and Alexander — have so far departed from the faith that Paul has subjected them to apostolic discipline (v. 20). The letter addresses Timothy's task in this environment: what he is to teach, how the law properly functions, and why the gospel Paul has been entrusted with stands as the standard by which all other teaching must be measured.

The letter belongs to the genre of the mandate letter — a letter from a superior to a delegate, authorizing and instructing the delegate to act on the sender's behalf. Such letters were common in the ancient world. A governor dispatching instructions to a subordinate, a military commander writing orders to a field officer — these are the social analogues. The letter's repeated use of parangelia ("charge, command, instruction" — vv. 3, 5, 18) and the language of entrustment (paratithemai, v. 18; episteuthe, v. 11) reinforce this function. Timothy is not being offered suggestions; he is receiving orders backed by apostolic and divine authority.

What the author argues immediately after (1 Timothy 2): Chapter 2 opens with a new set of instructions: "First of all, then, I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way" (2:1-2). The transition from chapter 1 to chapter 2 is coherent. Chapter 1 identified the problem (false teaching that misuses the law and promotes speculation) and established the standard (sound teaching according to the gospel of God's glory). Chapter 2 moves to the content of what proper community life looks like when governed by that standard. The prayer for all people, including rulers, flows from the statement in 2:3-4 that "this is good and pleasing in the sight of God our Savior, who desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth." The designation "God our Savior" (theou soteros hemon) echoes directly 1:1, binding the two chapters together. The universal scope of God's saving intention in 2:4 connects to the universal scope of the saying in 1:15 ("Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners"). And the description of Paul's own appointment — "For this I was appointed a preacher and an apostle... a teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth" (2:7) — continues the theme of Paul's personal commissioning from 1:1 ("apostle by the command of God") and 1:11-12 (the gospel entrusted to Paul, who was appointed to service). Chapter 2 does not change the subject; it begins to fill in the positive content of what the Ephesian community should do, in contrast to what the false teachers are promoting.

How chapter 1 connects to what follows: Chapter 1 functions as the letter's foundation. It establishes Paul's authority (vv. 1-2), identifies the crisis (vv. 3-7), clarifies the proper use of the law and its relationship to the gospel (vv. 8-11), grounds everything in Paul's own experience of mercy as the paradigmatic demonstration of Christ's saving purpose (vv. 12-17), and commissions Timothy for the task with a warning about what happens when faith and conscience are abandoned (vv. 18-20). Every subsequent section of the letter builds on this foundation. The instructions about prayer and community conduct in chapters 2-3 are the positive teaching that replaces the speculations of the false teachers. The qualifications for overseers and deacons in chapter 3 describe the kind of leaders the community needs — in contrast to the self-appointed law-teachers of 1:7. The warnings about future apostasy in 4:1-5 develop the theme of departure from faith that appears in 1:6 and 1:19-20. The instructions about various groups (widows, elders, slaves) in chapters 5-6 are applications of "sound teaching" (1:10). And the final charge in 6:20 ("Guard the deposit entrusted to you") closes the ring opened in 1:18 ("This charge I entrust to you"). Chapter 1 is the lens through which the entire letter must be read.

Verses 1-2: The Greeting — Apostolic Authority and Spiritual Kinship

"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus according to the command of God our Savior and of Christ Jesus our hope, to Timothy, my true child in faith: Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord."

"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus" (Paulos apostolos Christou Iesou). Unlike 1 Thessalonians, where Paul used no title at all, here the title apostolos appears immediately. The presence or absence of apostolic titles in Paul's greetings is not random; it corresponds to the rhetorical situation. In letters to communities where his authority is unquestioned (1 Thessalonians, Philippians, Philemon), Paul omits or softens the title. In letters where his authority requires assertion — whether because it is disputed (Galatians, 2 Corinthians) or because the letter functions as a formal authorization (here) — the title is stated. Timothy does not doubt Paul's apostleship, but the letter is not for Timothy's eyes alone. It will be read in the Ephesian assembly, where the false teachers will hear it. The title functions as a credential behind the charge Timothy has received.

"According to the command of God our Savior and of Christ Jesus our hope" (kat' epitagen theou soteros hemon kai Christou Iesou tes elpidos hemon). The preposition kata with the accusative (kat' epitagen) means "according to, in accordance with, by virtue of." Paul's apostleship is not self-generated, not appointed by a human council, not adopted as a career. It exists kat' epitagen — by formal command. The noun epitage means "command, order, injunction, decree" — the language of official mandate. A first-century hearer would recognize this as the vocabulary of authorized commission: an epitage comes from someone who has authority to issue binding orders.

The source of the command is dual: "God our Savior" (theou soteros hemon) and "Christ Jesus our hope" (Christou Iesou tes elpidos hemon). The first designation requires careful attention.

"God our Savior" (theos soter): In the LXX, God is called soter ("savior, deliverer, rescuer") with consistent reference to his acts of deliverance in Israel's history. The term does not float in abstraction; it is grounded in specific rescues. In the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:15 LXX), Israel is rebuked for abandoning "the God who made him" and "the God his savior" (ton theon ton sosanta auton) — where the saving refers to the exodus and wilderness deliverance. In Isaiah 45:15, the prophet declares: "Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior" (ho theos tou Israel soter). In Isaiah 45:21: "There is no other god besides me, a righteous God and a Savior; there is none besides me." The context is the Babylonian exile and God's assertion that he alone, not the gods of the nations, can deliver. In the Psalms, God is repeatedly the source of salvation: Psalm 24:5 LXX (25:5): "You are the God of my salvation" (ho theos tes soterias mou); Psalm 26:1 LXX (27:1): "The LORD is my light and my salvation" (to soterion mou). In every case, the title soter is tied to observable acts of rescue — from Egypt, from exile, from enemies, from death. A first-century Jewish hearer, encountering "God our Savior," would hear not a metaphysical attribute but a summary of God's demonstrated character: the God who rescues his people, as he has done before.

The phrase recurs throughout this letter (2:3; 4:10) and in Titus (1:3; 2:10; 3:4), making it a signature designation. Its prominence here, in the very first phrase, announces the letter's theological orientation: the God in whose name Paul writes is the God whose defining activity is saving.

"Christ Jesus our hope" (Christos Iesous he elpis hemon): Christ Jesus is not merely the bearer of a hopeful message but is himself identified as the hope. The abstract noun elpis ("hope, expectation") is predicated of a person: Christ Jesus is the hope. This is a compressed identification. In the broader Pauline corpus, the content of hope is the resurrection and the coming glory (Romans 5:2; 8:24-25; Colossians 1:27, "Christ in you, the hope of glory"). Here the language is more direct: Christ Jesus himself is the hope. The first-century hearer would understand this in eschatological terms — the one whose return they await, whose coming will bring the salvation that "God our Savior" has purposed.

The two designations — God as Savior, Christ Jesus as Hope — establish the theological poles within which the entire letter operates: God initiates salvation, Christ embodies the hope of its completion.

"To Timothy, my true child in faith" (Timotheō gnesiō teknō en pistei). The adjective gnesios means "genuine, legitimate, born in wedlock" — as opposed to nothos ("illegitimate, bastard"). Applied to Timothy, it identifies him as Paul's legitimate spiritual offspring, not a pretender to the relationship. The phrase en pistei ("in faith") defines the sphere in which the parent-child relationship exists: it is constituted by faith, not by biology. Paul is Timothy's father in the faith because Paul brought him to faith — he is the one through whom Timothy entered the community of believers (cf. 1 Corinthians 4:15, where Paul tells the Corinthians: "I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel").

The designation matters in context. The false teachers in Ephesus have set themselves up as authorities (v. 7, "desiring to be teachers of the law"). Timothy's authority is grounded differently: it comes not from self-appointment but from apostolic lineage. He is Paul's genuine child, bearing Paul's commission.

"Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord" (charis eleos eirene apo theou patros kai Christou Iesou tou kyriou hemon). Paul's standard greeting in other letters is "grace and peace" (charis kai eirene — Romans 1:7; 1 Corinthians 1:3; 2 Corinthians 1:2; Galatians 1:3; Ephesians 1:2; Philippians 1:2; Colossians 1:2; 1 Thessalonians 1:1; 2 Thessalonians 1:2; Philemon 3). Here and in 2 Timothy 1:2, a third term is inserted: eleos ("mercy"). The addition is not accidental. Mercy will become the dominant note of the chapter's center: Paul will describe himself as one who "received mercy" (eleethon, vv. 13, 16) despite being a blasphemer and persecutor. The greeting anticipates the theme. The word eleos in the LXX most frequently translates the Hebrew hesed — covenant faithfulness, steadfast love, loyal mercy. When eleos appears in the greeting, it signals that the letter will be concerned not merely with grace (unmerited favor) or peace (restored relationship) but specifically with God's covenant mercy extended to those who do not deserve it.

Verses 3-7: The Problem — False Teaching in Ephesus

"As I urged you when I was going to Macedonia, remain in Ephesus so that you may charge certain persons not to teach different doctrine, nor to devote themselves to myths and endless genealogies, which promote speculations rather than the stewardship from God that is by faith. The aim of our charge is love that issues from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith. Certain persons, by swerving from these, have wandered away into vain discussion, desiring to be teachers of the law, without understanding either what they are saying or the things about which they make confident assertions."

"As I urged you when I was going to Macedonia, remain in Ephesus" (kathos parekalesa se prosmeinai en Ephesō poreuomenos eis Makedonian). The verb parakalein ("to urge, to exhort, to encourage, to comfort") here carries its hortatory sense: Paul urged Timothy, appealed to him, pressed upon him the importance of remaining. The verb prosmeinai ("to remain, to stay, to continue in a place") implies that Timothy might have preferred to leave. Whether because the task was difficult, because he wanted to travel with Paul, or because the opposition was daunting, Timothy's staying in Ephesus required apostolic urging. Paul did not simply leave Timothy behind; he appealed to him to stay.

"So that you may charge certain persons not to teach different doctrine" (hina parangeiles tisin me heterodidaskalein). The verb parangelein ("to charge, to command, to give orders") is military and official language. It describes the issuing of orders that require compliance, not suggestions that invite discussion. Timothy is not to engage the false teachers in academic debate; he is to charge them — to issue authoritative commands backed by apostolic mandate.

The recipients of this charge are identified only as tines ("certain persons") — Paul does not name them yet (names come at v. 20). The indefinite pronoun may be diplomatically vague, or it may indicate that the problem is not limited to two or three individuals but involves a broader group whose members Timothy knows.

The content of the charge is negative: me heterodidaskalein — "not to teach different doctrine." The verb heterodidaskalein appears only here and in 1 Timothy 6:3 in all surviving Greek literature of this period. It is a compound: heteros ("other, different") + didaskalein ("to teach"). The prefix heteros is significant. Greek distinguished between allos ("another of the same kind") and heteros ("another of a different kind"). The teaching these people promote is not merely additional to Paul's teaching; it is qualitatively different, belonging to a different category. Paul uses the same distinction in Galatians 1:6-7: "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting the one who called you in the grace of Christ and are turning to a different gospel (heteron euangelion) — not that there is another one (allo), but there are some who trouble you and want to distort the gospel of Christ." Different in kind, not merely in detail. The false teachers are not offering supplementary instruction; they are promoting doctrine that belongs to a different species from the gospel Paul entrusted to Timothy.

"Nor to devote themselves to myths and endless genealogies" (mede prosechein mythois kai genealogiais aperantois). The verb prosechein ("to give heed to, to devote oneself to, to pay attention to") describes sustained intellectual engagement — these teachers are not casually mentioning myths but devoting themselves to them, making them the substance of their instruction.

The noun mythos in first-century Greek means "tale, story, narrative, fable" — a narrative that may or may not correspond to reality. In Plato, mythos is distinguished from logos as story is from reasoned argument. In Paul's usage (here; 4:7; 2 Timothy 4:4; Titus 1:14), mythoi are always negative: they are the wrong kind of narrative, stories that distort rather than illuminate. Titus 1:14 specifies them as "Jewish myths" (Ioudaikois mythois), linking them to speculative elaborations of the Hebrew scriptural tradition.

The noun genealogia ("genealogy, tracing of descent") appears only here and in Titus 3:9 in the New Testament. It refers to the practice of constructing and interpreting chains of ancestry. The adjective aperantos ("endless, without limit, boundless") characterizes these genealogies as interminable — they go on without conclusion, producing speculation without resolution.

What are these myths and genealogies? The text provides several clues that, taken together, point in a specific direction:

(1) The teachers "desire to be teachers of the law" (v. 7), indicating that their activity is Torah-centered. (2) Titus 1:14 calls them "Jewish myths," and Titus 3:9 links "genealogies" directly with "quarrels about the law." (3) The myths and genealogies promote "speculations" (zeteseis) rather than edification (v. 4).

The most natural first-century referent is the elaborate midrashic and haggadic tradition that grew around the biblical narratives. The Hebrew scriptures contain extensive genealogical material (Genesis 5, 10, 11, 25, 36; Numbers 26; 1 Chronicles 1-9), and Second Temple Jewish interpretive practice built extensive speculative systems on these genealogies — identifying angelic figures with particular patriarchs, constructing chains of hidden wisdom transmitted through bloodlines, and building theological arguments from the narrative details of the patriarchal stories. The Book of Jubilees, for instance, retells Genesis and Exodus with elaborate genealogical and chronological frameworks, and various other Second Temple texts (the Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs, portions of 1 Enoch) develop complex speculative systems around biblical genealogies and narratives. The "myths and endless genealogies" Paul opposes are most likely this kind of Torah-based speculation — not the scriptural text itself, but the unbounded elaboration of it that replaces the gospel's simplicity with esoteric complexity.

"Which promote speculations rather than the stewardship from God that is by faith" (haitines zeteseis parechousin mallon e oikonomian theou ten en pistei). The relative pronoun haitines ("which," qualitative relative — "which are of such a character that") introduces the effect of the myths and genealogies. They produce zeteseis ("speculations, controversies, debates, investigations"). The noun zetesis (from zetein, "to seek, to investigate") describes intellectual inquiry that goes nowhere — questions that multiply without resolution, investigations that generate further investigation rather than conclusion.

The contrast is sharp: rather than (mallon e, "rather than, instead of") the oikonomia of God. The noun oikonomia has a range of meaning in first-century usage: "household management, stewardship, administration, arrangement, plan." The oikos ("house") + nomos ("law, rule") compound denotes the ordering of a household — how a household is managed, how its affairs are arranged. Applied to God, oikonomia describes God's arrangement, God's plan, the way God manages his purposes. In Ephesians 1:10, Paul uses the term for God's plan "to unite all things in Christ" (eis oikonomian tou pleromatos ton kairon). In Ephesians 3:9, it is "the plan of the mystery hidden for ages in God." In Colossians 1:25, Paul describes his own role as fulfilling "the stewardship from God that was given to me for you, to make the word of God fully known."

The genitive theou ("of God") identifies whose oikonomia this is: it is God's plan, God's arrangement, God's stewardship — not the speculative constructions of self-appointed teachers. And this oikonomia operates en pistei ("in faith, by faith, through faith"). The prepositional phrase is critical: God's plan is accessed and activated through faith, not through speculative investigation of genealogies. The false teachers have replaced the faith-based reception of God's plan with the inquiry-based elaboration of scriptural minutiae. The problem is not that they study scripture but that their study has become an end in itself, generating questions rather than faith.

"The aim of our charge is love that issues from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith" (to de telos tes parangelias estin agape ek katharas kardias kai syneideseos agathes kai pisteos anypokritou). This verse is the chapter's theological keystone. It identifies the telos — the goal, aim, purpose, end-point — of the parangelia (the charge, the command, the authoritative instruction). Everything Paul instructs Timothy to do is directed toward this triple outcome:

(1) Love from a pure heart (agape ek katharas kardias). The preposition ek ("from, out of") identifies the source: love must arise from a clean heart, not from an impure or divided one. The adjective katharos ("clean, pure, undefiled") in the LXX describes ritual and moral purity. Psalm 50:12 LXX (51:10): "Create in me a clean heart, O God" (kardian katharan ktison en emoi, ho theos). Psalm 23:4 LXX (24:4): "He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (athos chersin kai katharos te kardia). The heart (kardia) in Hebrew anthropology is not the seat of emotions only; it is the center of thought, will, and decision. A pure heart is one that is undivided, uncontaminated, oriented toward God without admixture. Love that arises from such a heart is not calculated performance but the natural overflow of an interior rightly oriented.

(2) A good conscience (syneideseos agathes). The noun syneidesis ("conscience, moral awareness, inner witness") is composed of syn ("with") + eidesis ("knowledge") — a knowing-with-oneself, the inner capacity to evaluate one's own actions. The word is relatively rare in the LXX but appears frequently in Paul's letters and in the Pastoral Epistles. A "good" (agathe) conscience is one that does not accuse, one that testifies to right conduct. The term will become crucial at the chapter's end (vv. 19-20), where the rejection of good conscience is identified as the mechanism by which faith is destroyed.

(3) Sincere faith (pisteos anypokritou). The adjective anypokritos is composed of the alpha-privative (a-/an-) + hypokritos ("actor, one who plays a part"). Literally: "without play-acting, unfeigned, unhypocritical." In the Greco-Roman theater, the hypokrites was the actor who wore a mask and spoke lines that were not his own. A faith that is anypokritos is faith without a mask — genuine, not performed for an audience. Paul uses the same adjective for love in Romans 12:9 ("Let love be genuine," he agape anypokritos) and 2 Corinthians 6:6. The false teachers, by implication, have a faith that is not anypokritos — it is performed, external, a role they play rather than a reality they inhabit.

The three elements — pure heart, good conscience, sincere faith — form an anthropological sequence that moves from interior (heart) through moral awareness (conscience) to relational trust (faith). All three must be present and operating for love to result. The charge Paul gives Timothy is not finally about correct doctrine in the abstract but about the production of love through the properly functioning interior life of the community. Correct teaching serves this end; false teaching obstructs it.

"Certain persons, by swerving from these, have wandered away into vain discussion" (hon tines astochesantes exetraphesan eis mataiologian). The verb astochein ("to miss the mark, to deviate from the target, to swerve") appears in the New Testament only in the Pastoral Epistles (here; 6:21; 2 Timothy 2:18). It is an archery metaphor: these teachers aimed and missed. The target they missed is the triple aim just stated — love from a pure heart, good conscience, and sincere faith. Having missed this target, they "turned aside" (exetraphesan, from ektrepein, "to turn aside, to turn away, to deviate") into mataiologia ("vain discussion, empty talk, futile babble"). The noun mataiologia combines mataios ("vain, empty, futile, purposeless") with logos ("word, speech, discourse"). Their teaching is not merely wrong; it is empty — it leads nowhere, accomplishes nothing, produces no love.

"Desiring to be teachers of the law" (thelontes einai nomodidaskaloi). The verb thelein ("to want, to desire, to wish") indicates aspiration: these people want to occupy the role of nomodidaskalos — teacher of the law. The compound noun nomodidaskalos appears elsewhere only in Luke 5:17 ("teachers of the law were sitting there, who had come from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem") and Acts 5:34 (Gamaliel is called "a teacher of the law respected by all the people"). In Luke and Acts, the nomodidaskalos is the recognized authority on Torah, the one qualified to interpret and apply the Mosaic law. The false teachers in Ephesus aspire to this role — they want the status and authority of Torah experts.

"Without understanding either what they are saying or the things about which they make confident assertions" (me noountes mete ha legousin mete peri tinon diabebaiountai). The participle noountes (from noein, "to understand, to perceive, to grasp with the mind") is negated: they do not understand. The object of their incomprehension is twofold: (a) ha legousin — "what they are saying," the content of their own speech; and (b) peri tinon diabebaiountai — "the things about which they make confident assertions." The verb diabebaiousthai ("to assert strongly, to affirm with confidence, to insist emphatically") describes the manner of their teaching: it is assertive, bold, assured. The irony is pointed: they speak with maximum confidence about things they understand minimally. Their ignorance is not quiet and modest but loud and insistent. They do not know what they are talking about, and they talk about it with great certainty.

The portrait of the false teachers in verses 3-7 is complete: they teach different doctrine, devote themselves to myths and genealogies, produce speculation rather than faith, miss the mark of love and conscience and genuine faith, wander into empty talk, aspire to the authority of Torah interpreters, and assert confidently what they do not understand. Everything in the rest of the chapter will address the reality they have distorted and abandoned.

Verses 8-11: The Proper Function of the Law

"Now we know that the law is good, if one uses it lawfully, understanding this, that the law is not laid down for the righteous person, but for the lawless and disobedient, for the ungodly and sinners, for the unholy and profane, for those who strike their fathers and mothers, for murderers, the sexually immoral, men who practice homosexuality, enslavers, liars, perjurers, and whatever else is contrary to sound teaching, in accordance with the gospel of the glory of the blessed God with which I have been entrusted."

"Now we know that the law is good, if one uses it lawfully" (Oidamen de hoti kalos ho nomos ean tis auto nomimos chretai). The verb oidamen ("we know") is the first person plural perfect of oida — "we know, we are aware, it is settled knowledge among us." Paul appeals to shared understanding: this is not a novel claim but an established conviction. The law (ho nomos) is kalos — "good, beautiful, noble, excellent." This is the same assessment Paul rendered in Romans 7:12 ("the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good") and 7:16 ("I agree with the law, that it is good"). The goodness of the law is not in question. What is in question is its use.

The conditional clause ean tis auto nomimos chretai ("if one uses it lawfully") contains a wordplay: nomos used nomimos — the law used law-fully, legitimately, according to its proper purpose. The adverb nomimos means "in accordance with the rules, properly, legitimately" — as an athlete competes nomimos (2 Timothy 2:5, "if anyone competes as an athlete, he is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules"). The law has a proper use, and it has an improper use. The false teachers (who desire to be teachers of the law) are using it improperly — they are wielding a good instrument for the wrong purpose. The law is good; their use of it is not nomimos.

"Understanding this, that the law is not laid down for the righteous person" (eidos touto, hoti dikaiō nomos ou keitai). The participle eidos ("knowing, understanding") introduces the content of what lawful use entails. The verb keitai ("is laid down, is set in place, is established") is the present passive of keimai. The law has been laid down, set in place, established — but not for the dikaios ("righteous person, just person, one who is right"). The claim is functional, not ontological: the law's prohibitions address behavior. A person who is already righteous — who already lives in accordance with the law's demands — does not need the law's restraint, because the law's restraint addresses the very behaviors the righteous person does not commit. The law is a wall built to contain what needs containing; the person already within the boundary has no need of the wall.

This is not a claim that righteous people exist apart from the law, or that the law has no instructional value for anyone. It is a claim about the law's disciplinary and restraining function: that function addresses the unrighteous.

"But for the lawless and disobedient..." (anomois kai anypotaktois...). Paul now provides a list of those for whom the law is laid down. The list is not random; it follows a discernible structure that maps onto the Decalogue — the Ten Commandments — demonstrating that Paul is thinking precisely about Torah when he describes the law's proper function.

The list begins with three pairs of general categories:

(1) "The lawless and disobedient" (anomois kai anypotaktois). Anomos ("without law, lawless, in violation of law") is the broadest term: those who exist outside or against the law's authority. Anypotaktos ("insubordinate, disobedient, not subject to") describes those who refuse to submit to proper authority. Together they describe the fundamental posture of those who need the law's restraint: lawlessness and refusal of order.

(2) "The ungodly and sinners" (asebesi kai hamartolois). Asebes ("ungodly, impious, without reverence for God") describes the failure of duty toward God — the refusal to honor the divine. In the LXX, the asebes is the person who lives as if God does not matter (Psalm 1:1, 4-6 LXX; Proverbs 11:5, 31 LXX). Hamartolos ("sinner, one who misses the mark") is the broadest term for moral failure. Together they describe the religious and moral dimensions of the condition the law addresses.

(3) "The unholy and profane" (anosiois kai bebelois). Anosios ("unholy, lacking in holiness, wicked") describes the absence of the sacred quality that God's people are called to possess. Bebelos ("profane, common, accessible to all, unhallowed") is the opposite of hagios ("holy, set apart"). In the LXX, bebelos describes what is common or polluted, what has been stripped of its sacred character (Ezekiel 21:25 LXX [21:30]; 1 Samuel 21:4-5 LXX, where "common bread" is contrasted with "holy bread"). Together they describe persons who have lost or rejected any quality of sanctification.

These three pairs establish the general categories. The list then moves to specific offenses that follow the order of the Decalogue's commandments:

"For those who strike their fathers and mothers" (patroloais kai metroloais). The compound nouns patroloias ("one who strikes one's father") and metroloias ("one who strikes one's mother") describe violence against parents. This corresponds to the fifth commandment: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16). The extreme violation of this command is physical assault on one's parents. In the Torah, the penalty is severe: "Whoever strikes his father or his mother shall be put to death" (Exodus 21:15). The LXX uses the verb typtein ("to strike"), and the compound noun patroloias captures this in a single word.

"For murderers" (androphonois). The noun androphonos ("man-slayer, murderer") corresponds to the sixth commandment: "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13; Deuteronomy 5:17).

"The sexually immoral, men who practice homosexuality" (pornois, arsenokoitais). The noun pornos ("sexually immoral person, one who engages in illicit sexual behavior") and arsenokoites ("a male who lies with a male") correspond to the seventh commandment: "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14; Deuteronomy 5:18) and its broader application to sexual transgression. The word arsenokoites requires particular attention. It is a compound of arsen ("male") + koite ("bed, lying"), and it appears to be drawn directly from the LXX text of Leviticus 18:22: kai meta arsenos ou koimethese koiten gynaikos ("and with a male you shall not lie the lying of a woman") and Leviticus 20:13: hos an koimethe meta arsenos koiten gynaikos ("whoever lies with a male the lying of a woman"). The two key terms — arsenos and koiten — are combined to form the compound noun. The word describes the specific act prohibited in these Levitical texts. Paul's usage here and in 1 Corinthians 6:9 appears to derive from direct engagement with the LXX wording of these prohibitions.

"Enslavers" (andrapodistais). The noun andrapodistes means "slave-dealer, kidnapper, one who enslaves free persons." This corresponds to the eighth commandment: "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15; Deuteronomy 5:19) — specifically, the most extreme form of theft, which is the theft of persons. In Exodus 21:16: "Whoever steals a man and sells him, and anyone found in possession of him, shall be put to death." And Deuteronomy 24:7: "If a man is found stealing one of his brothers of the people of Israel, and if he treats him as a slave or sells him, then that thief shall die." The andrapodistes is the person who treats a human being as plunder.

"Liars, perjurers" (pseustais, epiorkois). The noun pseustes ("liar, one who speaks falsehood") and epiorkos ("perjurer, one who swears falsely") correspond to the ninth commandment: "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16; Deuteronomy 5:20). The perjurer (epiorkos, from epi + horkos, "upon an oath") is one who swears falsely — who invokes God's name in an oath and then breaks it. This connects also to the third commandment against taking God's name in vain (Exodus 20:7) — perjury profanes the divine name by using it to guarantee falsehood.

"And whatever else is contrary to sound teaching" (kai ei ti heteron te hygiainouse didaskalia antikeitai). The catch-all clause extends the list beyond its Decalogue framework to include anything that opposes "sound teaching" (hygiainousa didaskalia). The adjective hygiainein means "to be healthy, to be sound, to be in good condition." It is a medical metaphor: sound teaching is healthy teaching, teaching that promotes health and well-being. The metaphor is distinctive to the Pastoral Epistles, where it appears repeatedly (here; 6:3; 2 Timothy 1:13; 4:3; Titus 1:9, 13; 2:1, 2). Unsound teaching, by implication, is diseased — it produces sickness in those who receive it. The false teachers of verses 3-7 are promoting teaching that is unhealthy.

The Decalogue structure of the vice list is significant for the chapter's argument. The false teachers desire to be "teachers of the law" (v. 7), but they do not understand the law they claim to teach. Paul demonstrates his own understanding of the law by showing that its prohibitions address specific behaviors — and that these prohibitions align with what is "contrary to sound teaching." The law and sound teaching serve the same purpose: they address and restrain transgression. The false teachers, by contrast, use the law to generate speculation about myths and genealogies — a use that has nothing to do with the law's actual function.

"In accordance with the gospel of the glory of the blessed God with which I have been entrusted" (kata to euangelion tes doxes tou makariou theou, ho episteuthon ego). The phrase kata to euangelion ("in accordance with the gospel") modifies "sound teaching": sound teaching is teaching that accords with the gospel. The law and the gospel are not at odds; the law's proper function (restraining transgression) is consistent with the gospel's content. What the false teachers do with the law is not consistent with the gospel.

The gospel is further described as "the gospel of the glory of the blessed God" (to euangelion tes doxes tou makariou theou). The genitive tes doxes ("of the glory") may be objective (the gospel that reveals and proclaims God's glory) or attributive (the glorious gospel). In either case, the gospel is characterized by doxa — God's glory, God's radiant self-disclosure, the visible weight of God's presence and character.

The adjective makarios ("blessed, happy, fortunate") applied to God is notable. In standard Greek usage, makarios described the state of the gods — their blissful, self-sufficient existence beyond human trouble. In the LXX, the word is used primarily of humans who walk in God's ways (Psalm 1:1, makarios ho aner, "blessed is the man"; Psalm 32:1 LXX [33:1], "blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven"). Applied to God here and again at 6:15 ("the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords"), it affirms God's supreme and unassailable blessedness. The God whose glory the gospel reveals is not anxious, threatened, or diminished by the confusion in Ephesus. He is makarios — utterly and eternally blessed.

"With which I have been entrusted" (ho episteuthon ego). The verb pisteuein in the passive with the accusative means "to be entrusted with." Paul has received the gospel not as a possession to exploit but as a deposit to guard and transmit. The language of entrustment will return at 6:20 ("Guard the deposit," ten paratheken phylaxon) and is fundamental to the letter's understanding of ministry: those who handle the gospel are stewards, not owners. Paul's entrustment with the gospel is his authorization and his obligation.

Verses 12-17: Paul's Own Story — The Paradigm of Mercy

"I thank him who has given me strength, Christ Jesus our Lord, because he judged me faithful, appointing me to his service, though formerly I was a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent. But I received mercy because I had acted ignorantly in unbelief; and the grace of our Lord overflowed for me with the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus. The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life. To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen."

"I thank him who has given me strength, Christ Jesus our Lord, because he judged me faithful, appointing me to his service" (Charin echo to endynamosanti me Christo Iesou to kyrio hemon, hoti piston me hegesato themenos eis diakonian). The phrase charin echo ("I have gratitude, I give thanks") introduces a personal testimony. The verb endynamoun ("to strengthen, to empower, to fill with power") describes Christ's action: Christ is the one who gave Paul the capacity to serve. The strength is not Paul's own but Christ's, channeled to Paul for the task. The verb hegeisthai ("to consider, to regard, to count") means that Christ considered or judged Paul pistos ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") — and on that basis, placed him into diakonia ("service, ministry, attendance"). The word diakonia in first-century usage described the work of serving, attending, caring for — the practical ministry of carrying out someone else's purpose. Paul's appointment was not to a position of self-aggrandizement but to service.

This passage functions as Paul's personal credential, but its purpose is not self-promotion. It introduces the theme of mercy (vv. 13-16) by first establishing the contrast: the Paul who now serves was formerly the most unlikely candidate for service.

"Though formerly I was a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent" (to proteron onta blasphemon kai diokten kai hybristen). The temporal phrase to proteron ("formerly, in the past, before") marks a decisive break between past and present. Three terms describe Paul's former life:

(1) Blasphemos ("blasphemer, one who speaks against God or sacred things"). Paul's pre-conversion activity — persecuting the community of Jesus-followers — was, from the perspective of the gospel, an act of blasphemy: it was speech and action directed against God's Messiah and God's people.

(2) Dioktes ("persecutor, one who pursues with hostile intent"). Paul's own testimony elsewhere confirms this: "I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13); "as to zeal, a persecutor of the church" (Philippians 3:6); the accounts in Acts 8:3, 9:1-2, 22:4-5, 26:9-11 describe Paul's systematic campaign against the early believers, including imprisonment, voting for death sentences, and pursuing them to foreign cities.

(3) Hybristes ("insolent person, violent aggressor, one who acts with wanton cruelty"). The word carries a stronger sense than mere rudeness. In classical Greek, hybris was the most despised vice — the arrogant violence of the powerful against the weak, the insolence that treats others as less than human. In Romans 1:30, hybristai ("insolent ones") appear in a vice list describing those whom God has given over to a debased mind. Paul's persecution was not coolly administrative; it was marked by the violence and arrogance of one who believed he was doing God a favor by destroying God's people.

"But I received mercy because I had acted ignorantly in unbelief" (alla eleethon hoti agnodn epoiesa en apistia). The verb eleein ("to have mercy, to show mercy") in the passive: Paul was shown mercy, was the recipient of mercy. The reason given is critically important: hoti agnodn epoiesa en apistia — "because I acted ignorantly in unbelief."

The participle agnodn (from agnoein, "to be ignorant, to not know, to be unaware") describes Paul's cognitive state: he did not know. His persecution was real and terrible, but it was conducted without knowledge of what he was actually doing. He thought he was serving God; he was in fact opposing God's Messiah. The phrase en apistia ("in unbelief") further specifies: his ignorance was bound up with unbelief — he had not yet believed in Christ, and his unbelief made him unable to see the truth of what he was opposing.

This explanation connects to a fundamental principle in the Torah. Numbers 15:27-31 distinguishes between sin committed "unwittingly" (bishgagah; LXX: akousios/akousids) and sin committed "with a high hand" (beyad ramah; LXX: en cheiri hyperephanias). For unwitting sin, atonement is available: "the priest shall make atonement before the LORD for the person who makes an error, when it is unintentional, to make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven" (Numbers 15:28). But for the sin committed defiantly, deliberately, with a high hand: "that person shall be cut off from among his people. Because he has despised the word of the LORD and has broken his commandment, that person shall be utterly cut off; his iniquity shall be on him" (Numbers 15:30-31).

Paul is claiming that his terrible sins fell into the first category: they were committed in ignorance, not in defiant rejection of known truth. This is not a minimization of what he did — he has just called himself a blasphemer, persecutor, and violent aggressor. It is an explanation of why mercy was possible. He did not know what he was doing (cf. Jesus' prayer for his executioners in Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing"; and Peter's address to the Jerusalem crowd in Acts 3:17: "I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers"). Ignorance does not excuse the action, but it does place it within the category for which mercy is available.

This distinction will become important at the end of the chapter, where Hymenaeus and Alexander are described as having "rejected" (aposamenoi) good conscience (v. 19). Their action is not ignorant but deliberate — they have pushed away what they knew to be right. The contrast between Paul's ignorant sin and their knowing rejection is part of the chapter's architecture.

"And the grace of our Lord overflowed for me with the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus" (hyperepleonasen de he charis tou kyriou hemon meta pisteos kai agapes tes en Christo Iesou). The verb hyperpleonazein ("to overflow, to superabound, to be present in extreme excess") appears only here in the New Testament. It is an intensified form of pleonazein ("to abound, to be more than enough"), with the prefix hyper- ("above, beyond, exceedingly") pushing the concept past normal bounds. Grace did not merely cover Paul's sin; it overflowed — it exceeded the sin by an immeasurable margin. The idea echoes Romans 5:20: "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (hou de epleonasen he hamartia, hyperperisseusen he charis).

The grace came meta ("with, accompanied by") faith and love — these are not separate from grace but its companions. The faith and love are located en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") — they exist within the sphere of Christ, they are characteristics of life in Christ. Paul's receipt of mercy was not a bare judicial transaction; it came loaded with the transformative realities of faith and love that replaced the blasphemy and violence of his former life.

"The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost" (pistos ho logos kai pases apodoches axios, hoti Christos Iesous elthen eis ton kosmon hamartolous sosai, hon protos eimi ego). The formula pistos ho logos ("the saying is trustworthy, the word is faithful") appears five times in the Pastoral Epistles (here; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8). It functions as an endorsement formula, marking a statement as reliable, tested, and worthy of communal acceptance. The additional phrase kai pases apodoches axios ("and worthy of all/full acceptance") intensifies: this is not merely trustworthy but deserving of universal reception. The formula suggests that the statement it endorses is not Paul's invention but an established saying in the community — a recognized confession or summary.

The saying itself is compressed and comprehensive: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners" (Christos Iesous elthen eis ton kosmon hamartolous sosai). Three elements:

(1) The subject: Christ Jesus — the Messiah, Jesus. (2) The action: elthen eis ton kosmon — "came into the world." The verb erchesthai ("to come") with eis ton kosmon ("into the world") implies a prior existence outside the world — a coming from somewhere else into the sphere of human existence. (3) The purpose: hamartolous sosai — "to save sinners." The infinitive sosai (aorist active of sozein, "to save, to rescue, to deliver") states the purpose of the coming. The object is hamartolous — "sinners," without qualification, without ethnic boundary, without limitation. Not "to save the righteous" or "to save Israel" or "to save the elect" but "to save sinners."

The universal scope of this saying connects directly to what follows in 2:4 ("God desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth") and 2:6 ("who gave himself as a ransom for all"). The saving purpose is not restricted.

"Of whom I am the foremost" (hon protos eimi ego). Paul identifies himself as the protos ("first, foremost, chief") of sinners. The present tense eimi ("I am," not "I was") is deliberate. Paul does not say "I was formerly the worst sinner"; he says "I am the foremost." This is not false modesty or rhetorical exaggeration. It is the permanent self-assessment of one who can never forget what he did: he persecuted God's people with violence and arrogance. The past is not erased by mercy; it remains as the measure of mercy's depth. If mercy was shown to the foremost sinner, then no subsequent sinner stands beyond its reach. Paul's identity as the protos of sinners is not a badge of shame but a proof of grace's sufficiency.

"But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life" (alla dia touto eleethon, hina en emoi proto endeixetai Christos Iesous ten hapasan makrothymian, pros hypotyposin ton mellonton pisteuein ep' auto eis zoen aionion).

The conjunction dia touto ("for this reason, on this account") identifies a purpose behind Paul's receipt of mercy that extends beyond Paul himself. The hina clause ("in order that") states the purpose: en emoi proto — "in me, as the foremost" — Christ Jesus might display (endeixetai, aorist middle subjunctive of endeiknymi, "to show forth, to demonstrate, to display publicly") ten hapasan makrothymian — "the complete patience" or "the entire long-suffering."

The noun makrothymia ("patience, long-suffering, forbearance, slowness to anger") is composed of makros ("long") + thymos ("anger, passion, wrath"). It describes the quality of being slow to anger, of bearing with offenses over time rather than striking back immediately. In the LXX, makrothymia is an attribute of God himself: Exodus 34:6 ("The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger," makrothymos — the foundational self-description of God); Numbers 14:18 ("The LORD is slow to anger," makrothymos kyrios); Psalm 86:15 LXX (85:15), "you, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness." The adjective hapas ("all, complete, entire, the whole of") intensifies: not merely patience but the complete patience, the full scope of long-suffering. In Paul's case, Christ displayed the entire range of his willingness to bear with sinners — because the sin was so extreme and the mercy so abundant.

The word hypotyposis ("outline, sketch, pattern, model, example") describes the function Paul's experience serves for others. He is a prototype — a living demonstration of what Christ's mercy can do with the worst of sinners. The beneficiaries are identified: ton mellonton pisteuein ep' auto eis zoen aionion — "those who were to believe in him for eternal life." Future believers are the intended audience of Paul's story. If Christ saved the foremost sinner, then every subsequent person considering whether mercy is available for them can look at Paul and know: it is. The purpose of Paul's mercy is pedagogical and paradigmatic — it teaches future generations about the extent of Christ's patience.

"To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen." (to de basilei ton aionon, aphtharto, aorato, mono theo, time kai doxa eis tous aionas ton aionon, amen). The contemplation of mercy produces doxology. Paul cannot reflect on what he has received without erupting into praise. The doxology assigns four attributes to God:

(1) "King of the ages" (basilei ton aionon). God is the ruler over all ages, all eons, all periods of time. The phrase has deep roots in Jewish liturgical tradition. Tobit 13:6, 10 (LXX): "Exalt him before every living being, because he is our Lord and he is our God... Blessed be God who lives forever, because his kingdom..." The concept of God as king over time itself — not merely king within a period but sovereign over the succession of ages — is foundational to Jewish monotheism. Psalm 145:13 (LXX 144:13): "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and your dominion endures throughout all generations."

(2) "Immortal" (aphtharto). The adjective aphthartos ("incorruptible, imperishable, not subject to decay or death") is composed of the alpha-privative + phtheirein ("to destroy, to corrupt, to decay"). God is beyond the reach of corruption and death. In Romans 1:23, Paul describes the fundamental error of idolatry as exchanging "the glory of the immortal God" (tou aphthartou theou) for images of mortal creatures.

(3) "Invisible" (aorato). The adjective aoratos ("unseen, invisible, not able to be seen") describes God's nature as beyond direct visual perception. Colossians 1:15 calls Christ "the image of the invisible God" (eikon tou theou tou aoratou). Romans 1:20 states that God's "invisible attributes" (ta aorata autou) have been perceived through the things that have been made. God is known through his works and his self-revelation, not through direct sight.

(4) "The only God" (mono theo). The adjective monos ("only, alone, sole") makes the monotheistic claim explicit. This echoes the Shema — Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one." It is a confession of absolute monotheism, appropriate in a letter addressed to a community in Ephesus — a city dominated by the cult of Artemis and populated by numerous other pagan deities. There is only one God, and he is the one who showed mercy to Paul.

"Be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen" (time kai doxa eis tous aionas ton aionon, amen). The two gifts ascribed to God — time ("honor, value, respect, worth") and doxa ("glory, radiance, weight, splendor") — are offered without limit: eis tous aionas ton aionon ("unto the ages of the ages"), the most emphatic expression of temporal infinity available in Greek. The Hebrew amen seals the doxology as a communal affirmation: "so be it, it is established, it is true."

Verses 18-20: The Charge Renewed — Warning by Example

"This charge I entrust to you, Timothy, my child, in accordance with the prophecies previously made about you, that by them you may wage the good warfare, holding faith and a good conscience. By rejecting this, some have made shipwreck of their faith, among whom are Hymenaeus and Alexander, whom I have handed over to Satan that they may learn not to blaspheme."

"This charge I entrust to you, Timothy, my child" (Tauten ten parangelian paratithemai soi, teknon Timothee). The parangelia ("charge, command") first introduced in verse 3 and defined in verse 5 now returns. The verb paratithesthai ("to set before, to deposit, to commit to someone's care, to entrust") is the middle voice of paratithenai. In its middle form, it carries the sense of personally committing something valuable to another's keeping — the way one deposits treasure with a trusted guardian. The language of deposit and entrustment runs throughout the letter: Paul was "entrusted" with the gospel (v. 11, episteuthon); Timothy is now entrusted with the charge (paratithemai); and the letter will close with the command to "guard the deposit" (6:20, ten paratheken phylaxon). The gospel is treated as a valuable asset that must be received, guarded, and transmitted without corruption. Ministry is stewardship, not ownership.

The address teknon Timothee ("child Timothy") recalls the greeting (v. 2, "my true child in faith") and reinforces the personal dimension of the letter. The charge comes not from an impersonal bureaucracy but from a father to a son.

"In accordance with the prophecies previously made about you" (kata tas proagousas epi se propheiteias). The preposition kata ("in accordance with, corresponding to") indicates that the charge aligns with something that preceded it: prophetic utterances (propheiteiai) that were spoken over Timothy at some earlier point. The verb proagein ("to go before, to precede") in the participial form (proagousas) describes these prophecies as having come earlier — they preceded this moment and pointed toward it.

The early communities of the Jesus movement practiced prophecy as a recognized gift of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:10, 28; 14:1-5, 29-32; Acts 13:1-3; 21:10-11). In Acts 13:1-3, the Holy Spirit speaks through prophets to set apart Barnabas and Saul for a specific work, and the community responds with fasting, prayer, and the laying on of hands. A similar event apparently occurred in Timothy's case: prophetic utterances identified him as called to a specific ministry, and the community confirmed this with the laying on of hands (cf. 4:14: "Do not neglect the gift you have, which was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you"). The charge Paul now gives Timothy is not a novel assignment but the continuation and specification of what the Spirit already indicated through the prophetic community.

"That by them you may wage the good warfare" (hina strateuē en autais ten kalen strateian). The hina clause states the purpose: the prophecies are the foundation on which Timothy wages ten kalen strateian ("the good warfare, the noble campaign"). The noun strateia ("military service, campaign, warfare") and the verb strateuein ("to serve as a soldier, to wage war, to campaign") are military metaphors. Timothy's work in Ephesus is not a leisurely academic exercise; it is a battle. He faces opponents (the false teachers), casualties (Hymenaeus and Alexander), and the possibility of defeat (shipwreck of faith). The prophecies previously spoken over him are his commissioning documents — the equivalent of a soldier's orders. They remind him that he did not volunteer for this fight; he was called to it.

The adjective kalos ("good, noble, beautiful, excellent") describes the warfare as worthy, honorable, noble — not because fighting is inherently good but because the cause is right. The same adjective appears throughout the letter to mark things that are worthy: "good" teaching (4:6), "good" confession (6:12, 13), "good" foundation (6:19). The warfare is kale because it serves the gospel.

"Holding faith and a good conscience" (echon pistin kai agathen syneidesin). The participle echon ("holding, having, possessing") describes the equipment Timothy must carry into the warfare: faith (pistin) and a good conscience (agathen syneidesin). These are not merely desirable qualities; they are the weapons and armor of the campaign. Without them, the warfare cannot be waged. The pairing echoes verse 5, where the aim of the charge was love from a pure heart, good conscience (syneideseos agathes), and sincere faith (pisteos anypokritou). The same elements that constitute the goal of sound teaching are also the instruments by which the teacher is equipped. Timothy cannot lead others toward faith and good conscience if he does not possess them himself.

"By rejecting this, some have made shipwreck of their faith" (hen tines aposamenoi peri ten pistin enauagesan). The relative pronoun hen ("which," feminine singular) refers back to agathen syneidesin ("good conscience") — it is the conscience, specifically, that has been rejected. The verb apothesthai ("to push away, to reject, to repudiate, to thrust aside") in the middle voice (aposamenoi) indicates a deliberate, self-initiated act: these persons pushed their good conscience away from themselves. This was not a gradual erosion, not an accidental loss, not an external assault. It was a knowing rejection — they took the conscience that warned them and shoved it aside.

The result is stated in a vivid nautical metaphor: peri ten pistin enauagesan — "concerning the faith, they suffered shipwreck." The verb nauagein ("to suffer shipwreck, to be wrecked at sea") describes the catastrophic destruction of a vessel. The preposition peri with the accusative ("concerning, with respect to") identifies the domain of the shipwreck: it is their faith (pistin) that has been wrecked. The metaphor implies that faith is the ship, conscience is the hull or the steering mechanism. When the hull is breached (conscience rejected), the ship breaks apart (faith is destroyed). The connection is causal and mechanical: the rejection of conscience is the direct cause of the destruction of faith. You cannot push away your moral awareness and retain your trust in God. Conscience and faith are structurally interdependent; destroy one and you destroy the other.

The contrast with Paul's own story is pointed and deliberate. Paul was a blasphemer who acted in ignorance (v. 13) — he did not know what he was doing, and mercy was available. These persons have rejected their conscience — they did know, and they pushed it away. Paul's sin was committed agnodn ("ignorantly"); their departure is committed aposamenoi ("by deliberate rejection"). The difference between Paul's case and theirs is not the severity of the sin but the posture of the sinner: ignorance versus knowing repudiation.

"Among whom are Hymenaeus and Alexander" (hon estin Hymenaios kai Alexandros). Two individuals are named. Naming was a serious act in the ancient world; to name someone publicly in a letter that would be read before the assembly was to expose them to communal judgment.

Hymenaeus appears again in 2 Timothy 2:17-18, where he and a companion named Philetus "have swerved from the truth, saying that the resurrection has already happened. They are upsetting the faith of some." The specific content of Hymenaeus's error — claiming the resurrection has already occurred — connects to the broader pattern of eschatological confusion and false teaching that 2 Thessalonians 2:2 also addresses ("to the effect that the day of the Lord has already come"). These are teachers who relocate eschatological events from the future to the past, thereby undermining the community's hope.

Alexander may be the same person mentioned in 2 Timothy 4:14 ("Alexander the coppersmith did me great harm"), though the name was common and identification is uncertain.

"Whom I have handed over to Satan that they may learn not to blaspheme" (hous paredoka to Satana hina paideuthōsin me blasphemein). The verb paradidonai ("to hand over, to deliver, to turn over to") is the same used in 1 Corinthians 5:5 regarding the man in the Corinthian community who was living with his father's wife: "you are to deliver this man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord." The parallel is structurally important. In both cases:

(1) The handing over is an act of apostolic authority, not mob violence. (2) The one handed over is expelled from the community's protection into the domain where Satan operates. (3) The purpose is corrective, not annihilative.

In the Corinthian case, the stated purpose is "that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" — ultimate salvation, achieved through severe present discipline. Here, the stated purpose is hina paideuthōsin me blasphemein — "that they may be educated/disciplined not to blaspheme." The verb paideuein ("to train, to educate, to discipline, to instruct, to correct by punishment") is the verb from which paideia ("education, training, discipline, instruction") derives. In the LXX and Jewish wisdom tradition, paideia is one of the most important concepts in the formation of character. Proverbs 3:11-12 (LXX): "My son, do not despise the LORD's discipline (paideias kyriou) and do not be weary of his reproof, for the LORD disciplines (paideuei) the one he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights." Deuteronomy 8:5: "Know then in your heart that, as a man disciplines (paideuei) his son, the LORD your God disciplines you." The paideia of God is severe but purposeful: it aims at restoration, not destruction.

The content of what they are to learn is stated negatively: me blasphemein — "not to blaspheme." The word blasphemein connects back to verse 13, where Paul described his own former life as that of a blasphemos. Paul the former blasphemer received mercy because he acted in ignorance. Hymenaeus and Alexander are blasphemers who have rejected conscience — their blasphemy is not ignorant but deliberate. Yet even for them, the discipline has a pedagogical aim: they are to learn. The verb paideuein implies that learning is still possible, that the door to correction is not sealed shut. The discipline is harsh — being expelled from the community's fellowship into the domain of Satan, experiencing whatever suffering that entails — but it aims at an outcome: the cessation of blasphemy and, by implication, restoration to right standing.

The verse does not guarantee that Hymenaeus and Alexander will learn. It states the purpose of the discipline, not its guaranteed outcome. The possibility of learning remains open; whether they seize it depends on their response to the discipline.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

1 Timothy 1 is the foundation document for Timothy's mission in Ephesus. Every section serves the purpose of establishing what sound teaching is, why the false teaching fails, and what is at stake for those who handle the gospel rightly or wrongly.

(1) Verses 1-2 establish Paul's authority as the source of Timothy's commission. Paul is an apostle by divine command, not human appointment. Timothy is his genuine child in the faith, receiving a charge grounded in the deepest relational bond the gospel creates. The greeting's inclusion of "mercy" anticipates the chapter's central theme.

(2) Verses 3-7 identify the crisis. Certain persons in Ephesus are teaching different doctrine, promoting myths and endless genealogies that generate speculation rather than God's plan, which operates through faith. They aspire to be Torah teachers but understand neither the content of their teaching nor the confidence with which they assert it. They have missed the mark: the aim of sound instruction is love from a pure heart, good conscience, and sincere faith. These teachers have departed from all three and wandered into empty talk.

(3) Verses 8-11 clarify the proper function of the law. The law is good when used for its intended purpose: identifying and restraining transgression. Paul demonstrates this by listing specific transgressions that correspond to the Decalogue, showing that the law's prohibitions serve the same end as sound teaching, which in turn accords with the gospel of God's glory. The false teachers misuse the law by making it a platform for speculation rather than an instrument for moral instruction.

(4) Verses 12-17 present Paul himself as the paradigmatic case of the gospel's power. The foremost sinner — blasphemer, persecutor, violent aggressor — received mercy because he acted in ignorance. The grace of Christ overflowed, bringing with it faith and love. The trustworthy saying summarizes the entire purpose of Christ's coming: to save sinners. Paul's story serves as the prototype for all future believers: if Christ's patience was sufficient for the worst case, it is sufficient for every case. The contemplation of this mercy produces a doxology that ascribes to God the attributes of eternal kingship, immortality, invisibility, and sole divinity.

(5) Verses 18-20 close the ring opened in verse 3. The charge is formally entrusted to Timothy, grounded in the prophecies that first identified his calling. He is to wage the good warfare by holding faith and good conscience — the same qualities the false teachers have abandoned. Two named individuals illustrate the catastrophic result of rejecting conscience: shipwrecked faith. Yet even their discipline has a corrective aim — they have been handed over to Satan not for destruction but for education.

The chapter's controlling architecture is the contrast between two relationships to conscience and faith. On one side stands Paul, who sinned in ignorance, received mercy, and now serves as the paradigm of Christ's patience. On the other side stand Hymenaeus, Alexander, and the unnamed false teachers, who have deliberately rejected conscience and made shipwreck of their faith. Timothy stands between the two as the commissioned agent of sound teaching, charged to hold fast to faith and good conscience while waging the warfare necessary to protect the community from those who have abandoned both. The gospel that saves sinners (v. 15) is the same gospel that demands faithful stewardship (v. 11) and disciplines those who corrupt it (v. 20). Mercy and accountability are not in tension; they are both expressions of the purpose of the God who saves.
