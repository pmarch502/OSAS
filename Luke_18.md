# Luke 18 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 17):** Luke 17 divides into two major movements. The first (17:1-19) addresses the conduct expected within the community of followers: warnings about causing others to stumble (17:1-2), the command to forgive repeatedly when a brother repents (17:3-4), the nature of faith even as small as a mustard seed (17:5-6), and the parable of the unworthy slave who, having done everything commanded, says only "we are unworthy slaves; we have done only that which we ought to have done" (17:10). The ten lepers episode (17:11-19) dramatizes a related theme: ten are cleansed, but only one -- a Samaritan, an outsider -- returns to give thanks. Jesus asks, "Were not ten cleansed? But the nine -- where are they? Was no one found who returned to give glory to God except this foreigner?" (17:17-18). The nine who were healed but did not return mirror the pattern of those who receive God's gifts but fail to respond with the appropriate posture of gratitude and dependence.

The second movement (17:20-37) shifts to eschatology. The Pharisees ask when the kingdom of God is coming, and Jesus responds, "The kingdom of God is not coming with signs to be observed; nor will they say, 'Look, here it is!' or, 'There it is!' For behold, the kingdom of God is in your midst" (17:20-21). He then turns to the disciples and describes the coming of the Son of Man in terms drawn from Israel's history: it will be sudden like lightning (17:24), but first "He must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation" (17:25). The days before the Son of Man's appearing will be like the days of Noah and the days of Lot -- people will be eating, drinking, marrying, buying, selling, planting, building, conducting ordinary life until sudden destruction falls (17:26-30). Lot's wife, who looked back, is held up as a warning: "whoever seeks to keep his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life will preserve it" (17:33). The discourse ends with a description of sudden, selective separation -- two in one bed, two grinding at a mill, one taken and one left -- and the disciples' question, "Where, Lord?" answered with the enigmatic proverb, "Where the body is, there also the vultures will be gathered" (17:37).

The eschatological discourse creates an urgent pastoral problem. The Son of Man will come, but first he must suffer and be rejected. There will be an interval -- a period of waiting during which the disciples will "long to see one of the days of the Son of Man" and will not see it (17:22). What should they do during this interval? How should they live in the gap between the present reality and the Son of Man's vindication?

**What the author argues immediately after (Luke 19:1-27):** Luke 19 opens with the Zacchaeus narrative (19:1-10). Zacchaeus is a chief tax collector and wealthy (architelonēs kai autos plousios). He climbs a tree to see Jesus, and Jesus calls him down and announces he must stay at Zacchaeus's house. The crowd grumbles: "He has gone to be the guest of a man who is a sinner" (19:7) -- the same complaint as 15:2. Zacchaeus responds by pledging to give half his possessions to the poor and to restore fourfold anything he has defrauded. Jesus declares, "Today salvation has come to this house, because he too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of Man has come to seek and to save that which was lost" (19:9-10). This is immediately followed by the parable of the minas (19:11-27), told because Jesus was near Jerusalem and the crowd thought the kingdom of God was going to appear immediately. A nobleman goes to a distant country to receive a kingdom and return. He gives ten servants one mina each to trade with during his absence. When he returns, he rewards the faithful servants and condemns the one who buried his mina in a cloth, and his citizens who rejected his kingship are slaughtered. The parable addresses the interval between the nobleman's departure and return -- the same interval that the eschatological discourse of 17:22-37 described.

**How chapter 18 connects these:** Luke 17 ended with the urgent question of the Son of Man's coming -- the certainty of vindication, the inevitability of an intervening period of suffering and waiting, and the sudden, decisive nature of the judgment. Chapter 18 answers the question that this eschatological teaching naturally provokes: what does faithfulness look like during the waiting? The chapter opens with a parable about persistent prayer during the interval before vindication comes (18:1-8) and closes with a question -- "when the Son of Man comes, will He find the faith on the earth?" -- that hangs over everything that follows. The rest of the chapter then illustrates what "the faith" the Son of Man is looking for actually looks like: it looks like a tax collector beating his breast and crying for mercy rather than a Pharisee reciting his achievements (18:9-14); it looks like a child receiving what is given rather than an adult earning what is deserved (18:15-17); it looks like the willingness to surrender everything and follow rather than the grief of clutching one's wealth (18:18-30); and it looks like a blind beggar crying out persistently, despite rebuke, for mercy from the Son of David (18:35-43). At the center of all this, Jesus reveals that the Son of Man's own path to vindication runs through Jerusalem, through suffering, through death and resurrection (18:31-34) -- the very pattern of suffering-then-vindication that the disciples must trust during their own waiting. Luke 19 then demonstrates the concrete fruit of this faith: Zacchaeus, a wealthy chief tax collector, does what the ruler of 18:18-23 could not -- he gives his wealth to the poor and follows Jesus -- and Jesus declares that salvation has come to his house and that the Son of Man came to seek and save the lost. The parable of the minas then addresses the same interval, asking what the servants do with what they have been given while the king is away. Chapter 18 thus stands as the hinge between the eschatological urgency of chapter 17 and the concrete responses of chapter 19, answering the question of what it means to have faith during the time of waiting.

## Verses 1-8: Persistent Prayer and the Vindication of God's Chosen

"Now He was telling them a parable to show that at all times they ought to pray and not lose heart, saying, 'In a certain city there was a judge who did not fear God and did not respect any person. And there was a widow in that city, and she kept coming to him, saying, "Give me justice against my opponent." For a while he was unwilling; but afterward he said to himself, "Even though I do not fear God nor respect any person, yet because this widow bothers me, I will give her justice; otherwise by continually coming she will wear me out."' And the Lord said, 'Hear what the unrighteous judge says; and will not God bring about justice for His elect who cry to Him day and night, and will He delay long over them? I tell you that He will bring about their justice quickly. However, when the Son of Man comes, will He find the faith on the earth?'"

Luke introduces this parable with a rare editorial statement of purpose: "to show that at all times they ought to pray and not lose heart" (pros to dein pantote proseuchesthai autous kai mē enkakein). The verb enkakeō means "to lose heart, to become weary, to give up" -- it describes the specific temptation that arises during a prolonged period of waiting when the promised outcome has not yet materialized. The pronoun "them" (autous) refers back to the disciples addressed in 17:22, who were told about the coming of the Son of Man and the long, difficult interval preceding it. The parable is addressed to people who are waiting for vindication that has not yet come and who are tempted to stop praying.

The judge is described by two negations: he "did not fear God" (ton theon mē phoboumenos) and "did not respect any person" (anthrōpon mē entrepomenos). These two qualities -- the fear of God and regard for people -- are the twin foundations of just judgment throughout Israel's scriptures. Jehoshaphat's charge to the judges he appointed runs: "Consider what you are doing, for you do not judge for man but for the LORD who is with you when you render judgment. Now then let the fear of the LORD be upon you; be very careful what you do, for the LORD our God will have no part in unrighteousness or partiality or the taking of a bribe" (2 Chronicles 19:6-7). Deuteronomy 1:17 establishes that judgment belongs to God. Leviticus 19:15 forbids partiality. This judge lacks every quality the Torah demands of those who administer justice. He is the antithesis of a righteous judge, and his characterization is designed to make the eventual argument from lesser to greater as sharp as possible.

The widow is identified only by her status. In first-century Palestine, a widow without a male advocate was among the most vulnerable members of society. She had limited legal standing to bring suits or enforce claims on her own. The Torah repeatedly commands the protection of widows as a measure of societal faithfulness: "You shall not afflict any widow or orphan" (Exodus 22:22); "He executes justice for the orphan and the widow" (Deuteronomy 10:18); "Cursed is he who distorts the justice due an alien, orphan, and widow" (Deuteronomy 27:19). The prophets use the treatment of widows as a diagnostic of Israel's spiritual condition: "Learn to do good; seek justice, reprove the ruthless, defend the orphan, plead for the widow" (Isaiah 1:17); "Do not oppress the widow or the orphan" (Zechariah 7:10). A judge who neglects a widow is not merely unjust in the abstract -- he violates one of the Torah's most explicit and frequently repeated obligations.

Her request is: "Give me justice against my opponent" (ekdikēson me apo tou antidikou mou). The verb ekdikeō means "to vindicate, to execute justice on behalf of, to grant legal protection." It does not mean revenge. She is asking for the legal system to function -- for the judge to do what judges exist to do. The word antidikos ("adversary, opponent") is a legal term for the opposing party in a lawsuit. She has a legitimate grievance, and she needs the institutional power of the court to address it. Without the judge's ruling, she has no recourse.

Her method is persistence. The verb ērcheto (imperfect tense -- "she kept coming") indicates repeated, ongoing action. She came again and again. The judge's internal monologue reveals his motivation for finally acting: "because this widow bothers me" (dia to parechein moi kopon tēn chēran tautēn) -- literally, "because this widow causes me trouble." Then: "otherwise by continually coming she will wear me out" (hina mē eis telos erchomenē hypōpiazē me). The verb hypōpiazō is vivid -- it literally means "to strike under the eye, to give a black eye." It is a boxing term (Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 9:27 for disciplining his body). The metaphor is of relentless pounding. The judge does not grant justice because he has been moved by compassion or persuaded by the merits of the case. He grants justice because the widow's persistence has become unbearable. He capitulates to make her stop.

Jesus's application uses the standard rabbinic argument from lesser to greater (qal vahomer): "Hear what the unrighteous judge says; and will not God bring about justice for His elect who cry to Him day and night?" (ho de theos ou mē poiēsē tēn ekdikēsin tōn eklektōn autou tōn boōntōn autō hēmeras kai nyktos). The argument runs: if even a thoroughly corrupt judge will eventually grant justice simply because of persistence, how much more will God -- who is perfectly righteous, who does fear justice, who does care for the oppressed -- vindicate his chosen people who cry out to him continuously?

The term "His elect" (tōn eklektōn autou) requires careful attention. In the LXX, the language of election (eklogē, eklektos, eklegomai) consistently refers to corporate, covenantal identity -- God's chosen people, Israel, selected for a purpose and a vocation, not individual selection for eternal destiny. In Isaiah 42:1, God's servant is "my chosen one" (ho eklektos mou); in Isaiah 65:9, 15, 22, the "elect" are the remnant of faithful Israel contrasted with those who have abandoned God. In the Psalms, Israel is "His chosen ones" (Psalm 105:6, 43; 106:5). The term here designates the community of those who belong to God and who are suffering injustice -- the people who, during the interval before the Son of Man's vindication, endure opposition and cry out for God to act. The question is not about their individual eternal security but about whether God will act on behalf of his suffering people.

The phrase "and will He delay long over them?" (kai makrothymei ep' autois) uses the verb makrothymeō, which can mean "to be patient, to be long-suffering, to delay." The question probes the anxiety of the waiting period: is God's apparent inaction during the interval evidence that he does not care, or is it patience with a purpose? Jesus's answer is decisive: "He will bring about their justice quickly" (poiēsei tēn ekdikēsin autōn en tachei). The adverb en tachei ("quickly, speedily, without delay") does not necessarily mean "immediately" in clock time but rather "certainly and without hesitation" -- when God acts, he will act decisively and completely. The vindication will come, and when it comes, it will come swiftly.

Then the devastating question in verse 8b: "However, when the Son of Man comes, will He find the faith on the earth?" (plēn ho huios tou anthrōpou elthōn ara heurēsei tēn pistin epi tēs gēs). The conjunction plēn ("however, nevertheless, but") marks a turn. The question shifts from whether God will act (he will) to whether the people will still be faithful when he does. The definite article before pistin -- tēn pistin, "the faith," not just "faith" in general -- is significant. It points to a specific quality: the faith of persistent, trusting, never-giving-up prayer exemplified by the widow. Will the Son of Man, when he arrives, find people who have kept crying out, who have not lost heart, who have not given up on God's justice? The question is left open -- unanswered -- and its openness is itself the pastoral point. It does not predict apostasy; it warns against it. The form of the question (ara, which expects uncertainty in the answer) communicates that the outcome depends on whether the hearers will persevere. The faith the Son of Man is looking for is not a single moment of belief but an ongoing posture of dependent, persistent trust -- the widow's posture -- sustained throughout the long period of waiting.

This parable is directly continuous with the eschatological discourse of 17:22-37. The disciples were told they would long for the Son of Man's day and not see it (17:22). They were told the Son of Man must first suffer and be rejected (17:25). They were told the day would come suddenly, like lightning, like the flood in Noah's time, like fire on Sodom (17:24, 26-30). The parable now addresses the existential reality of that interval: the temptation to stop praying, to lose heart, to conclude that God is as indifferent as the unjust judge. The answer is that God is not indifferent -- but the question remains whether his people will still be praying when he arrives.

## Verses 9-14: Two Men in the Temple -- Self-Justification and Mercy

"And He also told this parable to some people who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and viewed others with contempt: 'Two men went up into the temple to pray, one a Pharisee and the other a tax collector. The Pharisee stood and was praying this to himself: "God, I thank You that I am not like other people: swindlers, unjust, adulterers, or even like this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I pay tithes of all that I get." But the tax collector, standing some distance away, was even unwilling to lift up his eyes to heaven, but was beating his breast, saying, "God, be merciful to me, the sinner!" I tell you, this man went to his house justified rather than the other; for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted.'"

Luke's editorial introduction identifies the target audience with precision: "some people who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and viewed others with contempt" (pros tinas tous pepoithotas eph' heautois hoti eisin dikaioi kai exouthenountas tous loipous). Two characteristics define this group. First, self-trust regarding their own righteousness: the perfect participle pepoithotas (from peithō, "to persuade, to trust") indicates a settled, completed state -- they have persuaded themselves and remain persuaded. Their righteousness is self-assessed and self-certified. Second, contempt for others: the verb exoutheneō means "to treat as nothing, to despise, to regard with utter contempt." The combination is pointed -- their confidence in their own standing produces not gratitude but disdain for everyone else. This characterization echoes 16:15, where Jesus told the Pharisees: "You are those who justify yourselves before people, but God knows your hearts; for that which is highly esteemed among people is detestable in the sight of God." The audience of this parable is the same group, whether or not the word "Pharisee" is used in the introduction.

The setting is the temple (to hieron), the central place of Israel's worship and the only legitimate site for sacrifice. Going "up" (anebēsan) is both geographical (the temple mount was elevated) and liturgical -- one ascends to God's presence. Two men make this ascent. Their identities establish a maximum social contrast within Jewish life. The Pharisee is a member of the most scrupulous religious movement in first-century Judaism, committed to extending the purity of the temple into daily life through meticulous observance of the Torah and the oral traditions. The tax collector (telonēs) is a member of one of the most despised social groups -- agents of the Roman taxation system who were widely regarded as collaborators with the occupying power and were frequently guilty of extortion and fraud. A first-century Jewish audience would immediately know which of these two men "belonged" in the temple and which did not.

The Pharisee "stood and was praying this to himself" (statheis pros heauton tauta prosēucheto). The phrase pros heauton is ambiguous -- it can modify "stood" ("stood by himself," i.e., separately) or "praying" ("prayed to himself," i.e., his prayer was directed at himself rather than at God). Either reading works within the parable's logic: the Pharisee is isolated and self-referential. His prayer takes the grammatical form of thanksgiving -- "God, I thank You" (ho theos, eucharistō soi) -- but the content is a catalog of his own merits delivered by contrast with others' failings. "I am not like other people: swindlers, unjust, adulterers, or even like this tax collector" (ouk eimi hōsper hoi loipoi tōn anthrōpōn, harpages, adikoi, moichoi, ē kai hōs houtos ho telonēs). The prayer does not ask God for anything. It informs God of what the Pharisee has achieved. The demonstrative houtos ("this one") used for the tax collector is dismissive -- it is the language of pointing at an object rather than acknowledging a person.

His positive claims: "I fast twice a week; I pay tithes of all that I get" (nēsteuō dis tou sabbatou, apodekateuō panta hosa ktōmai). Fasting twice a week exceeded the Torah's requirements. The Torah mandates fasting only on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29-31; 23:27-32; Numbers 29:7). The Pharisaic practice of fasting on Monday and Thursday (attested in Didache 8:1 and later rabbinic sources) was a voluntary discipline that went beyond what God had commanded. Tithing "all that I get" also exceeds the written requirement. The Torah commands tithing of agricultural produce, livestock, and certain other goods (Leviticus 27:30-33; Numbers 18:21-24; Deuteronomy 14:22-29), but the Pharisees extended this to include even garden herbs (cf. Luke 11:42, where Jesus says, "You pay tithe of mint and rue and every kind of garden herb, and yet disregard justice and the love of God"). The Pharisee's observance is genuine. He is not lying. He really does fast, and he really does tithe meticulously. The problem is not the falsehood of his claims but the posture from which he makes them: he stands before God as one who has earned his standing, reciting his achievements as the basis for his relationship with God. His prayer is a performance review in which he gives himself the highest marks.

The tax collector "standing some distance away" (makrothen hestōs) places himself at the periphery. He does not approach. He will not even "lift up his eyes to heaven" (ouk ēthelen oude tous ophthalmous eparai eis ton ouranon). In Jewish prayer practice, lifting the eyes toward heaven was a standard gesture of address to God (cf. Psalm 123:1; John 17:1). The tax collector cannot bring himself to make even this basic gesture of prayer. Instead, he "was beating his breast" (etypten to stēthos autou). Breast-beating was a gesture of grief, mourning, and self-accusation -- the outward expression of inward anguish over one's own condition. It is the posture of lamentation, not worship.

His prayer is seven words in Greek: ho theos, hilasthēti moi tō hamartōlō -- "God, be merciful to me, the sinner." The verb hilaskomai is theologically weighted. In the LXX, it is used for the act of atonement -- the covering or dealing with sin that restores the broken relationship between God and the sinner. It appears in the context of the mercy seat (hilastērion) in Exodus 25:17-22, the place where atonement was made on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). The tax collector is not asking for generic kindness or compassion. He is invoking the entire sacrificial system -- the mechanism by which God deals with sin and restores the guilty to relationship. His prayer is essentially: "God, do for me what the Day of Atonement does -- cover my sin, atone for my guilt." The definite article before hamartōlō -- tō hamartōlō, "the sinner" -- is not the language of general human sinfulness ("a sinner among many") but of specific, personal conviction: I am the sinner, the one whose guilt is immediately before me. He does not compare himself to anyone else. His vision is entirely occupied by his own need and God's mercy.

Jesus's verdict inverts every social and religious expectation: "I tell you, this man went to his house justified rather than the other" (katebē houtos dedikaiōmenos eis ton oikon autou par' ekeinon). The verb dikaioō ("to justify, to declare righteous") is the central term. The tax collector -- the despised collaborator, the one who could not even lift his eyes -- went home justified. The Pharisee -- the scrupulous observer, the one who exceeded the Torah's demands -- did not. The word dedikaiōmenos is a perfect passive participle: "having been justified" -- the passive voice indicates that the justification comes from God, not from the man himself. It was done to him, not achieved by him. This directly contradicts the posture described in 16:15, where the Pharisees "justify themselves" (dikaiountes heautous). The Pharisee's self-justification is not justification at all. True justification is God's act, given to the one who asks for mercy rather than recites merits.

The concluding principle -- "for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted" (hoti pas ho hypsōn heauton tapeinōthēsetai, ho de tapeinōn heauton hypsōthēsetai) -- is verbatim identical to 14:11, where it concluded the teaching about seating at the Pharisee's banquet. Its repetition creates a structural frame around the entire section from 14:11 to 18:14. The passive verbs (tapeinōthēsetai, "will be humbled"; hypsōthēsetai, "will be exalted") are divine passives: God is the one who humbles and exalts. The principle echoes Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:7-8: "The LORD makes poor and rich; He brings low, He also exalts. He raises the poor from the dust, He lifts the needy from the ash heap"), the Magnificat (Luke 1:52: "He has brought down rulers from their thrones and has exalted those who were humble"), and the beatitudes and woes (Luke 6:20-26). It is one of the most deeply embedded patterns in Luke's theology: God reverses the positions that human beings assign to themselves and one another.

This parable answers the question of verse 8 from a new angle. The Son of Man asks whether he will find "the faith" on earth. The Pharisee has religion -- abundant, meticulous religion -- but he does not have the faith the Son of Man is looking for. His faith is in himself: his fasting, his tithing, his moral superiority. The tax collector has the faith: desperate, self-emptying dependence on God's mercy with no claim of merit. The faith the Son of Man seeks is not religious performance but the posture of the one who has nothing to offer except need.

## Verses 15-17: Receiving the Kingdom as Infants Receive

"And they were bringing even their babies to Him so that He would touch them; but when the disciples saw it, they began rebuking them. But Jesus called for them, saying, 'Permit the children to come to Me, and do not hinder them, for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these. Truly I say to you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child will not enter it at all.'"

The word Luke uses for those being brought to Jesus is brephē -- "infants, babies" -- not the more general paidia ("children") used in verse 16. Brephē are pre-verbal, pre-rational. They are human beings who can do nothing for themselves, who cannot articulate a prayer, who cannot fast or tithe or recite their moral accomplishments. They are pure recipients. Luke's choice of this specific word, placed immediately after the parable contrasting the Pharisee's self-sufficiency with the tax collector's helplessness, is deliberate.

The parents bring the infants "so that He would touch them" (hina autōn haptētai). The purpose is physical contact -- the laying on of hands, a gesture of blessing. The disciples rebuke (epetimōn) those bringing them. The verb epitimaō is a strong word -- the same word used for rebuking demons (4:35, 41; 9:42) and silencing storms (8:24). The disciples treat the bringing of infants as an intrusion, an interruption of more important business. Their rebuke reveals their assumption about who has access to Jesus and what constitutes legitimate engagement with him.

Jesus's response reverses the disciples' judgment: "Permit the children to come to Me, and do not hinder them" (aphete ta paidia erchesthai pros me kai mē kōlyete auta). The verb kōlyō ("to prevent, to hinder, to forbid") will appear again in a different context in Acts, where it becomes the decisive word in the debate about Gentile inclusion: "Can anyone forbid water, that these should not be baptized?" (Acts 10:47; cf. 11:17). The same impulse that would exclude infants from Jesus's touch is the impulse that would exclude outsiders from God's community. Jesus commands that it be stopped.

The reason: "for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these" (tōn toioutōn estin hē basileia tou theou). The dative tōn toioutōn ("of such ones, of ones like these") indicates that the kingdom belongs to people who are characterized by what infants are characterized by: complete inability to earn, achieve, claim, or demand. The kingdom is possessed by those who have nothing to bring.

The saying that follows universalizes the point: "whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child will not enter it at all" (hos an mē dexētai tēn basileian tou theou hōs paidion, ou mē eiselthē eis autēn). The verb dechomai ("to receive, to welcome, to accept") is the key. It is the same verb used in 15:2 for Jesus "receiving" sinners, in 16:4 and 16:9 for being "received" into homes and eternal dwellings, and throughout Luke for the acceptance of what is offered. The kingdom must be received -- it is a gift given, not a wage earned, not a status achieved. The phrase hōs paidion ("as a child") does not specify which quality of childhood is in view -- innocence, trust, obedience. The context supplies the answer: a child (and especially a brephos, an infant) receives without having done anything to deserve what is received. The child's only qualification is need. This is exactly the posture of the tax collector, who came with nothing but his guilt and went home justified. It is the opposite of the Pharisee, who came with a resume and went home without justification.

The double negative ou mē with the subjunctive eiselthē is the strongest negation available in Greek: "will absolutely not enter." The exclusion is total. There is no alternative path into the kingdom for those who will not receive it as a gift. The door is open, but only to those who come through it empty-handed.

This episode connects the preceding parable to what follows. The tax collector modeled child-like reception of God's mercy. The rich ruler in the next section will model the opposite: he will come to Jesus with his accomplishments and his possessions, and he will be unable to receive the kingdom because he cannot let go of what he holds.

## Verses 18-30: The Ruler Who Could Not Let Go

"A ruler questioned Him, saying, 'Good Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?' And Jesus said to him, 'Why do you call Me good? No one is good except God alone. You know the commandments: "Do not commit adultery, Do not murder, Do not steal, Do not bear false witness, Honor your father and mother."' And he said, 'All these things I have kept from my youth.' When Jesus heard this, He said to him, 'One thing you still lack; sell all that you possess and distribute the proceeds to the poor, and you shall have treasure in heaven; and come, follow Me.' But when he had heard these things, he became very sad, for he was extremely rich. And Jesus looked at him and said, 'How hard it is for those who are wealthy to enter the kingdom of God! For it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God.' Those who heard it said, 'Then who can be saved?' But He said, 'The things that are impossible with people are possible with God.' Peter said, 'Behold, we have left our own homes and followed You.' And He said to them, 'Truly I say to you, there is no one who has left house or wife or brothers or parents or children, for the sake of the kingdom of God, who will not receive many times as much at this time and in the age to come, eternal life.'"

A "ruler" (archōn) approaches Jesus. Luke does not specify what kind of ruler -- synagogue leader, member of a local council, civil official -- but the title conveys social position, authority, and respectability. He is a man of standing in his community. His question -- "What shall I do to inherit eternal life?" (ti poiēsas zōēn aiōnion klēronomēsō) -- combines two concepts that exist in tension. "What shall I do" (ti poiēsas) frames eternal life as something achievable through action: what must I accomplish? But "inherit" (klēronomēsō, from klēronomeō) frames it as a gift received within a family: an inheritance is not earned but received by virtue of one's relationship to the one who gives it. The ruler's question thus contains its own contradiction. He wants to earn an inheritance -- to achieve what can only be received.

Jesus's initial response -- "Why do you call Me good? No one is good except God alone" (ti me legeis agathon; oudeis agathos ei mē heis ho theos) -- is not a denial of his own goodness but a challenge to the ruler's use of the word. "Good" (agathos) in its absolute sense belongs to God alone. The ruler used it as a flattering form of address -- "Good Teacher" -- the kind of respectful title one gives to a religious authority. Jesus redirects: do you understand what you are saying? If goodness in its true sense belongs only to God, then calling someone "good" is either careless flattery or a profound theological claim. The challenge forces the ruler to consider whether he is engaging with Jesus at the appropriate level of seriousness.

Jesus then lists commandments from the second table of the Decalogue -- those governing human relationships: "Do not commit adultery, Do not murder, Do not steal, Do not bear false witness, Honor your father and mother." The order differs from both the Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 sequences, but this was common in first-century citation of the commandments (cf. Romans 13:9, which also reorders them). What is notable is what Jesus omits. He does not mention the commandments of the first table -- those governing the relationship between the person and God (no other gods, no idols, no misuse of God's name, Sabbath observance). And he does not mention the tenth commandment: "Do not covet." Both omissions are significant. The first table is precisely what the ruler's problem concerns -- his ultimate allegiance is divided, as will become clear. The tenth commandment -- do not covet, do not grasp at what is not yours -- is the interior disposition that his wealth reveals he has not mastered.

The ruler's response is confident: "All these things I have kept from my youth" (tauta panta ephylaxamēn ek neotētos). The verb phylassō means "to guard, to keep, to observe." He claims not merely familiarity but faithful observance of the commandments throughout his life. Like the Pharisee in the temple, his religious resume is genuine. He is not lying. The problem is not that he has failed to keep the second-table commandments but that he believes this keeping constitutes everything required.

Jesus's response identifies the one remaining deficiency: "One thing you still lack" (eti hen soi leipei). The phrase eti hen ("still one thing") is precise -- not many things, not a general deficiency, but one specific failure. "Sell all that you possess and distribute the proceeds to the poor, and you shall have treasure in heaven; and come, follow Me" (panta hosa echeis pōlēson kai diados ptōchois, kai hexeis thēsauron en ouranois, kai deuro akolouthei moi). The instruction has three parts: sell everything, give the proceeds to the poor, and follow Jesus. The first two parts are the practical application of everything Jesus has taught about wealth since chapter 12: "Sell your possessions and give to charity; make yourselves money belts which do not wear out, an unfailing treasure in the heavens" (12:33). They are the enactment of the shrewd manager's logic in 16:1-9: use worldly wealth to "make friends" who will welcome you into eternal dwellings. They are the opposite of the rich man in 16:19-31, who feasted while Lazarus starved. The third part -- "come, follow Me" -- is the call to discipleship itself, the same call issued to Peter and James and John (5:10-11) and to Levi (5:27-28). The ruler is being invited to join the community of followers. But the invitation requires the surrender of what he holds most tightly.

"But when he had heard these things, he became very sad, for he was extremely rich" (perilypos egeneto; ēn gar plousios sphodra). The adjective perilypos means "deeply grieved, surrounded by sorrow" -- the same word used in Mark 6:26 for Herod's grief when he must fulfill his oath to behead John the Baptist. The ruler's grief is genuine. He is not indifferent. He understands what is being asked, and the prospect grieves him. But he does not do it. His wealth holds him more tightly than the call of Jesus. Luke notes the reason with devastating simplicity: "for he was extremely rich" (ēn gar plousios sphodra). The qualifier sphodra ("exceedingly, extremely") intensifies the problem. The more he has, the harder it is to let go.

The ruler has failed the test that verses 15-17 established. The kingdom must be received "like a child" -- with empty hands, with no claims, with nothing to cling to. The ruler cannot empty his hands. His wealth is not merely an obstacle; it is the thing he trusts in the way the tax collector trusted in God's mercy. His wealth functions as his god. Jesus's comment to the watching crowd makes this explicit: "How hard it is for those who are wealthy to enter the kingdom of God!" (pōs dyskolōs hoi ta chrēmata echontes eis tēn basileian tou theou eisporeuontai). The adverb dyskolōs ("with difficulty, hardly") understates what the next verse will make absolute.

"For it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God" (eukopōteron gar estin kamelon dia trēmatos belonēs eiselthein ē plousion eis tēn basileian tou theou eiselthein). This is hyperbolic imagery presenting a physical impossibility. A camel (kamēlos) was the largest animal commonly encountered in Palestine. The eye of a needle (trēma belonēs -- trēma means "hole," belonē means "sewing needle") was the smallest opening in daily life. There is no evidence for the sometimes-proposed interpretation that the "needle's eye" was a small gate in Jerusalem's walls through which a camel could squeeze if it knelt; this appears to be a medieval invention designed to soften the saying. Jesus describes something that cannot happen. The entry of a rich person into the kingdom is not merely difficult; it is, by human resources alone, impossible.

The crowd's response is the natural one: "Then who can be saved?" (kai tis dynatai sōthēnai). The verb sōzō ("to save") here carries its full weight -- not just physical deliverance but eschatological salvation, entry into God's kingdom, eternal life. In first-century Jewish thought, wealth was often understood as a sign of God's blessing (cf. Deuteronomy 28:1-14, where material prosperity is promised as a reward for covenant faithfulness). If the wealthy -- those who appear to be blessed by God -- cannot enter, then who can? The question expresses genuine bewilderment: the audience's entire framework for understanding who is "in" and who is "out" has been overturned.

Jesus's answer shifts the ground entirely: "The things that are impossible with people are possible with God" (ta adynata para anthrōpois dynata para tō theō estin). The word adynata ("impossible things") is emphatic. Jesus does not soften the camel-through-needle image or explain it away. He affirms that human resources are genuinely insufficient for entry into the kingdom. But he adds that God's power is not limited by human impossibility. The entry of anyone into the kingdom -- rich or poor -- is ultimately God's doing, not a human achievement. This connects directly to the child-like reception of verse 17: the kingdom is entered not by earning but by receiving, and the receiving itself is made possible by God. The saying echoes Genesis 18:14, where God asks Abraham regarding Sarah's impossible pregnancy: "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" The pattern is the same: what is humanly impossible is divinely possible.

Peter's interjection -- "Behold, we have left our own homes and followed You" (idou hēmeis aphentes ta idia ēkolouthēsamen soi) -- is both an implicit question ("What about us?") and a statement of fact. The disciples have done what the ruler could not: they left their possessions and their homes and followed. Peter does not claim to have earned the kingdom by this, but he observes that he and the other disciples have made the sacrifice the ruler refused.

Jesus's response affirms the disciples' choice without turning it into a transaction: "There is no one who has left house or wife or brothers or parents or children, for the sake of the kingdom of God, who will not receive many times as much at this time and in the age to come, eternal life" (oudeis estin hos aphēken oikian ē gynaika ē adelphous ē goneis ē tekna heneken tēs basileias tou theou, hos ouchi mē apolabē pollaplasiona en tō kairō toutō kai en tō aiōni tō erchomenō zōēn aiōnion). The promise has two temporal dimensions. "At this time" (en tō kairō toutō) -- in the present age, within the community of believers -- those who have left family and home find a new family and a new home in the fellowship of the kingdom community. The early church's practice of shared possessions (Acts 2:44-45; 4:32-37) demonstrates what this "many times as much" looks like concretely. "In the age to come" (en tō aiōni tō erchomenō) -- in the eschatological future -- the promise is "eternal life" (zōēn aiōnion), the very thing the ruler asked about. The ruler asked how to inherit eternal life; Jesus told him to sell everything and follow; the ruler refused; but those who have done what the ruler refused will receive what the ruler sought. The path to eternal life runs through the surrender the ruler could not make.

The contrast between the ruler and the disciples models the choice presented throughout the chapter. The tax collector in the temple and the infants brought to Jesus and the disciples who left everything represent one way of approaching the kingdom: with empty hands, trusting God. The Pharisee in the temple and the ruler who goes away sad represent the other: with full hands, trusting oneself. The kingdom is entered through the first way, not the second.

## Verses 31-34: The Son of Man's Path Through Suffering

"Then He took the twelve aside and said to them, 'Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and all things which are written through the prophets about the Son of Man will be accomplished. For He will be handed over to the Gentiles, and will be mocked and treated shamefully and spit upon, and after they have scourged Him, they will kill Him; and the third day He will rise again.' But the disciples understood none of these things, and the meaning of this statement was hidden from them, and they did not comprehend the things that were said."

Jesus takes the Twelve aside from the larger group and speaks privately. The destination is named: Jerusalem (Hierosolyma). In Luke's narrative, the entire journey from 9:51 onward has been oriented toward Jerusalem: "He set His face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51). Every teaching, every healing, every encounter since then has been conducted along this road. Now the purpose is stated explicitly.

"All things which are written through the prophets about the Son of Man will be accomplished" (telesthēsetai panta ta gegrammena dia tōn prophētōn tō huiō tou anthrōpou). The verb teleō means "to complete, to accomplish, to bring to fulfillment" -- it is the verb of consummation, of purpose being fully realized. The perfect passive participle gegrammena ("things that have been written") indicates that what is about to happen is not an accident or a defeat but the fulfillment of what was already inscribed in the prophetic writings. The phrase "through the prophets" (dia tōn prophētōn) invokes the entire prophetic tradition -- the same "Moses and the Prophets" that Abraham declared sufficient for the rich man's brothers in 16:29, the same "Law and Prophets" proclaimed until John in 16:16. The scriptures that the Pharisees claim to uphold and that the rich man's brothers were told to heed -- these scriptures speak of the Son of Man's suffering, and everything they say will be fulfilled.

The specifics are enumerated: "He will be handed over to the Gentiles" (paradothēsetai gar tois ethnesin) -- the passive voice (the divine passive) indicates that even the handing over to the Gentiles is within God's purpose. "Mocked" (empaichthēsetai), "treated shamefully" (hybristhēsetai), "spit upon" (emptysthēsetai) -- three terms describing public degradation and humiliation. "Scourged" (mastigōsantes) and "killed" (apoktenousin) -- physical torture and death. These correspond to the treatment of the prophets throughout Israel's history and to the specific language of Isaiah's suffering servant passages (Isaiah 50:6: "I gave My back to those who strike Me, and My cheeks to those who pluck out the beard; I did not cover My face from humiliation and spitting"; Isaiah 53:3-7). The Son of Man's path runs through the full spectrum of human cruelty.

"And the third day He will rise again" (kai tē hēmera tē tritē anastēsetai). The verb anistēmi ("to rise, to stand up") is the standard resurrection verb. The three-day pattern echoes Hosea 6:2: "He will revive us after two days; He will raise us up on the third day, that we may live before Him." The suffering and death are not the end but the passage to vindication -- the same pattern the parable of the widow established. The period of injustice, oppression, and apparent divine inaction will end with God's decisive act of vindication. The Son of Man must suffer before the glory (cf. 17:25).

The disciples' response is described with threefold emphasis on their failure to comprehend: "the disciples understood none of these things" (kai autoi ouden toutōn synēkan), "the meaning of this statement was hidden from them" (kai ēn to rhēma touto kekrymmenon ap' autōn), "and they did not comprehend the things that were said" (kai ouk eginōskon ta legomena). The triple statement is emphatic. The perfect passive participle kekrymmenon ("having been hidden") suggests a divine concealment -- God has hidden the meaning from them for the time being (cf. 9:45, which uses nearly identical language after the second prediction of suffering). They are not stupid; they are in a condition of divinely permitted incomprehension. The full meaning of the Son of Man's suffering, death, and resurrection will become clear only after the events have occurred (cf. 24:25-27, 44-47, where the risen Jesus opens the scriptures and their minds to understand).

This prediction is placed at the center of the chapter's structure for a reason. The parable of the widow asked the disciples to persist in prayer during the interval before vindication. The parables and encounters that surround this prediction illustrate what faithful waiting looks like. But now Jesus reveals that his own path -- the Son of Man's path -- is the archetype of the pattern: suffering first, then vindication. The disciples' perseverance during the interval is grounded in the Son of Man's own experience of the same pattern. He will suffer, he will be killed, and he will rise. Their waiting is not aimless or hopeless but modeled on his own journey through death to life.

## Verses 35-43: The Blind Man Who Cried Out for Mercy

"As Jesus was approaching Jericho, a blind man was sitting by the road begging. Now hearing a crowd going by, he began to inquire what this was. They told him that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by. And he called out, saying, 'Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!' Those who led the way were sternly telling him to be quiet; but he kept crying out all the more, 'Son of David, have mercy on me!' And Jesus stopped and commanded that he be brought to Him; and when he came near, He questioned him, 'What do you want Me to do for you?' And he said, 'Lord, I want to regain my sight!' And Jesus said to him, 'Receive your sight; your faith has saved you.' Immediately he regained his sight and began following Him, glorifying God; and when all the people saw it, they gave praise to God."

This episode closes the chapter and, like a capstone, draws together all the themes that have preceded it. The blind man enacts every quality the chapter has presented as the faith the Son of Man is looking for.

The setting is specific: Jesus is approaching Jericho, the last major city before the ascent to Jerusalem. The journey that began in 9:51 is nearing its destination. The blind man sits "by the road" (para tēn hodon), begging. His situation combines two forms of deprivation: he is blind and he is destitute. In first-century Palestine, blindness was a permanent condition that excluded a person from most forms of productive labor. Begging was the only option available to many who suffered from it. The blind man is, like the widow in the opening parable, entirely without resources. He has no power, no social standing, no means of self-help. He can only ask.

When he hears the crowd and learns that "Jesus of Nazareth is passing by" (Iēsous ho Nazōraios parerchetai), he cries out with a title that goes beyond what the crowd used: "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (Iēsou huie Dauid, eleēson me). The crowd identified Jesus as "the Nazarene" -- a geographical designation. The blind man identifies him as "Son of David" -- a messianic title drawn from Israel's prophetic tradition. The promise to David in 2 Samuel 7:12-16 that God would establish his descendant's kingdom forever generated the expectation of a future Davidic king who would restore Israel. Isaiah 11:1-5 describes the shoot from Jesse's root who will judge with righteousness and faithfulness. The blind man, who cannot see Jesus, recognizes who he is more clearly than the crowd who can see.

His cry -- "have mercy on me" (eleēson me) -- echoes the tax collector's prayer: "God, be merciful to me" (ho theos, hilasthēti moi). Both men approach with the same posture: need, not merit. Both ask for mercy, not reward. The blind man's prayer is the same cry that appears throughout the Psalms: "Have mercy on me, O God" (eleēson me, ho theos -- Psalm 51:1 LXX [50:3]); "Have mercy on me, O LORD, for I am in distress" (Psalm 31:9 [30:10]). It is the fundamental prayer of the helpless to the one who has power.

"Those who led the way were sternly telling him to be quiet" (hoi proagontes epetimōn autō hina sigēsē). The verb epitimaō ("to rebuke, to sternly charge") is the same word used in verse 15 for the disciples' rebuke of those bringing infants. The pattern repeats: those around Jesus attempt to prevent the marginalized from reaching him. The infants were rebuked; the blind man is rebuked. In both cases, Jesus overrides the gatekeepers.

The blind man's response to being silenced is identical to the widow's response to the unjust judge's refusal: persistence. "He kept crying out all the more" (autos pollō mallon ekrazen). The comparative pollō mallon ("much more, all the more") indicates that the rebuke intensified his effort rather than silencing him. He is the living embodiment of the parable that opened the chapter: pray and do not lose heart. The widow kept coming to the judge; the blind man keeps crying out to the Son of David. Both refuse to be discouraged by rejection.

Jesus stops. The verb histēmi in the passive (statheis) conveys that Jesus came to a halt -- the crowd's momentum toward Jerusalem is interrupted by one man's cry. He commands that the man be brought to him and asks: "What do you want Me to do for you?" (ti soi theleis poiēsō). The question is not for Jesus's information -- the man is blind and has been crying for mercy. It is for the man's own sake: it gives him the opportunity to articulate his need before the one who can meet it. It is the inverse of the Pharisee's prayer in the temple. The Pharisee told God what he had done for God; the blind man tells Jesus what he needs Jesus to do for him.

The blind man's answer: "Lord, I want to regain my sight" (Kyrie, hina anablepsō). The title Kyrie ("Lord") carries more weight than a polite "sir" -- in the context of the messianic address "Son of David," it acknowledges authority and lordship. The verb anablepō means "to look up, to recover sight" -- it is used both literally (physical sight) and metaphorically in the LXX and the New Testament. The blind man asks for the reversal of his condition.

Jesus's response: "Receive your sight; your faith has saved you" (anablepson; hē pistis sou sesōken se). The verb sōzō ("to save") here, as throughout Luke, carries both physical and spiritual dimensions. In 7:50, Jesus tells the sinful woman who anointed his feet, "Your faith has saved you; go in peace." In 8:48, he tells the woman with the hemorrhage, "Your faith has made you well; go in peace." In 17:19, he tells the Samaritan leper who returned to give thanks, "Your faith has made you well." The phrase hē pistis sou sesōken se is a formula that recurs across Luke's Gospel, and in each case it describes not merely a physical healing but a comprehensive deliverance -- the wholeness that comes from trusting in Jesus. The blind man's faith is "the faith" (tēn pistin) that verse 8 asked about. It is the faith of persistent, dependent, mercy-seeking trust -- the faith of the widow, the tax collector, the infant. It is the faith the Son of Man is looking for.

"Immediately he regained his sight and began following Him, glorifying God" (parachrēma aneblepsen kai ēkolouthei autō doxazōn ton theon). Three things happen. First, the healing is instantaneous (parachrēma, "immediately, at once"). Second, he "began following Him" (ēkolouthei autō, imperfect tense -- ongoing action). The verb akoloutheō ("to follow") is the technical term for discipleship in the Gospels. The rich ruler was told "come, follow Me" and could not; the blind man, without being told, follows. He has received what the ruler could not let go enough to receive. Third, he glorifies God (doxazōn ton theon). His response to healing is worship -- the upward orientation that the tax collector could barely manage and the Pharisee had replaced with self-congratulation.

"And when all the people saw it, they gave praise to God" (kai pas ho laos idōn edōken ainon tō theō). The chapter that opened with the question of whether the Son of Man would find faith on earth closes with an entire crowd giving praise to God. The blind man's faith, far from being private, generates communal worship. His persistent cry -- which the crowd tried to silence -- results in a public display of God's power that moves everyone who witnesses it to praise.

## The Chapter's Unified Argument

Luke 18 is structured as a sustained answer to the question posed at its opening: when the Son of Man comes, will he find the faith on the earth? Every section of the chapter illustrates, from a different angle, what that faith is and what it is not.

**The widow and the judge (vv. 1-8)** establish the framework: the faith the Son of Man seeks is the faith of persistent, never-giving-up prayer during the interval before divine vindication. God will bring about justice for his chosen people, but the danger is that they will stop crying out before he acts. The faith required is not a single moment of belief but an ongoing, unrelenting posture of dependence on God's justice.

**The two men in the temple (vv. 9-14)** define the interior quality of that faith by contrast. The Pharisee has religious accomplishment but trusts in himself; his prayer is a performance review. The tax collector has nothing but need and throws himself on God's mercy. The Son of Man's faith looks like the tax collector, not the Pharisee. Justification comes from God's act of mercy toward the one who asks, not from the self-assessment of the one who recites his merits. The concluding principle -- the one who exalts himself will be humbled, the one who humbles himself will be exalted -- frames this as a pattern of divine reversal that governs the entire kingdom.

**The infants (vv. 15-17)** crystallize the point in its simplest form: the kingdom belongs to those who receive it as infants receive -- without merit, without contribution, without even the capacity to understand what they are receiving. The kingdom cannot be entered any other way.

**The ruler (vv. 18-30)** provides the negative example: a man who has kept the commandments, who has genuine religious accomplishment, who asks the right question about eternal life -- but who cannot enter the kingdom because he cannot surrender what he holds. His wealth functions as the alternative trust that prevents child-like reception. The exchange with Peter and the disciples then shows the positive example: those who have left everything for the kingdom's sake will receive far more, both in the present age and in the age to come.

**The prediction of suffering (vv. 31-34)** reveals the deepest ground of the chapter's argument. The Son of Man himself will walk the path of suffering before vindication -- mocked, shamed, scourged, killed, and raised on the third day. The disciples' perseverance during the interval of waiting is grounded in the Son of Man's own experience of the same pattern. The one who asks "will he find the faith?" is himself about to undergo the ultimate test of faith -- trusting God through suffering and death. The disciples cannot yet understand this; the meaning is hidden from them. But when it unfolds, it will become the foundation for everything the chapter has taught about persistent, trusting, self-emptying faith.

**The blind man (vv. 35-43)** is the living answer to the chapter's opening question. He embodies every quality the chapter has described as "the faith": the widow's persistence (he cries out despite rebuke), the tax collector's humility (he asks for mercy, not reward), the infant's helplessness (he cannot help himself and can only receive), and the willingness to follow that the ruler lacked (he immediately follows Jesus). His faith saves him -- the verb sōzō carrying its full weight of deliverance, healing, and restoration. And his response -- following Jesus and glorifying God, with all the people joining in praise -- demonstrates that the faith the Son of Man is looking for is not merely a private disposition but a public reality that transforms those who witness it.

The chapter thus moves from the question ("will he find the faith?") through a series of portraits that define what the faith looks like (the widow, the tax collector, the infant, the disciples who left everything, the blind man) and what it does not look like (the Pharisee, the ruler), with the Son of Man's own journey through suffering to resurrection standing at the center as both the ground of the faith and its ultimate expression. The thread connecting every section is the opposition between self-sufficiency and dependent trust: those who come to God with full hands -- full of religious merit, full of wealth, full of self-assessment -- cannot enter the kingdom. Those who come with empty hands -- crying out for mercy, receiving like infants, following with nothing -- find that God does what is impossible for human beings to do on their own.
