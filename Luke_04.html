Luke 4 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Luke 3): Luke 3 opens with an elaborate chronological synchronism placing John's ministry within the reigns of Tiberius Caesar, Pontius Pilate, Herod Antipas, Philip, and Lysanias, with Annas and Caiaphas serving as high priests. The word of God comes to John in the wilderness (not in the temple, not in Jerusalem), and he preaches a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins — fulfilling the role that Gabriel and Zechariah described in chapter 1. The author quotes Isaiah 40:3-5, extending the quotation further than Matthew or Mark do, all the way to "all flesh will see the salvation of God" — a universalizing note characteristic of Luke. John preaches ethical repentance in concrete terms (share your tunics, stop extorting, be content with wages), warns that Abrahamic descent alone does not guarantee a place in God's purposes ("God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham"), and announces one coming after him who will baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire, whose winnowing fork will separate wheat from chaff. Jesus is baptized, the Spirit descends on him in bodily form as a dove, and a voice from heaven declares "You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased" — echoing Psalm 2:7 (the enthronement of the king) and Isaiah 42:1 (the servant in whom God delights). The chapter ends with a genealogy that traces Jesus's lineage not forward from Abraham (as Matthew does) but backward from Joseph all the way to "Adam, the son of God." The effect is to place Jesus not only within Israel's story but within the story of all humanity, and to end with the phrase "son of God" — the very identity that will be tested in chapter 4.

What the author argues immediately after (Luke 5): Jesus calls his first disciples through a miraculous catch of fish (unique to Luke), and Simon Peter responds with "Go away from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man!" — a response of awe and self-awareness in the presence of divine power. Jesus then heals a leper, commanding him to show himself to the priest and make the offering Moses commanded "as a testimony to them." The news spreads, crowds gather, and Jesus withdraws to pray. He heals a paralytic, and the controversy with the Pharisees and scribes begins: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" Jesus calls Levi (a tax collector), eats with tax collectors and sinners, and the Pharisees challenge him. Jesus responds with the parable of the physician who comes for the sick, not the healthy. The chapter ends with the parable of new wine in new wineskins. Chapter 5 thus moves from the demonstration of authority (over fish, disease, sin) to the emerging conflict about whom Jesus associates with and what kind of renewal he represents.

How chapter 4 connects these: Luke 4 is the bridge between Jesus's identification (baptism, genealogy) and his public ministry (calling disciples, healing, teaching, generating controversy). The chapter answers two questions that the preceding material raises. First, if Jesus is "the beloved Son" (3:22) and the genealogy ends at "Adam, the son of God" (3:38), what does it mean for him to be the Son of God? The wilderness testing answers this by showing what kind of Son he is — not one who uses divine status for self-gratification or political dominion, but one who lives by every word of God. Second, what is the content and purpose of his mission? The Nazareth synagogue scene answers this by having Jesus define his own ministry through Isaiah 61, and then provoke his hometown to fury by demonstrating from Israel's own scriptures that God's saving acts have always overflowed Israel's boundaries. The remainder of the chapter (Capernaum exorcism, healings, decision to move on) begins the practical outworking of the program announced at Nazareth. Chapter 4 thus functions as the programmatic chapter for the entire Gospel: it states the thesis that the rest of the narrative will develop.

Verses 1-2: Jesus Led into the Wilderness

"Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan and was led around by the Spirit in the wilderness for forty days, being tempted by the devil. And He ate nothing during those days, and when they had ended, He became hungry."

The author makes two things explicit at the outset. First, Jesus is "full of the Holy Spirit" (pleres pneumatos hagiou). This is not a passing touch of inspiration; the word pleres indicates saturation, completeness. The Spirit descended on him at the baptism (3:22), and now he is described as full of the Spirit — the same language used of Stephen in Acts 6:5 and 7:55, and of Barnabas in Acts 11:24. Second, he "was led around by the Spirit" (egeto en to pneumati). The Spirit is the agent directing Jesus into the wilderness. This is not a random wandering; it is a Spirit-directed movement. The passive voice (egeto) indicates that Jesus submits to the Spirit's leading rather than initiating the journey himself.

The wilderness (eremos) and the period of forty days immediately invoke Israel's wilderness experience. Israel wandered in the wilderness for forty years after the Exodus (Deuteronomy 8:2: "You shall remember all the way which the LORD your God has led you in the wilderness these forty years, that He might humble you, testing you, to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep His commandments or not"). Moses fasted forty days on Sinai (Exodus 34:28, Deuteronomy 9:9). Elijah traveled forty days to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). The number forty in Israel's tradition is consistently the period of testing, formation, and preparation for a divinely assigned task.

The parallel between Jesus and Israel is structural, not decorative. Israel was called God's "son" (Exodus 4:22: "Israel is My son, My firstborn"; Hosea 11:1: "When Israel was a youth I loved him, and out of Egypt I called My son"). Israel was led into the wilderness by God. Israel was tested there. Israel failed the tests repeatedly — grumbling for food, demanding signs, worshiping the golden calf. Jesus, identified as "Son of God" at his baptism and in the genealogy, now enters the same wilderness, faces the same categories of testing, and — as the narrative will show — responds differently than Israel did.

"Being tempted by the devil" (peirazomenos hypo tou diabolou). The word peirazo means both "to test" and "to tempt" — in the LXX, the same word is used for God's testing of Abraham (Genesis 22:1) and for Israel's testing of God (Exodus 17:2, Psalm 95:9). The devil (diabolos, "slanderer," the Greek equivalent of the Hebrew satan, "adversary") is the agent of the testing. The author does not explain the devil's origin or nature; he assumes the reader understands the figure from Israel's scriptures (1 Chronicles 21:1, Job 1-2, Zechariah 3:1-2) as an adversarial figure who stands against God's purposes and tests those whom God has called.

Verses 3-4: The First Test — Bread

"And the devil said to Him, 'If You are the Son of God, tell this stone to become bread.' And Jesus answered him, 'It is written: "Man shall not live on bread alone."'"

The devil's opening word is "if" (ei), which in Greek can mean "if" (conditional, uncertain) or "since" (conditional, assumed true). Given that the voice from heaven has just declared Jesus to be the Son of God (3:22), and the genealogy has just ended with "son of God" (3:38), the devil is not questioning Jesus's identity but probing what that identity means in practice: "Since you are the Son of God, use that status to meet your own physical need."

The test is not about whether Jesus can turn stone to bread. It is about whether he will use his divine sonship as a tool for self-provision. After forty days of fasting, the hunger is real. The temptation is to resolve a legitimate need through an exercise of divine prerogative that bypasses trust in God's provision.

Jesus responds with a quotation from Deuteronomy 8:3: "Man shall not live on bread alone." The full verse reads: "He humbled you and let you be hungry, and fed you with manna which you did not know, nor did your fathers know, that He might make you understand that man does not live by bread alone, but man lives by everything that proceeds out of the mouth of the LORD." The context is Moses's speech to Israel about their wilderness experience. God deliberately let them go hungry and then fed them with manna — bread they did not make and could not store — to teach them that their life depended not on their own ability to procure food but on God's word and provision.

Israel in the wilderness failed this test. When they were hungry, they grumbled against Moses and Aaron (Exodus 16:2-3), longed for the food of Egypt, and complained that God had brought them into the wilderness to die. Jesus, facing the same test — hunger in the wilderness as the Son of God — responds with the lesson Israel was supposed to learn but did not. He lives by the word of God, not by the exercise of power to feed himself.

Verses 5-8: The Second Test — Authority over the Kingdoms

"And he led Him up and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world in a moment of time. And the devil said to Him, 'I will give You all this domain and its glory; for it has been handed over to me, and I give it to whomever I wish. Therefore if You worship before me, it shall all be Yours.' Jesus answered him, 'It is written: "You shall worship the Lord your God and serve Him only."'"

Luke's order of the second and third tests differs from Matthew's. In Matthew, the temple test is second and the kingdoms test is third (climactic). In Luke, the kingdoms test is second and the temple test is third, placing the temple in Jerusalem as the climax. This may reflect Luke's literary interest in Jerusalem as the center of the narrative — Jesus's journey toward Jerusalem will be a major structural feature of the Gospel (9:51-19:44), and the Gospel both begins (1:5-23) and ends (24:52-53) in the temple.

The devil claims authority over "all the kingdoms of the world" (pasas tas basileias tes oikoumenes) and states that this authority "has been handed over to me" (emoi paradedotai). The verb paradidomi ("to hand over") is in the perfect passive tense: it was handed over at some point in the past, and the state continues. The devil claims to be the current administrator of worldly political power and to have the right to delegate it. Jesus does not dispute this claim. The author presents it as part of the test without endorsing or refuting it — what matters for the narrative is the choice Jesus faces.

The offer is: worship me, and receive immediate dominion over the world. The word for worship (proskyneo) means literally "to bow down before," "to prostrate oneself." In Israel's scriptures, this act is reserved exclusively for God. The test is whether Jesus will take a shortcut to the kingdom — bypassing the path God has appointed — by giving to the devil what belongs only to God.

Jesus responds with Deuteronomy 6:13: "You shall worship the Lord your God and serve Him only." The full verse in Deuteronomy reads: "You shall fear only the LORD your God; and you shall worship Him and swear by His name." The context is Moses's instruction to Israel as they are about to enter the Promised Land — the land flowing with milk and honey, with cities they did not build and wells they did not dig. Moses warns them: when you have all these good things, do not forget the LORD and do not follow other gods (Deuteronomy 6:10-15). Israel repeatedly failed this test, turning to worship the gods of the nations in exchange for prosperity and security (Judges 2:11-13, 1 Kings 11:4-8, 2 Kings 17:7-18). Jesus refuses to do what Israel did. He will not worship any power other than God, regardless of what is offered.

The test also has a specifically messianic dimension. Gabriel announced that Jesus would receive "the throne of His father David" and would "reign over the house of Jacob forever" (1:32-33). The devil is offering the same thing — dominion — but through a different means. The question is not whether Jesus will have authority over the kingdoms, but by what path and at what cost. The path the devil offers requires allegiance to the devil. The path God offers — as the rest of the Gospel and Acts will show — runs through suffering, rejection, death, and resurrection.

Verses 9-12: The Third Test — The Temple Pinnacle

"And he led Him to Jerusalem and had Him stand on the pinnacle of the temple, and said to Him, 'If You are the Son of God, throw Yourself down from here; for it is written: "He will command His angels concerning You to guard You," and, "On their hands they will bear You up, so that You will not strike Your foot against a stone."' And Jesus answered and said to him, 'It is said: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test."'"

The devil now takes Jesus to Jerusalem, to the pinnacle (pterygion, literally "little wing") of the temple — the highest point of the temple complex. This is significant in Luke's narrative architecture: Jerusalem and the temple are the geographical and theological center of the story. The testing that began in the wilderness now reaches the temple itself.

The devil quotes scripture for the first time: Psalm 91:11-12. The quotation is accurate as far as it goes. Psalm 91 is a psalm of trust in God's protection: "He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High will abide in the shadow of the Almighty" (91:1). The psalm promises divine protection from terror, pestilence, arrows, plague, and specifically promises angelic guardianship: "For He will give His angels charge concerning you, to guard you in all your ways. They will bear you up in their hands, that you do not strike your foot against a stone" (91:11-12).

The devil's logic is: Scripture promises divine protection. You are the Son of God. Therefore, prove it by forcing God to act on His promise. Throw yourself down, and let the angels catch you.

The test is subtle. It uses God's own word as the basis for demanding that God perform. It turns trust into manipulation: instead of trusting God's protection in the course of obedient living, it engineers a crisis that will compel God to intervene. It is, in essence, the demand for a sign — the insistence that God demonstrate His faithfulness on the tester's terms rather than His own.

Jesus responds with Deuteronomy 6:16: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test." The full verse reads: "You shall not put the LORD your God to the test, as you tested Him at Massah." The reference to Massah points to Exodus 17:1-7, where Israel, thirsty in the wilderness, quarreled with Moses and "tested the LORD, saying, 'Is the LORD among us, or not?'" (Exodus 17:7). The name Massah itself means "testing." Israel's sin at Massah was not merely complaining about thirst; it was demanding that God prove His presence by performing on command. Jesus recognizes the devil's proposal as the same kind of test and refuses it.

All three of Jesus's responses come from Deuteronomy, specifically from chapters 6 and 8 — Moses's farewell instruction to Israel about how to live in faithful dependence on God. The pattern is unmistakable: Jesus faces the tests that Israel faced in the wilderness and responds with the very scriptures that described Israel's failures and instructed Israel on how to respond correctly. Where Israel grumbled for bread, Jesus trusts God's word. Where Israel worshiped other gods, Jesus worships God alone. Where Israel tested God at Massah, Jesus refuses to test God. The Son of God succeeds where the son of God (Israel) failed.

Verse 13: The Devil's Departure

"When the devil had finished every temptation, he left Him until an opportune time."

The phrase "every temptation" (panta peirasmon) indicates completeness — the devil has exhausted his categories of testing for now. But the departure is temporary: "until an opportune time" (achri kairou). The word kairos means not merely a point in time but an opportune or appointed moment. The author signals that the testing is not over; the devil will return. Luke 22:3 ("Satan entered into Judas") and 22:53 ("this hour and the power of darkness") mark the return of this adversarial presence at the Passion. The wilderness testing is the first round, not the only round.

Verses 14-15: Return to Galilee

"And Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit, and news about Him spread through all the surrounding district. And He began teaching in their synagogues and was praised by all."

Jesus returns not merely "with" the Spirit but "in the power of the Spirit" (en te dynamei tou pneumatos). The Spirit-filling of verse 1 has now become Spirit-empowerment for public ministry. The sequence is deliberate: baptism (anointing), wilderness (testing), public ministry (empowered action). This mirrors the pattern of Israel's leaders: Moses was called (burning bush), tested (Pharaoh's resistance, wilderness), and then led the people. David was anointed by Samuel, tested through years of fugitive existence under Saul, and then became king. The testing phase is not an obstacle to ministry but the necessary preparation for it.

The initial reception is positive: Jesus teaches in synagogues and "was praised by all" (doxazomenos hypo panton). This universal praise sets up the dramatic reversal that is about to occur in Nazareth. The author tells the reader that Jesus is being widely acclaimed before narrating the one place where that acclaim will turn to murderous rage.

Verses 16-21: Jesus in the Synagogue at Nazareth — The Reading

"And He came to Nazareth, where He had been brought up; and as was His custom, He entered the synagogue on the Sabbath, and stood up to read. And the book of the prophet Isaiah was handed to Him. And He opened the book and found the place where it was written: 'The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me, because He anointed Me to bring good news to the poor. He has sent Me to proclaim release to the captives, and recovery of sight to the blind, to set free those who are oppressed, to proclaim the favorable year of the Lord.' And He closed the book, gave it back to the attendant, and sat down; and the eyes of all the people in the synagogue were fixed on Him. And He began to say to them, 'Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.'"

This passage is the most important scene in Luke's Gospel for understanding the author's presentation of Jesus's mission. Several elements require careful attention.

The synagogue setting. The synagogue was the local institution of Jewish communal worship and Torah instruction, distinct from the temple. By the first century, synagogues existed throughout the Jewish world. The Sabbath service included the reading of Torah (the weekly parashah) and a reading from the Prophets (the haftarah), followed by a homily or exposition. A visiting teacher or recognized member of the community could be invited to read and comment. Jesus's participation follows established practice: "as was His custom" (kata to eiothos auto) — this is his regular pattern, not a one-time event.

The Isaiah scroll. Jesus is handed the scroll of Isaiah and "found the place" (heuren ton topon) where the passage is written. Whether he chose the passage deliberately or it was the assigned reading is debated, but the author's wording ("he found the place") suggests purposeful selection. The passage is Isaiah 61:1-2a, with a phrase from Isaiah 58:6 inserted ("to set free those who are oppressed"):

Isaiah 61:1-2 in the LXX reads: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me; he has sent me to bring good news to the poor, to heal the brokenhearted, to proclaim release to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord and the day of vengeance of our God, to comfort all who mourn."

Jesus's reading departs from the LXX text in two notable ways. First, he inserts "to set free those who are oppressed" (aposteilai tethrausmenous en aphesei), drawn from Isaiah 58:6, where the prophet defines the fast that God requires: "Is this not the fast which I choose: to loosen the bonds of wickedness, to undo the bands of the yoke, and to let the oppressed go free [aposteilai tethrausmenous en aphesei] and break every yoke?" The insertion brings the theme of liberation from oppression into the center of the quotation. Second — and this is the most consequential textual decision in the scene — Jesus stops reading in the middle of Isaiah 61:2. He reads "to proclaim the favorable year of the Lord" and stops. He does not read the next clause: "and the day of vengeance of our God."

The Isaiah 61 passage in its original context describes the mission of a figure anointed by God's Spirit to bring a comprehensive restoration — good news to the poor, release to captives, sight to the blind, freedom to the oppressed, and the announcement of the "year of the Lord's favor." The "favorable year" (eniautos kyriou dektos) echoes the Jubilee legislation of Leviticus 25, where every fiftieth year debts were cancelled, slaves were freed, and ancestral land was returned. The Jubilee was Israel's great institution of economic and social restoration, a regular resetting of the conditions that produced inequality and bondage. Isaiah 61 takes the Jubilee concept and universalizes it as a description of God's eschatological intervention.

Jesus reads this passage, closes the scroll, sits down (the posture of a teacher about to give instruction), and declares: "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (semeron peplērotai hē graphē hautē en tois ōsin hymōn). The word "today" (semeron) is emphatic: not "someday," not "when the Messiah comes," but now, in this moment, in this synagogue, as you listen. The verb "has been fulfilled" (peplērotai) is in the perfect tense — the action is completed and its effects continue. The Scripture is not being predicted or anticipated; it is being accomplished.

The claim is extraordinary. Jesus is declaring that the Spirit-anointed figure of Isaiah 61 is himself, that the anointing described has already occurred (at his baptism, 3:22), and that the era of restoration Isaiah described is now beginning. He is not merely reading a text; he is announcing its arrival.

The omission of "the day of vengeance of our God" is deliberate and significant. In Isaiah 61:2, the year of favor and the day of vengeance are paired — they are two aspects of the same divine intervention. By stopping before the vengeance clause, Jesus defines his present mission as the inauguration of God's favor, not the execution of God's judgment. This does not mean the author rejects the concept of judgment (Luke's Gospel contains extensive judgment material: 3:7-9, 3:17, 10:13-15, 11:31-32, 12:49-53, 13:1-5, 17:26-37, 21:5-36), but it distinguishes the present moment from the moment of judgment. The mission Jesus announces is characterized by good news, release, healing, and freedom — not by vengeance. The implication, within the logic of Isaiah 61 itself, is that there will be a later moment when the judgment clause takes effect.

Verses 22-24: Initial Reaction and Jesus's Response

"And all the people in the synagogue were speaking well of Him, and wondering at the gracious words which were falling from His lips; and they were saying, 'Is this not Joseph's son?' And He said to them, 'No doubt you will quote this proverb to Me: "Physician, heal yourself! Whatever we heard was done in Capernaum, do here in your hometown as well."' And He said, 'Truly I say to you, no prophet is welcome in his hometown.'"

The initial reaction is positive: they speak well of him (emartyoun auto) and marvel at "the gracious words" (tois logois tes charitos) proceeding from his mouth. But their comment — "Is this not Joseph's son?" (ouchi huios estin Ioseph houtos) — reveals the limit of their understanding. They know him as a local man, the son of the carpenter. Their admiration is tinged with the question: How can someone from among us make such claims?

Jesus does not wait for the admiration to curdle into rejection. He preemptively addresses what he knows they are thinking. "Physician, heal yourself" (iatre therapeuson seauton) is a proverbial saying meaning: demonstrate your powers here at home before expecting us to believe you. The reference to Capernaum indicates that reports of Jesus's activity there have reached Nazareth, and the hometown expects preferential treatment — perform here what you performed there.

Jesus's response — "no prophet is welcome in his hometown" (oudeis prophetes dektos estin en te patridi autou) — identifies himself as a prophet and establishes a principle from Israel's history: prophets are characteristically rejected by those closest to them. The word dektos ("welcome," "acceptable") is the same word used in verse 19 for "the favorable year of the Lord" (eniautos kyriou dektos). The verbal connection is striking: the year of the Lord's favor (dektos) is being proclaimed by one who is not found favorable (dektos) in his own hometown. The rejection of the prophet mirrors the rejection of the message.

Verses 25-27: Elijah, Elisha, and the Gentiles

"But I say to you in truth, there were many widows in Israel in the days of Elijah, when the sky was shut up for three years and six months, when a great famine came over all the land; and yet Elijah was sent to none of them, but only to Zarephath, in the land of Sidon, to a woman who was a widow. And there were many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, but only Naaman the Syrian."

These are the sentences that transform admiration into murderous rage. Jesus cites two episodes from the Elijah-Elisha cycle in 1-2 Kings, and both make the same point: God's saving action overflowed Israel's boundaries and went to Gentiles when Israel proved unresponsive.

Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:8-24). During the three-and-a-half-year drought that Elijah himself had announced (1 Kings 17:1), God sent the prophet not to any of the many widows in Israel but to a widow in Zarephath, a town in the territory of Sidon — Gentile territory, the homeland of Jezebel herself. This widow, a non-Israelite, fed the prophet, and in return her jar of flour and jug of oil did not run out, and when her son died, Elijah raised him from the dead. The precedent is clear: when Israel under Ahab and Jezebel rejected the prophet's message, God directed His saving acts toward a Gentile.

Elisha and Naaman the Syrian (2 Kings 5:1-14). Naaman was the commander of the army of Aram (Syria) — not just a Gentile but an enemy military leader. He had leprosy. Despite the many lepers in Israel, Elisha healed none of them; he healed Naaman. Naaman initially resisted the prophet's instruction (to wash in the Jordan), but when he obeyed, he was cleansed, and he declared: "Behold now, I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel" (2 Kings 5:15).

Jesus's argument is that his own rejection by his hometown is not unprecedented but follows a scriptural pattern. When those closest to the prophet refuse to receive the message, God extends His saving work to others — including Gentiles. The implication for the synagogue audience is sharp: if you reject what God is doing through me, do not assume that God's purposes will therefore fail. God has always had the freedom to go where His message is received, even if that means going outside Israel.

This argument anticipates the entire trajectory of Luke-Acts. The Gospel will show Jesus extending mercy to Samaritans (10:25-37, 17:11-19), commending the faith of Gentiles (7:1-10), and warning that "they will come from east and west and from north and south, and will recline at the table in the kingdom of God" while some who expected to be there are excluded (13:28-29). Acts will narrate the progressive extension of the gospel from Jerusalem to Judea and Samaria to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8), with the pivotal moment being Peter's vision and the conversion of Cornelius (Acts 10-11), followed by Paul's repeated pattern of going first to the synagogue, being rejected, and then turning to the Gentiles (Acts 13:46, 18:6, 28:28).

The Nazareth synagogue scene is, in microcosm, the story of Luke-Acts as a whole: the announcement of God's favor, the initial positive reception, the emerging resistance when the implications become clear, the rejection by those who assume priority, and the movement of God's saving acts beyond the expected boundaries.

Verses 28-30: The Attempt to Kill Jesus

"And all the people in the synagogue were filled with rage as they heard these things; and they got up and drove Him out of the city, and led Him to the brow of the hill on which their city had been built, in order to throw Him down the cliff. But passing through their midst, He went His way."

The response is extreme: they are "filled with rage" (eplesthēsan pantes thymou). The word thymou ("wrath," "fury") indicates not mere disagreement but violent anger. They drive him out of the city and attempt to throw him off a cliff. The transition from admiration (v. 22) to attempted murder (v. 29) is complete within a few verses.

What provoked this reaction? It was not the claim to fulfill Isaiah 61; they marveled at that. It was the Elijah-Elisha argument — the assertion that God has historically directed His saving work toward Gentiles when Israel proved unfaithful. The claim that God's favor is not Israel's exclusive possession, that it can flow past Israel to the nations, is what produced the rage. The people of Nazareth heard Jesus place them in the role of the unfaithful Israel of Ahab's day, and they responded not with repentance but with violence — thereby confirming the very pattern Jesus described.

Jesus's escape is narrated simply: "passing through their midst, He went His way" (autos de dielthōn dia mesou autōn eporeueto). The author does not explain how this happened. The absence of explanation creates an impression of sovereign authority — they could not hold him because his hour had not come, because the mission announced in verses 18-19 was just beginning. The simplicity of the statement stands in contrast to the violence of the crowd: they are in frenzy; he walks through them and leaves.

Verses 31-32: Teaching in Capernaum

"And He came down to Capernaum, a city of Galilee, and He was teaching them on the Sabbath; and they were amazed at His teaching, because His message was with authority."

Jesus moves from Nazareth (in the hills) to Capernaum (on the shore of the Sea of Galilee, lower in elevation — hence "came down"). Capernaum will function as his base of operations throughout the Galilean ministry. The response in Capernaum contrasts with Nazareth: the people are "amazed" (exeplēssonto) at his teaching because it carries "authority" (exousia). The word exousia means not merely persuasiveness or eloquence but the right and power to command. Mark's parallel (Mark 1:22) specifies "not as the scribes" — the scribes taught by citing earlier authorities; Jesus teaches with an authority that does not derive from any human chain of transmission. The author does not explain the basis of this authority at this point; the exorcism that follows will demonstrate it concretely.

Verses 33-37: The Exorcism in the Synagogue

"In the synagogue there was a man possessed by the spirit of an unclean demon, and he cried out with a loud voice, 'Leave us alone! What business do You have with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have You come to destroy us? I know who You are — the Holy One of God!' And Jesus rebuked him, saying, 'Be quiet and come out of him!' And when the demon had thrown him down in the midst of the people, he came out of him without doing him any harm. And amazement came upon them all, and they began talking with one another, saying, 'What is this message? For with authority and power He commands the unclean spirits and they come out.' And the report about Him was spreading into every locality in the surrounding district."

This is the first exorcism in Luke's Gospel, and its placement is deliberate: immediately after the programmatic announcement at Nazareth, Jesus demonstrates the reality of what he proclaimed. He announced "release to the captives" (v. 18); now he releases a man held captive by an unclean spirit.

The phrase "the spirit of an unclean demon" (pneuma daimoniou akathartou) is distinctive. The word "unclean" (akathartos) places the demon in the category of ritual impurity — the opposite of holiness. Its presence in the synagogue, the place of worship and Torah instruction, represents a contradiction: the unholy has invaded the holy space. This is the spiritual dimension of the condition Jesus came to address.

The demon's speech reveals several things. "What business do You have with us?" (ti hēmin kai soi) is a formula of confrontation: literally "What is there between us and you?" It marks a boundary: the demon recognizes that Jesus's presence creates a conflict. "Have You come to destroy us?" (ēlthes apolesai hēmas) — the verb apollymi means to destroy, to bring to ruin. The demon perceives Jesus's mission as destructive to the demonic realm. The plural "us" (hēmas) suggests the demon speaks not only for itself but for the entire category of unclean spirits. "I know who You are — the Holy One of God" (ho hagios tou theou) — this title appears in the LXX in reference to Aaron (Psalm 106:16 [105:16 LXX]) and possibly to Elisha (2 Kings 4:9, where the Shunammite woman calls him "a holy man of God"). The demon identifies Jesus by a title that marks him as supremely consecrated to God — the exact opposite of the demon's own unclean nature. The irony is that the demon knows what the people of Nazareth did not recognize.

Jesus's response is a double command: "Be quiet" (phimōthēti, literally "be muzzled") and "come out of him" (exelthe ap' autou). The silencing command is significant. Jesus does not want the demon's testimony, even though it is accurate. Throughout Luke (and the other Synoptics), Jesus consistently refuses to let demons publicize his identity (4:41). The reason, within the narrative logic, appears to be that his identity must be revealed on his terms and through his actions, not through the declarations of unclean spirits. The identity of the "Holy One of God" must be understood through the program announced in Isaiah 61, not through the desperate outcries of the demonic.

The demon obeys: it throws the man down but comes out "without doing him any harm" (mēden blapsan auton). The result is complete liberation without collateral damage. The crowd's response identifies two qualities: "authority" (exousia) and "power" (dynamis). Authority is the right to command; power is the ability to enforce. Jesus possesses both.

Verses 38-39: Healing of Simon's Mother-in-Law

"Then He got up and left the synagogue, and entered Simon's home. Now Simon's mother-in-law was suffering from a high fever, and they asked Him to help her. And standing over her, He rebuked the fever, and it left her; and she immediately got up and began serving them."

This is the first healing of an individual in Luke's Gospel (the exorcism was also a first, but in a different category). Several details are notable. The author mentions Simon without introduction, as though the reader already knows who he is; the formal calling of Simon does not occur until 5:1-11. This suggests either that the author assumes prior knowledge (from Mark or from common tradition) or that Simon's name is so well known to the intended audience that no introduction is needed.

Jesus "rebuked" (epetimēsen) the fever. The same verb was used for rebuking the demon (v. 35). The author treats the fever as something that can be addressed with the same kind of authoritative command used against unclean spirits. Whether this implies the fever has a spiritual origin, or whether it simply reflects the author's presentation of Jesus's authority over all forms of affliction, the effect is the same: Jesus speaks, and the condition departs.

The immediacy of the healing is emphasized: she "immediately got up and began serving them" (parachrēma de anastasa diēkonei autois). The word parachrēma ("immediately," "at once") is characteristic of Luke. The completeness of the healing is demonstrated by the fact that she resumes normal activity without any period of convalescence. The word diakoneo ("to serve") indicates practical hospitality — she returns to the role of hostess, her health fully restored.

Verses 40-41: Healings at Sunset

"While the sun was setting, all those who had any who were sick with various diseases brought them to Him; and laying His hands on each one of them, He was healing them. Demons also were coming out of many, shouting, 'You are the Son of God!' But rebuking them, He would not allow them to speak, because they knew Him to be the Christ."

The healings occur "while the sun was setting" — after the Sabbath has ended, when people would again be free to carry their sick (carrying a burden on the Sabbath was restricted). The author emphasizes the individual attention: Jesus laid hands "on each one of them" (heni hekasto auton). Despite the large number of sick, Jesus does not perform a mass healing; he touches each person individually. This detail underscores the personal nature of his ministry — the program of Isaiah 61 is not an abstraction but is enacted person by person.

The demons again identify Jesus, this time as "the Son of God" (ho huios tou theou). The author adds the editorial note: "because they knew Him to be the Christ" (to christon auton einai). This is the first time in the narrative that the author explicitly connects "Son of God" with "Christ" (christos, "anointed one," the Greek translation of the Hebrew mashiach). The demons' knowledge is accurate but unwelcome: Jesus silences them again. The identity must not be proclaimed by the forces Jesus came to overthrow.

Verses 42-44: Jesus's Mission to Other Cities

"When day came, Jesus left and went to a secluded place; and the crowds were searching for Him, and came to Him and tried to keep Him from going away from them. But He said to them, 'I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other cities also, because I was sent for this purpose.' So He kept on preaching in the synagogues of Judea."

Jesus withdraws to a "secluded place" (erēmos topos) — literally a "deserted place," using the same root word as the wilderness of the temptation. This pattern of withdrawal for prayer appears repeatedly in Luke (5:16, 6:12, 9:18, 9:28-29, 11:1, 22:41-44) and is one of the author's distinctive emphases.

The crowds want to keep Jesus in Capernaum. He refuses, and his refusal contains the first use of a key Lukan phrase: "I must" (me dei). The word dei indicates divine necessity — not merely personal preference or strategic calculation, but a compulsion that arises from God's purpose. Throughout Luke, dei marks the non-negotiable elements of God's plan: Jesus "must" preach to other cities (4:43), "must" suffer many things (9:22), "must" go to Jerusalem (13:33), "must" stay at Zacchaeus's house (19:5), and what is written about him "must" be fulfilled (22:37, 24:7, 24:26, 24:44). This single word dei is one of the most important theological markers in Luke's Gospel: it identifies the events of Jesus's ministry, suffering, death, and resurrection as the unfolding of God's predetermined plan.

"The good news of the kingdom of God" (euangelisthai me dei tēn basileian tou theou). This is the first occurrence of "the kingdom of God" (basileia tou theou) in Luke. The term draws from Daniel 2:44 ("the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed") and Daniel 7:14, 27 (the dominion given to the Son of Man and to the saints of the Most High). In the context of Isaiah 52:7 — "How lovely on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news... who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" — the "good news" and the "kingdom" are connected: the good news is that God has become king, that His reign is being enacted. Jesus identifies his mission as the proclamation of this reality.

"I was sent for this purpose" (hoti eis touto apestalēn). The passive voice "I was sent" (apestalēn, from apostellō) identifies God as the sender without naming Him. Jesus's mission is not self-initiated; he is an agent sent by another. The word apostellō is the root of apostolos ("apostle," "one sent"); Jesus is here the original "sent one," and those he will later send (the apostles) will extend his mission.

"The synagogues of Judea" — some manuscripts read "Galilee," and the geographical referent is debated. If "Judea" is original, the author may be using it in a broad sense to mean the land of the Jews generally (as he appears to do elsewhere, e.g., Acts 10:37, "throughout all Judea, beginning from Galilee"), or he may be anticipating the geographic sweep of Jesus's ministry. The point, either way, is that Jesus's mission extends beyond any single locale.

Summary of the Chapter's Argument

Luke 4 establishes the identity and mission of Jesus through three interconnected movements:

The wilderness testing (vv. 1-13) answers the question raised by the baptism and genealogy: What kind of Son of God is Jesus? He is the Son who, unlike Israel in the wilderness, trusts God's word rather than demanding bread, worships God alone rather than grasping for power through compromise, and refuses to manipulate God through presumption. All three responses come from Deuteronomy's account of Israel's wilderness failures, establishing Jesus as the faithful Son who succeeds where the national "son" failed. The testing is not over — the devil departs "until an opportune time" — but the pattern has been set.

The Nazareth synagogue scene (vv. 14-30) answers the question: What is the content and scope of Jesus's mission? The content is defined by Isaiah 61: good news to the poor, release to captives, sight to the blind, freedom to the oppressed, the year of the Lord's favor. The scope is defined by the Elijah-Elisha precedents: when Israel rejects the prophet, God's saving work moves to others, including Gentiles. The omission of "the day of vengeance" distinguishes the present mission from the future judgment. The hometown rejection that follows the Elijah-Elisha argument enacts the very pattern Jesus described: those who assume privileged access to God's favor are the first to reject the prophet when the implications of his message become clear.

The Capernaum ministry (vv. 31-44) demonstrates in action what the Nazareth sermon declared in words. The exorcism demonstrates release for captives of unclean spirits. The healing of Simon's mother-in-law demonstrates restoration of the sick. The evening healings demonstrate the personal, individual nature of Jesus's work. The demons' recognition of Jesus as "the Holy One of God" and "the Son of God" / "the Christ" confirms the identity that the humans of Nazareth refused to accept. And Jesus's declaration that he "must" preach the kingdom of God to other cities establishes that his mission is driven by divine necessity, not by popular demand, and that it will not be confined to any single location.

The chapter moves from the private (wilderness) to the public (synagogue) to the practical (healings and exorcisms), from identity (who Jesus is) to mission (what he was sent to do) to demonstration (doing it). It also introduces the dynamic that will drive the rest of the Gospel and Acts: the tension between the announcement of God's favor and the resistance of those who assumed that favor was theirs by right. The good news of the kingdom, proclaimed by the Spirit-anointed Son who passed through the testing that Israel failed, will go where it is received — and as Elijah and Elisha demonstrated, that may well be beyond the boundaries that the hearers expected.
