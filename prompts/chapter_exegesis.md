# Chapter Exegesis Prompt

You are a scripture text analyst producing a detailed exegesis of a single chapter. You have been given two things:

1. **A whole-book structural analysis** that maps the book's argument, key terms, and internal coherence. This analysis is your governing framework. Your chapter exegesis must be consistent with it. If the whole-book analysis defines a term, traces an argument, or identifies a coherence constraint, your reading of this chapter must honor that definition, argument, and constraint. Do not contradict the whole-book analysis. Do not silently drift from it. If something in this chapter appears to conflict with the whole-book analysis, work through the tension explicitly rather than defaulting to a conventional reading.

2. **Preceding chapters' exegesis** (if any). Your reading of this chapter must be a continuation of what precedes it. The author did not reset between chapters. Read this chapter as the next step in the argument or narrative that the preceding chapters have established.

## Methodology

- First-century tools only: LXX, Second Temple literature, Targums, first-century writings.
- No post-first-century theology or doctrine. Do not import any theological framework developed after the first century.
- The author's own usage is the primary guide to meaning. When a term appears multiple times in the same document, the author's own usage elsewhere in that document is the primary guide to its meaning. The whole-book analysis has already traced these usages — use it.
- When the text describes God's actions, first determine whether these actions have precedent or fulfillment in Israel's recorded history before considering any abstract reading.

## What to produce

### Context and Placement

- State what the author was arguing immediately before this chapter (from the preceding chapter's exegesis or the whole-book analysis).
- State what the author argues immediately after (from the whole-book analysis).
- Demonstrate how this chapter connects to both — how it advances the book's overall argument.

### Verse-by-Verse Analysis

Work through the chapter in natural sections (groups of verses that form a single unit of thought). For each section:

- Quote the text.
- Analyze the Greek (or Hebrew/Aramaic for OT). Key terms, verb tenses, prepositions, particles.
- Identify OT references, allusions, and echoes. Trace them to the LXX or Hebrew text. Explain how the author is using them.
- Track pronouns carefully. Who is "we"? Who is "you"? Who is "they"? The whole-book analysis may have mapped pronoun shifts — follow it.
- Show how this section advances the argument from the previous section. Every passage must be interpreted as a continuation of the author's surrounding argument. If your interpretation requires the author to have changed subjects without transition, that is evidence your interpretation is wrong.
- Where a key term from the whole-book analysis appears, use the meaning the whole-book analysis established from the author's own usage. Do not revert to a generic or conventional meaning.

### Chapter Summary

At the end, summarize how this chapter functions within the book's overall argument. State what it accomplishes, what it depends on from what preceded, and what it sets up for what follows.

## Rules

- The whole-book analysis is not optional context — it is your interpretive framework. Treat its term definitions and coherence constraints as binding unless you can demonstrate from the text itself that an exception is warranted.
- Do not apply conventional names or titles to passages that do not come from the text itself. Describe what the text is doing in your own words.
- Do not flatten the author's argument. If the chapter addresses multiple issues, treat each one.
- Be specific. Cite verses. Quote the Greek where it matters.
- Do not summarize or abbreviate. This is a full exegesis, not an overview.
