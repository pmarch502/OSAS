Matthew 26

Context Within the Author's Argument

What came before (Matthew 25): Chapter 25 continued the private teaching to the disciples that began at 24:3, extending Jesus's discourse on readiness and accountability through three passages. The parable of the ten virgins (25:1-13) depicted ten young women who all expected the bridegroom's arrival, but only five had the practical foresight to bring extra oil; when the bridegroom was delayed and then arrived at midnight, the five without oil were shut out with the words "I do not know you" (25:12). The parable of the talents (25:14-30) described a master who entrusted his property to three servants according to their ability; two traded and doubled their sums, earning the commendation "well done, good and faithful servant" (25:21, 23), while the third buried his talent in the ground out of fear and was cast into "the outer darkness" (25:30). The final passage (25:31-46) depicted the Son of Man enthroned in glory, separating "all the nations" (panta ta ethne, 25:32) as a shepherd separates sheep from goats, on the basis of whether they had fed the hungry, given drink to the thirsty, welcomed the stranger, clothed the naked, and visited the sick and imprisoned. The criterion of judgment was startling: "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (25:40). The three passages share a common structure — a decisive separation at the point of reckoning — and a common theme: what people do during the period of the master's absence determines their fate at his return. Chapter 25 thus completed the fifth and final major teaching block in the document, which began at 23:1 and moved from indictment of the religious leadership (chapter 23) through prediction of the temple's destruction and the tribulation surrounding it (chapter 24) to exhortation about faithful conduct during the interim (chapter 25).

What comes after (Matthew 27): Chapter 27 narrates the delivery of Jesus to Pilate (27:1-2), Judas's remorse and death (27:3-10), the Roman trial (27:11-26), the mockery and crucifixion (27:27-44), Jesus's death (27:45-50), the tearing of the temple curtain and the accompanying signs (27:51-54), and the burial (27:57-66). The chapter moves from the Jewish proceedings of chapter 26 to the Roman proceedings and execution, completing the sequence of betrayal, trial, and death that chapter 26 sets in motion. The connection is seamless: chapter 26 ends with Peter weeping bitterly in the courtyard of the high priest; chapter 27 opens "when morning came" (proi de genomenes) with the chief priests and elders taking counsel to put Jesus to death and delivering him to Pilate the governor.

How chapter 26 connects them: Chapter 25 concluded Jesus's final discourse with the scene of the Son of Man on his glorious throne, judging all the nations. Chapter 26 opens with Jesus declaring, "You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified" (26:2). The transition is extraordinary: the one who has just described himself seated on the throne of glory now announces that he will be handed over (paradidotai) to be crucified. The author moves from the eschatological judge to the condemned prisoner in a single sentence. This is not a change of subject but its deepening — the authority the Son of Man exercises at the final judgment is the authority gained through the suffering that chapter 26 now narrates. The chapter then unfolds as the events predicted throughout the document begin to be fulfilled: the Son of Man will be "delivered up" (paradidotai, the same verb used for Judas's betrayal and for the handing over of the disciples in 24:9-10), the shepherd will be struck and the sheep scattered (26:31, citing Zechariah 13:7), and the one who taught about endurance under pressure will himself endure the ultimate pressure. Chapter 26 is the hinge between teaching and passion, between word and event, between the Jesus who instructs the community about faithfulness and the Jesus whose own faithfulness is tested to its limit. It connects to chapter 27 by delivering Jesus from the Jewish trial to the Roman proceedings, and the whole sequence from 26:1 through 27:66 constitutes a single continuous narrative of Jesus's final hours.

The Announcement of the Passover and the Plot (vv. 1-5)

"When Jesus had finished all these sayings (kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iesous pantas tous logous toutous), he said to his disciples, 'You know that after two days the Passover is coming (meta duo hemeras to pascha ginetai), and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified (kai ho huios tou anthropou paradidotai eis to staurothēnai).'"

The opening formula "when Jesus had finished all these sayings" (hote etelesen ho Iesous pantas tous logous toutous) is a structural marker the author has used to close each of the five major teaching blocks in the document: 7:28 (Sermon on the Mount), 11:1 (mission discourse), 13:53 (parables discourse), 19:1 (community discourse), and now 26:1 (the Olivet discourse including chapters 23-25). The addition of "all" (pantas) — "all these sayings" — is unique to this instance and signals finality. The teaching is complete. What follows is no longer discourse but action: the events the teaching anticipated now begin to unfold.

Jesus speaks of the Passover and his own death in a single sentence, binding them together. The verb paradidotai (is delivered up, is handed over) is in the present passive — not future tense, but present, as though the handing over is already occurring. The passive voice is theologically loaded throughout this document: it is a "divine passive," indicating that the agent behind the action is God. The Son of Man is being delivered up — Judas will be the human instrument, the chief priests the immediate conspirators, but the verb points beyond them to the divine purpose. The same verb paradidomi has been used for John the Baptist being "delivered up" (4:12) and for the prediction that the disciples will be "delivered up" (10:17, 19; 24:9). The pattern is consistent: God's servants are handed over to suffering as part of God's purpose.

The word Pascha (Passover) carries the full weight of the Exodus narrative. Passover commemorated the night when the LORD "passed over" the houses of the Israelites in Egypt, striking the firstborn of Egypt while sparing those whose doorposts bore the blood of the lamb (Exodus 12:1-13, 21-28). The lamb was slaughtered, its blood applied as a sign, and the Israelites ate the meal in haste, ready for departure. By placing Jesus's death at Passover, the author aligns the crucifixion with Israel's foundational act of deliverance. The lamb was killed so that death would pass over God's people. The Son of Man is delivered up at the very moment the nation commemorates its liberation from slavery.

"Then the chief priests and the elders of the people gathered in the palace of the high priest, whose name was Caiaphas (synechthesan hoi archiereis kai hoi presbyteroi tou laou eis ten aulen tou archiereōs tou legomenou Kaiaphas), and plotted together in order to arrest Jesus by stealth and kill him (hina ton Iesoun dolo kratesōsin kai apokteinōsin). But they said, 'Not during the feast (me en te heorte), lest there be an uproar among the people (hina me thorybos genetai en to lao).'"

The author presents two simultaneous scenes: Jesus announcing his death to the disciples, and the chief priests and elders plotting it. The irony is precise: while the leaders conspire to arrest Jesus "by stealth" (dolo — by cunning, by treachery), Jesus has already publicly announced what they are secretly planning. He knows what they are doing before they do it. The verb synago (to gather together) is the same root from which synedrion (Sanhedrin, council) derives. This is an official gathering of the leadership.

Caiaphas is named. He served as high priest from approximately 18 to 36 CE under the appointment of the Roman prefect Valerius Gratus and continued under Pontius Pilate. The high priesthood in this period was a Roman-controlled political appointment, not the hereditary succession prescribed by Torah. The "palace" (aule — courtyard, court) of the high priest will become the setting for the trial later in the chapter.

Their stipulation "not during the feast" reveals their fear of the people (laos). The Passover brought massive crowds to Jerusalem — Josephus estimates the population of Jerusalem swelled enormously during the pilgrim festivals (War 6.420-427). The leaders' concern is pragmatic: a public arrest during the festival could trigger a riot (thorybos — tumult, uproar, commotion). They want Jesus dead, but they want to control the timing. The irony, which the narrative will make clear, is that they will fail even in this — Jesus will be arrested, tried, and executed precisely during the feast, because the timing is not theirs to control. The Passover timing is dictated not by their conspiracy but by the convergence Jesus himself announced in verse 2.

The Anointing at Bethany (vv. 6-13)

"Now when Jesus was at Bethany in the house of Simon the leper (en Bethania en oikia Simōnos tou leprou), a woman came up to him with an alabaster flask of very expensive ointment (alabastron myrou barytimou), and she poured it on his head (katecheen epi tes kephales autou) as he reclined at table."

The scene shifts to Bethany, east of Jerusalem on the Mount of Olives. The host is "Simon the leper" (Simōnos tou leprou) — whether he was formerly leprous and healed, or currently bearing the condition, the designation identifies him by a social stigma. Jesus is at table in the house of a man defined by his impurity. The woman is unnamed; the author provides no identification, no backstory. What matters is her action.

The ointment (myron) is described as barytimou (very expensive, of great value). She pours it on Jesus's head (epi tes kephales). The act of pouring oil on the head has a specific resonance in Israel's history: it is the act of anointing. Kings were anointed by pouring oil on their heads — Samuel anointed Saul (1 Samuel 10:1), Samuel anointed David (1 Samuel 16:13), Zadok the priest anointed Solomon (1 Kings 1:39). The prophet or priest poured oil on the head of the one God had chosen for kingship. The word Christos (Christ, Messiah) itself means "anointed one" (from chrio, to anoint). At the very moment when the religious leaders are plotting to kill Jesus, an unnamed woman performs the act that defines his identity: she anoints his head, enacting what his title declares.

"And when the disciples saw it, they were indignant (aganaktesantes), saying, 'Why this waste (eis ti he apoleia haute)? For this could have been sold for a large sum and given to the poor (edynato gar touto prathenai pollou kai dothenai ptochois).'"

The disciples react with indignation (aganakteo — to be deeply displeased, to be angry). Their objection is economic: the ointment could have been converted to money and distributed to the poor. The word apoleia (waste, destruction, loss) is notable — the same word will be used at 27:20 when Pilate asks what to do with Jesus, and it carries the connotation of irreversible loss. From the disciples' perspective, the ointment is ruined, its potential value destroyed. Their reasoning sounds virtuous: care for the poor is a Torah obligation (Deuteronomy 15:7-11). But their calculation is wrong.

"But Jesus, aware of this (gnous de ho Iesous), said to them, 'Why do you trouble the woman (ti kopous parechete te gynaiki)? For she has done a beautiful thing to me (ergon gar kalon eirgasato eis eme). For you always have the poor with you (pantote gar tous ptochous echete meth' heautōn), but you will not always have me (eme de ou pantote echete). In pouring this ointment on my body, she has done it to prepare me for burial (pros to entaphiasai me epoiesen). Truly, I say to you, wherever this gospel is proclaimed in the whole world (en holō tō kosmō), what she has done will also be told in memory of her (lalethēsetai kai ho epoiesen haute eis mnēmosynon autes).'"

Jesus's response reframes the act entirely. The phrase "she has done a beautiful thing to me" (ergon kalon) uses kalon — noble, good, beautiful in the sense of fitting and worthy. The word echoes kalon at 5:16 ("let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works, ta kala erga"). Her act is a kalon ergon, a work that reveals something true.

The statement "you always have the poor with you" echoes Deuteronomy 15:11: "For there will never cease to be poor in the land. Therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land.'" Jesus is not dismissing care for the poor — the Deuteronomy passage commands it — but pointing out that this moment is unique. "You will not always have me" (eme de ou pantote echete) introduces the reality of his imminent absence. The poor will remain after Jesus's death; the opportunity to anoint his body will not.

The interpretive key is his statement: "she has done it to prepare me for burial" (pros to entaphiasai me). The verb entaphiazo means to prepare a body for burial — to wrap it, anoint it, perform the burial rites. Whether the woman understood this is not stated. Jesus declares the meaning of her act: she has anointed his body for death. The anointing that in Israel's history inaugurated a king here prepares a body for the grave. The Messiah's anointing is simultaneously his preparation for burial. The two meanings — enthronement and entombment — converge in a single act.

The promise that her act will be proclaimed "wherever this gospel is proclaimed in the whole world" (en holō tō kosmō) is remarkable. The phrase echoes 24:14 ("this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world"). Her act is embedded permanently within the gospel proclamation. The word mnēmosynon (memorial, remembrance) is the same word used in the LXX for the memorial portions of offerings (Leviticus 2:2, 9, 16; 5:12) — the part of the sacrifice that ascends to God as a "remembrance." Her deed is a memorial offering.

Judas's Agreement to Betray (vv. 14-16)

"Then one of the twelve, whose name was Judas Iscariot (tote poreutheis heis tōn dōdeka ho legomenos Ioudas Iskariōtēs), went to the chief priests and said, 'What will you give me if I deliver him over to you (ti thelete moi dounai kagō hymin paradōsō auton)?' And they paid him thirty pieces of silver (hoi de estēsan autō triakonta argyria). And from that moment he sought an opportunity to betray him (kai apo tote ezētei eukairian hina auton paradō)."

The word tote (then) connects Judas's action to what immediately precedes. The author places the betrayal immediately after the anointing. The woman poured out expensive ointment on Jesus; Judas goes to negotiate a price for handing him over. The juxtaposition is deliberate: extravagant devotion followed by calculated treachery, both from people within the circle around Jesus.

Judas is identified as "one of the twelve" (heis tōn dōdeka) — the designation emphasizes that the betrayer is not an outsider but a member of the innermost circle. The phrase recurs throughout the chapter (v. 14, v. 47) as a persistent reminder that the betrayal comes from within.

The verb paradidōmi (to hand over, to deliver up) is the same verb Jesus used of himself in verse 2: "the Son of Man will be delivered up" (paradidotai). What Jesus announced as a divine passive, Judas now offers to perform as a human action. The divine purpose and the human treachery converge on the same verb.

The price — "thirty pieces of silver" (triakonta argyria) — echoes Zechariah 11:12-13: "Then I said to them, 'If it seems good to you, give me my wages; but if not, keep them.' And they weighed out as my wages thirty pieces of silver. Then the LORD said to me, 'Throw it to the potter' — the lordly price at which I was priced by them. So I took the thirty pieces of silver and threw them into the house of the LORD, to the potter." In Zechariah, the thirty pieces of silver are the contemptuous valuation placed on the shepherd whom God had sent to tend his flock — a paltry sum, the price of a slave gored by an ox (Exodus 21:32). The author will make this Zechariah connection explicit at 27:3-10, when Judas returns the silver and the chief priests use it to buy the potter's field. The price is not merely a historical detail but a scriptural fulfillment: the shepherd of Israel is valued at the price of a slave.

The phrase "from that moment" (apo tote) marks the decisive turn. The same phrase appeared at 4:17 ("from that time Jesus began to preach") and 16:21 ("from that time Jesus began to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer many things"). Each occurrence of apo tote marks a new phase in the narrative. Here it marks the point at which the betrayal becomes active: Judas begins seeking an "opportune time" (eukairia — a favorable occasion, a moment of advantage).

Preparation for the Passover Meal (vv. 17-19)

"Now on the first day of Unleavened Bread (te de prōte tōn azymōn) the disciples came to Jesus, saying, 'Where will you have us prepare for you to eat the Passover (pou theleis hetoimasōmen soi phagein to pascha)?' He said, 'Go into the city to a certain man (pros ton deina) and say to him, "The Teacher says (ho didaskalos legei), My time is at hand (ho kairos mou engys estin). I will keep the Passover at your house with my disciples (pros se poiō to pascha meta tōn mathētōn mou)."' And the disciples did as Jesus directed them, and they prepared the Passover (kai hētoimasan to pascha)."

The first day of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 14 by common reckoning) was the day the Passover lamb was slaughtered in the afternoon in preparation for the evening meal. The disciples' question assumes that Jesus will celebrate the Passover in the customary manner, and he does. The instruction reveals Jesus's control over the arrangements: he has already made provision with "a certain man" (ton deina — an indefinite reference, a particular person whose name the author either does not know or deliberately omits).

The phrase "the Teacher says" (ho didaskalos legei) is the self-designation Jesus employs to identify himself to the host. The title didaskalos (teacher) has been used by others to address Jesus throughout the document (8:19, 9:11, 12:38, 17:24, 19:16, 22:16, 24, 36), but here Jesus uses it of himself, indicating that the host will recognize the title as belonging to a specific person.

The critical phrase is "my time is at hand" (ho kairos mou engys estin). The word kairos (appointed time, decisive moment, season) is distinct from chronos (ordinary time, duration). Kairos denotes a moment charged with significance, a point at which events converge. Jesus has spoken of the "time" in agricultural metaphors (13:30, the harvest at the "time" of reaping) and in relation to judgment (8:29, the demons' cry "have you come here to torment us before the time?"). Now his own kairos has arrived. The adjective engys (near, at hand) is the same word used at 24:33 ("when you see all these things, you know that he/it is near") — what was predicted as approaching is now upon him.

The Passover is prepared (hētoimasan to pascha). The verb hetoimazo (to prepare, to make ready) echoes the discourse on readiness throughout chapters 24-25. The virgins were told to be "ready" (hetoimoi, 25:10); the master commended the servant he "found ready." Now the disciples prepare the meal that enacts the final readiness — the Passover that will be reinterpreted as Jesus's body and blood.

The Identification of the Betrayer (vv. 20-25)

"When it was evening (opsias de genomenēs), he reclined at table with the twelve (anekeito meta tōn dōdeka). And as they were eating, he said, 'Truly, I say to you, one of you will betray me (heis ex hymōn paradōsei me).' And they were very sorrowful and began to say to him one after another, 'Is it I, Lord (mēti egō eimi, kyrie)?'"

The meal begins in the evening, as prescribed for Passover (Exodus 12:8). The reclining posture (anakeimai) was the customary position for the Passover meal in the first century — participants reclined on couches or cushions, leaning on the left arm and eating with the right hand. The Mishnah specifies that even the poorest in Israel must recline at the Passover meal (Pesachim 10:1), as reclining signified the freedom the meal commemorated. The posture of slaves was upright; the posture of free people was reclining. The Passover was eaten in the posture of the free.

Jesus's announcement — "one of you will betray me" — uses the pronoun hymōn (of you), pointing directly to the twelve. The betrayer is at the table. The verb paradōsei (will hand over) is the future active of paradidōmi, the same verb that has been accumulating significance since verse 2. Each disciple's response — "Is it I, Lord?" (mēti egō eimi, kyrie) — uses the interrogative particle mēti, which expects a negative answer: "It's not I, is it?" The question reveals their uncertainty about themselves. None of them is confident enough to exclude himself from the possibility of betrayal.

"He answered, 'He who has dipped his hand in the dish with me will betray me (ho embasas met' emou ten cheira en tō tryblō houtos me paradōsei). The Son of Man goes as it is written of him (ho men huios tou anthrōpou hypagei kathōs gegraptai peri autou), but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed (ouai de tō anthrōpō ekeinō di' hou ho huios tou anthrōpou paradidotai)! It would have been better for that man if he had not been born (kalon ēn autō ei ouk egennēthē ho anthrōpos ekeinos).'"

The identification — "the one who dipped his hand in the dish with me" — draws on Psalm 41:9 (LXX 40:10): "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." The shared dish represents intimate fellowship. In the ancient Near East, eating together created a bond of trust and mutual obligation. To betray one who shares your table is the most fundamental violation of covenant hospitality. The Passover meal itself was a covenantal act — the household ate together as God's redeemed people. Betrayal at this table is betrayal of the covenant community.

Jesus then makes a statement of extraordinary theological density: "The Son of Man goes as it is written of him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed." Two realities are held together without resolution. First, the Son of Man "goes" (hypagei — departs, goes away) according to what "is written" (gegraptai — the perfect passive of grapho, indicating what stands written in scripture as a settled reality). The death of the Son of Man is not an accident but the fulfillment of scripture — it is "written," fixed in the divine plan revealed through the prophets. Second, the human agent of that divine plan bears full moral responsibility. The "woe" (ouai) pronounced on the betrayer is the same word used in the seven pronouncements of chapter 23. The divine purpose does not excuse the human instrument. The betrayer's act fulfills scripture, but the betrayer is not thereby absolved. The pronouncement "it would have been better for that man if he had not been born" is the most severe judgment the author records from Jesus's mouth — existence itself is worse than non-existence for this person.

"Judas, who would betray him, answered, 'Is it I, Rabbi (mēti egō eimi, rhabbi)?' He said to him, 'You have said so (sy eipas).'"

Judas's question is identical in form to the other disciples' question — "Is it I?" — with one difference: Judas addresses Jesus as "Rabbi" (rhabbi), while the other disciples addressed him as "Lord" (kyrie). The author's usage of these terms is significant. Throughout the document, "Lord" (kyrios) is the address of those who recognize Jesus's authority in its fullest sense (8:2, 25; 14:28, 30; 15:22, 25, 27; 17:4; 18:21; 20:30-33). "Rabbi" or "Teacher" is the address used by outsiders and those with a more limited view (the Pharisees at 12:38, the Sadducees at 22:24, the Herodians at 22:16). The distinction between the eleven saying "Lord" and Judas saying "Rabbi" marks the separation that has already occurred. Judas stands outside, even while sitting at the table.

Jesus's response — "You have said so" (sy eipas, literally "you said it") — neither confirms nor denies directly. The phrase places the admission on Judas's own lips: you yourself have spoken it. The same construction will appear at 26:64 (to the high priest) and 27:11 (to Pilate). In each case, Jesus turns the question back on the questioner, allowing the truth to be spoken without Jesus making a formal declaration that could be used against him.

The Reinterpretation of the Passover Meal (vv. 26-30)

"Now as they were eating (esthiontōn de autōn), Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples (labon ho Iesous arton kai eulogēsas eklasen kai dous tois mathētais), and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body (labete phagete, touto estin to sōma mou).'"

The meal is already in progress — they are eating the Passover. Jesus takes bread (arton), blesses it (eulogeo — to speak well of, to bless; the standard Jewish blessing over bread was "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth"), breaks it, and distributes it. Every element follows the pattern of a Jewish meal: the host takes bread, recites the berakah (blessing), breaks the loaf, and shares it with the company. But Jesus adds words that transform the significance: "this is my body" (touto estin to sōma mou).

The Passover meal already involved interpretation. The Mishnah records that the head of the household was to explain the elements of the meal: "This is the bread of affliction that our fathers ate in the land of Egypt" (Pesachim 10:5, drawing on Deuteronomy 16:3). The bitter herbs represented the bitterness of slavery. The lamb represented the sacrifice whose blood marked the doorposts. The unleavened bread represented the haste of the departure. The meal was a pedagogical reenactment: each generation was to understand itself as having come out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5: "In every generation a person is obligated to regard himself as if he himself had come out of Egypt"). Jesus takes this interpretive tradition and applies it to himself. The bread is not the bread of affliction eaten in Egypt; it is "my body." The Passover narrative is being recentered on his person.

The word sōma (body) here does not refer to flesh as mere tissue but to the whole person in their physical existence. In Hebraic thought, the body is the person as they are present and active in the world. "This is my body" identifies the broken bread with Jesus's own person, given over.

"And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks (eucharistēsas) he gave it to them, saying, 'Drink of it, all of you (piete ex autou pantes), for this is my blood of the covenant (touto gar estin to haima mou tes diathēkēs), which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (to peri pollōn ekchynnomenon eis aphesin hamartiōn).'"

The cup follows the bread. The verb eucharisteo (to give thanks) is closely related to eulogeo (to bless); both describe the prayer spoken over the element. The instruction "drink of it, all of you" (pantes — all, every one) is emphatic: every person at the table is to participate. No one is excluded from the cup.

The interpretive words over the cup are dense with scriptural resonance. "My blood of the covenant" (to haima mou tes diathēkēs) combines two foundational realities from Israel's experience.

First, covenant blood. At Sinai, Moses took the blood of the sacrificial oxen and threw half of it on the altar and half on the people, saying, "Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exodus 24:8, LXX: idou to haima tes diathēkēs). The covenant between God and Israel was ratified by blood — the blood mediated the relationship, sealing the obligations on both sides. Jesus's words directly echo Moses's words at Sinai, but with a decisive substitution: it is not the blood of oxen but "my blood" (to haima mou). The new covenant is ratified not by animal sacrifice but by Jesus's own death.

Second, the new covenant of Jeremiah. Jeremiah 31:31-34 (LXX 38:31-34) announced: "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant (diathēkēn kainēn) with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not like the covenant that I made with their fathers on the day when I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt... For I will forgive their iniquity (hileōs esomai tais adikiais autōn), and I will remember their sin no more." The Jeremiah passage links the new covenant explicitly to the forgiveness of sin. Jesus's phrase "for the forgiveness of sins" (eis aphesin hamartiōn) activates this connection: the blood of the new covenant accomplishes what Jeremiah promised — the forgiveness that the old covenant, with its repeated sacrifices, could point toward but not complete.

The phrase "poured out for many" (peri pollōn ekchynnomenon) evokes Isaiah 53:12: "he poured out his soul to death (LXX: paredothē eis thanaton he psychē autou)... yet he bore the sin of many (autos hamartias pollōn anēnegken)." The "many" (pollōn) in Isaiah 53 is not a limiting term (some but not all) but a Hebraic idiom for "the multitude" — the many who benefit from the servant's suffering. The pouring out (ekcheo) of blood is the language of sacrifice: the blood of the sacrificial animal was poured out at the base of the altar (Leviticus 4:7, 18, 25, 30, 34). Jesus's blood is poured out as a sacrifice, and its effect is the forgiveness of sins.

The word aphesis (release, forgiveness, pardon) appeared at the very beginning of the document in connection with John's baptism — though the author, unlike Luke (3:3), does not describe John's baptism as specifically "for the forgiveness of sins." Here, at the last supper, Jesus locates the forgiveness of sins not in the temple's sacrificial system, not in John's baptism, but in his own poured-out blood. The Passover lamb's blood protected from death; Jesus's covenant blood accomplishes forgiveness.

"I tell you I will not drink again of this fruit of the vine (ou me piō ap' arti ek toutou tou genēmatos tes ampelou) until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom (heōs tes hēmeras ekeinēs hotan auto pinō meth' hymōn kainon en te basileia tou patros mou)."

Jesus declares a vow of abstinence — he will not drink wine again until a future moment "in my Father's kingdom." The phrase ap' arti (from now on) marks the present moment as a boundary. The word kainon (new) modifies "it" (the fruit of the vine) — what is drunk in the kingdom will be new, transformed, different from the present experience. The declaration points forward to a future communion that the present meal anticipates but does not fulfill. The present meal is a foretaste; the full reality awaits the kingdom. The phrase "with you" (meth' hymōn) is significant: the future drinking is communal, shared between Jesus and the disciples. The relationship that the meal enacts continues beyond death into the kingdom.

"And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives (kai hymnēsantes exēlthon eis to oros tōn elaiōn)."

The hymn (hymnēsantes — from hymneō, to sing a hymn) is almost certainly the Hallel, Psalms 113-118, which was sung at the conclusion of the Passover meal. The Mishnah specifies that Psalms 113-114 were sung before the meal and Psalms 115-118 after (Pesachim 10:6-7). Psalm 118 — the final psalm of the Hallel — contains the words "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (118:22), which Jesus quoted against the religious leaders at 21:42, and "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" (118:26), which the crowds shouted at Jesus's entry into Jerusalem (21:9) and which Jesus himself cited at 23:39 as the words Jerusalem would say before seeing him again. The Passover meal thus closes with the very words that framed Jesus's messianic entry and prophetic departure.

They go out to the Mount of Olives — the same location where Jesus sat and delivered the discourse of chapters 24-25. The mountain of eschatological teaching now becomes the mountain of agony and arrest.

The Prediction of Desertion and Peter's Protest (vv. 31-35)

"Then Jesus said to them, 'You will all fall away because of me this night (pantes hymeis skandalisthēsesthe en emoi en te nykti taute), for it is written (gegraptai gar), "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered (pataxō ton poimena, kai diaskorpisthēsontai ta probata tes poimnēs)."'"

The verb skandalizo (to cause to stumble, to fall away) has been central to the document. At 11:6, "blessed is the one who is not scandalized by me." At 13:21, the rocky-ground hearer "falls away" when tribulation comes. At 13:57, Jesus's own townspeople are "scandalized" by him. At 15:12, the Pharisees are "scandalized" by his teaching. At 18:6-9, extended warnings about causing "little ones" to stumble. At 24:10, "many will fall away" under persecution. Now Jesus predicts that all (pantes) the disciples will be scandalized "because of me" (en emoi) — his arrest and suffering will be the rock on which they stumble. The very thing he warned against in the discourse will happen to his own inner circle. The one who said "the one who endures to the end will be saved" (24:13) now tells his disciples that none of them will endure this night.

The scriptural citation is from Zechariah 13:7: "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered." In Zechariah's original context, the passage describes a divine act against God's own shepherd: "Awake, O sword, against my shepherd, against the man who stands next to me, declares the LORD of hosts. Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered." The striking of the shepherd is not a human initiative but a divine command — the LORD calls the sword against his own shepherd. The author's use of this text reinforces the divine passive of verse 2: the Son of Man is "delivered up" according to what is "written." The shepherd's striking is God's act, and its consequence is the scattering of the flock. The disciples' desertion is not merely human failure; it is the fulfillment of Zechariah's oracle. The shepherd must be struck; the sheep must scatter.

"But after I am raised up (meta de to egerthēnai me), I will go before you to Galilee (proaxō hymas eis tēn Galilaian)."

Immediately after the prediction of scattering, Jesus announces regathering. The scattering is not permanent. The verb proagō (to go before, to lead the way) is a shepherd's verb — the shepherd goes ahead of the flock, and the sheep follow (cf. John 10:4, though the author's own usage at 21:9 describes Jesus "going before" them). The destination is Galilee — the place where the ministry began (4:12-17), where the disciples were first called (4:18-22), and where the risen Jesus will meet them (28:7, 10, 16). The return to Galilee after the scattering reverses the Zechariah pattern: the shepherd who is struck rises, and the scattered sheep are regathered.

"Peter answered him, 'Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away (ei pantes skandalisthēsontai en soi, egō oudepote skandalisthēsomai).' Jesus said to him, 'Truly, I say to you, this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times (tris aparnēse me).' Peter said to him, 'Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you (ou mē se aparnēsomai)!' And so said all the disciples (homoiōs kai pantes hoi mathētai eipan)."

Peter's response sets himself apart from the others: "though they all fall away... I will never." The word oudepote (never, at no time) is emphatic. Peter claims an exception from the universal prediction. Jesus counters with a more specific prediction: not merely that Peter will fall away (skandalizo), but that he will deny (arneomai) Jesus — and not once but three times, and not at some indefinite future but "this very night" (en taute te nykti), before the rooster crows at dawn. The verb arneomai (to deny, to disown, to refuse to acknowledge) appeared at 10:33: "whoever denies me before others, I also will deny before my Father who is in heaven." The word Jesus used to warn about the consequences of denial is now the word he uses to predict Peter's action. Peter's protest — "even if I must die with you, I will not deny you" — uses the strongest negation available (ou mē — by no means). And "all the disciples said the same" (homoiōs kai pantes). Every one of them declares willingness to die rather than deny. The narrative will show that every one of them was wrong.

The Agony in the Garden (vv. 36-46)

"Then Jesus went with them to a place called Gethsemane (Gethsēmani — from the Aramaic gat shemanim, "oil press"), and he said to his disciples, 'Sit here, while I go over there and pray (heōs hou apelthōn ekei proseuxōmai).' And taking with him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee (ton Petron kai tous duo huious Zebedaiou), he began to be sorrowful and troubled (ērxato lypeisthai kai adēmonein)."

The setting is Gethsemane, an olive garden on the lower slopes of the Mount of Olives. The name itself — "oil press" — is resonant with the pressing that is about to occur. Jesus takes with him the same three disciples who witnessed the transfiguration (17:1): Peter, James, and John. The inner circle that saw his glory now sees his anguish. The two scenes form a pair: the mountain of radiance and the garden of agony both reveal who Jesus is, and the same witnesses are present for both.

The verbs are striking. Lypeo (to grieve, to be sorrowful) describes deep emotional pain. Adēmoneo (to be troubled, to be distressed, to be in anguish) is a rarer word conveying acute inner turmoil — a restless distress that shakes the entire person.

"Then he said to them, 'My soul is very sorrowful, even to death (perilypos estin hē psychē mou heōs thanatou). Remain here, and watch with me (grēgoreite met' emou).'"

The phrase "my soul is very sorrowful, even to death" echoes Psalm 42:5, 11 (LXX 41:6, 12) and 43:5 (LXX 42:5): "Why are you cast down, O my soul (hina ti perilypos ei, psychē)?" and Jonah 4:9 (LXX): "I am deeply grieved, even to death" (perilypos eimi egō heōs thanatou). The word perilypos (surrounded by grief, overwhelmed with sorrow) describes grief so total it borders on death — the sorrow itself is nearly lethal. The command "watch with me" (grēgoreite met' emou) uses the same imperative from 24:42 and 25:13 — the call to vigilance that governed the eschatological discourse is now directed at these three disciples in a specific, immediate situation. The one who taught others to "stay awake" now asks them to stay awake with him.

"And going a little farther (proelthōn mikron), he fell on his face (epesen epi prosōpon autou) and prayed, saying, 'My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me (pater mou, ei dynaton estin, parelthato ap' emou to potērion touto); nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will (plēn ouch hōs egō thelō all' hōs sy).'"

The posture — falling face down (epesen epi prosōpon) — is the posture of total prostration before God, the position of utmost vulnerability and submission (cf. Abraham in Genesis 17:3, Moses and Aaron in Numbers 16:22). The address "my Father" (pater mou) is intimate, personal.

The "cup" (potērion) is a well-established prophetic metaphor. In the Hebrew scriptures, the cup is the cup of God's wrath: Psalm 75:8 (LXX 74:9): "In the hand of the LORD there is a cup with foaming wine, well mixed; he pours out from it, and all the wicked of the earth shall drain it down to the dregs." Isaiah 51:17, 22: "Wake yourself, wake yourself, stand up, O Jerusalem, you who have drunk from the hand of the LORD the cup of his wrath, who have drunk to the dregs the bowl, the cup of staggering." Jeremiah 25:15-16: "Take from my hand this cup of the wine of wrath, and make all the nations to whom I send you drink it." Ezekiel 23:31-34 describes the cup of horror and desolation. In every prophetic instance, the cup is what God gives to those under judgment — it is the concentrated experience of divine wrath against sin. Jesus asks whether it is possible for this cup to pass him by. The request is genuine, not performative. The agony is real. But the prayer is framed by submission: "not as I will, but as you will" (ouch hōs egō thelō all' hōs sy). The will of the Father takes precedence over the human desire to avoid suffering.

"And he came to the disciples and found them sleeping (heuriskei autous katheudontas). And he said to Peter, 'So, could you not watch with me one hour (houtōs ouk ischysate mian hōran grēgorēsai met' emou)? Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation (grēgoreite kai proseuchesthe, hina mē eiselthēte eis peirasmon). The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak (to men pneuma prothymon hē de sarx asthenēs).'"

The three disciples who promised to die with Jesus cannot stay awake with him for one hour. The irony is pointed: Peter declared "even if I must die with you" (v. 35), but he cannot remain conscious while Jesus prays. The command "watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (eis peirasmon) connects to the Lord's Prayer at 6:13: "lead us not into temptation" (mē eisenegkēs hēmas eis peirasmon). The word peirasmos (testing, trial, temptation) refers not to ordinary moral temptation but to the critical test — the crisis moment that determines whether faithfulness holds or breaks. The upcoming arrest is that peirasmos for the disciples. Their only defense against it is watchfulness and prayer. The observation "the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak" (to pneuma prothymon, hē sarx asthenēs) does not excuse the failure but explains its mechanism: their intention is genuine (prothymon — eager, willing, ready) but their human capacity (sarx — flesh, bodily existence) cannot sustain it.

"Again, for the second time, he went away and prayed, 'My Father, if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done (pater mou, ei ou dynatai touto parelthein ean mē auto piō, genēthētō to thelēma sou).' And again he came and found them sleeping, for their eyes were heavy (ēsan gar autōn hoi ophthalmoi bebarēmenoi). So, leaving them again, he went away and prayed for the third time, saying the same words again (ton auton logon eipōn palin)."

The second prayer has shifted. The first prayer asked whether the cup could pass; the second acknowledges that it cannot pass unless he drinks it and submits to the Father's will: "your will be done" (genēthētō to thelēma sou). This is the exact language of the Lord's Prayer at 6:10: genēthētō to thelēma sou (your will be done). The prayer Jesus taught his disciples is the prayer he himself prays in his extremity. The one who taught the community to pray "your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" now enacts that prayer in the most costly possible way.

Three times he prays, three times he finds them sleeping. The threefold pattern anticipates Peter's threefold denial later in the chapter. The one who cannot stay awake will not be able to stand when tested.

"Then he came to the disciples and said to them, 'Sleep and take your rest later on (katheudete to loipon kai anapauesthe). See, the hour is at hand (idou ēggiken hē hōra), and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners (paradidotai eis cheiras hamartōlōn). Rise, let us be going (egeiresthe, agōmen); see, my betrayer is at hand (idou ēggiken ho paradidous me).'"

The phrase "the hour is at hand" (ēggiken hē hōra) parallels "my time is at hand" (ho kairos mou engys estin) from verse 18. The decisive moment has arrived. The verb paradidotai (is being handed over) is again present tense — the process is already underway. "Into the hands of sinners" (eis cheiras hamartōlōn) identifies the recipients of the handover: those who oppose God's purposes. Yet the verb's divine passive persists — sinners receive him, but God delivers him.

The Arrest (vv. 47-56)

"While he was still speaking (eti autou lalountos), Judas came, one of the twelve (Ioudas heis tōn dōdeka ēlthen), and with him a great crowd with swords and clubs (ochlos polys meta machairōn kai xylōn), from the chief priests and elders of the people (apo tōn archiereōn kai presbyterōn tou laou)."

The phrase "one of the twelve" (heis tōn dōdeka) recurs a third time (after vv. 14 and 20), relentlessly emphasizing Judas's position inside the inner circle. The armed crowd comes "from the chief priests and elders" — the same groups who gathered at Caiaphas's palace in verses 3-4. Their plot is now being executed, though on a timetable they did not choose.

"Now the betrayer had given them a sign, saying, 'The one I will kiss is the man; seize him (hon an philēsō autos estin; kratēsate auton).' And he came up to Jesus at once and said, 'Greetings, Rabbi (chaire, rhabbi)!' And he kissed him (katephilēsen auton)."

The kiss (philēma) was a standard greeting between teacher and student, between friends, between family. In the LXX, kisses mark intimate relationships: Jacob kissing Rachel (Genesis 29:11), Esau and Jacob's reconciliation (Genesis 33:4), the kisses of greeting in the Psalms. Judas uses the gesture of intimacy as the instrument of betrayal. The verb kataphileo (to kiss fervently, to kiss warmly) is intensive — this is not a perfunctory peck but a demonstrative embrace. The greeting "Rabbi" (rhabbi) is the same address Judas used at verse 25, confirming the pattern: the other disciples say "Lord"; Judas says "Rabbi."

"Jesus said to him, 'Friend, do what you came to do (hetaire, eph' ho parei).' Then they came up and laid hands on Jesus and seized him (ekratēsan auton)."

The address "friend" (hetaire) is not the word philos (a true friend, one who loves) but hetairos (companion, associate, comrade). The author has used this word twice before, both times in parables where the address is directed at someone who has failed: the laborers in the vineyard (20:13, "friend, I am doing you no wrong") and the guest at the wedding feast without a garment (22:12, "friend, how did you get in here without a wedding garment?"). In each case, hetaire marks the moment when someone is addressed courteously before being confronted with their failure. The word maintains formal civility while acknowledging the breach. Jesus does not rebuke Judas; he identifies the reality of what is happening and allows it to proceed.

"And behold, one of those who were with Jesus stretched out his hand and drew his sword and struck the servant of the high priest and cut off his ear (aphelen autou to ōtion)."

One of Jesus's companions draws a sword and strikes. The violence is immediate, instinctive — the response of someone who believes the situation calls for armed resistance. The target is "the servant of the high priest" (ton doulon tou archiereōs), a representative of the very authority that ordered the arrest.

"Then Jesus said to him, 'Put your sword back into its place (apostrepson tēn machairan sou eis ton topon autēs). For all who take the sword will perish by the sword (pantes gar hoi labontes machairan en machaira apolountai). Do you think that I cannot appeal to my Father, and he will at once send me more than twelve legions of angels (dyo kai deka legionas angelōn)? But how then should the Scriptures be fulfilled (pōs oun plērōthōsin hai graphai), that it must be so (hoti houtōs dei genesthai)?'"

Jesus's rebuke is absolute: "Put your sword back." The principle he states — "all who take the sword will perish by the sword" — is proverbial in form but operates here as a specific instruction to his community: violence is not the means of God's kingdom. The irony of the numbers is pointed: a Roman legion comprised approximately 6,000 soldiers; twelve legions would be 72,000 angels, a force that vastly exceeds the armed crowd arresting him. The power to resist is available but refused. The reason for the refusal is scriptural necessity: "how then should the Scriptures be fulfilled?" (pōs plērōthōsin hai graphai). The dei (must, it is necessary) from 16:21 ("the Son of Man must suffer many things") and 24:6 ("this must take place") recurs. The events are unfolding according to a script written in Israel's prophets, and resistance would contradict the divine plan.

"At that hour Jesus said to the crowds (en ekeinē tē hōra eipen ho Iesous tois ochlois), 'Have you come out as against a robber (hōs epi lēstēn), with swords and clubs to capture me? Day after day I sat in the temple teaching (kath' hēmeran en tō hierō ekathezomēn didaskōn), and you did not seize me. But all this has taken place that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled (hina plērōthōsin hai graphai tōn prophētōn). Then all the disciples left him and fled (tote hoi mathētai pantes aphentes auton ephygon).'"

The word lēstēs (robber, brigand, insurrectionist) appeared at 21:13 ("you have made it a den of robbers") and carries the connotation of a violent revolutionary. Jesus points out the absurdity: he has been publicly teaching in the temple every day, accessible to anyone. The nighttime arrest with weapons is theatrical and cowardly. But even this — the manner of his arrest — fulfills "the Scriptures of the prophets."

The final sentence fulfills Jesus's prediction from verse 31: "you will all fall away." The word pantes (all) echoes the pantes of Peter's protest ("though they all fall away, I will never"). All (pantes) the disciples abandoned him (aphentes — from aphiēmi, to leave, to abandon, to let go) and fled (ephygon — from pheugō, to flee). The shepherd has been struck, and the sheep have scattered, exactly as Zechariah 13:7 predicted and Jesus quoted. The community's dissolution is complete. Not one disciple remains.

The Trial Before the High Priest (vv. 57-68)

"Then those who had seized Jesus led him to Caiaphas the high priest (pros Kaiaphan ton archierea), where the scribes and the elders had gathered (hopou hoi grammateis kai hoi presbyteroi synēchthēsan). And Peter was following him at a distance (ho de Petros ēkolouthei autō apo makrothen), as far as the courtyard of the high priest (heōs tēs aulēs tou archiereōs), and going inside he sat with the guards to see the end (to telos)."

The trial is before Caiaphas, the same high priest named at verse 3 in whose courtyard the conspiracy was hatched. The scribes and elders have "gathered" (synēchthēsan) — the same verb used at verse 3 for the plotting session. What was planned is now executed.

Peter follows "at a distance" (apo makrothen). The phrase is loaded: he has not entirely fled (unlike the others), but he is not with Jesus either. He occupies the intermediate space — near enough to watch, far enough to avoid identification. He sits with the guards (meta tōn hypēretōn) — the servants of the very institution that is trying Jesus. The word telos (end, outcome) is used here in its ordinary sense of "the way things turn out." Peter wants to see what will happen, but he positions himself among the opposition to do so.

"Now the chief priests and the whole council (holon to synedrion) were seeking false testimony against Jesus (epseudomartyrian) that they might put him to death (hopōs auton thanatōsōsin), but they found none, though many false witnesses came forward (pollōn proselthontōn pseudomartyrōn). At last two came forward and said, 'This man said, "I am able to destroy the temple of God (dynasthai katalysai ton naon tou theou), and to rebuild it in three days."'"

The Sanhedrin (synedrion) — the supreme judicial body of Jewish governance — is "seeking false testimony" (pseudomartyrian). The author's description is blunt: the court is not seeking truth but fabricating a case. The word pseudomartyria (false testimony) violates the ninth commandment directly: "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16). The irony is severe: the body charged with upholding Torah violates Torah in the process of condemning Jesus.

The charge about destroying the temple uses the verb katalyō (to destroy, to throw down) — the same verb Jesus used at 24:2 when he predicted the temple's destruction ("there will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down"). The witnesses attribute to Jesus a claim that he will destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days. The author characterizes this as false testimony, but the distortion is subtle: Jesus predicted the temple's destruction (24:2) but did not claim he would be the one to destroy it, and the "three days" language connects not to the physical temple but to his own resurrection, which the author will narrate. The false witnesses take authentic elements of Jesus's teaching and recombine them into a charge that misrepresents what he said.

"And the high priest stood up and said, 'Have you no answer to make (ouden apokrine)? What is it that these men testify against you?' But Jesus remained silent (ho de Iesous esiōpa)."

Jesus's silence fulfills Isaiah 53:7: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth." The Passover lamb, which had been slaughtered on this very day, was to be "without blemish" (Exodus 12:5) and was led silently to its death. The servant of Isaiah 53 bears the same silent submission. The word esiōpa (remained silent, kept silence) is the imperfect tense — Jesus maintained his silence, a sustained refusal to defend himself against false charges.

"And the high priest said to him, 'I adjure you by the living God (exorkizō se kata tou theou tou zōntos), tell us if you are the Christ, the Son of God (ei sy ei ho Christos ho huios tou theou).' Jesus said to him, 'You have said so (sy eipas). But I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power (ap' arti opsesthe ton huion tou anthrōpou kathēmenon ek dexiōn tes dynameōs) and coming on the clouds of heaven (kai erchomenon epi tōn nephelōn tou ouranou).'"

The high priest places Jesus under oath (exorkizō — to adjure, to put under a solemn charge) "by the living God" — making the question a matter of sworn testimony. The double title — "the Christ" (ho Christos, the Messiah, the anointed one) and "the Son of God" (ho huios tou theou) — combines the messianic expectation with the claim to a unique relationship with God. Both titles have appeared earlier: "Christ" at 1:1, 16, 17; 2:4; 11:2; 16:16, 20; 22:42; 23:10. "Son of God" at 3:17; 4:3, 6; 8:29; 14:33; 16:16; 17:5.

Jesus's response "you have said so" (sy eipas) parallels his answer to Judas at verse 25 — the same construction that neither flatly affirms nor denies but places the statement on the questioner's lips. But then Jesus adds his own declaration, introduced by "but I tell you" (plēn legō hymin), which shifts from the high priest's categories to Jesus's own.

The declaration combines two scriptures. First, Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109:1): "The LORD said to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'" Jesus has already used this psalm in his exchange with the Pharisees at 22:41-46, where he asked how David's son could be David's lord. "Seated at the right hand of Power" (kathēmenon ek dexiōn tes dynameōs) places the Son of Man in the position of ultimate authority, sharing God's throne. The word dynamis (Power) is a circumlocution for God, used to avoid pronouncing the divine name.

Second, Daniel 7:13: "I saw in the night visions, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man." As discussed at 24:30, the Danielic "son of man" comes on the clouds to receive dominion and glory from the Ancient of Days. The scene in Daniel 7 is an enthronement: the son of man arrives at the divine throne and is given universal authority.

The combination of Psalm 110 and Daniel 7 produces a statement of extraordinary scope: the one who stands bound and accused before the Sanhedrin declares that his judges will see him enthroned at God's right hand, invested with the authority of the Danielic Son of Man. The phrase ap' arti (from now on) indicates that this enthronement is not merely future but begins "from now on" — from this very moment, the Son of Man's exaltation is underway. The trial that appears to be Jesus's defeat is presented as the beginning of his vindication.

"Then the high priest tore his robes (tote ho archiereus dierēxen ta himatia autou) and said, 'He has uttered blasphemy (eblasphēmēsen). What further witnesses do we need (ti eti chreian echomen martyrōn)? You have now heard his blasphemy (nyn ēkousate tēn blasphēmian). What is your judgment (ti hymin dokei)?' They answered, 'He deserves death (enochos thanatou estin).'"

The tearing of robes was the prescribed response to hearing blasphemy (Mishnah, Sanhedrin 7:5). The high priest declares that Jesus has blasphemed (blasphēmeō — to speak irreverently about God, to claim divine prerogatives). From the Sanhedrin's perspective, a bound prisoner claiming to sit at God's right hand and come on the clouds of heaven is arrogating to himself what belongs to God alone. The verdict is "deserving of death" (enochos thanatou — liable to the death penalty). Under Jewish law as codified later in the Mishnah (Sanhedrin 7:5), blasphemy was punishable by stoning, though the penalty required the explicit pronunciation of the divine name, which by the Mishnah's standards Jesus had not done. Whether the Sanhedrin's proceedings met their own legal requirements is a question the author does not directly address; what the author presents is a court that sought false testimony, failed to find it, and then convicted on the basis of Jesus's own declaration of who he is.

"Then they spit in his face and struck him (ethen erapisan auton). And some slapped him, saying, 'Prophesy to us, you Christ (prophēteuson hēmin, Christe)! Who is it that struck you?'"

The physical abuse — spitting, striking, slapping — echoes Isaiah 50:6, the third servant song: "I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting." The servant in Isaiah willingly endures the abuse as part of his mission. The demand "Prophesy to us, Christ!" is mocking: they have blindfolded him (as Mark 14:65 makes explicit) and demand that he identify who struck him as a test of prophetic ability. The irony the author embeds is multi-layered: Jesus has already prophesied — the destruction of the temple, the betrayal by Judas, the scattering of the disciples, Peter's denial — and every prophecy is being fulfilled as they mock his prophetic claim.

Peter's Denial (vv. 69-75)

"Now Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard (ho de Petros ekathēto exō en te aulē). And a servant girl came up to him and said, 'You also were with Jesus the Galilean (kai sy ēstha meta Iesou tou Galilaiou).' But he denied it before them all (ērnēsato emprosthen pantōn), saying, 'I do not know what you mean (ouk oida ti legeis).'"

The narrative returns to Peter, who has been sitting with the guards in the courtyard since verse 58. The first challenge comes from a servant girl (paidiskē) — not a soldier, not a member of the Sanhedrin, but a female household servant, the lowest-ranking person in the courtyard hierarchy. The phrase "you also were with Jesus the Galilean" (kai sy ēstha meta Iesou) identifies Peter by his association. He denies "before them all" (emprosthen pantōn). The verb arneomai (to deny) is the specific word Jesus predicted at verse 34: "you will deny me three times." The prediction begins its fulfillment.

"And when he went out to the entrance (exelthonta de eis ton pylōna), another servant girl saw him, and she said to the bystanders, 'This man was with Jesus of Nazareth (houtos ēn meta Iesou tou Nazōraiou).' And again he denied it with an oath (palin ērnēsato meta horkou): 'I do not know the man (ouk oida ton anthrōpon).'"

The second denial escalates. Peter has moved to the gateway (pylōna), the transition between the courtyard and the street — he is edging toward escape. Another servant girl identifies him. This time Peter denies "with an oath" (meta horkou). The word horkos (oath) is significant: at 5:33-37, Jesus taught "do not take an oath at all... Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'; anything more than this comes from evil." Peter, in the act of denying Jesus, violates Jesus's teaching about oaths. The content of the oath — "I do not know the man" (ouk oida ton anthrōpon) — uses the phrase "the man" (ton anthrōpon) to distance himself from Jesus. He does not say "I do not know Jesus" or "I do not know my teacher" but reduces Jesus to a generic, unnamed person.

"After a little while (meta mikron de) the bystanders came up and said to Peter, 'Certainly you too are one of them, for your accent betrays you (kai gar hē lalia sou dēlon se poiei).' Then he began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear (tote ērxato katathematizein kai omnynai), 'I do not know the man (ouk oida ton anthrōpon)!' And immediately the rooster crowed (kai eutheōs alektōr ephōnēsen)."

The third denial is the most extreme. The bystanders identify Peter by his accent — his Galilean dialect betrays his origin (the Talmud records that Galileans were mocked for their distinctive pronunciation, Eruvin 53b). Peter "began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear" (katathematizein kai omnynai). The verb katathematizo means to bind oneself with a curse — to call down divine punishment on oneself if the statement is false. Peter has progressed from a simple denial to a sworn denial to a denial under self-curse. Each step takes him further from Jesus and deeper into the violation of Jesus's own teaching (5:33-37). The formula "I do not know the man" is repeated for the third time.

"And immediately (eutheōs) the rooster crowed." The fulfillment is precise to the detail Jesus specified: "before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times" (v. 34). The word eutheōs (immediately) underscores the exact correspondence between prediction and fulfillment.

"And Peter remembered the saying of Jesus (emnēsthē ho Petros tou rhēmatos Iesou eirēkotos), 'Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times.' And he went out and wept bitterly (kai exelthōn exō eklausen pikrōs)."

The verb mimnēskomai (to remember, to be reminded) marks the moment of recognition. Peter recalls Jesus's words and realizes what he has done. The weeping is described as pikrōs (bitterly) — the adverb denotes the sharpest, most acrid form of grief. The word carries the taste of bitterness, as in the bitter herbs of the Passover meal. Peter's tears are the Passover's bitter herbs made personal — the taste of slavery, not to Egypt but to his own fear and failure.

The chapter closes with Peter weeping outside. He has denied Jesus three times, each denial more emphatic than the last, and the rooster's crow has confirmed that Jesus's prediction was exact. Peter, who declared he would die rather than deny, has denied rather than risk dying. The narrative leaves him outside, in the dark, broken.

The Chapter's Function in the Author's Larger Argument

1. It enacts what the preceding discourse predicted. The transition from chapters 23-25 (teaching) to chapter 26 (events) is not merely sequential but fulfilling. Jesus taught about falling away under persecution (24:10, skandalisthēsontai); the disciples fall away (26:31, skandalisthēsesthe). He taught about betrayal within the community (24:10); Judas betrays him (26:14-16, 47-50). He taught about the need to stay awake (24:42, 25:13, grēgoreite); the disciples sleep in Gethsemane (26:40, 43, 45). He taught that "the one who endures to the end will be saved" (24:13); Peter does not endure but denies (26:69-75). The chapter is the lived test of the discourse's instructions, and the disciples fail it. Their failure does not invalidate the teaching but illustrates exactly why it was needed.

2. It binds Jesus's death to the Passover and the covenant. The author places the crucifixion within the Passover framework not as incidental timing but as interpretive structure. The Passover lamb was slaughtered for the deliverance of God's people; Jesus is "delivered up" (paradidotai) at Passover. The covenant at Sinai was ratified with blood (Exodus 24:8); Jesus speaks of "my blood of the covenant" (26:28). Jeremiah promised a new covenant linked to the forgiveness of sins (Jeremiah 31:31-34); Jesus declares his blood is "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28). Isaiah's servant bore the sin of many (Isaiah 53:12); Jesus's blood is poured out "for many" (peri pollōn). The cumulative effect is to present Jesus's death as the fulfillment and replacement of the Passover sacrifice, establishing the new covenant that the prophets announced.

3. It demonstrates the pattern of scriptural fulfillment through human agency. The chapter repeatedly holds together divine purpose and human responsibility. "The Son of Man goes as it is written of him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed" (26:24). Judas's betrayal fulfills Zechariah 11:12-13 (the thirty pieces of silver), but Judas bears full moral culpability. The disciples' desertion fulfills Zechariah 13:7 (the shepherd struck, the sheep scattered), but their failure is real failure, not mere play-acting. Jesus's arrest fulfills "the Scriptures of the prophets" (26:56), but the arresting party acts out of genuine hostility, not obedient compliance. The pattern consistently refuses to resolve the tension between divine sovereignty and human responsibility — both are maintained without either absorbing the other.

4. It reveals Jesus's identity through the convergence of roles. In a single chapter, Jesus is the Passover lamb (whose death delivers from death), the anointed king (the woman pours oil on his head), the covenant mediator (whose blood ratifies the new covenant), the suffering servant of Isaiah (who is silent before his accusers), the Son of Man of Daniel 7 (who will be seen seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds), and the shepherd of Zechariah (who is struck so that the sheep scatter). These are not separate roles assumed sequentially but a single identity in which all these scriptural strands converge. The chapter's narrative structure enacts this convergence: the one who is anointed is the one who is betrayed is the one who pours out his blood is the one who is struck is the one who will be enthroned.

5. It tests every character against the standard of the discourse. The unnamed woman at Bethany does a "beautiful thing" — she recognizes what the disciples do not, that Jesus's body is being prepared for burial. Judas, one of the twelve, converts intimacy into betrayal and the Messiah into a commodity valued at a slave's price. Peter declares absolute loyalty and delivers absolute denial. The other disciples promise to die rather than deny and then flee at the first sign of danger. Jesus himself is tested more severely than any of them — facing the "cup" of divine wrath in Gethsemane, asking whether it can pass, and then submitting to the Father's will. He is the only one who endures to the end. The chapter functions as a sifting: every person's faithfulness is tested, and only Jesus's holds. The implication for the community reading the text is clear: the disciples' failure is not a reason for despair but a warning and a mirror. If the twelve failed under pressure, the reader is not exempt. The question of 24:45 — "Who then is the faithful and wise servant?" — hangs over every character in chapter 26, and the only one who answers it is Jesus.

6. It locates forgiveness within the narrative of failure. The chapter is saturated with failure — Judas's betrayal, the disciples' desertion, Peter's denial. But it is also the chapter that introduces the forgiveness of sins: "this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28). The failures that the chapter narrates are the very failures that the cup addresses. The covenant blood is poured out not for the sinless but "for many" — for the ones who fall away, who betray, who deny. The promise to meet the scattered disciples in Galilee (26:32) ensures that desertion is not the final word. The shepherd who is struck will rise and go before them. The flock that scatters will be regathered. The bitter weeping of Peter (26:75) is not the end of Peter's story — it is the beginning of his need for the very forgiveness the cup provides.
