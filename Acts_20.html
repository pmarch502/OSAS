Acts 20 — Neutral Reading

Context and Placement

What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 19): Paul arrived in Ephesus and found about twelve disciples who knew only the baptism of John. He corrected their incomplete faith, baptized them in the name of the Lord Jesus, and laid hands on them; the Holy Spirit came upon them with tongues and prophecy (19:1-7). Paul then entered the synagogue and spoke boldly for three months, reasoning and persuading about the kingdom of God. When some became stubborn and spoke evil of the Way, Paul withdrew, taking the disciples with him, and moved to the hall of Tyrannus, where he taught daily for two years, "so that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord, both Jews and Greeks" (19:10). God performed extraordinary miracles through Paul — even handkerchiefs and aprons from his body healed the sick and expelled evil spirits (19:11-12). Itinerant Jewish exorcists, the seven sons of a figure called Sceva, attempted to invoke the name of Jesus as a magical formula. The evil spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I recognize, but who are you?" and the demonized man overpowered all seven, exposing the difference between genuine authority and borrowed formula (19:13-16). Fear fell on the city, the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified, and many believers who had been secretly retaining magical practices came forward to confess. They burned their books of magic arts publicly — fifty thousand pieces of silver in value — and "the word of the Lord continued to increase and prevail mightily" (19:20). Paul then resolved in the Spirit to go through Macedonia and Achaia to Jerusalem, and afterward to Rome: "I must also see Rome" (19:21). The chapter climaxed with the riot of Demetrius the silversmith, whose guild's livelihood making silver shrines of Artemis was threatened by Paul's preaching that "gods made with hands are not gods" (19:26). The theater filled with a confused mob chanting "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for two hours. The town clerk eventually restored order by pointing out that no sacrilege or blasphemy had been committed, that the courts were open for legitimate complaints, and that the riot endangered the city's standing with Rome. The assembly was dismissed without any charge against the followers of the Way.

What the author argues immediately after (Acts 21): Paul and his companions sail from Miletus to Cos, Rhodes, and Patara, then take a ship to Phoenicia, landing at Tyre, where they find disciples who "through the Spirit" urge Paul not to go to Jerusalem (21:4). At Caesarea, the prophet Agabus takes Paul's belt, binds his own feet and hands, and declares: "Thus says the Holy Spirit, 'This is how the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt and deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles'" (21:11). Paul's companions and the local believers plead with him not to go up, but Paul replies: "What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus" (21:13). They cease their urging, saying, "Let the will of the Lord be done" (21:14). Paul goes up to Jerusalem, is received by James and the elders, and reports what God has done among the Gentiles. But the Jerusalem leaders inform him that thousands of believing Jews are "zealous for the law" and have been told that Paul teaches Jews in the diaspora to forsake Moses, to stop circumcising their children, and to abandon the customs (21:21). To demonstrate that the reports are false, Paul is asked to sponsor four men who are under a Nazirite vow, purifying himself with them in the temple. Paul agrees and enters the temple. But Jews from Asia recognize him, accuse him of bringing a Gentile (Trophimus the Ephesian) into the temple, and drag him out. A mob forms; they begin beating Paul. The Roman tribune intervenes with soldiers, arrests Paul in chains, and the crowd screams, "Away with him!" (21:36). The trajectory set in Acts 20 — Paul's declaration that bonds and afflictions await him, his farewell to the Ephesian elders, his insistence on going to Jerusalem despite every warning — reaches its immediate fulfillment in chapter 21. Everything Paul predicted about his own future begins to happen.

How chapter 20 connects these: Acts 19 narrated Paul's most extended and successful urban ministry — the word of the Lord reaching all of Asia through Ephesus, the power of Jesus's name vindicated over magical practice, and the gospel surviving the most violent civic opposition in the book. Acts 21 will narrate the beginning of Paul's suffering — the warnings, the arrest, the chains. Acts 20 is the transition between triumph and suffering, between the completed mission and the approaching passion. The chapter moves Paul physically from Ephesus through Macedonia and Greece to Troas and down the coast to Miletus, but the physical journey serves a deeper narrative purpose. In the farewell speech to the Ephesian elders at Miletus (20:17-38), Paul provides the retrospective lens through which the reader is to understand the entire Ephesian ministry: it was carried out "with all humility and with tears and with trials" (20:19); it held back nothing that was profitable (20:20); it proclaimed "repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus Christ" to both Jews and Greeks (20:21); and the community it produced is now entrusted to God and to the word of his grace, because Paul will not return and because threats — both external ("fierce wolves") and internal ("from among your own selves") — are coming (20:29-30). The chapter is Paul's testament. It links backward to the ministry described in chapters 18-19 and forward to the passion that begins in chapter 21. The author places it here so that the reader carries Paul's own words into the arrest narrative: the man being beaten in the temple is the man who said, "I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God" (20:24).

Verses 1-6: Paul's Journey Through Macedonia, Greece, and Back to Troas

"After the uproar ceased, Paul sent for the disciples, and after encouraging them, he said farewell and departed for Macedonia" (20:1).

The transition from the Ephesian riot is compressed. "After the uproar ceased" (meta de to pausasthai ton thorubon) — the noun thorubos means "uproar, tumult, disturbance, commotion." The cessation of the riot is the immediate trigger for Paul's departure. He does not flee during the riot; he departs after it ends. Before leaving, he "sent for the disciples" (metapempsarnenos tous mathetas) — the verb metapempo means "to send for, to summon." Paul gathers the believing community for a final meeting. He "encouraged" them (parakalesas) — the verb parakaleo, used throughout Acts for the strengthening and consolation of believers (11:23, 14:22, 15:32, 16:40), carries the force of both comfort and exhortation. The farewell is marked by the word apaspasamenos ("having said farewell, having embraced"), a word denoting physical and emotional leave-taking that will recur at the end of the chapter in the weeping farewell at Miletus (20:37).

The departure is toward Macedonia — the first leg of the journey Paul had resolved upon in 19:21: "to pass through Macedonia and Achaia and go to Jerusalem." The resolve formed in the Spirit during the Ephesian ministry is now being executed.

"When he had gone through those regions and had given them much encouragement, he came to Greece" (20:2).

The author compresses what was likely several months of travel through Macedonia — Philippi, Thessalonica, Berea, and possibly other communities — into a single sentence. The word paraklesis ("encouragement, exhortation, comfort") is the noun form of the verb used in verse 1. The pattern from 18:23 ("strengthening all the disciples") continues: Paul revisits existing communities and builds them up. The "regions" (ta mere ekeina — "those parts") are the Macedonian territories where communities were established in Acts 16-17. "He came to Greece" (elthen eis ten Hellada) — the author uses "Greece" (Hellas) rather than the Roman provincial name "Achaia," a rare usage in Acts. The destination is Corinth, where Paul had spent eighteen months (18:11) and where the community he planted continued under Apollos's strengthening (18:27-28).

"There he spent three months, and when a plot was made against him by the Jews as he was about to set sail for Syria, he decided to return through Macedonia" (20:3).

The three-month stay in Greece (likely Corinth) is the period during which many scholars place the composition of Paul's letter to the Romans, based on the convergence of details: Paul is about to depart for Jerusalem with the collection (Romans 15:25-26), he is in the region of Achaia (Romans 15:26), and he plans to go to Rome after Jerusalem (Romans 15:28; cf. Acts 19:21). The author of Acts does not mention the collection explicitly in this passage, though Paul's speech at Miletus will allude to the economic dimensions of his ministry (20:33-35), and the list of traveling companions in verse 4 includes representatives from the contributing churches — a detail consistent with Paul's own instructions that each church should send delegates with the gift (1 Corinthians 16:3-4).

A plot (epiboule — "a design against, a conspiracy") by the Jews forces a change of itinerary. Paul had intended to sail directly to Syria (likely to Antioch, and from there to Jerusalem), but the plot compels him to go overland through Macedonia instead. The word epiboule is used in Acts 9:24 for the plot of the Damascene Jews against Paul and in 23:30 for the plot against Paul in Jerusalem. The sea route to Syria would have placed Paul on a ship with Jewish pilgrims heading to Jerusalem for Passover (the timing is indicated by 20:6, which mentions the days of Unleavened Bread). Confined on a ship with those conspiring against him, Paul would have been vulnerable. The overland route through Macedonia, while longer, was safer.

"He was accompanied by Sopater the Berean, son of Pyrrhus, and by the Thessalonians Aristarchus and Secundus, and Gaius of Derbe, and Timothy, and the Asians Tychicus and Trophimus" (20:4).

The list of Paul's traveling companions is notable for its geographic breadth. Sopater (or Sosipater, cf. Romans 16:21) is from Berea. Aristarchus (already mentioned in 19:29, where he was seized during the Ephesian riot; later named as Paul's fellow prisoner in Colossians 4:10 and as a fellow worker in Philemon 24) and Secundus are from Thessalonica. Gaius is from Derbe in Galatia (distinct from the Gaius of 19:29, who was Macedonian). Timothy, Paul's long-standing companion since Acts 16:1-3, is from Lystra. Tychicus (mentioned in Ephesians 6:21, Colossians 4:7, 2 Timothy 4:12, Titus 3:12) and Trophimus (who will be the unwitting cause of the Jerusalem riot in 21:29) are from the province of Asia.

The geographic distribution is significant: Berea, Thessalonica, Derbe, Lystra, and Asia. These men represent the churches that have contributed to the collection for the Jerusalem saints. Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 16:3 specified that the churches should appoint representatives to accompany the gift to Jerusalem: "when I arrive, I will send those whom you accredit by letter to carry your gift to Jerusalem." The traveling party is a living demonstration of the Gentile churches' solidarity with the Jerusalem community — delegates from across the mission field, carrying the fruit of Gentile generosity to the mother church. Though the author of Acts does not explicitly name the collection here, the composition of the travel party makes it visible to any reader who knows the context.

"These went on ahead and were waiting for us at Troas" (20:5).

The pronoun shift is the most significant word in the verse. "Us" (hemin) marks the resumption of the "we" narrative — the first-person plural that indicates the author's own presence as a participant in the events described. The "we" sections in Acts (16:10-17, 20:5-15, 21:1-18, 27:1-28:16) are among the most debated features of the book. In whatever way the authorship question is resolved, the literary effect is clear: the narrator shifts from third-person report to first-person testimony. The reader is being told: I was there.

The "we" narrative last appeared in Philippi (16:10-17), where it began with the Macedonian vision and ended with the departure from Philippi after Paul and Silas's release from prison. It now resumes as Paul and the narrator leave Philippi together. The implication is that the narrator remained in Philippi during the intervening period — from Paul's departure in Acts 16 through the journeys of chapters 17-19 — and now rejoins Paul's traveling party.

"But we sailed away from Philippi after the days of Unleavened Bread, and in five days we came to them at Troas, where we stayed for seven days" (20:6).

The "days of Unleavened Bread" (tas hemeras ton azymon) is the seven-day festival immediately following Passover (Exodus 12:15-20, 23:15; Leviticus 23:6; Deuteronomy 16:1-8). Paul and the narrator observe the festival in Philippi before departing. This is consistent with Paul's continued observance of Jewish calendar and practice — the same pattern seen in his Nazirite vow at Cenchreae (18:18) and his haste to reach Jerusalem for Pentecost (20:16). The man who proclaimed freedom from the law for Gentile believers continues to live as a Jew within the rhythms of Israel's liturgical calendar.

The five-day crossing from Philippi (via its port Neapolis) to Troas is notably slow. The same voyage took only two days on the outbound journey (16:11). The difference is explained by prevailing winds: sailing eastward across the north Aegean against the prevailing northeasterly Etesian winds would be slower than the westward voyage with them. The detail is the kind of observation a participant would record.

Verses 7-12: The Gathering at Troas, Eutychus

"On the first day of the week, when we were gathered together to break bread, Paul talked with them, intending to depart on the next day, and he prolonged his speech until midnight" (20:7).

Several details require attention. "The first day of the week" (en de te mia ton sabbaton — literally "on the one of the sabbaths," using the Jewish method of counting days from the Sabbath) is the day after the Sabbath. This is the day the early community associated with the resurrection — "on the first day of the week" the women found the empty tomb (Luke 24:1; the same phrase, te mia ton sabbaton). Paul instructs the Corinthians: "On the first day of every week, each of you is to put something aside and store it up" (1 Corinthians 16:2). The author notes this temporal marker without commentary; he does not explain why the community gathers on this day. The practice is simply recorded as existing.

"When we were gathered together" (synegmenon hemon) — the first-person plural confirms the narrator's presence. "To break bread" (klasai arton) — this phrase in Acts carries communal and covenantal significance. At Pentecost, the believers "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (2:42), and "day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes, they received their food with glad and generous hearts" (2:46). The "breaking of bread" is the shared meal of the community, the practice Jesus instituted at the last supper: "he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me'" (Luke 22:19). The author of Luke-Acts uses the phrase consistently for this communal meal, and the reader of his two-volume work would hear the resonance.

Paul speaks (dielegeto — imperfect of dialegomai, "he was reasoning, discoursing, conversing" — the same verb used for his synagogue activity in 17:2, 17:17, 18:4, 18:19, 19:8, 19:9). The imperfect tense indicates sustained, ongoing conversation — not a single address but extended discourse and discussion. The departure planned for the next day gives the speech its urgency: Paul is leaving, and there is much to say. He extends his speaking until midnight (mechri mesonyktiou).

"There were many lamps in the upper room where we were gathered" (20:8).

The detail about the lamps (lampades hikanai — "a considerable number of lamps") in the upper room (to hyperoon — the same word used for the upper room in Jerusalem where the disciples prayed before Pentecost, 1:13) is not merely atmospheric. Oil lamps in a crowded, enclosed space produce heat, smoke, and diminished oxygen — conditions that contribute to drowsiness. The detail prepares the reader for what happens next and signals the narrator's presence as an eyewitness recording sensory details.

"And a young man named Eutychus, sitting in the window, sank into a deep sleep as Paul talked still longer. And being overcome by sleep, he fell down from the third story and was taken up dead" (20:9).

Eutychus (Eutychos — the name means "fortunate" or "lucky," a common name for slaves in the Greco-Roman world) is seated in the window (epi tes thyridos — the window-opening in the wall, which in a third-story room would open to a significant drop). The heat, the smoke, the late hour, and the length of the speech combine to produce deep sleep (kataphero — "to be borne down, to be weighed down, to sink"). The verb is used in an intensifying sequence: he was "sinking into a deep sleep" (katapheromenos hypno bathei) and then, "being overcome by sleep" (katanechtheis apo tou hypnou), he fell. The author specifies that he fell "from the third story" (apo tou tristegou) — a fall of considerable height.

"He was taken up dead" (erthe nekros). The word nekros means "dead" — not "as if dead," not "apparently dead," but dead. The author uses the same straightforward word he uses everywhere for death. The verb ero (airo, "to lift up, to take up") indicates the body was gathered from the ground.

"But Paul went down and bent over him, and taking him in his arms said, 'Do not be alarmed, for his life is in him'" (20:10).

Paul's action echoes two prophetic precedents. First, Elijah with the widow's son at Zarephath: "Then he stretched himself upon the child three times and cried to the Lord, 'O Lord my God, let this child's life come into him again'" (1 Kings 17:21, LXX: epekalesato ton kyrion kai eipen... epistrepsato de he psyche tou paidiou toutou eis auton). Second, Elisha with the Shunammite's son: "He went in and shut the door behind the two of them and prayed to the Lord. Then he went up and lay on the child, putting his mouth on his mouth, his eyes on his eyes, and his hands on his hands. And as he stretched himself upon him, the flesh of the child became warm" (2 Kings 4:33-34). The verb epepesen (from epipipto, "to fall upon, to embrace, to throw oneself upon") used of Paul is the same physical gesture of the prophet bodily covering the dead — the prophetic act of restoration through contact.

Paul's declaration — "Do not be alarmed, for his life is in him" (me thorubeisthe, he gar psyche autou en auto estin) — uses psyche ("life, soul, vital force") in the same sense as the Elijah narrative, where the child's psyche was restored to him (1 Kings 17:22, LXX: kai egeneto houtos kai aneboesen he psyche tou paidiou eis auton). Paul's statement that the young man's life "is in him" declares the restoration complete. The life has returned; the alarm is unnecessary.

The author records this event with the same spare economy he uses for other restorations to life in Acts: Peter and Tabitha (9:36-42), where Peter said "Tabitha, arise" and she opened her eyes and sat up. In both cases, the restoration is narrated without spectacle — the emphasis falls on the continuation of the community's life rather than on the miracle itself.

"And they took the youth away alive, and were not a little comforted" (20:12).

"They took him away alive" (egagon ton paida zonta) — the word pais ("boy, youth, young man") and the adjective zonta ("living") confirm the restoration. The community's response is comfort (parekletheesan ouk oligos — "they were comforted not a little," another litotes meaning "they were greatly comforted"). The comfort is not merely relief that a companion survived a fall; it is the comfort that the power of life operates in their midst. The verb parakaleo connects to Paul's own activity in verse 1 (encouraging the disciples) and verse 2 (giving much encouragement) — the same word that describes Paul's deliberate ministry of strengthening now describes the community's experience when death is reversed in their presence.

"And when Paul had gone up and had broken bread and eaten, he conversed with them a long while, until daybreak, and so departed" (20:11).

Paul resumes the gathering. "He went up" (anabas) — back to the upper room. "He broke bread and eaten" (klasas ton arton kai geusamenos) — the bread-breaking that was the purpose of the assembly (v. 7) now takes place, after midnight, after the fall and restoration. The meal is followed by further conversation (homilesas eph' hikanon — the verb homileo, "to converse, to talk with," and the phrase eph' hikanon, "for a considerable time") until daybreak (achri auges — augee means "daylight, dawn"). Paul has spoken from evening through midnight, restored Eutychus, broken bread, and talked until dawn. He then departs (exelthen — "he went out"). The all-night vigil has the character of a final farewell — the kind of extended, unhurried presence Paul can offer to a community he is leaving for the last time.

Verses 13-16: The Coastal Voyage from Troas to Miletus

"But going ahead to the ship, we set sail for Assos, intending to take Paul aboard there, for so he had arranged, intending himself to go by land" (20:13).

The companions travel by ship; Paul goes overland on foot to Assos. The distance from Troas to Assos by road is approximately thirty miles — a day's walk. Paul's choice to travel alone on foot after an all-night speaking session is unexplained by the author. The most natural reading is that Paul wanted time alone — time for reflection, prayer, or simply the solitude that the constant press of community and travel denied him. The detail is the kind of itinerary note a traveling companion would record.

"And when he met us at Assos, we took him on board and came to Mitylene" (20:14).

Assos was a port on the southern coast of the Troad, and Mitylene was the chief city of the island of Lesbos, directly south. The "we" narrative continues, placing the narrator on the ship.

"And sailing from there we came the following day opposite Chios; the next day we touched at Samos; and the day after that we came to Miletus" (20:15).

The itinerary traces a coastal route southward along the western shore of Asia Minor: Mitylene (Lesbos), then past Chios (another island), then to Samos (the island opposite Ephesus), and finally to Miletus, a city approximately thirty miles south of Ephesus on the coast. The daily stages are recorded with the precision of a ship's log.

"For Paul had decided to sail past Ephesus, so that he might not have to spend time in Asia, for he was hastening to be at Jerusalem, if possible, on the day of Pentecost" (20:16).

Paul deliberately bypasses Ephesus. The reason is time: he wants to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost (ten hemeran tes pentekostes). Pentecost (Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks) fell fifty days after Passover. Paul and the narrator left Philippi after the days of Unleavened Bread (v. 6); the clock is ticking. Stopping in Ephesus would consume time that Paul cannot spare — the relationships are too deep, the community too large, the issues too many for a brief visit. The verb espeudon (from speudo, "to hasten, to make haste, to be eager") conveys urgency.

The verb ekrine (from krino, "to decide, to judge, to determine") indicates a deliberate judgment, not a whim. Paul has calculated the available time and concluded that an Ephesus stop is incompatible with a Pentecost arrival. But the Ephesian community cannot be left without a farewell. The solution is to summon the elders to Miletus (v. 17) — a compromise that allows Paul to address the Ephesian leadership without entering the city.

The desire to reach Jerusalem for Pentecost continues the pattern of Paul's observance of Israel's festival calendar (cf. 18:18, 20:6). Pentecost was one of the three pilgrimage festivals (Deuteronomy 16:16) when Jews were expected to appear before the Lord. It was also the festival on which the Spirit was poured out (Acts 2:1) — the author's reader knows this. Paul's desire to be in Jerusalem for the festival that inaugurated the Spirit's coming to the community of believers adds a layer of meaning beyond mere calendar observance.

Verses 17-21: Paul's Address to the Ephesian Elders — Review of His Ministry

"Now from Miletus he sent to Ephesus and called the elders of the church to come to him" (20:17).

Paul summons the "elders" (tous presbyterous tes ekklesias). The word presbyteros ("elder") designates the leaders of the local community. Paul and Barnabas "appointed elders in every church" on the first journey (14:23). The Jerusalem church had elders alongside the apostles (11:30, 15:2, 4, 6, 22, 23; 16:4; 21:18). The elder is a recognized leadership role drawn from Jewish synagogue practice, where the elders (zekenim) constituted the governing body of the community. In verse 28, Paul will call these same men "overseers" (episkopous), indicating that the terms presbyteros and episkopos describe the same persons and the same function at this stage — those who shepherd the community.

The distance from Miletus to Ephesus is approximately thirty miles. A messenger sent to Ephesus would require a day to travel there; the elders would need a day to assemble and travel to Miletus. The delay of at least two days was a cost Paul was willing to pay — what he could not afford was an open-ended Ephesian visit, but a focused meeting with the leadership was essential.

"And when they came to him, he said to them: 'You yourselves know how I lived among you the whole time from the first day that I set foot in Asia, serving the Lord with all humility and with tears and with trials that happened to me through the plots of the Jews'" (20:18-19).

Paul's speech begins with an appeal to the elders' own knowledge: "You yourselves know" (hymeis epistasthe). The verb epistamai means "to know, to understand, to be acquainted with" — Paul is not telling them something new; he is invoking shared experience. They were present. They saw how he lived.

The phrase "the whole time from the first day" (ton panta chronon aph' hes hemeras protes) establishes the scope: the entirety of Paul's Ephesian ministry, from his arrival (19:1) through the riot and departure (19:41-20:1) — a period of over two years. The word "lived" is not in the Greek text; the phrase is literally "how I was with you the whole time" (pos meth' hymon ton panta chronon egenomen), emphasizing presence and manner of life rather than any specific activity.

Three terms characterize Paul's ministry. First, "serving the Lord" (douleuon to kyrio). The verb douleuo means "to serve as a slave, to be enslaved to, to render service." Paul describes his apostolic work not with the language of authority or leadership but with the language of slavery. He is a doulos — a slave of the Lord. This is the self-designation he uses in his letters (Romans 1:1: "Paul, a servant [doulos] of Christ Jesus"; Philippians 1:1: "Paul and Timothy, servants [douloi] of Christ Jesus"). The term echoes the LXX designation of Moses (Exodus 14:31, Numbers 12:7: doulos kyriou, "servant of the Lord"), Joshua (Joshua 24:29: doulos kyriou), and the prophets (Jeremiah 7:25: tous doulous mou tous prophetas, "my servants the prophets"). Paul places himself in the line of Israel's servants who were commissioned by God and whose service was rendered not to the community but to the Lord through the community.

Second, "with all humility" (meta pases tapeinophrosynes). The word tapeinophrosyne ("lowliness of mind, humility") is rare in pre-Christian Greek literature, where humility was generally not a virtue but a characteristic of the low-born. In the LXX, however, God's attention to the lowly is a persistent theme: "The Lord is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Psalm 34:18, LXX 33:19); "Though the Lord is high, he regards the lowly" (Psalm 138:6, LXX 137:6); Hannah's song celebrates God who "raises up the poor from the dust" (1 Samuel 2:8). Paul's claim of humility is not self-deprecation; it is a description of the posture from which he served — not from above, not as a lord over the community, but from below.

Third, "with tears and with trials" (kai dakryon kai peirasmoon). The tears are not rhetorical; they are the emotional cost of ministry. Paul mentions tears in his letters: "For I wrote to you out of much affliction and anguish of heart and with many tears" (2 Corinthians 2:4). The "trials" (peirasmoi — "tests, temptations, trials, adversities") are specified: they came "through the plots of the Jews" (en tois peirasmois tois symbasin moi en tais epiboulais ton Ioudaion). The word epiboule ("plot, conspiracy") is the same word used in verse 3 for the Jewish plot in Greece that forced the change of travel route. Paul's ministry in Ephesus was not a sequence of triumphs but a sustained engagement marked by emotional anguish and physical danger from those who opposed him.

"How I did not shrink from declaring to you anything that was profitable, and teaching you in public and from house to house" (20:20).

The verb hypostelloo (here in the form hypesteilamen, "I did not shrink back, I did not hold back, I did not conceal") means "to draw back, to withdraw, to shrink from, to conceal." It is a verb used in the LXX for cowardly withdrawal from battle (Habakkuk 2:4, LXX: ean hyposteiletai, "if he shrinks back"). Paul's claim is that he withheld nothing profitable — no aspect of the message was suppressed out of fear, desire to please, or calculation of audience preference. The word sympheron ("profitable, beneficial, advantageous") identifies the criterion: not what was popular or palatable but what was beneficial for the hearers.

The teaching occurred in two venues: "in public" (demosiai — in the open, in the public square, in gatherings accessible to all) and "from house to house" (kat' oikous — literally "according to houses," meaning in private household settings). The two venues correspond to the two phases of Paul's Ephesian ministry: the hall of Tyrannus (19:9-10), which was a public venue, and the household gatherings that would have constituted the community's regular meeting places. The combination indicates comprehensive coverage — Paul taught everywhere, to everyone, holding nothing back.

"Testifying both to Jews and to Greeks of repentance toward God and of faith in our Lord Jesus Christ" (20:21).

The content of Paul's testimony is compressed to two elements: "repentance toward God" (ten eis theon metanoian) and "faith in our Lord Jesus Christ" (kai pistin eis ton kyrion hemon Iesoun Christon). The pairing is not accidental. Repentance (metanoia — "a change of mind, a turning around, a reorientation of the whole person") is directed "toward God" (eis theon) — it is a turning back to the God of Israel, an acknowledgment that one has been going in the wrong direction. Faith (pistis — "trust, reliance, faithfulness, commitment") is directed "in our Lord Jesus Christ" — it is the specific identification of the Messiah through whom God's saving action is now operative.

The audience is comprehensive: "both Jews and Greeks" (Ioudaiois te kai Hellesin). The same pairing appears in 19:10 ("all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord, both Jews and Greeks") and throughout Paul's ministry. The message is the same to both groups. Both require repentance; both are offered faith in the same Lord. The distinction between Jew and Greek, which governs the order of presentation (to the Jew first, then to the Greek, as in the synagogue-first pattern), does not affect the content.

Verses 22-24: Paul's Resolve to Go to Jerusalem

"And now, behold, I am going to Jerusalem, constrained by the Spirit, not knowing what will happen to me there" (20:22).

The phrase "constrained by the Spirit" (dedemenosei to pneumati) is literally "bound in the Spirit" or "bound by the Spirit." The verb deo means "to bind, to tie, to fetter." Paul describes himself as a prisoner of the Spirit — not free to choose his own path, not at liberty to avoid Jerusalem, but bound and led toward it. The same tension between divine compulsion and human uncertainty that marked 19:21 ("Paul resolved in the Spirit") appears here in stronger form: Paul is bound, and he does not know what will happen. The Spirit compels him forward without revealing the outcome in detail.

"Except that the Holy Spirit testifies to me in every city that imprisonment and afflictions await me" (20:23).

The ignorance of verse 22 is qualified: Paul does not know the specifics, but the Spirit has been providing a consistent testimony. "In every city" (kata polin — "city by city") the Holy Spirit "testifies" (diamartyretai — from diamartyromai, "to testify solemnly, to witness thoroughly, to affirm with emphasis") that "bonds and afflictions" (desma kai thlipseis) await him. The word desma ("bonds, fetters, chains") will find its fulfillment in 21:33 (the tribune orders Paul bound with two chains) and throughout the imprisonment narrative. The word thlipseis ("afflictions, tribulations, pressures") is the word used throughout the LXX and early community for the sufferings of God's people (Psalm 34:19, LXX 33:20: "Many are the afflictions of the righteous"; Daniel 12:1: "a time of trouble such as never has been"). Paul is not ignorant of the general character of what lies ahead — suffering and imprisonment — but the specific form and sequence are unknown.

The Spirit's testimony in "every city" indicates that prophetic warnings have come repeatedly during the journey through Macedonia and Greece. Acts 21 will narrate two specific instances: the disciples in Tyre who "through the Spirit" urge Paul not to go to Jerusalem (21:4) and the prophet Agabus who dramatizes Paul's binding (21:10-11). But the pattern precedes those episodes — it has been a recurring feature of the entire journey.

"But I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God" (20:24).

This is the central declaration of the speech. Paul weighs his own life (psyche — "life, soul, self") against his mission and finds the life dispensable. The phrase "I do not account my life of any value" (ouden logon poioumai ten psychen timian emautoo — literally "I make my life of no account as precious to myself") subordinates survival to vocation. The word timian ("precious, valuable, honored") is negated: Paul does not treat his own life as something to be preserved at the cost of unfulfilled mission.

The mission is described with two phrases. First, "to finish my course" (teliosai ton dromon mou) — the word dromos ("course, race, track") is the language of athletic competition. In the Greek games familiar to every first-century audience, the dromos was the track on which runners competed. The verb teliosai (from telioo, "to bring to completion, to finish, to perfect") means reaching the end of the race. The metaphor implies that the course was set by someone other than the runner — the runner does not choose the track; he runs on the one laid out for him. Paul's course has been determined by the one who commissioned him.

Second, "the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus" (ten diakonian hen elabon para tou kyriou Iesou). The word diakonia ("service, ministry, commission") identifies Paul's work not as a self-chosen career but as a received assignment. The verb elabon ("I received") is the aorist of lambano — Paul received this ministry at a specific moment, from a specific person: the Lord Jesus. The content of the ministry is stated: "to testify to the gospel of the grace of God" (diamartyrasthai to euangelion tes charitos tou theou). The "gospel of the grace of God" (to euangelion tes charitos tou theou) is a phrase that defines the message by its central content: grace (charis — "gift, favor, unmerited kindness, the generosity of God"). The gospel is not news about human achievement or merit; it is news about God's grace. This phrase will be echoed in verse 32, where Paul commends the elders "to God and to the word of his grace."

Verses 25-27: Paul's Declaration That They Will See His Face No More

"And now, behold, I know that none of you among whom I have gone about proclaiming the kingdom will see my face again" (20:25).

Paul's declaration is absolute: "none of you" (pantes hymeis) "will see my face again" (ouketi opsesthe to prosopon mou). The content of his proclamation is identified again as "the kingdom" (ten basileian) — the same subject he preached in the Ephesian synagogue: "the kingdom of God" (19:8). This is the comprehensive term for the entire apostolic message that will close the book of Acts: Paul in Rome "proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance" (28:31). The declaration transforms the speech from encouragement to testament — Paul is speaking as a man who will not return. Every word that follows carries the weight of last words.

"Therefore I testify to you this day that I am innocent of the blood of all" (20:26).

The phrase "innocent of the blood of all" (katharos eimi apo tou haimatos panton) repeats the watchman declaration of 18:6 ("Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent"), but here it extends to "all" (panton) — not merely the Corinthian synagogue that rejected him but the entire scope of those he has addressed in Ephesus and Asia. The background is again Ezekiel's watchman (Ezekiel 33:1-9): the watchman who sounds the warning is free of blood-guilt; the watchman who fails to sound the warning bears the blood of those who perish. Paul has sounded the warning. He is katharos ("clean, pure, free of guilt"). The blood of any who perish through rejection of the message is not on Paul's hands.

"For I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (20:27).

This restates verse 20 with expanded scope. The verb hypesteilamen ("I did not shrink back") repeats, but the object changes from "anything profitable" (v. 20) to "the whole counsel of God" (pasan ten boulen tou theou). The word boule ("counsel, purpose, plan, intention, will") is the word used in Acts for God's determined plan: Herod and Pilate acted against Jesus but only did "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (4:28, boule is used); the Pharisee Gamaliel warned the council not to oppose what might be "of God" (5:39); and the same word describes God's purposes elsewhere in Acts (2:23, 13:36). Paul claims to have declared not merely selected truths but the entirety of God's determined purpose — the full plan, without omission or accommodation.

Verses 28-31: The Warning About Wolves and False Teachers

"Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood" (20:28).

This is the densest verse in the speech, and every phrase requires examination.

"Pay careful attention" (prosechete) — the verb prosecho means "to turn the mind toward, to give heed, to be attentive to, to watch." The imperative is urgent: be on guard. The attention is directed two ways: "to yourselves" (heautois) and "to all the flock" (panti to poimnio). The elders must watch their own lives first, then the community's. The order is not accidental: a leader who does not attend to himself cannot protect those in his charge.

"The flock" (to poimnion) is the word for a small flock of sheep. The pastoral metaphor pervades Israel's scriptures. God is the shepherd of Israel: "The Lord is my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1); "He will tend his flock like a shepherd" (Isaiah 40:11). Israel's leaders are described as shepherds — and frequently indicted for failing in their charge: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who have been feeding themselves! Should not shepherds feed the flock?" (Ezekiel 34:2). The prophet Zechariah warns of the "worthless shepherd who deserts the flock" (Zechariah 11:17). Jesus identified himself as "the good shepherd" who "lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11) and warned of hired hands who flee when the wolf comes (John 10:12-13). By calling the Ephesian community "the flock," Paul activates the entire scriptural tradition of God's people as sheep requiring faithful shepherds.

"In which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers" (en ho hymas to pneuma to hagion etheto episkopous). The word episkopous ("overseers, supervisors, guardians") is the word from which "bishop" later derives, but at this stage in the community's life, it describes the same persons Paul summoned as "elders" (presbyterous) in verse 17. The two terms identify the same role from different angles: presbyteros emphasizes the dignity of the office (the elder, the senior member); episkopos emphasizes the function (the one who watches over, the overseer, the guardian). The critical claim is that the Holy Spirit appointed them. Their authority does not derive from Paul's preference or the community's election alone but from the Spirit's action. The verb etheto (from tithemi, "to place, to set, to appoint") is the same verb used in 1 Corinthians 12:28: "God has appointed in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers." The elders' appointment is traced to divine initiative.

"To care for the church of God" (poimainein ten ekklesian tou theou). The verb poimaino means "to shepherd, to tend, to pasture, to feed and protect." It is the verb God uses in Ezekiel 34:15: "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (LXX: ego poimano ta probata mou). It is the verb the risen Jesus used in John 21:16: "Tend my sheep" (poimaine ta probata mou). The elders' task is stated in the most direct possible terms: they are to do what God does — shepherd the flock.

"The church of God" (ten ekklesian tou theou) — the community belongs to God, not to the elders, not to Paul, and not to itself. The genitive "of God" (tou theou) is possessive: it is God's church, God's assembly, God's gathered people. This is the same designation Paul uses in his letters (1 Corinthians 1:2, 10:32, 11:22, 15:9; 2 Corinthians 1:1; Galatians 1:13; 1 Thessalonians 2:14; 1 Timothy 3:5).

"Which he obtained with his own blood" (hen periepoiesato dia tou haimatos tou idiou). The verb peripoieo means "to acquire for oneself, to obtain, to preserve, to gain possession of." In the LXX, this verb is used for God's acquisition of Israel: "the Lord has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession" (Deuteronomy 14:2, LXX: exelexato se kyrios ho theos sou genesthai se auto laon periousion — using the related noun periousios). Isaiah 43:21: "The people whom I formed for myself" (laon mou hon periepoiesameen). The verb places the church in the same category as Israel — a people God has acquired for himself.

The phrase "with his own blood" (dia tou haimatos tou idiou) is startling. Blood (haima) in Israel's sacrificial system is the medium of atonement and covenant-making. "The life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you on the altar to make atonement for your lives, for it is the blood that makes atonement by the life" (Leviticus 17:11). Moses sealed the covenant by sprinkling blood on the people: "Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you" (Exodus 24:8). Jesus at the last supper declared: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). The church has been purchased not with silver or gold but with blood — the language of sacrificial cost, of covenant-making, of life poured out. The phrase "his own" (tou idiou) makes the blood personal to God — it is God's own blood, shed through the one in whom God acted, the Lord Jesus whose death is understood as God's own costly act of acquisition.

"I know that after my departure fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock" (20:29).

The warning shifts from past to future. "After my departure" (meta ten aphixin mou) — the noun aphixis means "departure, going away." Paul's absence will create a vulnerability. The threat is described with the most vivid image in the pastoral repertoire: "fierce wolves" (lykoi bareis — literally "heavy wolves, weighty wolves, grievous wolves"). The wolf is the natural predator of sheep. Jesus used the image: "Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves" (Matthew 7:15). He sent the disciples out "as sheep in the midst of wolves" (Matthew 10:16; Luke 10:3). Ezekiel condemned Israel's princes as "wolves tearing the prey, shedding blood, destroying lives to get dishonest gain" (Ezekiel 22:27). The wolves Paul describes will come "in among you" (eis hymas — they will enter the community from outside) and will "not spare the flock" (me pheidomenoi tou poimniou — from pheidomai, "to spare, to have compassion on, to treat gently"). The wolves are not merely present; they are destructive. They will not handle the community with care; they will tear it apart.

"And from among your own selves will arise men speaking twisted things, to draw away the disciples after them" (20:30).

The threat is not only external. "From among your own selves" (ex hymon auton) — from within the elder body itself, from the very men Paul is now addressing, distorted teaching will emerge. The phrase is emphatic: not from outsiders, not from pagans, not from persecutors, but from your own ranks. The men who arise will speak "twisted things" (diestrammena — from diastrepho, "to turn aside, to pervert, to distort, to twist"). The word is used in Acts 13:10 when Paul confronted Elymas: "will you not stop making crooked the straight paths of the Lord?" It describes not the invention of entirely new teaching but the distortion of existing truth — taking what is straight and bending it.

The purpose of the distortion is identified: "to draw away the disciples after them" (tou apospan tous mathetas opiso auton). The verb apospao means "to draw away, to drag away, to pull apart." The preposition opiso ("after, behind") indicates the direction: the disciples will be drawn away from the Lord and drawn after the false teachers. The distortion is not disinterested error; it is self-serving — it aims at creating a personal following. The false teachers want disciples for themselves, not for the Lord.

Paul's warning about wolves and internal distortion functions as the darker counterpart to his commendation of the community. The same community that has been built by years of faithful teaching is also the community most vulnerable to corruption when the faithful teacher departs. The elders who were appointed by the Holy Spirit to shepherd the flock include, in Paul's prophetic assessment, some who will themselves become the source of danger.

"Therefore be alert, remembering that for three years I did not cease night or day to admonish every one with tears" (20:31).

The verb gregoreo ("be alert, be watchful, stay awake") is the word Jesus used in Gethsemane: "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Matthew 26:41; Mark 14:38; Luke 22:46). The command to vigilance is grounded in Paul's own example: "for three years" (trietian — a round number covering the approximately two years and three months of the Ephesian ministry, or possibly including the time from Paul's initial brief visit in 18:19-21) Paul admonished them without ceasing (ouk epausamen — "I did not stop"), night and day (nykta kai hemeran — the order night-then-day follows the Jewish reckoning from evening to evening), and with tears (meta dakryon). The verb noutheteo (noutheton, "to admonish, to warn, to counsel, to instruct by correction") means not merely teaching but corrective instruction — warning against error, identifying danger, redirecting the mistaken. Paul's three-year ministry was not only proclamation and encouragement but sustained, emotionally costly, individual-by-individual correction. The phrase "every one" (hena hekaston — "each single one") insists on the individual scale: Paul admonished not the community in the aggregate but each person individually.

Verses 32-35: Commendation to God and the Word of His Grace

"And now I commend you to God and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up and to give you the inheritance among all those who are sanctified" (20:32).

Paul is leaving, and the community must continue without him. His final act is not the appointment of a successor or the establishment of an institutional structure but a commendation — he places them in the hands of another. The verb paratithemai (paratithemi, "to set before, to place beside, to entrust, to commend") is the word used for entrusting something valuable to someone's care. Jesus used it at his death: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46; Psalm 31:5, LXX 30:6). Paul commits the community to two realities: "God" (to theo) and "the word of his grace" (to logo tes charitos autou). The word of grace is not a separate entity from God; it is the medium through which God acts in the community. It is the same "gospel of the grace of God" Paul described in verse 24.

The word of grace has two capacities. First, it is "able to build you up" (to dynamenoo oikodomesai). The verb oikodomeo means "to build a house, to construct, to edify." The metaphor is architectural: the community is a building under construction, and the word of grace is the agent of construction. Paul uses this building metaphor extensively in his letters: "you are God's building" (1 Corinthians 3:9); "Let all things be done for building up" (1 Corinthians 14:26); "speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way... so that the body builds itself up in love" (Ephesians 4:15-16). The word does not merely inform; it constructs. It builds the community into a habitable structure.

Second, the word of grace is able "to give you the inheritance among all those who are sanctified" (dounai ten kleronomian en tois hegiasmenois pasin). The word kleronomia ("inheritance, allotted portion, heritage") is deeply rooted in Israel's story. The inheritance is the land God promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — the nahalah (Hebrew) or kleronomia (LXX) that was divided among the tribes (Joshua 13-21). The concept of inheritance saturates the Psalms and prophets: "The Lord is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my lot. The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places; indeed, I have a beautiful inheritance" (Psalm 16:5-6, LXX 15:5-6). In the apostolic proclamation, the inheritance has been redefined beyond land to encompass the full scope of what God gives to his people through the Messiah. "Those who are sanctified" (tois hegiasmenois — perfect passive participle of hagiazo, "to sanctify, to make holy, to set apart") identifies the recipients of the inheritance: those whom God has set apart for himself. The community of the sanctified is the people God has acquired with his own blood (v. 28), and their inheritance is the fullness of what God has prepared for them.

Paul does not commend the community to human leadership, to institutional arrangements, or to apostolic succession. He commends them to God and to God's word. The word of grace — the message about God's unmerited generosity shown in Jesus Christ — is the ongoing resource that will build the community and secure its inheritance after Paul's departure. The elders must be vigilant (v. 31), but the community's ultimate security rests not in their vigilance but in the word's power.

"I coveted no one's silver or gold or apparel" (20:33).

Paul shifts to the economic dimension of his ministry. The verb epethymesa (from epithymeo, "to desire, to covet, to long for, to set the heart upon") is the verb of the tenth commandment: "You shall not covet" (ouk epithymeseis — Exodus 20:17, LXX; Deuteronomy 5:21, LXX). By using this specific verb, Paul declares that he has not violated the commandment against covetousness in his dealings with the community. The three items — "silver, gold, apparel" (argyriou e chrysiou e himatismou) — represent the full spectrum of portable wealth in the ancient world. Clothing in particular was a significant form of wealth; garments were valuable, durable, and frequently listed alongside silver and gold as stored assets (cf. Joshua 7:21, where Achan confesses to stealing "a beautiful cloak from Shinar, and two hundred shekels of silver, and a bar of gold"; James 5:2: "your garments are moth-eaten").

This declaration is not gratuitous. The warning about false teachers who will "draw away the disciples after them" (v. 30) carries an implicit economic dimension — teachers who build personal followings often extract financial support from them. Paul's claim of economic disinterestedness distinguishes his ministry from the pattern he has just warned against.

"You yourselves know that these hands ministered to my necessities and to those who were with me" (20:34).

Paul holds up his hands — the hands of a tentmaker (18:3), the hands through which God performed miracles (19:11) — and declares that they supported not only himself but "those who were with me" (tois ousin met' emou). Paul's manual labor did not merely free him from dependence on the community; it generated enough surplus to support his co-workers. The appeal to eyewitness knowledge is again explicit: "You yourselves know" (autoi ginoskete). The elders saw Paul working with his hands. The claim is verifiable.

"In all things I have shown you that by working hard in this way we must help the weak and remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he himself said, 'It is more blessed to give than to receive'" (20:35).

Paul's final appeal grounds his example in a saying of Jesus: "It is more blessed to give than to receive" (makarion estin mallon didonai e lambanein). This saying does not appear in any of the four written Gospels. It is an agraphon — an unwritten saying of Jesus preserved in oral tradition and quoted by Paul as authoritative. The word makarion ("blessed, happy, fortunate") is the word that opens the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3-11; Luke 6:20-22). The saying compresses into a single statement the ethic that governed Paul's economic practice: the one who gives is in the position of greater blessing than the one who receives. Paul has modeled this by working with his hands to give to others rather than receiving from the community.

The verb hypedeixa (from hypodeiknymi, "to show by example, to demonstrate, to prove by doing") means Paul did not merely teach this principle; he lived it out before them as an example to be imitated. The word "weak" (ton asthenoumton — "those who are weak, those who are sick, those who lack strength") identifies the specific beneficiaries: not the community in general but its most vulnerable members. Paul's labor was directed toward those least able to provide for themselves.

Verses 36-38: The Farewell

"And when he had said these things, he knelt down and prayed with them all" (20:36).

The speech gives way to prayer. Paul kneels (theis ta gonata autou — "having placed his knees," a Semitic expression for kneeling). Kneeling for prayer, while not the only Jewish posture (standing was the normal posture in synagogue prayer), appears in the LXX for moments of particular intensity: Solomon knelt before the assembly at the temple dedication (1 Kings 8:54, LXX: apo ton gonaton autou, "from his knees"); Daniel knelt three times daily in prayer toward Jerusalem (Daniel 6:10). The kneeling here reflects the gravity of the moment — this is farewell prayer, not routine worship.

"And there was much weeping on the part of them all; they embraced Paul and kissed him" (20:37).

The physical expressions of grief are recorded without restraint. "Much weeping" (hikanos klauthmos — the word klauthmos means "weeping, wailing, lamentation," a stronger word than the quiet dakrya, "tears," that Paul mentioned of his own ministry in v. 31). The community weeps openly. They "embraced Paul" (epepeson epi ton trachelon tou Paulou — literally "fell upon the neck of Paul," the gesture of clinging embrace) and "kissed him" (katephiloun auton — from kataphileo, "to kiss repeatedly, to kiss fervently, to kiss with affection"). The verb kataphileo is intensified beyond the simple phileo; it denotes ardent, repeated kissing — the farewell embrace of those who believe they will never see this person again. The same verb is used of the father's greeting of the returning son in Jesus's parable: "his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20).

"Being sorrowful most of all because of the word he had spoken, that they would not see his face again. And they accompanied him to the ship" (20:38).

The source of the grief is identified with precision: it is not the general sadness of departure but the specific pain of the word Paul spoke — "that they would not see his face again" (hoti ouketi mellousi to prosopon autou theorein). The verb theorein ("to see, to behold, to gaze upon") means not a casual glance but sustained, deliberate seeing — they will not behold his face again. The finality is devastating. Every previous separation in Paul's ministry was temporary — he left Thessalonica but wrote letters and sent Timothy; he left Corinth but promised to return; he left Ephesus briefly in 18:21 with "I will return to you if God wills," and he did return. This departure has no return clause. The word is final.

"They accompanied him to the ship" (proepempon auton eis to ploion). The verb propempo ("to send forward, to escort, to accompany on the way") is the practical expression of love: they walked with him to the harbor, prolonging the contact until the last possible moment. The chapter ends not with theological argument but with a picture of grief-stricken community members standing at the waterside watching Paul's ship pull away.

The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 20 divides into four movements that together narrate the transition from Paul's active mission to his approaching passion, framed by a farewell speech that functions as both retrospective and prophecy.

The first movement (vv. 1-6) is itinerary: Paul travels from Ephesus through Macedonia to Greece, stays three months, and is forced by a Jewish plot to return overland through Macedonia rather than sailing directly to Syria. The list of traveling companions (v. 4) reveals the presence of delegates from churches across the mission field — representatives of the collection Paul is carrying to Jerusalem, though the author does not name the collection here. The resumption of the "we" narrative at verse 5 places the author in the traveling party for the first time since Philippi (16:17).

The second movement (vv. 7-12) narrates the gathering at Troas. The community assembles on the first day of the week to break bread. Paul speaks until midnight. Eutychus falls from a third-story window and is "taken up dead." Paul goes down, embraces him in the manner of Elijah and Elisha, and declares his life restored. The meal is eaten, conversation continues until dawn, and Paul departs. The episode demonstrates the power of life at work in the community even on the eve of Paul's departure — death intervenes but does not prevail. The restoration of Eutychus mirrors the broader pattern of Acts: the community encounters mortal threats and survives them through the power operative in those who serve the Lord.

The third movement (vv. 13-16) is coastal voyage: Paul walks alone to Assos, boards the ship, and sails south past Ephesus to Miletus. The decision to bypass Ephesus is practical — Paul is racing toward Jerusalem for Pentecost — but the result is the summoning of the Ephesian elders to Miletus for what becomes the most important speech in the second half of Acts.

The fourth movement (vv. 17-38) is the farewell speech to the Ephesian elders, the emotional and theological center of the chapter. The speech follows a structure common to farewell addresses in Israel's tradition. Moses delivered a farewell address before his death (Deuteronomy 31-33), reviewing his leadership, warning of future apostasy, and committing the people to God's care. Joshua's farewell (Joshua 23-24) rehearsed God's past faithfulness, warned against idolatry, and called for renewed covenant commitment. Samuel's farewell speech (1 Samuel 12) defended his integrity, reviewed Israel's history, and warned of the consequences of disobedience. Paul's speech follows this pattern precisely: he reviews his own conduct (vv. 18-21), warns of coming danger (vv. 29-30), defends his economic integrity (vv. 33-35), and commits the community to God (v. 32).

The speech operates on three temporal planes simultaneously. Looking backward, Paul summarizes the Ephesian ministry as one of humble service, tearful admonition, comprehensive teaching, and personal integrity — a ministry that held back nothing profitable and declared the whole counsel of God. Looking at the present moment, Paul identifies himself as a man bound by the Spirit toward suffering, with no expectation of return. Looking forward, he prophesies the coming of external predators ("fierce wolves") and internal corrupters ("from among your own selves"), and he commends the community to the only resource adequate to sustain it: God himself and the word of his grace.

The deepest thread running through the chapter is the question of what sustains a community of believers when the founding teacher departs. Paul's answer is not institutional — he does not establish a hierarchical structure, designate a successor, or create an authoritative office that perpetuates his own role. His answer is the word of grace: the community is commended "to God and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up and to give you the inheritance among all those who are sanctified" (v. 32). The word is not Paul; it is what Paul proclaimed. The word has its own power (dynamis) to build and to bestow inheritance. The elders are charged with vigilance (v. 28, 31), but the community's security rests finally in the word entrusted to it, not in the leaders appointed over it.

The chapter also establishes the terms in which the rest of Acts will unfold. Paul has declared that bonds and afflictions await him (v. 23), that he does not value his own life above completing his course (v. 24), and that he will not see the Ephesian elders' faces again (v. 25). When the arrest comes in chapter 21, when the trials stretch through chapters 22-26, when the shipwreck threatens in chapter 27, the reader already knows that Paul understood and accepted this trajectory. He was not caught by surprise. He was not a victim of circumstance. He was a man bound by the Spirit, running a course he received from the Lord Jesus, moving toward suffering he had been warned about in every city, carrying a message he described as "the gospel of the grace of God." The farewell at Miletus — the kneeling prayer, the weeping, the clinging embrace, the escort to the ship — is the last scene of Paul as a free man among the communities he built. Everything that follows is passion narrative.
