# Ephesians 6 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Ephesians 4-5):** The letter's second half began at 4:1 with the transitional oun ("therefore"), drawing ethical consequences from the theological exposition of chapters 1-3. The author urged the recipients to "walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (4:1), and the first implication was unity: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all (4:4-6). Christ's gifts to the community -- apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers -- serve the purpose of building up the body until it reaches maturity, "the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ" (4:13). The community is to grow up into Christ who is the head, with each part working properly so that the body builds itself up in love (4:15-16).

At 4:17 the author turned from corporate structure to personal conduct, commanding the recipients to "no longer walk as the gentiles walk, in the futility of their minds" (4:17). The old self (palaios anthropos), corrupted by deceitful desires, must be put off; the new self (kainos anthropos), created according to God in righteousness and holiness, must be put on (4:22-24). A series of concrete behavioral instructions followed: do not lie, do not let anger linger, do not steal but work, do not let corrupt speech come from your mouth, do not grieve the Holy Spirit with whom you were sealed for the day of redemption (4:25-30). Bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, slander, and malice must be put away; kindness, tenderheartedness, and mutual forgiveness must replace them -- "just as God in Christ forgave you" (4:31-32).

Chapter 5 opened with the command to "be imitators of God, as beloved children, and walk in love, just as Christ loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (5:1-2). The language of sacrifice echoes the LXX's descriptions of offerings that produce a pleasing aroma (osme euodias, cf. Genesis 8:21; Exodus 29:18; Leviticus 1:9, 13, 17). Christ's self-giving is the model for the community's conduct. The author then cataloged behaviors that must not characterize the community: sexual immorality, impurity, greed, filthy talk, foolish talk, crude joking (5:3-4). These are described as incompatible with inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God (5:5), and the author warned against deception on this point: "because of these things the wrath of God comes upon the sons of disobedience" (5:6) -- the same phrase (huioi tes apeitheias, "sons of disobedience") that appeared in 2:2 to describe those still under the ruler of the power of the air. The recipients are commanded: "do not become partners with them" (5:7).

The darkness-light contrast then dominated: "you were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light" (5:8). The fruit of the light consists in "all goodness and righteousness and truth" (5:9). Unfruitful works of darkness are to be exposed, not participated in (5:11-13). The community is to live wisely, redeeming the time because the days are evil (5:15-16), understanding the will of the Lord (5:17), being filled with the Spirit (5:18), speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in the heart to the Lord, giving thanks always for everything (5:19-20).

At 5:21, the author introduced a pivotal transition: hypotassomenoi allelois en phobo Christou -- "submitting to one another in the fear of Christ." This participle is grammatically linked to the preceding imperatives (be filled with the Spirit, speak, sing, give thanks, submit) and serves as the bridge to the household instructions that follow. The mutual submission "in the fear of Christ" governs everything that follows: it is the operating principle of the household code.

The household instructions began with wives and husbands (5:22-33). Wives are to submit to their own husbands "as to the Lord" (5:22), because the husband is the head of the wife "as Christ is the head of the church, his body, of which he is the Savior" (5:23). Husbands are to love their wives "just as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (5:25) -- the standard of love is Christ's self-sacrifice, not the exercise of authority. The author drew on Genesis 2:24 (LXX): "For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh" (5:31), and then declared: "This mystery is great -- but I am speaking with reference to Christ and the church" (5:32). The word mysterion here connects to the letter's central use of that term in 3:3-6: the mystery that was hidden and now revealed. The one-flesh union of husband and wife is a parable of the Christ-church union. The section concluded: "let each one of you love his own wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband" (5:33).

**What the author argues immediately after:** Ephesians 6 is the final chapter of the letter. There is no chapter 7 to follow. The chapter itself concludes with a personal note about Tychicus (6:21-22) and a benediction (6:23-24). The letter ends as it began -- with grace and peace -- but the final word is "grace be with all who love our Lord Jesus Christ with love incorruptible" (6:24). The phrase en aphtharsia ("in incorruptibility, with undying love") qualifies the love: it is imperishable, unfading, enduring.

**How chapter 6 connects these:** The household instructions that began at 5:22 continue without break into 6:1-9, completing the code with instructions to children and parents (6:1-4) and to slaves and masters (6:5-9). The governing principle remains the mutual submission of 5:21, now applied to every social stratum of the ancient household. Each pair is addressed in terms of their relationship to the Lord (kyrios): children obey "in the Lord" (6:1); fathers do not provoke but raise children "in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (6:4); slaves obey "as to Christ" (6:5) and serve "as to the Lord" (6:7); masters act knowing that "their Master and yours is in heaven" (6:9). The repeated reference to "the Lord" (kyrios) throughout the household code is not incidental decoration but the theological engine: the authority structures of the household are relativized and reframed by the lordship of Christ. No human authority is ultimate; all human relationships exist under Christ's sovereignty.

At 6:10, the letter shifts from the internal life of the household to the external struggle of the community. The author commands the recipients to "be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his might" (6:10) and to "put on the whole armor of God" (6:11). The struggle is identified: it is not against flesh and blood but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers of this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places (6:12). These "rulers and authorities" (archai kai exousiai) are the same powers named in 1:21, over which Christ has been exalted, and in 3:10, to whom God's manifold wisdom is displayed through the church. The armor of God passage (6:10-20) draws extensively on the LXX of Isaiah, where God himself is described as a warrior putting on armor (Isaiah 59:17) and where the messianic figure wears righteousness and faithfulness (Isaiah 11:5). The community is equipped with God's own armor to stand in the heavenly struggle that the letter has described since 1:3.

The chapter thus completes the letter's movement from theological declaration (chs. 1-3) through ethical instruction (chs. 4-6a) to spiritual combat (6:10-20). The community that has been created as God's temple (2:20-22), filled with God's fullness (3:19), and instructed to walk worthily (4:1) is now told to stand firm against the cosmic powers. The armor passage is not an appendix or afterthought; it is the culmination of the letter's argument. The community that exists "in the heavenly places" (1:3, 2:6) must contend with the forces that also inhabit "the heavenly places" (6:12). The resources for this struggle are drawn from everything the letter has described: truth, righteousness, the gospel of peace, faith, salvation, the Spirit's word, and prayer.

## Verses 1-4: Instructions to Children and Fathers

"Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right" (6:1).

The word tekna ("children") addresses actual children in the community -- young members of the households to which the letter is read aloud. The assumption that children are present and addressed directly is significant: they are not outside the assembly but within it, hearing the letter along with everyone else. The household of God (2:19, oikeioi tou theou) includes its youngest members.

The verb hypakouete ("obey," present active imperative of hypakouo, "to listen under, to hearken, to obey") is a compound: hypo ("under") + akouo ("to hear, to listen"). The word means literally to "hear under" -- to hear in a posture of responsiveness and compliance. The present imperative indicates ongoing, habitual action: keep on obeying. The object is tois goneusin hymon ("your parents") -- both father and mother, not the father alone. The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") locates the obedience within the sphere of Christ's lordship: the child's obedience to parents is an expression of obedience to the Lord. The phrase may also function as a qualifier: obey your parents in the Lord -- that is, insofar as that obedience is consistent with belonging to the Lord.

The justification is brief: touto gar estin dikaion -- "for this is right." The word dikaios ("right, just, righteous, fitting") in the LXX carries the weight of what accords with God's order. The rightness of children's obedience is not merely a social convention but a matter of justice -- it accords with the created order and the covenantal structure of family life.

"'Honor your father and mother' (which is the first commandment with a promise)" (6:2).

The author quotes the fifth commandment of the Decalogue: tima ton patera sou kai ten metera -- "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12 LXX; Deuteronomy 5:16 LXX). The verb timao ("to honor, to value, to esteem, to revere") is broader than hypakouo: obedience is the form that honor takes in childhood, but honor continues after the child has grown beyond the age of obedience. The commandment is lifelong; obedience is its childhood expression.

The parenthetical comment hetis estin entole prote en epangelia ("which is the first commandment with a promise") identifies a distinctive feature of this commandment. Within the Decalogue, this is the first commandment to which a specific promise is attached. The other commandments of the first table (concerning God) carry no explicit reward language. The fifth commandment is the hinge between duties toward God and duties toward other people, and it is the first to come with an attached blessing.

"'That it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land'" (6:3).

The promise is quoted from Deuteronomy 5:16 (LXX): hina eu soi genetai kai hina makrochronios gene epi tes ges -- "so that it may be well with you and so that you may be long-lived upon the earth." The original Deuteronomic promise specified "the land that the LORD your God is giving you" -- the land of Israel, the inheritance (kleronomia) promised to Abraham's descendants. The author of Ephesians modifies the text: instead of "the land that the LORD your God gives you," the text reads simply epi tes ges ("upon the earth" or "upon the land"). The specificity of the promised land has been universalized. This is consistent with the letter's argument: the inheritance that belonged to Israel has been opened to include the gentiles as co-heirs (3:6, synkleronoma). The promise of long life and well-being on the land is no longer restricted to the territory of Israel but extends to wherever the community of God's people exists. The transformation of "the land" into "the earth" mirrors the transformation of the ekklesia from a national assembly into a trans-ethnic community.

A first-century Jewish hearer would recognize this universalizing move as consistent with the trajectory already visible in the prophets. Isaiah 54:2-3: "Enlarge the place of your tent... for you will spread abroad to the right and to the left, and your offspring will possess the nations." The promise to the patriarchs was always moving toward universality (Genesis 12:3, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed").

"Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (6:4).

The address shifts to hoi pateres ("the fathers"). The verb parorgizete ("do not provoke to anger," present active imperative of parorgizo, "to provoke, to irritate, to exasperate") is a compound: para ("alongside, beyond") + orgizo ("to anger"). The negation me with the present imperative means "stop doing this" or "do not make a habit of this." The instruction assumes that fathers in the ancient world held enormous legal authority over their children -- the Roman patria potestas granted the father power of life and death over his household members. The author does not challenge the structure of paternal authority but constrains its exercise: authority is not a license to provoke, exasperate, or embitter. The word parorgismos ("provocation, anger") appeared in 4:26 ("do not let the sun go down on your parorgismo"), where the context was the management of anger within the community. The father who provokes his children to wrath contradicts the community's own ethic.

The positive counterpart is specified: alla ektrephe ("but bring up, but nourish, but rear"). The verb ektrepho ("to bring up, to nourish, to rear, to nurture") is a compound: ek ("out, forth") + trepho ("to feed, to nourish"). The word implies sustained, attentive nurturing -- the kind of care that grows a child from infancy to maturity. The content of this rearing is described with two words.

First: paideia ("discipline, training, correction, education"). In the LXX, paideia translates the Hebrew musar ("discipline, correction, instruction"). Proverbs is saturated with the concept: Proverbs 1:2 (LXX), "to know wisdom and paideia"; Proverbs 3:11, "My son, do not despise the paideia of the Lord"; Proverbs 22:6, "Train up a child in the way he should go." The word carries the sense of formative discipline -- not punitive harshness but the structured training that shapes character. In Hebraic usage, paideia includes correction, instruction, modeling, and the entire process by which a child is formed into a mature person.

Second: nouthesia ("instruction, admonition, warning, counsel"). The word is a compound: nous ("mind") + tithemi ("to place, to set"). Nouthesia is literally "mind-placing" -- the setting of understanding into another person's thinking. It combines instruction (teaching what is right) with admonition (warning against what is wrong). In 1 Corinthians 10:11, Paul used the verb noutheteo ("to admonish") for the purpose of Israel's historical examples: "these things happened to them as examples, and were written for our admonition (nouthesian)."

Both paideia and nouthesia are qualified by the genitive tou kyriou ("of the Lord"). The discipline and instruction are not the father's own invention but belong to the Lord. The father is a steward of the Lord's formative process, not an autonomous authority. The household hierarchy is, once again, subordinated to the Lord's authority: the father exercises authority, but the content of that authority -- what is taught, how discipline is administered, what values are transmitted -- comes from the Lord.

## Verses 5-9: Instructions to Slaves and Masters

"Slaves, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling, with a sincere heart, as you would obey Christ" (6:5).

The word douloi ("slaves, bondservants") addresses actual enslaved persons in the community. The institution of slavery pervaded the first-century Mediterranean world; the Roman economy depended on it. The author does not here address the morality of the institution itself but speaks to enslaved persons within the existing social order, instructing them on how to conduct themselves en kyrio -- within the sphere of the Lord's authority.

The phrase tois kyriois kata sarka ("your masters according to the flesh") is carefully worded. The word kyrios means both "master" (in the social sense) and "Lord" (in the theological sense). The author distinguishes between the two by adding kata sarka ("according to the flesh") -- these are masters in the fleshly, earthly realm, not the ultimate kyrios. The qualification implicitly limits their authority: they are lords kata sarka, but Christ is kyrios absolutely. The earthly master's authority is real but derivative and bounded.

The manner of obedience is described with two phrases. First: meta phobou kai tromou ("with fear and trembling"). This phrase appears in the LXX for the response of human beings before an overwhelming reality: Exodus 15:16 (LXX), "fear and trembling (phobos kai tromos) shall fall upon them" -- describing the nations' response to God's deliverance of Israel at the Red Sea. Paul used the identical phrase in 1 Corinthians 2:3 ("I was with you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling"), 2 Corinthians 7:15, and Philippians 2:12 ("work out your salvation with fear and trembling"). In Philippians 2:12, the fear and trembling attend the working out of salvation, not cowering before a human master. Here in Ephesians, the phrase is governed by the clause that follows: hos to Christo ("as to Christ"). The fear and trembling are not servile terror before the earthly master but the reverence that characterizes service rendered ultimately to Christ. The slave serves the human master, but the quality of the service -- its seriousness, its attentiveness, its depth -- derives from the fact that the slave serves as though serving Christ himself.

Second: en haploteti tes kardias hymon ("in the sincerity of your heart" or "in the singleness of your heart"). The word haplotes ("sincerity, simplicity, singleness, generosity") describes an undivided heart -- not a heart that calculates and performs but one that acts from genuine, uncomplicated intention. The kardia ("heart") is, as throughout the LXX, the center of the whole person -- will, intention, and character.

"Not by way of eye-service, as people-pleasers, but as slaves of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart" (6:6).

The word ophthalmodouleian ("eye-service, service performed only when being watched") is a compound apparently coined for this purpose (it appears only here and in Colossians 3:22 in extant Greek literature): ophthalmos ("eye") + douleia ("slavery, service"). It describes work done only under the master's gaze -- performance that vanishes when the supervisor turns away. The word anthropareskoi ("people-pleasers, those who seek to please humans") is similarly a compound: anthropos ("human being") + aresko ("to please"). The people-pleaser calibrates their behavior to human observation and human approval.

The contrast is sharp: alla hos douloi Christou ("but as slaves of Christ"). The enslaved person in the community has a dual identity: enslaved to a human master kata sarka, but a slave of Christ in the ultimate sense. The phrase douloi Christou ("slaves of Christ") is Paul's self-designation (Romans 1:1, Philippians 1:1, Galatians 1:10). The enslaved believer shares the same title as the apostle. The service they render is described as poiountes to thelema tou theou ek psyches ("doing the will of God from the soul"). The word thelema ("will, purpose, desire") is the same word used throughout the letter for God's will (1:1, 5, 9, 11; 5:17). The slave who obeys the earthly master is, when doing so with integrity, enacting God's will. The phrase ek psyches ("from the soul, from the whole being") parallels en haploteti tes kardias ("in sincerity of heart") from verse 5: the service comes from the deepest level of the person, not from the surface.

"Rendering service with a good will as to the Lord and not to man" (6:7).

The phrase met' eunoias douleuontes ("serving with good will") uses eunoia ("good will, eagerness, zeal, favorable disposition") -- a compound of eu ("well, good") + nous ("mind"). The word describes a disposition of positive engagement, not grudging compliance. The participle douleuontes ("serving, being a slave") is the verbal form of doulos ("slave"): the enslaved person is instructed to serve. The qualifier is again christological: hos to kyrio kai ouk anthropois -- "as to the Lord and not to human beings." The distinction between the earthly kyrios (kata sarka, v. 5) and the ultimate kyrios (Christ) is now made explicit in behavioral terms: the slave's posture of service is directed toward the Lord, even when the physical service is rendered to a human being.

"Knowing that whatever good anyone does, this he will receive back from the Lord, whether he is a slave or free" (6:8).

The participle eidotes ("knowing, being aware") introduces the motivational basis. The word hekastos ("each one, every individual") with ean ti ("whatever") poiese ("might do") agathon ("good") -- "whatever good each one does" -- is universalized: hoti touto komisetai para kyriou ("this he will receive back from the Lord"). The verb komizo ("to receive back, to recover, to be requited") in the middle voice means to receive what is due, to get back what one has earned or is owed. The word appears in 2 Corinthians 5:10: "so that each one may receive back (komise) the things done through the body, according to what he has done, whether good or bad." The Lord is the ultimate paymaster: whatever good the slave does will be repaid by the Lord himself.

The final phrase is striking: eite doulos eite eleutheros -- "whether slave or free." The social distinction between doulos and eleutheros is rendered irrelevant before the Lord's assessment. The Lord does not evaluate based on social status. The slave and the free person stand on the same footing before Christ's judgment seat. This affirmation does not abolish the social institution in the immediate context, but it fundamentally undermines its ultimacy: if the Lord treats slave and free identically, then the distinction is penultimate, not definitive. This is consistent with the letter's central argument about the elimination of divisions in Christ (2:14-16) and with Paul's declaration in Galatians 3:28: "there is neither slave nor free... for you are all one in Christ Jesus."

"Masters, do the same to them, and stop your threatening, knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him" (6:9).

The address to hoi kyrioi ("masters, lords") uses the same word that has been applied to Christ throughout the passage. The slaveowner is addressed with the word kyrios, but the point of the instruction is precisely that his kyrios-status is subordinate to the true kyrios in heaven.

The phrase ta auta poieite pros autous ("do the same things toward them") is remarkable in its economy. "The same things" -- the same sincerity of heart, the same good will, the same recognition that service is rendered to the Lord. The reciprocity is genuine: the master owes the slave the same quality of attitude that the slave owes the master. The hierarchical relationship is not abolished, but its moral content is equalized.

The specific prohibition is anientes ten apeilen ("giving up the threatening, stopping the threats"). The verb aniemi ("to loosen, to release, to give up, to cease") with the article ten apeilen ("the threatening") suggests that threatening was the default mode of management. The master is told to release, to let go of, the practice of intimidation. The participle is present tense, suggesting ongoing action: keep releasing this practice, keep abandoning it.

The motivational basis is the same as for the slaves, but now applied upward: eidotes hoti kai auton kai hymon ho kyrios estin en ouranois -- "knowing that both their Master and yours is in heaven." The word kai... kai ("both... and") makes the point unmistakable: the same kyrios is master of the slaves and master of the masters. The slaveowner who threatens his slaves threatens a fellow-slave of the heavenly kyrios. The phrase en ouranois ("in heaven") locates this ultimate Master in the realm from which all earthly authority derives (cf. 1:20-21, Christ seated at God's right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority).

The final clause: kai prosopolempsia ouk estin par' auto -- "and there is no partiality with him." The word prosopolempsia ("partiality, respect of persons, favoritism") is a compound: prosopon ("face, person") + lambano ("to take, to receive"). Literally, "face-taking" -- judging by the external appearance or social status of a person. In the LXX, the concept appears in Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God, mighty and awesome, who shows no partiality (hos ou thaumazei prosopon) and accepts no bribe." The impartiality of God is a fundamental attribute in Israel's scriptures (2 Chronicles 19:7; Job 34:19; cf. Acts 10:34, "God shows no partiality [prosopolemptes]"; Romans 2:11, "there is no prosopolempsia with God"). The master who favors himself over his slave is contradicting the character of the God he serves. The heavenly kyrios evaluates slave and master by the same standard.

The household code as a whole (5:22-6:9) is governed by a single logic: every human relationship is reconstituted by the lordship of Christ. Wives and husbands, children and parents, slaves and masters -- each pair operates under the authority of the kyrios who is above all. The remarkable feature is not that the code maintains existing social structures (which it largely does) but that it relativizes every position within those structures. No one is autonomous. No one exercises unaccountable authority. Every person in every role answers to the same Lord, and that Lord shows no favoritism.

## Verses 10-13: The Strength and the Struggle

"Finally, be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his might" (6:10).

The phrase tou loipou ("finally, henceforth, from now on, as for what remains") signals the transition to the letter's concluding section. The verb endynamousthe ("be strengthened, be empowered," present passive imperative of endynamoo, "to empower, to strengthen") is passive: the recipients are to be empowered by an external source, not to generate strength from within. The source is en kyrio ("in the Lord") and en to kratei tes ischyos autou ("in the strength of his might"). The words kratos ("might, dominion, prevailing strength") and ischus ("strength, force, power") appeared together in 1:19: kata ten energeian tou kratous tes ischyos autou ("according to the working of the might of his strength") -- the power that raised Christ from the dead. The same language that described resurrection power in 1:19 now describes the power available to the community in its struggle. The link is deliberate: the community fights with resurrection-caliber power.

The prayer of 3:16 asked that the recipients would be "strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner person" (krataiothenai dia tou pneumatos autou eis ton eso anthropon). The imperative of 6:10 calls for the realization of what 3:16 prayed for. The theological exposition has moved from prayer to command: what was interceded for is now required.

"Put on the whole armor of God, so that you may be able to stand against the schemes of the devil" (6:11).

The verb endysasthe ("put on, clothe yourselves with," aorist middle imperative of endyo, "to put on, to clothe oneself") echoes 4:24, where the recipients were told to "put on the new self" (endysasthai ton kainon anthropon). The clothing metaphor connects the two passages: putting on the new humanity (4:24) and putting on God's armor (6:11) are not separate acts but aspects of the same transformation. The new person wears God's armor. The phrase ten panoplian tou theou ("the whole armor of God, the full armor of God") uses a Greek military term: panoplia ("full armor, complete equipment") is a compound of pan ("all, every") + hoplon ("weapon, instrument of warfare"). The panoplia included every piece of equipment the soldier carried -- defensive and offensive. In the LXX, the word appears in Wisdom of Solomon 5:17: "He will take his zeal as his panoplia" (lemsetai panoplian ton zelon autou) -- where it is God who arms himself. The armor is tou theou ("of God") -- it belongs to God, originates from God, and is provided by God. The community does not fashion its own weapons; it wears God's equipment.

The purpose is stated: pros to dynasthai hymas stenai pros tas methodias tou diabolou -- "so that you may be able to stand against the schemes of the devil." The verb stenai ("to stand," aorist active infinitive of histemi) is the controlling verb of the passage: it appears again in verse 13 (stenaiantistenai, "to withstand... to stand") and verse 14 (stete, "stand"). The community is not instructed to advance, attack, or conquer; it is instructed to stand -- to hold the ground that has already been won by Christ. The word methodias ("schemes, stratagems, wiles, craftiness") is a compound: meta ("after") + hodos ("way, road") -- literally, "following a way after" someone, connoting the pursuit of a plan, the execution of a strategy, the laying of traps. In 4:14, the same word described the methodeia of error by which deceitful people manipulate immature believers: "tossed to and fro and carried about by every wind of teaching, by the cunning of people, by their craftiness in methodeia of error." The devil's methodiai are strategic, calculated, and deceptive -- not random but planned.

The word diabolos ("devil, slanderer, accuser") in the LXX translates the Hebrew satan ("adversary, accuser"). In Job 1-2 (LXX), the diabolos appears as the accuser who challenges Job's integrity before God. In Zechariah 3:1-2 (LXX), the diabolos stands at the right hand of Joshua the high priest to accuse him. In 1 Chronicles 21:1, satan (LXX: diabolos) incites David to number Israel. The diabolos in Ephesians is the personal adversary whose schemes the community must resist.

"For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places" (6:12).

The word pale ("wrestling, struggle, combat") refers to the ancient Greek athletic contest of wrestling -- close-quarters, hand-to-hand combat. The negation ouk... pros haima kai sarka ("not against blood and flesh") uses the Hebrew idiom reversed: the LXX and Hebrew scriptures use "flesh and blood" (Hebrew: basar wadam) for mortal, human existence (Sirach 14:18, "as green leaves on a thick tree, some fall and some grow, so is the generation of flesh and blood"). The struggle is explicitly not against human opponents. The human adversaries the community might face -- hostile neighbors, persecuting authorities, social pressures -- are not the real opponents. They are the surface phenomena; the underlying struggle is against non-human forces.

Four categories of adversary are named, each introduced by the preposition pros ("against"):

First: pros tas archas ("against the rulers, against the principalities"). The word arche ("rule, beginning, principality") appeared in 1:21: Christ is seated "far above all arche and authority." The same rulers over which Christ was exalted are the rulers against which the community struggles. The fact that Christ is above them (1:21) does not mean the community is exempt from encountering them; it means the community fights from a position of delegated authority, wearing the armor of the one who has already been enthroned above them.

Second: pros tas exousias ("against the authorities, against the powers"). The word exousia ("authority, power, jurisdiction") also appeared in 1:21 alongside arche. In 2:2, the "ruler of the authority (exousia) of the air" was described as the spirit at work in the sons of disobedience. The exousiai are the structured powers through which the diabolos exercises governance.

Third: pros tous kosmokratoras tou skotous toutou ("against the world-rulers of this darkness"). The word kosmokrator ("world-ruler, cosmic sovereign") is a compound: kosmos ("world, world-order") + krator ("ruler, sovereign"), from kratos ("strength, dominion"). The word appears in astrological and magical texts of the Greco-Roman world to designate planetary deities or spirits that control the fate of the cosmos. In Jewish usage, it designates spiritual powers that exercise dominion over the present age. The genitive tou skotous toutou ("of this darkness") identifies their domain: they rule over the darkness of the present age. The word skotos ("darkness") in the letter contrasts with phos ("light"): the recipients were once "darkness" but are now "light in the Lord" (5:8). The kosmokratores are the rulers of the domain from which the recipients have been transferred.

Fourth: pros ta pneumatika tes ponerias en tois epouraniois ("against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places"). The phrase ta pneumatika tes ponerias ("the spiritual things/forces of wickedness") uses the neuter plural pneumatika ("spiritual things, spiritual entities") with the genitive tes ponerias ("of wickedness, of evil") -- spirits characterized by wickedness. Their location is en tois epouraniois ("in the heavenly places") -- the same phrase that has been used throughout the letter: 1:3 (believers blessed in the heavenly places), 1:20 (Christ seated in the heavenly places), 2:6 (believers seated with Christ in the heavenly places), 3:10 (rulers and authorities in the heavenly places learning God's wisdom through the church). The heavenly places are not uniformly benign; they are also inhabited by hostile forces. The community that has been seated with Christ in the heavenly places (2:6) encounters opposition from forces that also occupy the heavenly places (6:12).

The four categories accumulate to emphasize the overwhelming nature of the opposition. The community does not face a single opponent but an organized hierarchy of cosmic powers. The repetition of pros ("against") four times creates a drumbeat of opposition. Yet the passage's overall thrust is not despair but confidence: the community is told to stand, not to flee, and the armor provided is God's own panoplia.

"Therefore take up the whole armor of God, that you may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand firm" (6:13).

The word dia touto ("therefore, on account of this") draws the conclusion from the identification of the adversaries in verse 12: because the struggle is against these cosmic powers, the full armor of God is necessary. The verb analabete ("take up, pick up," aorist active imperative of analambano, "to take up, to receive up") is the soldier's act of picking up equipment for battle. The aorist imperative suggests decisive action: take it up now, equip yourselves at once.

The purpose is repeated: hina dynethethe antistenai en te hemera te ponera -- "so that you may be able to resist in the evil day." The verb antistenai ("to resist, to withstand, to oppose, to stand against") is a compound: anti ("against") + histemi ("to stand"). The phrase he hemera he ponera ("the evil day") may refer to a specific eschatological moment of intensified conflict or to any day of crisis and assault. In the LXX, the concept of a day of testing, a day when evil presses in with particular force, appears in multiple forms: Psalm 41:1 (LXX 40:2), "blessed is the one who considers the poor; in the evil day (en hemera ponera) the Lord will deliver him"; Amos 6:3, "you who put far away the evil day (hemeran kaken)." The community must be prepared for the day when the full weight of the cosmic opposition bears down.

The final phrase: kai hapanta katergasamenoi stenai -- "and having accomplished all things, to stand." The verb katergazomai ("to accomplish, to carry out, to achieve, to work thoroughly") describes the completion of everything that needs to be done. The goal, stated for the third time, is stenai -- "to stand." The repetition is deliberate: the community's objective is not spectacular conquest but steadfast endurance. Stand. Do not fall. Do not yield ground. The ground has been won by Christ; the community's task is to maintain the position.

## Verses 14-17: The Components of God's Armor

"Stand therefore, having fastened on the belt of truth" (6:14a).

The imperative stete ("stand," aorist active imperative of histemi) is the fourth occurrence of the standing language (after stenai in v. 11, antistenai and stenai in v. 13). The positioning of the command at the head of the armor description makes it the governing instruction: everything that follows serves the objective of standing.

The first piece of armor: perizosamenoi ten osphyn hymon en aletheia -- "having girded your waist with truth." The verb perizonnymi ("to gird around, to fasten a belt, to cinch the waist") describes the soldier's act of securing the belt that held the tunic close to the body and supported the sword's scabbard. Without the belt, the tunic would hang loose and impede movement; the armor could not be properly mounted. The belt was the first piece of equipment put on, the foundation for everything else.

The content is en aletheia ("in truth, with truth"). The word aletheia ("truth, reality, truthfulness") has appeared in the letter at 1:13 ("the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation"), 4:15 ("speaking the truth in love"), 4:21 ("the truth is in Jesus"), 4:24 ("the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" -- en dikaiosyne kai hosioteti tes aletheias), 4:25 ("having put off falsehood, let each one speak the truth with his neighbor"), and 5:9 ("the fruit of the light is found in all goodness and righteousness and truth"). The author's own usage of aletheia in this document encompasses both the truth of the gospel message and the personal truthfulness required of community members. The belt of truth is the foundation on which all other armor rests: without truth -- both the reality of the gospel and the integrity of the person -- nothing else holds together.

The LXX background is Isaiah 11:5, which describes the messianic ruler: kai estai dikaiosyne ezosmenon ten osphyn autou kai aletheia eilemenos tas pleuras -- "righteousness shall be the belt of his waist, and faithfulness the belt around his side." The armor that God's community wears is the same armor that the messianic king wears. The community is clothed with the Messiah's own equipment.

"And having put on the breastplate of righteousness" (6:14b).

The second piece: endysamenoi ton thoraka tes dikaiosynes -- "having put on the breastplate of righteousness." The thorax ("breastplate, chest armor") protected the soldier's vital organs -- heart, lungs, and core. In the LXX, the thorax appears in 1 Samuel 17:5 for Goliath's armor and in 1 Kings 22:34 for the armor that protected (and ultimately failed to protect) King Ahab.

The definitive background is Isaiah 59:17 (LXX): kai enedysato dikaiosynen hos thoraka -- "and he put on righteousness as a breastplate." In Isaiah 59, the subject who puts on the breastplate is God himself. The prophet describes a scene of universal injustice: "justice is turned back, and righteousness stands far away; for truth has stumbled in the public squares, and uprightness cannot enter" (Isaiah 59:14). When God sees that there is no justice and no one to intervene, he arms himself for battle: "He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on his head; he put on garments of vengeance for clothing, and wrapped himself in zeal as a cloak" (Isaiah 59:17). In Isaiah's vision, God himself becomes the divine warrior who intervenes to establish justice when human agency has failed.

The author of Ephesians transfers God's own armor to God's community. The righteousness (dikaiosyne) that constitutes the breastplate is not the community's self-generated moral virtue but the righteousness of God -- the same righteousness described in 4:24 as the characteristic of the new self "created according to God in righteousness and holiness of the truth." The community wears God's righteousness as its protection.

"And, as shoes for your feet, having put on the readiness given by the gospel of peace" (6:15).

The third piece: hypodesamonoi tous podas en hetoimasia tou euangeliou tes eirenes -- "having shod your feet with the readiness of the gospel of peace." The verb hypodeo ("to bind under, to put on shoes, to shoe the feet") describes the soldier's footwear -- the heavy-soled sandals (caligae in Roman military terminology) that provided footing for standing firm on rough terrain and for marching.

The content is en hetoimasia ("in readiness, with preparation, with firm footing"). The word hetoimasia ("readiness, preparation, foundation, firm standing") appears in the LXX at Psalm 89:14 (LXX 88:15): dikaiosyne kai krima hetoimasia tou thronou sou -- "righteousness and justice are the foundation (hetoimasia) of your throne." In Psalm 89, hetoimasia means "firm base, established foundation." The community's firm footing is established by tou euangeliou tes eirenes ("the gospel of peace"). The word eirene ("peace") recalls the central argument of 2:14-17: Christ "is our peace," who "made both one," who "came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near." The gospel of peace is the message that the dividing wall has been broken, that the hostility has been killed, that access to the Father is shared by both groups in one Spirit. The community's firm standing in cosmic combat is grounded in the gospel that constitutes its identity: the message of reconciliation. The feet are shod not with aggression but with peace.

The background text is Isaiah 52:7 (LXX): hos hora epi ton oreon, hoi podes euangelizomenou akoen eirenes, hos euangelizomenos agatha -- "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the one who brings good news, who announces peace, who brings good news of good things." The feet equipped with the gospel of peace are the feet of Isaiah's herald who proclaims the good news of God's reign.

"In all circumstances take up the shield of faith, with which you can extinguish all the flaming darts of the evil one" (6:16).

The fourth piece: analabontes ton thyreon tes pisteos -- "having taken up the shield of faith." The word thyreos ("shield") designates not the small round shield (aspis) but the large rectangular shield (Latin: scutum) that covered the entire body. The word comes from thyra ("door"): the thyreos was door-shaped, large enough to crouch behind. The Roman scutum was typically about four feet tall and two and a half feet wide, made of wood covered with leather, and was the primary defensive weapon of the legionary.

The content is tes pisteos ("of faith"). The word pistis ("faith, trust, faithfulness, fidelity") has appeared at 1:13 (the recipients "believed" -- pisteusantes), 1:15 ("your faith in the Lord Jesus"), 2:8 ("by grace you have been saved through faith"), 3:12 ("boldness and access with confidence through faith in him"), 3:17 ("that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith"), and 4:5 ("one faith" in the list of seven unities), and 4:13 ("the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God"). Faith in this letter is the community's trust in what God has accomplished in Christ -- the reliance on the gospel as true and on God's power as operative. The shield of faith is the comprehensive protection that trust in God provides against every assault.

The function is stated: en ho dynesthe panta ta bele tou ponerou ta pepyromena sbesai -- "with which you will be able to extinguish all the flaming arrows of the evil one." The word bele ("arrows, darts, missiles") is the plural of belos ("missile, arrow"). The participle pepyromena ("having been set on fire, flaming," perfect passive participle of pyroo, "to set on fire, to burn") describes arrows tipped with pitch or other flammable material and ignited before being launched -- a standard military tactic in the ancient world. The Roman legionary's scutum was specifically designed to receive and extinguish fire-arrows: its leather covering was soaked in water before battle to prevent ignition. The parallel is precise: the shield of faith absorbs and extinguishes the incendiary attacks of the evil one. The word tou ponerou ("of the evil one") identifies the source: the attacks come from the poneros, the wicked one, the same adversary called diabolos in verse 11.

The word panta ("all") is important: faith does not extinguish some of the flaming arrows but all of them. The shield is comprehensive.

"And take the helmet of salvation" (6:17a).

The fifth piece: kai ten perikephalaian tou soteriou dexasthe -- "and receive the helmet of salvation." The word perikephalaia ("helmet") is a compound: peri ("around") + kephale ("head"). The helmet protects the head -- the seat of thought, perception, and decision.

The content is tou soteriou ("of salvation"). The word soterion ("salvation, deliverance, saving") in the LXX frequently refers to God's decisive act of rescue: Psalm 3:8 (LXX 3:9), "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (tou kyriou he soteria); Isaiah 12:2, "Behold, God is my salvation" (idou ho theos mou soter mou); Isaiah 52:10, "all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation (to soterion) of our God."

The background is again Isaiah 59:17 (LXX): kai perietheto perikephalaian soteriou epi tes kephales -- "and he placed a helmet of salvation upon his head." The same verse that provided the breastplate of righteousness provides the helmet of salvation. In Isaiah's vision, God himself wears this helmet as he goes forth to deliver his people. The community receives the helmet that belongs to the divine warrior. The salvation that protects the community's "head" -- its thinking, its perspective, its sense of identity -- is God's own saving act, the deliverance already accomplished in Christ (2:5, 8) and awaited in its fullness (1:14, the arrabon until the redemption of the possession; 4:30, "sealed for the day of redemption").

"And the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (6:17b).

The sixth and final piece: kai ten machairan tou pneumatos, ho estin rhema theou -- "and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God." The macharira ("sword, short sword, dagger") is the only offensive weapon in the list. Every other piece of armor is defensive: belt, breastplate, shoes, shield, helmet -- all serve the purpose of protection and standing firm. The sword alone is for striking.

The sword is tou pneumatos ("of the Spirit"). The genitive may indicate source (the sword provided by the Spirit), instrument (the sword wielded by the Spirit), or characterization (the sword that is spiritual in nature). The appositive clause ho estin rhema theou ("which is the word of God") identifies the sword: it is the rhema theou -- God's spoken word, God's utterance. The word rhema ("word, saying, utterance, spoken thing") differs from logos ("word, message, discourse") in emphasis: rhema tends to emphasize the specific, spoken instance of communication, while logos can denote the broader message or principle. The community's offensive weapon is God's own speech -- the specific, Spirit-empowered utterance of God's truth in the face of the adversary's assault.

In the LXX, the word of God functions as a weapon: Isaiah 49:2, "He made my mouth like a sharp sword" (etheke to stoma mou hosei machairan oxeian) -- the Servant of the LORD's mouth is God's weapon. Hosea 6:5, "I have hewn them by the prophets; I have slain them by the words of my mouth" (en tois logois stomatos mou). Wisdom of Solomon 18:15-16, describing the angel of death in Egypt: "Your all-powerful word leaped from heaven... carrying the sharp sword of your authentic command" (oxeian machairan ten anypokritonta epitagen sou pheronten). The word of God is not a passive text but an active force, a sharp weapon in the spiritual combat the community faces.

The entire armor sequence draws on two primary texts from Isaiah. Isaiah 11:5 provides the belt of truth/faithfulness worn by the messianic king. Isaiah 59:17 provides the breastplate of righteousness, the helmet of salvation, and the paradigm of God as warrior. The community is outfitted with the armor that belongs to God and to God's Messiah. The community does not fight in its own strength or with its own equipment; it stands dressed in divine armor, wielding the word of God as its only offensive weapon.

## Verses 18-20: Prayer as Combat

"Praying at all times in the Spirit, with all prayer and supplication. To that end, keep alert with all perseverance, making supplication for all the saints" (6:18).

The participle proseuchomenoi ("praying") is grammatically connected to the preceding imperatives (stand, take up, put on), making prayer not a separate activity but part of the armor itself -- or more precisely, the activity that activates and sustains the armor. The soldier fully armed but not praying is not yet ready for combat.

The phrase en panti kairo ("at every time, at every occasion, on every occasion") and en pneumati ("in the Spirit") locate the prayer temporally and spiritually: every moment, in the Spirit. The word pneuma ("Spirit") links back to "the sword of the Spirit" in verse 17 -- the same Spirit who provides the offensive weapon also powers the prayer that sustains the entire posture.

Four occurrences of pas ("all, every") in this verse create a fourfold emphasis on comprehensiveness: at all times (en panti kairo), with all prayer and supplication (dia pases proseuches kai deeseos), with all perseverance (en pase proskartereses), for all the saints (peri panton ton hagion). Nothing is left partial: every time, every kind of prayer, every measure of endurance, every member of the community. The spiritual combat is a community-wide, full-time engagement.

The word proskarteresis ("perseverance, persistence, steadfast endurance") appears only here in the New Testament. It is derived from the verb proskartereo ("to persist in, to be devoted to, to attend to constantly"), which appears in Acts 1:14 ("they were devoting themselves [proskarterountes] to prayer"), Acts 2:42 ("they devoted themselves [proskarterountes] to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship"), and Romans 12:12 ("be constant [proskarterountes] in prayer"). The word describes not a brief petition but sustained, attentive, unwavering engagement in prayer.

The intercession is peri panton ton hagion ("for all the saints, concerning all the holy ones"). The community prays not for itself alone but for the entire people of God. The word hagioi ("saints, holy ones") has been used throughout the letter for God's people as a whole: 1:1 (the recipients), 1:15 ("your love toward all the saints"), 1:18 ("the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints"), 2:19 ("fellow citizens with the saints"), 3:8 ("the least of all the saints"), 3:18 ("with all the saints"), 4:12 ("for the equipping of the saints"), 5:3 ("as is fitting among saints"). The prayer of the community in Ephesus (or wherever this letter is read) extends to every community of saints everywhere. The cosmic struggle is not fought by individual assemblies in isolation but by the universal community of God's people, connected in prayer.

"And also for me, that words may be given to me in opening my mouth boldly to make known the mystery of the gospel" (6:19).

The author requests prayer for himself: kai hyper emou ("and on behalf of me"). The petition is specific: hina moi dothe logos en anoixei tou stomatos mou -- "that utterance may be given to me in the opening of my mouth." The passive dothe ("may be given") indicates that the words come from God, not from the speaker's own resources. The word logos ("utterance, word, message") is the content to be spoken. The phrase en anoixei tou stomatos mou ("in the opening of my mouth") is a Semitism for speaking publicly and authoritatively (cf. Matthew 5:2, "he opened his mouth and taught them"; Acts 8:35, "Philip opened his mouth and beginning from this Scripture proclaimed to him the good news about Jesus"; Acts 10:34, "Peter opened his mouth and said").

The manner of speech is en parresia ("in boldness, with frankness, with openness, with freedom of speech"). The word parresia appeared in 3:12: "in whom we have boldness (parresian) and access with confidence through faith in him." The boldness that 3:12 described as the community's privilege before God is now the specific quality the author requests for his public proclamation. The content to be proclaimed is specified: gnorisai to mysterion tou euangeliou -- "to make known the mystery of the gospel." The word mysterion is the letter's central term: the mystery that the gentiles are co-heirs, co-members of the body, and co-sharers of the promise in Christ Jesus (3:6). The word euangelion ("gospel, good news") identifies the mystery as the content of the gospel proclamation. The mystery is not esoteric knowledge for initiates; it is the public message that constitutes the gospel itself.

"For which I am an ambassador in chains, that I may declare it boldly, as I ought to speak" (6:20).

The word presbevo ("I am an ambassador, I serve as an envoy") describes a diplomatic function: the presbeutes ("ambassador, envoy") represented a sovereign authority in a foreign territory. In 2 Corinthians 5:20, Paul used the same word: "we are ambassadors for Christ" (hyper Christou oun presbeuomen). The ambassador speaks not his own words but the words of the one who sent him. The author identifies himself as an ambassador for the mystery of the gospel -- a representative of God's hidden-now-revealed purpose.

The phrase en halysei ("in a chain, in chains") names the paradox: the ambassador is in chains. The word halysis ("chain, bond, fetter") identifies physical imprisonment. An ambassador in chains is a contradiction in terms -- ambassadors travel freely, bearing the authority of their sovereign; they are normally protected by diplomatic immunity. The author's chains do not nullify his ambassadorial status; they intensify the paradox and heighten the boldness required. The request is that he would speak en auto ("in it," referring to the mystery, or "in him," referring to Christ) hos dei me lalesai -- "as I ought to speak, as it is necessary for me to speak." The word dei ("it is necessary, one must") expresses divine obligation: speaking boldly is not optional but obligatory. The prayer request is for the ability to fulfill this obligation despite the chains.

The connection between the armor of God passage and the author's personal situation is organic. The community is instructed to stand against cosmic powers in the heavenly places (6:12); the author is himself standing against those powers in the form of his imprisonment. His chains are the physical expression of the cosmic conflict. The principalities and powers that operate behind the earthly authorities have bound the ambassador, but the ambassador requests prayer not for release but for boldness -- for the freedom to continue speaking the mystery even in chains. The cosmic battle of 6:10-17 is not abstract; it is embodied in the author's imprisonment.

## Verses 21-22: The Commendation of Tychicus

"So that you also may know how I am and what I am doing, Tychicus the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord will tell you everything" (6:21).

The phrase hina de eidete kai hymeis ("so that you also may know") shifts from theological instruction to personal communication. The words kai hymeis ("you also, you too") suggest that others already know the author's situation; now the recipients of this letter will also be informed.

The name Tychikos ("Tychicus") identifies a specific individual. Tychicus appears in Acts 20:4 as a companion of Paul from the province of Asia, and in Colossians 4:7-8 in virtually identical language: "Tychicus will tell you all about my activities. He is a beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow slave in the Lord." The near-verbatim parallel between Ephesians 6:21-22 and Colossians 4:7-8 is one of the most notable textual connections between the two letters.

Tychicus is described with two terms. First: ho agapetos adelphos ("the beloved brother"). The word adelphos ("brother") is the standard designation for a fellow member of the community -- not a biological sibling but a member of the same family of God (2:19, oikeioi tou theou, "members of God's household"). The adjective agapetos ("beloved, dear") indicates personal affection and esteem.

Second: pistos diakonos en kyrio ("faithful minister in the Lord" or "trustworthy servant in the Lord"). The word pistos ("faithful, trustworthy, reliable") describes his character. The word diakonos ("minister, servant, one who serves") is the same word used in 3:7 for Paul's own relationship to the gospel: "of which I was made a diakonos." Tychicus shares the same functional title as the author. The sphere of his service is en kyrio ("in the Lord") -- his ministry belongs to the Lord's sphere, consistent with the letter's pervasive use of this phrase.

The function of Tychicus is specified: panta gnorise hymin -- "he will make known to you all things." The verb gnorizo ("to make known, to reveal, to disclose") is the same verb used in 1:9 (God "making known" the mystery of his will), 3:3 ("the mystery was made known to me"), 3:5 (the mystery "has now been revealed"), 3:10 (God's manifold wisdom "made known" to the rulers and authorities), and 6:19 ("to make known the mystery of the gospel"). The word that has been used throughout the letter for God's cosmic revelation is now used for the mundane communication of Paul's circumstances. The parallel is unintentional grammatically but theologically suggestive: the same community that receives the disclosure of the cosmic mystery also receives the disclosure of the apostle's personal situation. Both belong to the same story.

"I have sent him to you for this very purpose, that you may know how we are, and that he may encourage your hearts" (6:22).

The purpose of Tychicus's mission is twofold. First: hina gnote ta peri hemon -- "so that you might know the things concerning us." The plural hemon ("us") broadens from the singular "me" in verse 21: Tychicus brings news not only about the author but about the wider circle of co-workers. Second: kai parakalese tas kardias hymon -- "and that he might encourage your hearts." The verb parakaleo ("to encourage, to comfort, to exhort, to urge alongside") is the standard word for pastoral encouragement (cf. 4:1, "I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, urge [parakalo] you to walk worthy of the calling"). The hearts (kardias) of the recipients -- the center of their persons, the seat of will and intention -- are to be strengthened by the news and by Tychicus's presence. The community's hearts, which Christ was prayed to dwell in permanently (3:17, "that Christ may dwell in your hearts"), are to be encouraged by the ambassador's faithful report from prison.

## Verses 23-24: The Benediction

"Peace be to the brothers, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (6:23).

The benediction opens with eirene tois adelphois -- "peace to the brothers." The word eirene ("peace") recalls the letter's central theme: Christ "is our peace" (2:14), he "made peace" (2:15), he "came and preached peace" (2:17), the recipients' feet are shod with "the readiness of the gospel of peace" (6:15). The peace wished upon the community in the benediction is the peace that the entire letter has been describing -- not mere absence of conflict but the reconciled, unified existence of the new humanity in Christ.

The word adelphois ("brothers") addresses the entire community as a family -- consistent with 2:19 (members of God's household). The addition kai agape meta pisteos ("and love with faith") pairs the two dispositions that characterize the community's life: agape ("love") -- the self-giving love modeled on Christ's love for the church (5:2, 25) -- and pistis ("faith") -- the trust in God's action that has been the instrument of salvation (2:8) and the shield against the evil one's attacks (6:16). The two are joined with meta ("with"): love accompanied by faith, or love together with faith. The community's internal life (love) and its orientation toward God (faith) are inseparable.

The source of these gifts is apo theou patros kai kyriou Iesou Christou -- "from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." The pairing echoes the letter's opening greeting (1:2, "grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ"). The letter ends where it began: with gifts flowing from the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

"Grace be with all who love our Lord Jesus Christ with love incorruptible" (6:24).

The final word of the letter is he charis meta panton ton agaponton ton kyrion hemon Iesoun Christon en aphtharsia -- "grace be with all who love our Lord Jesus Christ in incorruptibility" (or "with undying love"). The word charis ("grace") is the letter's foundational reality: "by grace you have been saved" (2:5, 8), "the riches of his grace" (1:7, 2:7), "the glory of his grace" (1:6), "the surpassing riches of his grace" (2:7), "the stewardship of God's grace" (3:2), "this grace was given" (3:8), "grace was given to each one of us" (4:7). Grace was the starting point, the operating principle, and now the final word.

The recipients of this grace are identified: panton ton agaponton ton kyrion hemon Iesoun Christon -- "all who love our Lord Jesus Christ." The present active participle agaponton ("loving, those who love") describes an ongoing disposition -- those who are characterized by love for the Lord. This is not a conditional statement that excludes some believers; it is a description of what believers are: people who love the Lord Jesus Christ.

The phrase en aphtharsia ("in incorruptibility, with imperishableness") qualifies either the grace (grace that is imperishable) or the love (love that is imperishable) or both. The word aphtharsia ("incorruptibility, immortality, imperishableness") is a compound: a- (negation) + phthora ("corruption, decay, destruction"). In Romans 2:7, those who seek "glory and honor and aphtharsia" receive eternal life. In 1 Corinthians 15:42, the resurrection body is raised "in aphtharsia." In 1 Corinthians 15:53-54, "this perishable must put on the imperishable (aphtharsia)." The word belongs to the vocabulary of resurrection and eternal life. The love for Christ is en aphtharsia -- undying, incorruptible, belonging to the age that does not decay. The letter that began with blessings "in the heavenly places" (1:3) and described a community seated with Christ in those heavenly places (2:6) ends with a grace that is imperishable and a love that does not corrupt.

## The Argument of the Chapter as a Whole

Ephesians 6 completes the letter's movement from theology to practice to spiritual combat. Three distinct but interconnected sections compose the chapter.

**The household code completed (6:1-9).** The instructions to children and parents (6:1-4) and to slaves and masters (6:5-9) continue the pattern established in 5:22 with wives and husbands. Each pair is addressed under the governing principle of 5:21: mutual submission in the fear of Christ. The repeated phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") or hos to Christo ("as to Christ") in every instruction demonstrates that the lordship of Christ reframes every human relationship. Children obey in the Lord; fathers discipline in the Lord's instruction; slaves serve as to Christ; masters treat slaves knowing the Lord in heaven shows no partiality. The effect is not the abolition of social structures but their radical relativization: every authority is derivative, every relationship is accountable, every person in every role answers to the same kyrios.

The most theologically charged element in this section is the instruction to masters in 6:9: "their Master and yours is in heaven, and there is no partiality with him." This statement, when combined with the identical treatment of slave and free in verse 8 ("whether slave or free"), places the institution of slavery under eschatological judgment. The heavenly Lord evaluates without reference to earthly social status. The master and the slave are equally douloi of the same kyrios. The hierarchical structure is maintained in the immediate social context but undermined in its theological foundations: if God shows no partiality, then the distinction between slave and free is not grounded in divine reality but in human convention. The letter does not issue a direct command to manumit, but it provides the theological premises from which such a command could logically follow.

**The armor of God and the cosmic struggle (6:10-20).** This section is the climax of the entire letter. The community that has been described as God's new creation (2:10), God's new humanity (2:15), God's temple (2:20-22), the demonstration of God's wisdom to cosmic powers (3:10), and the body of Christ growing into maturity (4:13-16) is now told what that identity requires in the face of opposition. The opposition is not human but cosmic: rulers, authorities, world-rulers of this darkness, spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places (6:12). The language of "heavenly places" (en tois epouraniois) connects this passage directly to every other use of the phrase in the letter: the community blessed in the heavenly places (1:3), Christ enthroned in the heavenly places (1:20), the community seated with Christ in the heavenly places (2:6), God's wisdom displayed to rulers and authorities in the heavenly places (3:10), and now the community's struggle against evil forces in the heavenly places (6:12). The heavenly places are not a single-toned realm of bliss but a contested space where God's purposes and the opposition's resistance intersect.

The armor is drawn from the LXX of Isaiah, primarily from two texts. Isaiah 59:17 provides the breastplate of righteousness and the helmet of salvation -- armor that God himself wears when he intervenes as divine warrior to establish justice. Isaiah 11:5 provides the belt of truth/faithfulness -- the equipment of the messianic king who judges with righteousness. The community is dressed in God's own armor and the Messiah's own equipment. The implication is profound: the community's combat is an extension of God's own combat against injustice and evil. The community does not fight its own war; it participates in God's war, wearing God's equipment, wielding God's word.

The controlling verb of the section is "stand" (histemi), which appears four times (vv. 11, 13 [twice], 14). The community is not called to attack, advance, or conquer; it is called to stand -- to hold the position that Christ has already won through his death, resurrection, and enthronement. The offensive element is limited to the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God (6:17), and to prayer (6:18-20). The community fights by speaking God's truth and by praying. These are not passive activities but aggressive ones -- yet they are aggressive in a mode that matches the community's identity: a community constituted by the word (1:13, "having heard the word of truth") maintains its position by wielding the word, and a community whose existence is grounded in God's gracious initiative (2:4-5) sustains its existence through prayer to the God who acts.

The author's prayer request (6:19-20) is not a personal aside but the concretization of the armor passage. The apostle in chains is the community's point of contact with the cosmic struggle: his imprisonment is the visible form of the invisible combat. He requests not rescue but boldness -- the parresia to speak the mystery even in chains. The mystery (mysterion) that has been the letter's central content -- the inclusion of the gentiles as full partners in Israel's inheritance (3:6) -- is the specific message the apostle must proclaim. The cosmic powers that oppose God's purposes are resisted not by physical force but by the continued proclamation of the message they seek to suppress.

**The benediction (6:23-24).** The letter closes with the words that have governed it throughout: peace, love, faith, and grace. The peace is the peace that Christ constitutes (2:14). The love is the love that Christ modeled (5:2, 25). The faith is the faith through which salvation was received (2:8). The grace is the grace that initiated the entire drama (1:6, 2:5, 8). The final phrase en aphtharsia ("in incorruptibility") points beyond the present struggle to the imperishable reality toward which the community moves. The community that stands in the evil day (6:13), wearing God's armor and wielding God's word, is sustained by a grace that does not decay and animated by a love that does not die.

## Observations on Pronoun Usage

The pronoun pattern in Ephesians 6 is consistent with the patterns established throughout the letter, though the distinctions between "you" and "we" are less prominent in this practical section.

The second-person "you" (hymeis, hymon, hymas) dominates the chapter: "obey your parents" (6:1), "honor your father and mother" (6:2), "that it may be well with you" (6:3), "your children" (6:4), "your earthly masters" (6:5), "your heart" (6:5), "be strong" (6:10), "put on the armor" (6:11), "you may be able to stand" (6:11, 13), "your waist" (6:14), "your feet" (6:15), "you will be able to extinguish" (6:16), "you also may know" (6:21), "your hearts" (6:22). The direct address to the recipients (predominantly gentile believers, as established in 2:11 and 3:1) continues throughout.

The first-person "I/me" appears in verses 19-22, where the author speaks of his own situation: "for me" (6:19), "I am an ambassador" (6:20), "I have sent" (6:22). The first-person plural "we" appears in 6:12 ("we do not wrestle") and 6:22 ("how we are"), where the author includes himself with the community in the shared struggle and situation.

The shift from "you" to "we" in verse 12 is significant: the cosmic struggle is not something the author instructs the community about from a position of detachment; he is in the same fight. The "we" of 6:12 includes the imprisoned apostle and the communities who receive his letter. The struggle against the cosmic powers is shared by the entire people of God, regardless of their specific social circumstances.

## Old Testament and LXX Resonances

The following Old Testament texts are active in the background of Ephesians 6:

- **Exodus 20:12 / Deuteronomy 5:16** ("Honor your father and your mother"): directly quoted in 6:2-3 as the basis for the instruction to children. The Deuteronomic form with its promise of long life and well-being is used, with the territorial specificity ("the land that the LORD your God gives you") universalized to "the earth."
- **Isaiah 59:17** ("He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on his head"): the primary source for the breastplate of righteousness (6:14b) and the helmet of salvation (6:17a). In Isaiah, God himself is the warrior who puts on this armor to intervene for justice when human agency fails. The author of Ephesians transfers God's own armor to the community.
- **Isaiah 11:5** ("Righteousness shall be the belt of his waist, and faithfulness the belt of his side"): the source for the belt of truth (6:14a). In Isaiah, this describes the messianic king who judges with righteousness. The community wears the Messiah's equipment.
- **Isaiah 52:7** ("How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the one who brings good news, who announces peace"): echoed in the footwear of the gospel of peace (6:15). The community's feet are shod with the same readiness that characterized Isaiah's herald of God's reign.
- **Isaiah 49:2** ("He made my mouth like a sharp sword"): background for the sword of the Spirit (6:17b). The Servant of the LORD's mouth is a weapon; the community wields the word of God as its offensive armament.
- **Deuteronomy 10:17** ("The LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords... who shows no partiality"): echoed in 6:9, where the heavenly Master is declared to show no prosopolempsia. God's impartiality is a foundational attribute in Israel's scriptures.
- **Genesis 2:24** (cited in 5:31): while belonging to the preceding chapter, its structural influence extends into 6:1-4, where the household code continues with the parent-child relationship that exists within the one-flesh household unit.
- **Psalm 89:14 (LXX 88:15)** ("Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne"): the word hetoimasia in 6:15 resonates with this psalm's use of the same word for the established foundation of God's throne.
- **Proverbs 1:2, 3:11, 22:6** (the paideia tradition): the instruction to fathers to raise children "in the paideia and nouthesia of the Lord" (6:4) draws on the entire Wisdom tradition's concept of formative discipline.
- **Job 1-2; Zechariah 3:1-2** (the satan/diabolos as accuser): the identification of the adversary as diabolos (6:11) and poneros (6:16) draws on the LXX's portrayal of the accusing, opposing spiritual figure.
- **Wisdom of Solomon 5:17** ("He will take his zeal as his full armor [panoplia]"): the concept of God's panoplia as complete divine armament, transferred to the community in 6:11, 13.
- **Daniel 10:13, 20** (angelic "princes" who govern nations): the cosmology of Ephesians 6:12, with its structured hierarchy of spiritual powers exercising dominion over territories and ages, reflects the worldview attested in Daniel and developed extensively in Second Temple Jewish literature (1 Enoch, Jubilees, Testament of Solomon).
