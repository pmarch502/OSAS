# Ephesians 4 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Ephesians 3):** Chapter 3 performed two functions within the letter's structure. First, in 3:1-13, the author explained his own role as the steward of the mystery. The mystery, disclosed "by revelation" (3:3), was stated with precision in 3:6: "the gentiles are co-heirs and co-members of the body and co-sharers of the promise in Christ Jesus through the gospel." Three syn- compounds -- synkleronoma, syssoma, symmetocha -- defined the mystery's content: the gentiles are not subordinate participants in Israel's inheritance but full, equal partners. The scope of this mystery was cosmic: "through the church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places" (3:10). The church -- the community of reconciled Jews and gentiles -- was itself the demonstration of God's wisdom to the cosmic powers. Paul's sufferings for this ministry were not the gentiles' shame but their glory (3:13).

Second, in 3:14-21, the author offered a prayer that the audience would be strengthened by the Spirit in the inner person (3:16), that Christ would dwell in their hearts through faith (3:17), that they would comprehend -- together with all the saints -- the immeasurable dimensions of Christ's love (3:18), and that they would be filled to all the fullness of God (3:19). The doxology that closed the chapter declared God able to do "far more abundantly than all that we ask or think, according to the power at work within us" (3:20), and ascribed glory to God "in the church and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, forever and ever" (3:21).

The structural position of chapters 1-3 within the letter is foundational. They constitute the indicative -- the declaration of what God has done: election and adoption (1:3-6), redemption and the disclosure of the mystery (1:7-10), the sealing of the Spirit (1:13-14), the making alive, raising, and seating of the dead (2:1-10), the breaking of the dividing wall and creation of one new humanity (2:11-22), the apostolic commission to proclaim the mystery (3:1-13), and the prayer for experiential comprehension of this new reality (3:14-21). Everything in chapters 1-3 describes what God has accomplished. Chapter 4 begins the imperative -- what the community must do in response.

**What the author argues immediately after (Ephesians 5):** Chapter 5 opens without a break in the ethical argument: "Therefore be imitators of God, as beloved children. And walk in love, as Christ loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (5:1-2). The verb peripateo ("to walk"), which has been the controlling metaphor since 4:1 ("walk worthy of the calling") and 4:17 ("no longer walk as the gentiles walk"), continues without interruption. The word agape ("love"), which concluded chapter 4 ("be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you," 4:32), now becomes the governing standard: "walk in love." The argument flows directly: the ethical life described in 4:25-32 is now grounded in the imitation of God's own character as displayed in Christ's self-giving sacrifice.

Chapter 5 then develops a series of contrasts that continue the pattern established in 4:17-32: light versus darkness (5:8-14), wisdom versus foolishness (5:15-17), drunkenness versus being filled with the Spirit (5:18). The instruction about being "filled with the Spirit" (5:18) connects backward to the prayer for Spirit-strengthening in 3:16 and to the command not to "grieve the Holy Spirit of God" in 4:30. The Spirit's indwelling, prayed for in chapter 3, is the basis for both the communal life described in chapter 4 and the ethical transformation described in chapter 5.

**How chapter 4 connects these:** Ephesians 4 is the hinge of the entire letter. The word oun ("therefore") in 4:1 signals the transition from theological exposition to ethical exhortation -- from what God has done to what the community must now do. The relationship is consequential: because God has accomplished what chapters 1-3 described, therefore the community must live as chapter 4 and following require. The chapter performs this transition in two movements. The first movement (4:1-16) addresses the unity and growth of the community: the seven unities that define the community's identity (4:4-6), the gifts Christ gives for the community's edification (4:7-11), and the growth toward maturity that results when these gifts function properly (4:12-16). The second movement (4:17-32) addresses the individual transformation required of community members: the abandonment of the former way of life (4:17-22), the renewal of the mind (4:23), the putting on of the new humanity (4:24), and specific behaviors that flow from this transformation (4:25-32). Both movements are grounded in the theology of chapters 1-3: the unity exhorted in 4:1-16 is the practical consequence of the "one new humanity" created in 2:15 and the "one body" of 2:16; the transformation exhorted in 4:17-32 is the practical consequence of being God's "workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (2:10).

## Verses 1-3: The Exhortation to Walk Worthily

"I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, urge you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (4:1).

The word oun ("therefore, consequently, accordingly") is the most consequential word in the letter's structure. It marks the transition from the entire theological argument of chapters 1-3 to the ethical instruction that will occupy chapters 4-6. The "therefore" gathers up everything the author has established -- the eternal election, the redemption by blood, the revelation of the mystery, the creation of one new humanity, the cosmic display of God's wisdom, the prayer for Spirit-empowered comprehension -- and draws a practical consequence. The ethical instruction is not freestanding moral advice; it is the entailment of a theological reality.

The self-identification ho desmios en kyrio ("the prisoner in the Lord") repeats the phrase from 3:1: ho desmios tou Christou Iesou ("the prisoner of Christ Jesus"). The exact repetition signals the resumption of the sentence that was interrupted in 3:1 by the long parenthetical explanation of 3:2-13. In 3:1, the author began: "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you, the gentiles--" and was diverted into explaining his stewardship of the mystery. The prayer of 3:14-21 concluded the theological section. Now, in 4:1, the prisoner speaks again, but instead of resuming the grammatically broken sentence, he begins the letter's new phase. The imprisonment is not incidental; it is cited as a credential. The one who exhorts the community to a worthy life is the one who has already paid a price for the gospel he preaches. His authority to call them to conduct rests on his demonstrated commitment.

The verb parakaleo ("I urge, I exhort, I entreat, I appeal") is Paul's standard verb for introducing ethical instruction. It appears in the same transitional function in Romans 12:1 ("I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice") and in 1 Thessalonians 4:1 ("we ask and urge you in the Lord Jesus, that as you received from us how you ought to walk and to please God"). The verb carries both authority and affection: it is not a command barked from above but an earnest appeal from a fellow participant in the calling.

The content of the exhortation is axios peripatesai tes kleseos hes eklethete ("to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called"). The verb peripateo ("to walk, to conduct one's life") is the metaphor for daily conduct that runs through the entire ethical section of the letter: 4:1 ("walk worthy"), 4:17 ("no longer walk as the gentiles walk"), 5:2 ("walk in love"), 5:8 ("walk as children of light"), 5:15 ("walk carefully"). The word is the Greek equivalent of the Hebrew halak, which pervades the Torah's descriptions of obedient and disobedient conduct (Genesis 5:22, "Enoch walked with God"; Deuteronomy 5:33, "you shall walk in all the way that the LORD your God has commanded you"). The same verb appeared in 2:2 ("you once walked following the course of this world") and 2:10 ("good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them"). The walk has been transformed: once it was in trespasses (2:2); now it is to be worthy of the calling.

The word axios ("worthily, in a manner worthy of, appropriately") describes conduct that corresponds to the reality it represents. The calling (klesis, "calling, summons, invitation") is the divine act by which the community was summoned into existence. The verb eklethete ("you were called," aorist passive of kaleo) is in the passive voice: the community did not call itself; it was called by God. The klesis in Ephesians encompasses the entire content of chapters 1-3: election before the foundation of the world (1:4), predestination to adoption (1:5), redemption (1:7), the revelation of the mystery (1:9), the sealing of the Spirit (1:13), the making alive (2:5), the creation of one new humanity (2:15), the reconciliation of both groups to God (2:16). To walk worthy of this calling is to conduct oneself in a way that corresponds to, reflects, and does not contradict all of this.

"With all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love" (4:2).

The author now specifies the qualities that characterize the worthy walk. Four virtues are named, and they are relational -- they describe how members of the community treat one another, not interior spiritual states divorced from social practice.

First: meta pases tapeinophrosynes ("with all humility"). The word tapeinophrosyne ("humility, lowliness of mind, modesty") is a compound: tapeinos ("low, humble, lowly") + phren ("mind, disposition"). In the Greco-Roman world, tapeinophrosyne was not considered a virtue but a vice -- the disposition of a slave, not of a free person. Aristotle's ideal was megalopsychia ("greatness of soul"), the proud self-regard of the virtuous man (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3). The valuation of humility as a virtue is distinctively rooted in Israel's prophetic tradition and in the teaching of Jesus. The LXX uses tapeinos for the poor and lowly whom God favors: Psalm 18:27 (LXX 17:28), "you save a humble people (laon tapeinon)"; Psalm 34:18 (LXX 33:19), "the LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit"; Isaiah 57:15, "I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (tapeinou). Proverbs 3:34 (LXX), "toward the scorners he is scornful, but to the humble (tapeinois) he gives grace" -- a text quoted in James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5. The word pases ("all") intensifies: not partial or selective humility but complete humility, humility in every dimension.

Second: kai prautetos ("and gentleness"). The word prautes ("gentleness, meekness, mildness, forbearance") describes strength under control -- not weakness but restrained power. In the LXX, Moses is described as praus: "Now the man Moses was very meek (praus), more than all people who were on the face of the earth" (Numbers 12:3, LXX). Jesus applied the word to himself: "I am gentle (praus) and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29). The beatitudes promise: "Blessed are the meek (praeis), for they shall inherit the earth" (Matthew 5:5, echoing Psalm 37:11 LXX [36:11], "the meek [praeis] shall inherit the land").

Third: meta makrothymias ("with patience, with long-suffering"). The word makrothymia ("patience, long-suffering, slowness to anger, endurance") is a compound: makros ("long, far") + thymos ("passion, anger, heat"). It describes the disposition that is slow to become angry, that endures provocation without retaliation. In the LXX, makrothymia is an attribute of God himself: Exodus 34:6 (LXX), "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger (makrothymos) and abounding in steadfast love." The community is being asked to embody toward one another the quality that God displays toward them.

Fourth: anechomenoi allelon en agape ("bearing with one another in love"). The verb anechomai ("to bear with, to endure, to tolerate, to put up with") implies that the community will encounter in one another things that are difficult to endure -- irritations, failures, offenses, weaknesses. The word allelon ("one another") is reciprocal: everyone bears with everyone. No one is exempt from needing to be borne with, and no one is exempt from the obligation to bear with others. The sphere of this mutual forbearance is en agape ("in love") -- love is the environment in which bearing with one another becomes possible and meaningful rather than resentful.

The qualities listed -- humility, gentleness, patience, bearing with one another in love -- are not random virtues. They are the specific dispositions required for the maintenance of a community composed of formerly hostile groups. A community of Jews and gentiles, with different customs, different histories, different food practices, different cultural assumptions, and a history of mutual contempt, would require exactly these qualities to hold together. The virtues are tailored to the letter's situation.

"Eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (4:3).

The verb spoudazontes ("being eager, being diligent, making every effort") is a present active participle: the eagerness is to be continuous and active. The word spoude ("eagerness, diligence, haste, earnestness") implies that maintaining unity requires effort -- it will not happen passively or automatically.

The object of the eagerness is terein ten henoteta tou pneumatos ("to maintain the unity of the Spirit" or "to keep the oneness that the Spirit produces"). The verb tereo ("to keep, to guard, to maintain, to preserve") implies that the unity already exists and must be guarded, not that it must be created from scratch. The unity is a divine accomplishment (the Spirit produced it); the community's task is preservation, not invention. The word henotes ("unity, oneness") appears only here and in 4:13 in the entire New Testament, making it a distinctive term in this letter's vocabulary. The genitive tou pneumatos ("of the Spirit") identifies the Spirit as the source or author of this unity: it is the Spirit's unity -- the oneness that the Spirit has created by incorporating both Jews and gentiles into one body (cf. 1 Corinthians 12:13, "in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body -- Jews or Greeks, slaves or free -- and all were made to drink of one Spirit"). This is the Spirit that was prayed for in 3:16, that sealed the gentile believers in 1:13, that gives both groups access to the Father in 2:18, and in whom the community is being built into God's dwelling (2:22).

The instrument of maintenance is en to syndesmo tes eirenes ("in the bond of peace"). The word syndesmos ("bond, ligament, that which binds together") is a compound: syn ("together") + desmos ("bond, chain, fetter"). In Colossians 3:14, love is called "the bond of perfection" (syndesmos tes teleiotetos). Here, peace is the binding agent. The word eirene ("peace") connects to 2:14 ("he himself is our peace"), 2:15 ("making peace"), and 2:17 ("he came and preached peace"). The peace that Christ created by breaking down the dividing wall (2:14) is now the ligament that holds the community together. Peace is not merely a feeling of tranquility; it is the structural bond of the community's unity.

## Verses 4-6: The Seven Unities

"There is one body and one Spirit -- just as you were called to the one hope that belongs to your call" (4:4).

The author now enumerates seven expressions of the community's oneness. The number seven, significant in Jewish thought as the number of completeness (Genesis 2:2, the completion of creation), may be deliberate: the seven unities describe the complete unity of the community. The enumeration moves from the communal (body, Spirit, hope) through the christological (Lord, faith, baptism) to the theological (God and Father), forming a trinitarian structure: Spirit (v. 4), Lord/Christ (v. 5), God and Father (v. 6).

The first unity: hen soma ("one body"). The word soma ("body") has been established as a key term in the letter. In 1:23, the ekklesia was identified as Christ's body: "which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all." In 2:16, Christ reconciled both groups "to God in one body through the cross." In 3:6, the gentiles were declared syssoma ("co-members of the same body"). The "one body" is the community of reconciled Jews and gentiles -- the single corporate entity that Christ created by abolishing the dividing wall. The oneness of the body is not an aspiration but a fact: Christ created one body, not two. The ethical exhortation to unity rests on the ontological reality that there is only one body.

The second unity: hen pneuma ("one Spirit"). The Spirit has been present throughout the letter: 1:13-14 (the Spirit sealed the gentile believers as the guarantee of the inheritance), 1:17 (Paul prayed for a spirit of wisdom and revelation), 2:18 (both groups have access to the Father in one Spirit), 2:22 (the community is God's dwelling in the Spirit), 3:5 (the mystery was revealed by the Spirit), 3:16 (the prayer for strengthening through the Spirit in the inner person). The one Spirit is the agent who creates, sustains, and indwells the one body. There is not a Jewish Spirit and a gentile Spirit; there is one Spirit who incorporates both groups into one reality.

The third unity: kathos kai eklethete en mia elpidi tes kleseos hymon ("just as you were also called in one hope of your calling"). The word elpis ("hope") connects to 1:18, where Paul prayed that the recipients would know "the hope of his calling" (ten elpida tes kleseos autou), and to 2:12, where the gentiles were described as "having no hope" (elpida me echontes). The gentiles who were formerly without hope now share in the one hope to which they were called. The hope is singular -- there is not one hope for Jews and another for gentiles. The content of the hope was described in 1:14 as the full "redemption of the possession" -- the eschatological completion of what the Spirit has guaranteed. The word klesis ("calling") repeats from 4:1, tying the hope to the calling that the community is to walk worthy of.

"One Lord, one faith, one baptism" (4:5).

The fourth unity: heis kyrios ("one Lord"). The word kyrios ("Lord, master, sovereign") is the title applied to Christ throughout the letter: 1:2-3, 15, 17; 2:21; 3:11, 14. In the LXX, kyrios is the standard rendering of the divine name YHWH (the tetragrammaton). The confession "one Lord" echoes the Shema -- Deuteronomy 6:4 (LXX): "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (kyrios ho theos hemon kyrios heis estin). The Shema was the foundational confession of Israel's faith. Paul applied the Shema's oneness language to Christ in 1 Corinthians 8:6: "yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist." The "one Lord" here in Ephesians 4:5 claims for Christ the oneness that the Shema attributed to YHWH. There is one Lord over the one body -- not separate lords for separate ethnic groups.

The fifth unity: mia pistis ("one faith"). The word pistis ("faith, trust, faithfulness, fidelity") may refer to the subjective act of believing (the one response of trust that all members share) or to the objective content believed (the one body of apostolic teaching that defines the community). In the context of a letter addressing the unity of Jews and gentiles, both senses are operative: there is one faith that unites both groups -- both the act of trusting in the same Lord and the content of what is trusted (the gospel that Paul described as his stewardship in 3:2-7). The faith by which the gentiles heard and believed (1:13) is the same faith that characterizes the entire community.

The sixth unity: hen baptisma ("one baptism"). The word baptisma ("baptism, immersion, washing") refers to the rite of water baptism that marked entry into the community. The "one baptism" parallels the "one Spirit" in function: as the Spirit is the invisible agent of incorporation into the one body, baptism is the visible sign of that incorporation. The oneness of the baptism means that there is one entry point for all: Jew and gentile, slave and free, male and female (cf. Galatians 3:27-28, "For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ. There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus"). The rite is the same for all; there is no separate or lesser baptism for any group.

"One God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all" (4:6).

The seventh unity: heis theos kai pater panton ("one God and Father of all"). The enumeration reaches its summit in the one God who is the source of everything. The word pater ("Father") connects to 1:2-3 (God as Father of our Lord Jesus Christ), 2:18 (access to the Father through the Son in the Spirit), 3:14-15 (the Father from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named). The genitive panton ("of all") is comprehensive: God is Father of all members of the community, and indeed of all that exists. This echoes the Shema tradition again: the one God of Israel is now confessed as Father of all -- the God who was once perceived as belonging exclusively to Israel is now recognized as Father of the entire community.

Three prepositional phrases describe God's relationship to "all": ho epi panton ("the one who is over all"), kai dia panton ("and through all"), kai en pasin ("and in all"). The preposition epi ("over") describes sovereignty and authority. The preposition dia ("through") describes active agency and mediation. The preposition en ("in") describes intimate indwelling and presence. Together they describe a God who is transcendent (over all), operative (through all), and immanent (in all). The scope of "all" (panton, pasin) may refer to all members of the community or to all things in the created order. Given the letter's cosmic scope (1:10, God's plan to unite all things in Christ; 1:22, all things under Christ's feet; 3:9, God who created all things), the broader reference is likely primary, though the immediate application is to the community: God is over, through, and in every member of the body, which is why the body is one.

The seven unities form a creedal summary that functions as the theological ground for the exhortation to unity. The community does not create unity; it maintains what already exists. The unity is not a human achievement but a divine reality: one body created by Christ (2:15-16), one Spirit who incorporates all (2:18), one hope to which all are called (1:18), one Lord who rules all (1:22), one faith that all share (1:13), one baptism through which all entered (cf. 1 Corinthians 12:13), one God who is Father of all (3:14-15). To fracture the community would be to deny the oneness of each of these realities.

## Verses 7-11: Grace Given and Gifts Distributed

"But grace was given to each one of us according to the measure of Christ's gift" (4:7).

The conjunction de ("but") introduces a qualification: the unity just described does not mean uniformity. Within the one body, each member has received a distinct gift. The phrase heni hekasto hemon ("to each one of us") is emphatic in its individuality: every single member has received something. The word charis ("grace") here functions not as the general saving grace of 2:5-8 but as the particular enabling grace given to each member for service -- what Paul calls charismata ("grace-gifts") in Romans 12:6 and 1 Corinthians 12:4. The connection between the two senses of charis is deliberate: the same grace that saved (2:8) now equips for service (4:7).

The phrase kata to metron tes doreas tou Christou ("according to the measure of Christ's gift") specifies the standard of distribution. The word metron ("measure, standard, proportion") indicates that the gift is not random but measured -- Christ determines the appropriate gift for each member. The word dorea ("gift, free gift") appeared in 3:7, where Paul's own ministry was described as "the gift of God's grace." The genitive tou Christou identifies Christ as the giver: the gifts come from Christ, distributed according to his sovereign assessment of what each member needs and what the body requires.

"Therefore it says, 'When he ascended on high he led a host of captives, and he gave gifts to men'" (4:8).

The formula dio legei ("therefore it says") introduces a scripture citation. The quotation is from Psalm 68:18 (LXX 67:19), but with a significant modification that requires careful attention.

The Hebrew text of Psalm 68:18 reads: "You ascended on high, you took captives, you received gifts among men" (alita lammarom, shabita shebi, laqachta mattanot ba-adam). The LXX renders this: anebe eis hypsos, echmaloteusas aichmaloesian, elabes domata en anthropo -- "You ascended on high, you captured captivity, you received gifts among men."

The author of Ephesians modifies the text: anabas eis hypsos echmaloteusen aichmaloesian, kai edoken domata tois anthropois -- "having ascended on high he captured captivity, and he gave gifts to men." Two changes are noteworthy. First, the second person ("you ascended") is changed to the third person ("he ascended"), applying the psalm to Christ rather than addressing God directly. Second, and most significantly, "received gifts" (elabes domata / laqachta mattanot) is changed to "gave gifts" (edoken domata). The direction of the gifts is reversed: in the original psalm, the conquering king receives tribute from the conquered; in the author's application, the ascending Christ gives gifts to his people.

This modification is not arbitrary. A similar interpretive tradition appears in the Aramaic Targum on Psalm 68:18, which renders the verse: "You ascended to the firmament, O prophet Moses; you took captivity captive; you learned the words of the Torah; you gave them as gifts to the sons of men." The Targum already applies the psalm to Moses' ascent of Sinai and transforms "received" into "gave" -- Moses received the Torah from God on the mountain and then gave it to the people. The author of Ephesians follows a similar interpretive logic but applies it to Christ: Christ ascended on high and gave gifts to the community. The giving is the consequence of the ascending: the victorious one distributes the spoils of his victory.

Psalm 68 itself is a triumphal processional psalm celebrating God's march from Sinai through the wilderness to Zion: "O God, when you went out before your people, when you marched through the wilderness, the earth quaked, the heavens poured down rain before God, the One of Sinai, before God, the God of Israel" (Psalm 68:7-8, LXX 67:8-9). The ascent "on high" in the psalm describes God's triumphal procession up Mount Zion after conquering his enemies. The author of Ephesians reads this triumphal ascent as a type of Christ's ascension: as God ascended Zion in triumph after defeating Israel's enemies, so Christ ascended to the heavenly places after conquering the powers. The phrase echmaloteusen aichmaloesian ("he captured captivity" or "he led captive a host of captives") describes the conquered foes being led in the triumphal procession -- a common image in ancient Near Eastern and Greco-Roman victory celebrations, where captured enemies were paraded behind the conqueror (cf. Colossians 2:15, "having disarmed the rulers and authorities, he put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him").

"(In saying, 'He ascended,' what does it mean but that he had also descended into the lower parts of the earth?" (4:9).

The author now provides an interpretive comment on the word "ascended" (anebe) from the psalm citation. The logical argument is: ascent presupposes a prior descent. If Christ ascended on high, he must first have descended. The phrase eis ta katotera mere tes ges ("into the lower parts of the earth") has been read in several ways.

First, it may refer to the incarnation: Christ descended from the heavenly places to the earth. In this reading, "the lower parts of the earth" means "the lower regions, namely the earth" -- the genitive tes ges is appositional ("the lower parts, that is, the earth"), and the contrast is between heaven (where Christ was) and earth (to which he came). This reading aligns with Philippians 2:6-8, where Christ "emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being born in human likeness."

Second, it may refer to Christ's death and descent to the realm of the dead (Sheol/Hades). In this reading, "the lower parts of the earth" means the underworld -- the region beneath the earth where the dead reside. Psalm 63:9 (LXX 62:10) uses similar language: "those who seek to destroy my life shall go into the lower parts of the earth" (eis ta katotera tes ges). The descent to Sheol would parallel the imagery of Psalm 68, where God's triumph includes dominion over death and the underworld.

In either reading, the author's point is the same: the one who ascended is the one who first came down. The descent establishes the scope of the victory: Christ's triumph is not a triumph over a limited domain but over the entire vertical range of the cosmos, from the lowest depth to the highest height.

"He who descended is the one who also ascended far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things)" (4:10).

The identity is stated: ho katabas autos estin kai ho anabas ("the one who descended is himself also the one who ascended"). The emphasis on identity (autos estin, "he himself is") insists that the descending and ascending figure is one and the same person. The ascent is described as hyperano panton ton ouranon ("far above all the heavens") -- the same phrase used in 1:21 for Christ's exaltation "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion." The plural "heavens" (ouranon) reflects the Jewish cosmological tradition of multiple heavens (cf. 2 Corinthians 12:2, where Paul speaks of "the third heaven"; Testament of Levi 3, which describes seven heavens). Christ has ascended above all of them -- above every layer of the cosmic structure, above every power that inhabits those layers.

The purpose of the ascent is stated: hina plerose ta panta ("that he might fill all things"). The verb pleroo ("to fill, to make full, to complete") connects directly to 1:23, where the church was described as "the fullness (pleroma) of him who fills (pleroumenou) all in all." The ascending Christ fills the entire cosmos with his presence and lordship. The descent-to-ascent pattern traces the full range of the cosmos from bottom to top; the "filling of all things" claims that the ascending Christ's authority pervades every point in that range. Nothing is outside his reach; no region of the cosmos is beyond his filling.

The connection to the gift-giving of verse 8 is this: the one who fills all things is the one who gives gifts to the community. The gifts are expressions of Christ's cosmic filling -- ways in which his lordship becomes concretely operative within the body. The ascended, cosmos-filling Christ distributes specific capacities to specific members of his body for the building up of the whole.

"And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers" (4:11).

The verb edoken ("he gave") repeats from the psalm citation in verse 8 (edoken domata, "he gave gifts"). The gifts Christ gave are not abstract capacities but persons -- individuals appointed to specific functions within the community. The "gifts to men" of the psalm citation are now specified as gifted persons given to the community.

Five (or four, depending on the syntax of the final pair) roles are named.

First: tous apostolous ("the apostles"). The word apostolos ("apostle, sent one, emissary") designates those sent with authority to represent the sender. In 1:1, Paul identified himself as "an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God." In 2:20, the community was described as "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets." In 3:5, the mystery was "revealed to his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit." The apostles are the foundational witnesses -- those commissioned directly by Christ to bear his message and establish communities. Their role is foundational and therefore by nature unrepeatable: one lays a foundation once (2:20).

Second: tous prophetas ("the prophets"). The word prophetes ("prophet, one who speaks forth") designates those who receive and communicate divine revelation. The "prophets" paired with "apostles" here correspond to the same pairing in 2:20 and 3:5. As argued in the analysis of those passages, the "prophets" in this letter's usage appear to be the new covenant prophets -- the Spirit-empowered speakers within the community -- though the continuity with Israel's prophetic tradition is also operative. Their function is to communicate God's word to the community for its edification, direction, and correction.

Third: tous euangelistas ("the evangelists"). The word euangelistes ("evangelist, bringer of good news") appears in the New Testament only here, in Acts 21:8 (Philip is called "the evangelist"), and in 2 Timothy 4:5 ("do the work of an evangelist"). The word derives from euangelizo ("to bring good news, to announce the gospel"), which the author used in 2:17 ("he came and preached peace") and 3:8 ("to preach to the gentiles the unsearchable riches of Christ"). The evangelists are those whose specific gift is the proclamation of the gospel to those who have not yet heard -- the extension of the community through the announcement of good news.

Fourth and fifth: tous poimenas kai didaskalous ("the shepherds and teachers"). The single article tous governing both nouns suggests that these may describe a single role with two aspects rather than two separate roles: "the shepherd-teachers" or "those who shepherd by teaching." The word poimen ("shepherd, pastor") draws on the rich LXX tradition of shepherd leadership. Ezekiel 34 is the foundational text: God condemned Israel's failed shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock (34:2-10) and promised to shepherd his people himself (34:11-16) and to raise up "one shepherd, my servant David" (34:23). Jeremiah 23:1-4 similarly indicted faithless shepherds and promised that God would "set shepherds over them who will care for them." The shepherd metaphor encompasses feeding, guiding, protecting, and caring for the flock. The word didaskalos ("teacher, instructor") describes the function of transmitting and explaining the content of the faith -- the teaching that forms the community's understanding and shapes its life.

The list of gifted persons corresponds to the building imagery of the letter. The apostles and prophets are the foundation (2:20); the evangelists extend the building by bringing in new members; the shepherd-teachers maintain and develop the community's life. Together they represent the range of functions needed for the community to be established, expanded, and matured.

## Verses 12-16: The Purpose of the Gifts -- Building Up the Body

"To equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ" (4:12).

The purpose of the gifted persons is stated in a chain of three prepositional phrases. The first: pros ton katartismon ton hagion ("for the equipping of the saints"). The word katartismos ("equipping, preparing, making complete, fitting out") derives from katartizo ("to put in order, to restore, to mend, to complete"). In classical Greek, katartizo was used for setting a broken bone, mending fishing nets (Matthew 4:21, Mark 1:19), and equipping an army. The katartismos is the preparation and fitting-out of the saints (ton hagion, "the holy ones" -- all the members of the community) so that they can perform their function.

The second phrase: eis ergon diakonias ("for the work of service/ministry"). The word diakonia ("service, ministry, attendance") is the activity of the diakonos ("servant, minister") -- the work of serving the community and serving God through the community. The gifted persons of verse 11 do not exist to perform all the ministry themselves; they exist to equip the saints to do the work of ministry. The ministry belongs to the whole body; the gifted persons' role is to prepare the whole body for it.

The third phrase: eis oikodomen tou somatos tou Christou ("for the building up of the body of Christ"). The word oikodome ("building, edification, construction") is the architectural metaphor that has run through the letter from 2:20-22 (built on the foundation, growing into a temple). The body of Christ is being built -- the temple imagery and the body imagery converge. The building up of the body is the ultimate purpose: the gifted persons equip the saints, the saints perform ministry, and the ministry builds up the body. The chain is purposive: gifted persons serve the saints, who serve the body.

"Until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ" (4:13).

The temporal marker mechri ("until") indicates that the building process described in verse 12 has a goal and a terminus. The goal is stated in three parallel expressions.

First: eis ten henoteta tes pisteos kai tes epignoseos tou huiou tou theou ("to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God"). The word henotes ("unity, oneness") reappears from 4:3 -- the only other occurrence in the New Testament. In 4:3, it was "the unity of the Spirit" to be maintained; here it is "the unity of the faith and of the knowledge" to be attained. The first unity is a present reality to be guarded; this second unity is a future goal to be reached. The faith (pistis) and knowledge (epignosis, "deep knowledge, full recognition") of the Son of God are the content of the unity: the community is growing toward a shared, mature understanding of who the Son of God is and what he has accomplished. The title "Son of God" (tou huiou tou theou) is rare in Ephesians -- this is its only occurrence in the letter -- and adds a dimension not present in the more common "Christ Jesus" or "the Lord": it names the relationship between Christ and the Father and identifies the community's faith and knowledge as directed toward a person, not merely a doctrine.

Second: eis andra teleion ("to a mature person" or "to a complete man"). The word aner ("man, adult male, person") here functions as a description of the corporate entity -- the body of Christ reaching full maturity. The word teleios ("mature, complete, perfect, full-grown") describes the goal of development: not infancy but adulthood, not incompleteness but wholeness. In 1 Corinthians 14:20, Paul urged: "in thinking be mature (teleioi)." The corporate body is to grow up into a fully developed entity.

Third: eis metron helikias tou pleromatos tou Christou ("to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ"). The word metron ("measure") appeared in 4:7 for the measure of Christ's gift to individuals; here it appears for the measure of the corporate body's growth. The word helikia ("stature, maturity, age") can mean physical height or stage of development. The pleroma ("fullness") connects to 1:23 ("the fullness of him who fills all in all"), 3:19 ("filled to all the fullness of God"), and 4:10 (Christ ascending to "fill all things"). The corporate body's goal is to grow to the stature that corresponds to the fullness of Christ -- to become the full-sized expression of Christ's presence and character in the world. The prayer of 3:19 -- "that you may be filled to all the fullness of God" -- is now described as the destination of the community's growth process.

"So that we may no longer be children, tossed to and fro by the waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by human cunning, by craftiness in deceitful schemes" (4:14).

The negative counterpart to maturity is stated: the community is not to remain nepioi ("infants, children, immature persons"). The word nepios in the LXX and in Paul's usage describes those who are undeveloped and vulnerable: Psalm 19:7 (LXX 18:8), "the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple (nepious)"; 1 Corinthians 3:1, "I could not address you as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh, as infants (nepiois) in Christ." Infants are characterized by instability -- they have no anchor.

The instability is described through two nautical metaphors. First: klydonizomenoi ("tossed by waves," present passive participle of klydonizo, "to be tossed by waves, to be buffeted by surf"). The verb derives from klydon ("a wave, a surge, a billow"). Second: peripheromenoi ("carried about, blown around," present passive participle of periphero, "to carry about, to blow in different directions"). Both images describe a ship without anchor or rudder, at the mercy of whatever force strikes it -- the opposite of the community "rooted and grounded" in love (3:17).

The source of the destabilizing forces is identified: panti anemo tes didaskalias ("by every wind of teaching/doctrine"). The word anemos ("wind") continues the nautical metaphor: false teachings are winds that blow the community off course. The instability comes not from internal weakness alone but from external forces -- "every wind of doctrine" suggests a multiplicity of competing teachings that pull the community in contradictory directions.

The human agents behind these destabilizing doctrines are described with three phrases. First: en te kybeia ton anthropon ("by the cunning of men" or "by the dice-playing of men"). The word kybeia ("dice-playing, gambling, trickery") derives from kybos ("a die, a cube"). The metaphor is of loaded dice -- deliberate manipulation and deception, the opposite of honest dealing. Second: en panourgia ("in craftiness, in cunning, in trickery"). The word panourgia ("cleverness for evil, craftiness, unscrupulousness") is a compound: pan ("all, every") + ergon ("work, deed") -- one who is ready to do anything, who stops at nothing. Third: pros ten methodeian tes planes ("toward the scheming of deception" or "with a view to the strategy of error"). The word methodeia ("scheming, strategy, method, stratagem") is a compound: meta ("after") + hodos ("way, road") -- a following after, a tracking, a systematic pursuit. The word plane ("deception, error, wandering") identifies the goal of the scheming: to lead the community astray. The word methodeia appears again in 6:11 for "the schemes of the devil" (tas methodeias tou diabolou), which suggests that the human agents of deception in 4:14 are instruments of the larger cosmic opposition described in 6:12.

"Rather, speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ" (4:15).

The contrast to the instability of verse 14 is stated: aletheuontes de en agape ("but speaking the truth in love" or "but being truthful in love"). The verb aletheuo ("to speak truth, to be truthful, to deal truly") in the LXX appears in Genesis 42:16 for "whether there is truth in you." The word encompasses not merely verbal honesty but entire truthfulness of life -- living and speaking in accordance with reality. The sphere of this truthfulness is en agape ("in love") -- truth without love becomes a weapon; love without truth becomes sentimentality. The community grows by truth spoken and lived within the context of love.

The result: auxesomen eis auton ta panta, hos estin he kephale, Christos ("we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, Christ"). The verb auxano ("to grow, to increase") appeared in 2:21 for the structure that "grows into a holy temple." The direction of growth is eis auton ("into him") -- the growth is toward Christ, into Christ. The phrase ta panta ("in every way, in all things") indicates that the growth is comprehensive, not limited to one dimension. Christ is identified as he kephale ("the head"), repeating the head-body relationship established in 1:22 ("gave him as head over all things to the church"). The body grows toward its head; the community matures toward Christlikeness. The head is not just the authority but the standard: Christ determines what the body's mature form looks like.

"From whom the whole body, joined and held together by every joint with which it is equipped, when each part is working properly, makes the body grow so that it builds itself up in love" (4:16).

The verse describes the organic mechanics of the body's growth. The phrase ex hou pan to soma ("from whom the whole body") identifies Christ as the source: the body's growth derives from the head. The two participiples describe how the body functions: synarmologoumenon ("being joined together") and symbibasomenon ("being held together" or "being knit together"). The word synarmologeo appeared in 2:21 for the structure "being joined together" as it grows into a temple; its repetition here merges the body and temple imagery into one. The word symbibazo ("to hold together, to unite, to instruct, to knit together") in Colossians 2:2 describes hearts being "knit together in love" and in Colossians 2:19 describes the body "knit together through its joints and ligaments."

The phrase dia pases haphes tes epichoregias ("through every joint of supply" or "by every supporting ligament") identifies the mechanism: the joints (haphe, "joint, contact, touch, connection") are the points where parts of the body connect, and these joints supply (epichorgia, "supply, provision, support") what the body needs. The word epichorgia derives from choregeo ("to supply, to furnish, to provide at one's own expense") -- originally the term for a wealthy citizen who financed a chorus for a dramatic performance. The supply is generous, sufficient, and enabling.

The phrase kat' energeian en metro henos hekastou merous ("according to the working in measure of each individual part") identifies the contributions: each part (meros, "part, member, portion") of the body has its own energeia ("working, operative power, effective functioning") in its own measure (metro). The word energeia appeared in 1:19 and 3:7 for God's operative power; here it describes the working of each member within the body. Each member's contribution is measured -- not identical to every other member's, but proportioned to its role.

The result: ten auxesin tou somatos poieitai eis oikodomen heautou en agape ("makes the body's growth toward the building up of itself in love"). The body builds itself up. The growth is organic and communal: no single member builds the body alone, and no member grows in isolation. The final phrase en agape ("in love") returns to the sphere named in 4:2 (bearing with one another in love), 3:17 (rooted and grounded in love), and 1:4 (chosen in love). Love is the environment in which the body grows, the atmosphere in which the joints function, the medium through which the parts supply one another. Without love, the body cannot grow; with love, each part's working contributes to the whole.

The mechanics described in this verse are not abstract: they describe the practical reality of a community in which each member exercises their particular gift for the benefit of the whole, connected to one another through relationships of mutual support, under the headship of Christ, in the medium of love. The passage describes what the "maintaining the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (4:3) looks like when it is functioning.

## Verses 17-19: The Gentile Way of Life to Be Abandoned

"Now this I say and testify in the Lord, that you must no longer walk as the gentiles do, in the futility of their minds" (4:17).

The transition at 4:17 marks the beginning of the chapter's second major movement. The first movement (4:1-16) addressed the corporate dimension of the worthy walk: the unity of the body and its growth toward maturity. This second movement (4:17-32) addresses the personal transformation required of each member: the abandonment of the former way of life and the adoption of a new one. The two movements are complementary: the body grows when its individual members are transformed, and the individual transformation serves the body's growth.

The formula touto oun lego kai martyromai en kyrio ("this therefore I say and testify in the Lord") is solemn and emphatic. The verb martyromai ("I testify, I bear witness, I affirm solemnly") elevates the instruction from advice to obligation. The phrase en kyrio ("in the Lord") grounds the testimony in the Lord's authority: what follows is not Paul's personal preference but the Lord's requirement.

The instruction is a prohibition: meketi hymas peripatein kathos kai ta ethne peripatei ("that you no longer walk as the gentiles also walk"). The word meketi ("no longer") marks a definitive break: whatever was true before is to cease. The verb peripateo ("to walk") returns for the third time in this chapter (after 4:1 and implicitly in the description of the former walk in 4:14). The word ethne ("gentiles, nations") here describes the unconverted pagan world -- not the gentile believers to whom the letter is addressed, but the gentile world from which they came. The author is telling gentile believers to stop living as gentiles live. The "you" (hymas) of the letter -- the gentile addressees -- are now distinguished from "the gentiles" (ta ethne) who remain in the old way of life. The conversion that brought them into the community requires a corresponding transformation of conduct.

The gentile way of life is characterized: en mataioteti tou noos auton ("in the futility of their mind"). The word mataiotes ("futility, emptiness, vanity, purposelessness") in the LXX is the defining word of Ecclesiastes: "Vanity of vanities (mataiotes mataioteton), says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 1:2, LXX). In Romans 8:20, the creation was "subjected to futility" (te mataioteti). The word describes a state of purposelessness -- effort without result, existence without meaning, activity without destination. The futility is located in the nous ("mind, understanding, way of thinking") -- the faculty by which reality is interpreted and decisions are made. The gentile mind is futile: it processes reality in a way that arrives nowhere.

"They are darkened in their understanding, alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them, due to their hardness of heart" (4:18).

The diagnosis continues with four linked conditions, each producing the next.

First: eskotomenoi te dianoia ("darkened in understanding"). The verb skotoo (or skotizo, "to darken, to make dark") in the perfect passive participle indicates a settled state: their understanding has been darkened and remains dark. The dianoia ("understanding, intellect, capacity for thinking") appeared in 2:3 for the "desires of the body and the mind (ton dianoion)" -- the same faculty that participated in the former disobedient life. The darkness is cognitive: the gentile mind cannot see clearly.

Second: apelotriomenoi tes zoes tou theou ("alienated from the life of God"). The same verb (apallotrioo, "to alienate, to estrange") appeared in 2:12, where the gentiles were "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel" (apelotriomenoi tes politeias tou Israel). There the alienation was from Israel's civic life; here it is from God's own life (tes zoes tou theou). The genitive tou theou identifies God as the source of the life from which they are estranged. The language implies that there is a "life of God" -- a quality of existence that belongs to God and that humans were meant to share -- and that the gentiles are cut off from it.

Third: dia ten agnoian ten ousan en autois ("because of the ignorance that is in them"). The word agnoia ("ignorance, lack of knowledge") is the causal factor: the alienation from God's life results from ignorance. The phrase ten ousan en autois ("the [ignorance] being in them") indicates that the ignorance is not merely a lack of information but an internal condition -- something rooted in their constitution.

Fourth: dia ten porosin tes kardias auton ("due to the hardness of their heart"). The word porosis ("hardness, callousness, petrification") derives from poros ("a kind of stone, calcium deposit") -- it describes a hardening, a calcification, a loss of sensitivity. In Mark 3:5, Jesus was grieved at the "hardness of heart" (porosis tes kardias) of those in the synagogue. In Romans 11:25, Paul wrote that "a partial hardening (porosis) has happened to Israel." The kardia ("heart") in Hebraic usage is the center of the person -- mind, will, and affection together (Deuteronomy 6:5). The hardened heart cannot respond to God; it is petrified, unable to feel, unable to perceive. The causal chain runs backward: hardness of heart produces ignorance, ignorance produces alienation from God's life, alienation produces darkened understanding, and darkened understanding produces the futile way of life.

This diagnostic corresponds to the prophetic tradition. Isaiah 6:9-10: "Make the heart of this people dull, and their ears heavy, and blind their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed." Ezekiel 11:19: "I will remove the heart of stone from their flesh and give them a heart of flesh." The hardened heart that produces ignorance and estrangement from God is a condition diagnosed by Israel's own prophets -- applied there to Israel, applied here to the gentile world.

"They have become callous and have given themselves up to sensuality, greedy to practice every kind of impurity" (4:19).

The moral consequence of the cognitive and spiritual condition described in verse 18 is now stated. The word apelgekotes ("having become callous, having become past feeling, having ceased to care") is a rare verb (apalgeo, "to cease to feel pain, to become insensible") that describes the loss of moral sensitivity -- the capacity to feel shame, guilt, or distress at wrongdoing has been deadened.

The result of this desensitization: heautous paredoken te aselgeia ("they gave themselves over to sensuality/licentiousness"). The verb paradidomi ("to give over, to hand over, to deliver up") echoes Romans 1:24, 26, 28, where God "gave them over" (paredoken autous) to the consequences of their choices. In Romans, God is the subject who gives over; here the gentiles are themselves the subject: they gave themselves over. The word aselgeia ("sensuality, licentiousness, debauchery, absence of restraint") describes unbridled indulgence -- conduct without moral boundaries. In the LXX, the related language describes the unrestrained behavior of those who have abandoned God's ways.

The further specification: eis ergasian akatharsias pases en pleonexia ("for the practice of every impurity with greediness"). The word ergasia ("work, practice, business, trade") suggests something systematic -- not occasional lapses but a settled practice, a "business" of impurity. The word akatharsia ("impurity, uncleanness") in the LXX is the opposite of the holiness to which the community is called (1:4, "holy and blameless"). The word pleonexia ("greediness, covetousness, insatiable desire for more") is a compound: pleon ("more") + echo ("to have") -- the desire to have more, the inability to be satisfied. The phrase en pleonexia modifies the practice of impurity: the impurity is practiced greedily, insatiably, with an endless craving for more. The portrait is of a humanity that has lost its capacity for moral feeling and has given itself over to an escalating, insatiable pursuit of what defiles.

## Verses 20-24: The Contrast -- What the Recipients Learned

"But that is not the way you learned Christ!" (4:20).

The phrase hymeis de ouch houtos emathete ton Christon ("but you did not so learn Christ") is the emphatic contrast. The de ("but") marks the sharp break between the gentile world described in 4:17-19 and the recipients' own experience. The verb manthano ("to learn, to come to know, to understand") with the accusative ton Christon ("Christ") as its direct object is unusual. One does not normally "learn" a person; one learns a subject or a skill. The phrasing suggests that what the recipients learned was not merely information about Christ but Christ himself -- his person, his character, his way of being, as the content of their instruction. "Learning Christ" is more than theological education; it is formation in the pattern of Christ's own life.

"Assuming that you have heard about him and were taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus" (4:21).

The conditional ei ge ("if indeed, assuming that") does not express doubt (as noted in the analysis of 3:2, where the same construction appeared); it assumes the truth of the condition. The verb ekousate ("you heard") refers to the initial hearing of the gospel (cf. 1:13, "when you heard the word of truth"). The verb edidachthete ("you were taught," aorist passive of didasko) refers to the instruction that followed the initial hearing -- the formation in the way of Christ that took place within the community. The phrase en auto ("in him") locates the teaching: the instruction happened within the sphere of Christ, not merely about Christ.

The phrase kathos estin aletheia en to Iesou ("as the truth is in Jesus") is remarkable for its use of the personal name "Jesus" without any title (Christ, Lord, Son of God). This is the only place in Ephesians where the bare name "Jesus" appears alone. The effect is to point to the actual, historical person -- the man Jesus, whose concrete life and teaching constitute the truth. The "truth" (aletheia) that is "in Jesus" is not an abstraction but is embodied in a historical person. What the recipients learned was not merely correct doctrine but the truth as it was lived and revealed in the person of Jesus.

"To put off your old self, which belongs to your former manner of life and is corrupt through deceitful desires" (4:22).

The content of what was taught is now stated in three infinitives (verses 22, 23, 24) that describe three dimensions of transformation. The first: apothesthai hymas... ton palaion anthropon ("that you put off the old person/humanity"). The verb apotithemi ("to put off, to lay aside, to take off as a garment") is the clothing metaphor: the old way of life is a garment to be removed. The same metaphor appears in Colossians 3:9 ("you have put off [apekdysamenoi] the old self [ton palaion anthropon]") and in Romans 13:12 ("let us cast off [apothometha] the works of darkness and put on the armor of light").

The phrase ton palaion anthropon ("the old person" or "the old humanity") does not refer to one's individual pre-conversion personality but to the entire mode of human existence that characterized the former life. The word palaios ("old, ancient, worn out, former") describes what belongs to the previous age -- the age of this world (2:2), the age of trespasses (2:1), the age of the ruler of the air (2:2). The anthropos ("person, human being") is singular and corporate: it describes a type of humanity, a way of being human, rather than merely an individual psyche. This "old humanity" is what was described in 2:1-3: dead in trespasses, walking according to the course of this world, living in the desires of the flesh, children of wrath by nature.

This old humanity is further characterized: ton kata ten proteran anastrophen ("the one according to your former manner of life"). The word anastrophe ("manner of life, conduct, behavior, way of turning about") is the same root as the verb anastrepho that appeared in 2:3 ("among whom we also all formerly conducted ourselves"). The old humanity corresponds to the former conduct -- the life described in 2:1-3 and 4:17-19.

The old humanity is ton phtheiromenon kata tas epithymias tes apates ("being corrupted according to the desires of deceit"). The present passive participle phtheiromenon ("being corrupted, being destroyed, decaying") indicates an ongoing process of deterioration -- the old humanity is not static but actively decomposing. The word phtheiro ("to corrupt, to destroy, to ruin") describes the decay of what was once alive. The agent of corruption is the desires (epithymiai) of deceit (apate). The word apate ("deceit, deception, delusion") identifies the desires as fraudulent -- they promise satisfaction but deliver corruption. This echoes the "deceitful schemes" (methodeia tes planes) of 4:14.

"And to be renewed in the spirit of your minds" (4:23).

The second infinitive: ananeousthai de to pneumati tou noos hymon ("and to be renewed in the spirit of your mind"). The verb ananeoo ("to be renewed, to make new again") appears only here in the New Testament. The prefix ana- ("again, anew") combined with neos ("new, young, fresh") describes a making-new-again -- a rejuvenation, a renovation of what had become old and worn. The passive voice indicates that the renewal is not self-generated but received.

The location of the renewal is to pneuma tou noos hymon ("the spirit of your mind"). The word pneuma ("spirit") here likely refers not to the Holy Spirit directly but to the innermost dimension of the human mind -- the spirit that animates and governs the nous ("mind, understanding, way of thinking"). The nous was the faculty that was futile (mataioteti tou noos, 4:17) and the understanding (dianoia) that was darkened (4:18). The renewal targets exactly the faculty that was corrupted: the inner dimension of the mind that shapes perception, interpretation, and decision-making. The renewal of the spirit of the mind is the reversal of the futility and darkness described in 4:17-18. What was darkened is to be illuminated; what was futile is to be purposeful; what was hardened is to be made responsive.

This corresponds to Paul's instruction in Romans 12:2: "Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind (te anakainosei tou noos)." The transformation of conduct begins with the transformation of the mind. The ethical instruction of 4:25-32 is not simply a list of rules to be obeyed by willpower; it flows from a renewed mind that perceives reality differently.

"And to put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (4:24).

The third infinitive: endysasthai ton kainon anthropon ("to put on the new person/humanity"). The verb endyo ("to put on, to clothe oneself with") completes the clothing metaphor: what was put off (v. 22) is replaced by what is put on. The kainon anthropon ("the new humanity") corresponds to and replaces the palaion anthropon ("the old humanity") of verse 22. The word kainos ("new, qualitatively new, unprecedented") is the same word used in 2:15 for the "one new humanity" (hena kainon anthropon) that Christ created from the two former groups. The verbal echo is deliberate: the "one new humanity" that Christ created by abolishing the dividing wall (2:15) is the same "new humanity" that each member of the community is to "put on" in their daily conduct. The corporate reality of 2:15 becomes the personal practice of 4:24. The community that was created as one new humanity must have its individual members live as that new humanity.

The new humanity is characterized: ton kata theon ktisthenta en dikaiosyne kai hosioteti tes aletheias ("the one created according to God in righteousness and holiness of truth"). The verb ktizo ("to create") returns from 2:10 ("we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus") and 2:15 ("that he might create in himself one new humanity"). The creation language is consistent: what God did in Christ is a new creation, not a renovation. The phrase kata theon ("according to God, after the likeness of God") identifies the pattern of the new creation: it is modeled on God's own character. This echoes Genesis 1:26-27 (LXX): "Let us make humanity (anthropon) according to our image (kat' eikona hemeteran) and according to our likeness." The new humanity is a re-creation of the original design -- humanity restored to the image and likeness of God that was corrupted in the fall.

The content of this God-likeness is specified: en dikaiosyne kai hosioteti tes aletheias ("in righteousness and holiness of the truth"). The word dikaiosyne ("righteousness, justice, right conduct") describes conduct that conforms to God's standard of right. The word hosiotes ("holiness, devoutness, piety") describes the quality of being set apart for God -- sacred, devoted, belonging to God's sphere. The genitive tes aletheias ("of truth") modifies both: the righteousness and holiness are "of truth" -- genuine, authentic, real -- as opposed to the "deceitful desires" (epithymiai tes apates) that characterized the old humanity (4:22). The old humanity was corrupted by the desires of deceit; the new humanity is characterized by the righteousness and holiness of truth. The contrast is between apate (deceit) and aletheia (truth) -- between the fraudulent promises of the former life and the genuine reality of the new.

The put-off/renewal/put-on sequence of verses 22-24 describes a single transformation with three dimensions: the removal of the old way of being human, the renovation of the inner cognitive faculty, and the adoption of a new way of being human patterned on God's own character. The transformation is both punctiliar (the decisive break with the old) and progressive (the ongoing renewal of the mind).

## Verses 25-32: The Specific Behaviors of the New Humanity

The remainder of the chapter provides specific ethical instructions that illustrate what it looks like to live as the new humanity. Each instruction follows a pattern: negative (what to stop doing), positive (what to do instead), and reason (why the change matters). The reasons consistently ground the behavior in the communal reality of the one body.

**Verses 25: Truth-telling**

"Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, for we are members one of another" (4:25).

The word dio ("therefore") draws the consequence from the put-off/put-on instruction of verses 22-24. The first application is truthfulness. The verb apothemenoi ("having put away," aorist middle participle of apotithemi) repeats the verb of verse 22 (apothesthai, "to put off"): what was described generally (put off the old humanity) is now being specified (put off falsehood). The word pseudos ("falsehood, lying, deception") is the specific garment being removed.

The positive command is a direct quotation from Zechariah 8:16 (LXX): laleite aletheian hekastos meta tou plesion autou -- "speak truth, each one with his neighbor." The prophet Zechariah was calling the restored community of Israel to faithful covenant life after the exile. The virtues he named were not abstract moral ideals but the behaviors required for a restored community to function: "These are the things that you shall do: Speak the truth to one another; render in your gates judgments that are true and make for peace" (Zechariah 8:16). The author of Ephesians applies this prophetic instruction to the new community: the restored Israel -- the one new humanity of Jews and gentiles -- must practice the same communal integrity that the prophet demanded of post-exilic Israel.

The reason: hoti esmen allelon mele ("because we are members of one another"). The word mele ("members, limbs, parts") is the body language from verses 12-16 applied directly to behavior. Because the community is one body, lying to a neighbor is lying to oneself -- it is a member of the body deceiving another member of the same body. Truth-telling is not merely a moral ideal; it is an organic necessity. A body whose parts deceive one another cannot function.

**Verses 26-27: Anger**

"Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil" (4:26-27).

The command orgizesthe kai me hamartanete ("be angry and do not sin") is a quotation from Psalm 4:4 (LXX 4:5): orgizesthe kai me hamartanete -- the same words exactly. The LXX of Psalm 4:4 renders the Hebrew rigzu ve-al-techeta'u, where the verb ragaz means "to tremble, to be agitated, to be moved with emotion." The LXX interpreted the emotional agitation as anger (orgizesthe). The imperative form ("be angry") may function as a concessive: "if you become angry, do not sin" -- acknowledging that anger will arise in community life while prohibiting the sin that anger can produce. The point is not that anger is commanded but that when anger occurs (as it inevitably will in a community of diverse humans), it must not be allowed to become sin.

The time limit: ho helios me epidueto epi parorgismo hymon ("do not let the sun go down on your anger"). The word parorgismos ("provocation, exasperation, angry outburst") is related to but slightly different from orge ("anger"): it describes the irritation or provocation that anger causes. The instruction to resolve anger before sundown reflects a Jewish principle of prompt reconciliation and may connect to the daily cycle of prayer and reflection. Unresolved anger that persists overnight hardens into resentment and division.

The warning: mede didote topon to diabolo ("and give no opportunity to the devil"). The word topos ("place, opportunity, room, foothold") describes an opening -- a vulnerability that can be exploited. The word diabolos ("devil, slanderer, accuser") in the LXX translates the Hebrew satan ("adversary, accuser"). The devil, introduced briefly here, will be elaborated in 6:11 ("put on the full armor of God, that you may be able to stand against the schemes of the devil"). The connection to the community's unity is clear: unresolved anger creates an opening for the cosmic adversary to disrupt the body. The "methodeia" (scheming) of 4:14 and 6:11 finds its foothold in the community's internal fractures.

**Verse 28: Stealing and Generosity**

"Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (4:28).

The instruction moves from negative (stop stealing) to positive (work honestly) to purposive (in order to share). The transformation is not merely moral restraint (stop taking) but a complete reversal of orientation (start giving). The one who formerly took from others is to become the one who gives to others. The phrase to agathon ("the good," here meaning "honest work" or "what is good") replaces the former behavior. The phrase tais idiais chersin ("with his own hands") emphasizes self-supporting labor -- the dignity of work that sustains both the worker and others. The ultimate purpose is hina eche metadidonai to chrean echonti ("in order to have something to share with the one who has need"). The word metadidomi ("to share, to give a portion, to distribute") indicates not merely having enough for oneself but having a surplus to distribute. The former taker becomes a giver; the former consumer becomes a provider. This instruction reflects the communal ethic of the early assemblies (Acts 2:44-45, 4:32-35) and embodies the "good works" for which the community was created (2:10).

**Verses 29-30: Speech and the Spirit**

"Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear" (4:29).

The word sapros ("rotten, corrupt, bad, unwholesome") in its literal sense describes rotten fruit or decayed wood (Matthew 7:17-18, "a bad tree bears bad fruit"). Applied to speech (logos, "word, speech, talk"), it describes language that corrupts, tears down, or poisons. The prohibition is absolute: pas logos sapros ("every rotten word") must not come out of their mouths.

The positive replacement: alla ei tis agathos pros oikodomen tes chreias ("but if any is good for building up, as the need requires"). The word oikodome ("building up, edification") is the same word from 4:12 and 4:16: the building up of the body. Speech is to serve the same purpose as the gifted persons -- it is to build up the community. The word chreia ("need, occasion, necessity") indicates that edifying speech is responsive to what is actually needed, not generic piety.

The purpose: hina do charin tois akouousin ("that it may give grace to those who hear"). The word charis ("grace") here means benefit, gift, something of value -- speech that deposits something good in the hearer. The recipients' words are to function the way God's grace functions: as free gifts that strengthen and benefit those who receive them.

"And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption" (4:30).

The command me lypeite to pneuma to hagion tou theou ("do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God") is a striking personalization of the Spirit. The verb lypeo ("to grieve, to cause sorrow, to distress") attributes emotional response to the Spirit. The Spirit is not an impersonal force but a person who can be grieved by the community's conduct. The LXX background is Isaiah 63:10: "But they rebelled and grieved (paroxynon) his Holy Spirit; therefore he turned to be their enemy, and himself fought against them." Isaiah describes Israel's wilderness rebellion as grieving God's Holy Spirit. The author of Ephesians applies the same language to the new community: just as Israel's rebellion grieved God's Spirit, the community's corrupt behavior -- the rotten speech, the unresolved anger, the falsehood -- grieves the same Spirit.

The relative clause en ho esphragisthete eis hemeran apolytroseoos ("by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption") connects back to 1:13-14, where the gentile believers were "sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it." The sealing was for a purpose: eis hemeran apolytroseoos -- "for the day of redemption." The word apolytrosis ("redemption") appeared in 1:7 ("we have redemption through his blood") and 1:14 ("until the redemption of the possession"). The "day of redemption" is the eschatological event when the full redemption is completed -- the consummation of what the Spirit's sealing guarantees. The community has been sealed for a future day; their present conduct must correspond to that future destiny. To grieve the Spirit who sealed them is to act in contradiction to the very one who guarantees their final deliverance.

The pairing of "do not grieve the Spirit" with the reminder of the sealing creates a significant dynamic. The Spirit's sealing is presented as a present reality that points toward a future completion. The community's obligation is to live in a way that honors the Spirit who guarantees their future. The command does not threaten the loss of the sealing; it calls for conduct appropriate to those who have been sealed. The logic is consequential, not transactional: because you have been sealed by this Spirit for that day, therefore do not grieve this Spirit by behavior that contradicts what the sealing represents.

**Verses 31-32: The Vices to Remove and the Virtues to Adopt**

"Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice" (4:31).

A catalogue of destructive behaviors is listed for removal. The verb artheto ("let it be removed," aorist passive imperative of airo, "to take away, to remove, to lift up") is in the passive: these vices are to be removed, cleared away, taken out.

Six items are named: pikria ("bitterness" -- a sour, resentful disposition that poisons relationships), thymos ("wrath, passionate outburst, hot anger" -- the explosive expression of rage), orge ("anger, settled indignation" -- the more enduring form of hostility, distinct from the flash of thymos), krauge ("clamor, outcry, loud disputing" -- the raised voice that accompanies quarrels), blasphemia ("slander, abusive speech, reviling" -- speech that damages another's reputation or character), and kakia ("malice, wickedness, ill-will" -- the underlying disposition of hostility from which the other behaviors spring). The phrase syn pase kakia ("with all malice") functions as a comprehensive summary: every form of ill-will is to be removed.

The list describes the behaviors that destroy the unity of the body. Each item attacks the relational bonds that hold the community together: bitterness corrodes relationships; wrath and anger fracture them; clamor and slander publicize and escalate the fractures; malice sustains and deepens them. The removal of these behaviors is not merely personal moral improvement; it is the preservation of the body's integrity.

"Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you" (4:32).

The positive replacement follows. Three qualities are named: chrestoi ("kind, benevolent, gracious"), eusplagchnoi ("tenderhearted, compassionate" -- a compound of eu, "well," and splagchnon, "bowels, inward parts," the seat of emotions in ancient thought; cf. Luke 1:78, "the tender mercy [splagchna eleous] of our God"), and charizomenoi heautois ("forgiving one another" or "freely giving grace to one another"). The verb charizomai ("to give freely, to grant, to forgive") shares the root of charis ("grace"); to forgive is to grace someone -- to extend unmerited favor.

The standard of this forgiveness is the verse's climax: kathos kai ho theos en Christo echarisato hymin ("just as God in Christ forgave you"). The verb echarisato ("forgave, graced") repeats the same root. The standard for the community's mutual forgiveness is not a human measure of proportionality but the divine act itself: God forgave you; you forgive one another in the same way. The phrase en Christo ("in Christ") locates the forgiveness in the same sphere where everything in this letter occurs: in the Messiah, in his death (2:13-16), in the reconciliation accomplished on the cross. The community's forgiveness of one another is grounded in and patterned on God's forgiveness of them.

This final verse gathers the chapter's entire argument into a single principle. The worthy walk (4:1) finds its ultimate expression in mutual forgiveness. The unity of the Spirit (4:3) is maintained when members forgive rather than harbor bitterness. The building up of the body (4:12, 16) requires the removal of destructive behaviors (4:31) and the adoption of forgiving ones (4:32). The new humanity (4:24) is characterized by righteousness and holiness that mirror God's own character -- and God's character is supremely displayed in the forgiveness extended to the undeserving. The chapter ends where the letter's theology began: with grace.

## Summary of the Argument

Ephesians 4 is the hinge between the letter's two halves. Chapters 1-3 declared what God has done; chapter 4 begins drawing out what the community must do in response. The word "therefore" (oun, 4:1) carries the weight of the entire theological argument into the ethical section.

The chapter's argument proceeds in two complementary movements.

The first movement (4:1-16) addresses the community's corporate life. The exhortation to walk worthily of the calling (4:1) is specified in the qualities needed for community life -- humility, gentleness, patience, forbearance in love (4:2) -- all directed toward maintaining the unity that the Spirit has created (4:3). The seven unities (4:4-6) provide the theological ground: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God. Within this unity, Christ distributes diverse gifts -- apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherd-teachers (4:11) -- whose purpose is to equip the saints for ministry and build up the body (4:12). The goal of this building is corporate maturity: the whole body growing into the fullness of Christ (4:13), no longer tossed about by deceptive teachings (4:14), but growing in truth and love toward Christ the head (4:15), each part contributing to the whole (4:16).

The second movement (4:17-32) addresses the personal transformation required of each member. The gentile recipients must no longer live as the unconverted gentile world lives -- in mental futility, darkened understanding, alienation from God's life, hardness of heart, and moral degradation (4:17-19). They learned a different way in Christ (4:20-21): to put off the old humanity with its deceitful desires (4:22), to be renewed in the spirit of their minds (4:23), and to put on the new humanity created after God's likeness in righteousness and holiness (4:24). Specific behaviors then illustrate this transformation: truth-telling grounded in mutual membership (4:25), anger resolved promptly (4:26-27), labor aimed at generosity (4:28), speech that builds up and gives grace (4:29), conduct that does not grieve the sealing Spirit (4:30), and mutual kindness and forgiveness patterned on God's own forgiveness in Christ (4:31-32).

The two movements are inseparable. The body grows only when its members are individually transformed; the individual transformation serves the body's corporate maturity. The personal and the communal are woven together throughout: the "one body" of 4:4 requires the "one another" conduct of 4:25-32; the "building up" of 4:12 requires the "building up" speech of 4:29; the "new humanity" of 4:24 echoes the "one new humanity" of 2:15. The chapter's deepest claim is that personal ethics and corporate unity are not separate topics but two dimensions of a single reality: life in the body of Christ.

## Observations on Pronoun Usage

The pronoun pattern in Ephesians 4 continues the letter's established practice but with a significant development. In chapters 1-3, the "we/us" referred primarily to Jewish believers (1:3-12) or to the united community (2:4-7, 3:12, 3:20), while "you" referred to the gentile recipients. In chapter 4, the author moves freely between "you" and "we" in the first movement (4:1-16), reflecting the accomplished unity: "each one of us" (4:7), "we all attain" (4:13), "we may no longer be children" (4:14), "we are to grow up" (4:15). The unity the chapter argues for is enacted in its own pronoun usage.

In the second movement (4:17-32), the "you" returns with specificity: "you must no longer walk as the gentiles do" (4:17), "you did not so learn Christ" (4:20), "you were taught" (4:21), "you put off" (4:22). These imperatives address the gentile recipients directly, calling them to abandon the gentile way of life they once shared. The word "gentiles" (ethne) in 4:17 now describes a group the recipients have left, not a group they belong to -- a transformation from their identification as "you, the gentiles in the flesh" (2:11) to people who are now distinguished from "the gentiles." The pronoun usage charts their changed identity: they were gentiles; they are now something else -- members of the one body, participants in the one new humanity.

At 4:25, the pronouns merge again: "we are members of one another" (esmen allelon mele). The final verse returns to the inclusive mode: "God in Christ forgave you" (4:32) -- the "you" who are addressed with specific gentile-audience commands throughout the chapter are reminded that they, together with the whole community, are recipients of God's forgiveness. The pronoun movement of the chapter mirrors the movement of the entire letter: from the gentile recipients' particular identity, through their transformation, into the shared life of the body.

## Old Testament and LXX Resonances

The following Old Testament texts are active in the background of Ephesians 4:

- **Psalm 68:18 (LXX 67:19)** ("You ascended on high, you took captives, you received gifts among men"): cited and modified in 4:8 to describe Christ's ascension and gift-giving. The psalm's triumphal processional imagery is applied to Christ's victory and distribution of gifts to the community.
- **Zechariah 8:16** ("Speak truth, each one with his neighbor"): quoted directly in 4:25. The prophet's instruction to the restored post-exilic community is applied to the new community of reconciled Jews and gentiles.
- **Psalm 4:4 (LXX 4:5)** ("Be angry and do not sin"): quoted in 4:26. The psalmist's instruction about managing strong emotion is applied to the community's handling of anger.
- **Isaiah 63:10** ("They rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit"): the background for 4:30 ("do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God"). Israel's wilderness rebellion against the Spirit provides the precedent for the warning to the new community.
- **Genesis 1:26-27** ("Let us make humanity according to our image"): the background for 4:24 ("the new self, created after the likeness of God"). The new humanity recapitulates the original creation of humanity in God's image.
- **Deuteronomy 6:4** (the Shema: "The LORD our God, the LORD is one"): the background for the confession "one Lord" in 4:5. The Shema's oneness language is applied to Christ.
- **Ezekiel 34** (the shepherd discourse): the background for "shepherds" in 4:11. God's promise to raise up faithful shepherds after condemning Israel's failed shepherds informs the designation of shepherd-teachers as Christ's gifts to the community.
- **Ezekiel 11:19, 36:26** ("I will remove the heart of stone and give a heart of flesh"): the background for the contrast between the hardened heart (4:18) and the renewed mind (4:23). The prophetic promise of inner transformation shapes the author's description of the old-to-new transition.
- **Isaiah 6:9-10** ("Make the heart of this people dull"): the prophetic diagnosis of spiritual blindness and hardness informs the description of the gentile condition in 4:17-18.
- **Exodus 34:6** ("The LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger"): the background for the patience (makrothymia) enjoined in 4:2. The community is to embody God's own character as revealed at Sinai.
- **Numbers 12:3** ("Moses was very meek"): the background for the gentleness (prautes) enjoined in 4:2. The paradigmatic leader of Israel exemplified the quality now required of all community members.
