# Acts 26 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Acts 25):** Three days after arriving in the province, the new governor Porcius Festus traveled to Jerusalem, where the chief priests and leading men brought charges against Paul and requested his transfer to Jerusalem -- while simultaneously planning an ambush to kill him on the way (25:1-3). Festus refused the transfer and convened a formal hearing in Caesarea, where the Jewish accusers brought "many and serious charges which they could not prove" (25:7). Paul denied all charges: "I have not done anything wrong either against the Law of the Jews, or against the temple, or against Caesar" (25:8). But Festus, "wanting to do the Jews a favor" -- repeating Felix's identical motivation from 24:27 -- proposed moving the trial to Jerusalem (25:9). Paul, recognizing the danger, exercised his right as a Roman citizen and appealed to Caesar (25:11). The appeal was irrevocable; Festus confirmed: "You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go" (25:12). When King Agrippa II and his sister Bernice arrived in Caesarea on a diplomatic visit, Festus presented Paul's case to the king, confessing his bewilderment: the accusers brought no criminal charges of the kind he expected, only "some points of disagreement about their own religion and about a dead man, Jesus, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19). Festus needed Agrippa's expertise because he had a prisoner to send to the Emperor but "nothing definite about him to write" (25:26). A formal hearing was convened in the auditorium with great pomp -- Agrippa, Bernice, military commanders, prominent citizens -- and Paul was brought before them. The chapter ended with Festus describing the absurdity of his position: "it seems absurd to me in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges against him" (25:27). The hearing that opens Acts 26 is not a trial. It is an advisory investigation designed to help Festus formulate charges for the Emperor.

**What the author argues immediately after (Acts 27):** The decision to send Paul to Rome is executed. "When it was decided that we would sail for Italy, they proceeded to turn Paul and some other prisoners over to a centurion of the Augustan cohort, named Julius" (27:1). The narrative shifts from judicial proceedings to the voyage itself, resuming the first-person "we" narration that indicates the author's presence as a companion on the journey. The centurion Julius treats Paul "with consideration" (27:3), allowing him to visit friends in Sidon -- a detail that confirms the pattern of Roman officials recognizing that Paul is not a dangerous criminal. The voyage proceeds through Cyprus, Myra, and along the coast of Crete, growing increasingly dangerous as the season advances. Paul warns of disaster (27:10), but the centurion follows the pilot's advice instead. The chapter narrates the storm, the shipwreck, and the eventual survival of all on board -- a sequence in which Paul's prophetic authority is vindicated and the Lord's promise that Paul must testify in Rome (23:11) is fulfilled through divine preservation amid natural catastrophe.

**How chapter 26 connects these:** Acts 25 created a specific narrative need: Festus has a prisoner he must send to the Emperor, but he cannot formulate the charges. Acts 27 will narrate the voyage that carries Paul to Rome. Acts 26 occupies the space between these two necessities. It provides the hearing that Festus convened to solve his documentation problem, and it delivers the definitive judicial verdict -- "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:32) -- that sets Paul irrevocably on the path to Rome. The chapter also serves a deeper narrative function: it presents Paul's most comprehensive, most rhetorically polished, and most theologically complete defense speech. The author has structured the second half of Acts around three major defense speeches by Paul, each before a different audience: the Jerusalem crowd (22:1-21), the governor Felix (24:10-21), and now King Agrippa (26:2-23). Each is longer and more developed than the last. Each adapts its emphasis to its hearers. And the sequence of outcomes escalates toward clarity: the Jerusalem crowd erupted in fury and cut Paul off (22:22); Felix adjourned without a verdict (24:22); Agrippa and the assembled dignitaries declare Paul innocent of anything deserving death or imprisonment (26:31). The fullest presentation of Paul's case produces the most unequivocal verdict. The chapter thus serves simultaneously as the resolution of Festus's procedural problem, the culmination of Paul's defense before human authorities, and the final judicial clearance that precedes the Rome journey.

## Verses 1-3: The Permission to Speak and Paul's Address to the Expert

"Now Agrippa said to Paul, 'You are permitted to speak for yourself.' Then Paul extended his hand and proceeded to make his defense" (26:1).

Agrippa, not Festus, grants Paul permission to speak. This is consistent with the dynamics established in chapter 25: though Festus is the governor and holds legal authority, Agrippa is the one with expertise in Jewish affairs, and the hearing exists for Agrippa's benefit as much as Festus's. The king presides over this session. The phrase "you are permitted" (epitrepetai soi) uses the impersonal passive -- the permission is institutional, not personal. Paul is being granted a formal hearing, not a casual conversation.

"Paul extended his hand" (ekteinas ten cheira). The gesture is characteristic of formal oratory in the Greco-Roman world. The extended hand signaled the beginning of a set speech, a deliberate and composed address rather than an improvised response. The verb ekteino ("to stretch out, to extend") describes a gesture of authority and rhetorical control. Paul is not a frightened prisoner mumbling excuses; he is an orator beginning a formal presentation. The gesture mirrors the formal setting -- the auditorium, the assembled dignitaries, the pomp of the royal entrance described in 25:23. Into the spectacle of power, the prisoner extends his hand and begins to speak with the composure of a man who controls his own narrative.

The word "defense" (apologeomai -- "to make a defense, to speak in one's own defense") is the legal term for a defendant's formal response to charges. Paul has used this word throughout his legal proceedings (22:1, 24:10, 25:8). But the hearing before Agrippa is not technically a trial -- Paul has already appealed to Caesar, and no local verdict can be rendered. The word apologia here means something broader than a legal plea: it is Paul's definitive account of himself, his mission, and why he is in chains.

"Regarding all the things of which I am accused by the Jews, King Agrippa, I consider myself fortunate that I am about to make my defense before you today, especially because you are an expert in all customs and questions among the Jews; therefore I beg you to listen to me patiently" (26:2-3).

Paul opens with a captatio benevolentiae -- the conventional flattering address to the judge that was standard in Greco-Roman forensic rhetoric. Tertullus used the same device before Felix (24:2-4), flattering the governor for nonexistent "peace" and "reforms." Paul's version is notably different: his compliment is specific and factual. Agrippa is indeed "an expert" (gnusten -- from gnostes, "one who knows, a connoisseur, an expert") in Jewish customs and questions. As custodian of the Jerusalem temple and holder of the right to appoint the high priest (Josephus, Antiquities 20.1.3, 20.9.4), Agrippa possessed a depth of knowledge about Jewish religious affairs that no Roman governor could match. Paul's compliment is not empty flattery; it identifies the specific qualification that makes Agrippa the right audience for what Paul is about to say. The content Paul will present -- the hope of Israel, the resurrection, the prophets, the Messiah -- requires a hearer who can evaluate it on its own terms, not reduce it to "a dead man, Jesus, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19), as Festus had done.

The word "customs" (ethon -- from ethos, "custom, practice, habitual usage") refers to Jewish religious practices -- the observances, rituals, and behavioral norms that governed Jewish communal life. The word "questions" (zetematon -- from zetema, "a question, a disputed point, a matter of inquiry") refers to the theological controversies that divided Jewish schools and sects. Agrippa knows both: the practices and the disputes. He can assess whether Paul's claims fall within the range of legitimate Jewish debate or outside it.

The request to "listen to me patiently" (makrothymos akousai mou) uses the adverb makrothymos, meaning "with long-suffering, with patience, forbearingly." The word is composed of makros ("long") and thymos ("passion, spirit"). Paul is asking for extended, patient attention -- not a quick hearing but a thorough one. The request implicitly acknowledges that what he has to say will take time and will require engagement, not dismissal.

## Verses 4-5: Paul's Jewish Identity and Pharisaic Credentials

"So then, all Jews know my way of life since my youth, which from the beginning was spent among my own nation and in Jerusalem, since they have known about me for a long time, if they are willing to testify, that I lived as a Pharisee according to the strictest sect of our religion" (26:4-5).

Paul establishes his identity. The phrase "my way of life" (ten biosin mou) uses a word (biosis) that appears only here in the New Testament -- it means "manner of living, way of conducting one's life." Paul is describing not a private belief system but a public, observable way of living that "all Jews know" (isasin pantes hoi Ioudaioi). His life has not been hidden or marginal; it has been lived in public, among his own people, in the national center of Jerusalem. The phrase "from the beginning" (ap' arches) and "since my youth" (ek neotetos) push the timeline back to the earliest stages of Paul's formation. His Jewish identity is not something acquired later or adopted strategically; it is the foundation of his entire existence.

The word "Pharisee" (Pharisaios) identifies Paul's affiliation within Jewish sectarian life. The Pharisees were one of the major haireseis ("sects, parties, schools") of Judaism, distinguished by their commitment to the oral tradition alongside the written Torah, their belief in bodily resurrection, and their affirmation of angels and spirits (23:8). The word "strictest" (akribestasten) is the superlative of akribes ("precise, exact, strict") -- the Pharisees are described as the most rigorous sect of Judaism. The word hairesis ("sect") here carries the same neutral meaning it has throughout Acts (5:17, 15:5, 24:5, 24:14) -- a distinguishable party within a larger tradition, not a pejorative label.

The conditional clause "if they are willing to testify" (ean thelosin martyrein) is a forensic challenge. Paul's accusers know his background; the question is whether they will acknowledge it. The conditional ean with the subjunctive expresses real possibility: they can testify to this, if they choose to. The implication is that his accusers' knowledge of Paul's Pharisaic credentials undermines their case. A man who lived according to the strictest sect of Judaism is not plausibly accused of being an enemy of Judaism.

Paul made the same claim of Pharisaic identity before the Sanhedrin: "I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees" (23:6). Before the Jerusalem crowd, he described his education "under Gamaliel, strictly according to the law of our fathers" (22:3). The consistency of this identification across three different audiences establishes that Paul does not regard his encounter with the risen Jesus as having cancelled his Pharisaic identity. He is still a Pharisee -- one who believes what the Pharisees believe, including and especially the resurrection of the dead. What has changed is the content of the hope: the resurrection the Pharisees await has begun in one person.

## Verses 6-8: The Hope of Israel and the Question of Resurrection

"And now I am standing trial for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers; the promise to which our twelve tribes hope to attain, as they earnestly serve God night and day. For this hope, O king, I am being accused by Jews" (26:6-7).

These verses constitute the thesis of Paul's defense. The word "hope" (elpis) appears three times in two verses, establishing the central theme. Paul's trial is not about sedition, not about temple defilement, not about political subversion. It is about "the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers" (tes eis tous pateras hemon epangelias genomenes hypo tou theou).

The phrase "the promise made by God to our fathers" (tes... epangelias... hypo tou theou) reaches back through the entire scriptural tradition. The word epangelia ("promise") in Acts refers to the comprehensive divine commitment that runs from Abraham forward: "the promise made to the fathers" (13:32), "the promise of the Holy Spirit" (2:33), "the promise" that is "for you and for your children and for all who are far off" (2:39). The promise encompasses the land, the offspring, the blessing to all nations (Genesis 12:1-3, 15:1-6, 17:1-8), the covenant with David (2 Samuel 7:12-16), and the prophetic vision of restoration and resurrection. Paul is not claiming a novel hope; he is claiming the ancestral hope of Israel itself.

The phrase "our twelve tribes" (to dodekaphulon hemon) is remarkable. The word dodekaphulon ("twelve-tribed") appears only here in the New Testament. It refers to the entire nation of Israel -- all twelve tribes, not just the two (Judah and Benjamin) that survived the Assyrian exile and constituted the recognizable Jewish population of the first century. The ten northern tribes, lost after the Assyrian deportation in 722 BCE, had become a subject of eschatological hope: the prophets envisioned the restoration of all Israel, the reunion of the divided nation. Ezekiel 37:15-28 describes the two sticks -- Judah and Israel -- being joined into one in God's hand. Isaiah 11:11-12 promises that the Lord "will assemble the banished ones of Israel and will gather the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth." By invoking "our twelve tribes," Paul claims that the hope for which he is on trial is not the private conviction of a marginal sect but the national expectation of the totality of Israel. The twelve tribes -- not just the Pharisees, not just the Jerusalem establishment, but the entire covenant people -- share this hope.

"As they earnestly serve God night and day" (en ekteneia nykta kai hemeran latreuon). The word ekteneia ("earnestness, intensity, fervor") describes the quality of Israel's worship. The phrase "night and day" (nykta kai hemeran) echoes the perpetual worship of the temple, where priestly courses served in continuous rotation. It also echoes Anna the prophetess, who "did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:37) -- and Anna was specifically one who was looking for "the redemption of Jerusalem" (Luke 2:38). The verb latreuon ("serving, worshiping") is the standard LXX word for the worship of Israel's God (Exodus 23:25: latreuseis kyrio to theo sou; Deuteronomy 6:13: kyrion ton theon sou phobethese kai auto latreuseis). Paul is describing a people whose entire existence is oriented toward the fulfillment of God's promise -- and claiming that the fulfillment for which they serve is what he preaches.

"For this hope, O king, I am being accused by Jews" (peri hes elpidos enkalomai hypo Ioudaion). The irony is sharp. Paul is accused by Jews for holding the hope that defines Judaism. The people whose twelve tribes serve God night and day for this very promise are prosecuting the man who claims the promise has been fulfilled. The word enkalomai ("I am accused, I am charged") is a legal term -- Paul frames the absurdity in juridical language.

"Why is it considered incredible among you people if God raises the dead?" (26:8).

The question -- ti apiston krinetai par' hymin ei ho theos nekrous egeirei -- is addressed to "you people" (par' hymin), which in context encompasses the entire mixed audience: Agrippa, Festus, Bernice, the military commanders, the civic leaders. But the question operates differently for different hearers.

For those with knowledge of Israel's scriptures -- Agrippa above all -- the question invokes precedent. God has raised the dead before: Elijah raised the widow's son at Zarephath (1 Kings 17:17-24), Elisha raised the Shunammite's son (2 Kings 4:32-37), and the dry bones of Ezekiel's vision were restored to life (Ezekiel 37:1-14). The second benediction of the Amidah -- the daily Jewish prayer recited in every synagogue -- blesses God as the one "who revives the dead" (mechayeh hametim). Resurrection is not an alien concept in Jewish faith; it is woven into worship, scripture, and prophetic vision. For a Jew to find resurrection "incredible" (apiston -- literally "unbelievable, not worthy of trust") would be to contradict the tradition that defines Jewish faith.

For the Roman and Greek hearers -- Festus, the military officers -- the question is more challenging. The Greek philosophical tradition generally regarded the body as a prison from which the soul escaped at death; bodily resurrection was not a Greek hope but a Greek absurdity. When Paul preached resurrection in Athens, the philosophers "sneered" (17:32). But Paul does not accommodate the Greek perspective here; he simply asks the question and lets it stand.

The rhetorical function of verse 8 is to bridge verses 6-7 and what follows. Paul has identified the hope of Israel as the subject of his trial. He now identifies resurrection as the specific content of that hope. And the rest of the speech will demonstrate that Jesus's resurrection is the fulfillment of that hope. The logical chain is: Israel hopes for the promise to the fathers; that promise includes the resurrection of the dead; God has raised Jesus from the dead; therefore, what Paul preaches is what Israel hopes for. The accusation against Paul is an accusation against Israel's own hope.

## Verses 9-11: Paul's Former Life as Persecutor

"So I thought to myself that I had to act in strong opposition to the name of Jesus of Nazareth. And this is just what I did in Jerusalem; not only did I lock up many of the saints in prisons, after receiving authority from the chief priests, but I also cast my vote against them when they were being put to death. And as I punished them often in all the synagogues, I tried to force them to blaspheme; and since I was extremely enraged at them, I kept pursuing them even to foreign cities" (26:9-11).

Paul now describes his life before the Damascus road. The transition from verse 8 is significant: having asked why resurrection is incredible, Paul immediately recounts his own former conviction that it was incredible -- so incredible that he devoted himself to destroying the movement that proclaimed it. Paul was not neutral about Jesus's resurrection; he was violently opposed to it. The shift that follows -- from persecutor to proclaimer -- is therefore all the more dramatic precisely because the opposition was so extreme.

The phrase "I thought to myself" (ego men oun edoxa emautoo) uses the verb dokeo ("to think, to suppose, to consider") -- Paul is describing a sincere conviction, not a cynical calculation. He genuinely believed he was obligated to oppose "the name of Jesus of Nazareth" (to onoma Iesou tou Nazoraiou). The word dei ("I had to" -- literally "it was necessary") is the same verb the Lord uses in 23:11 for divine necessity: "you must testify in Rome." Paul's earlier sense of necessity was genuine but wrong; the Lord's declaration of necessity is genuinely right. The same word -- dei -- describes both Paul's misguided compulsion to destroy and God's purpose to send him. The irony is that the man who felt divine necessity to persecute now serves under divine necessity to proclaim.

The name "Jesus of Nazareth" (Iesou tou Nazoraiou) is the full identifying designation -- the same name Tertullus used when he called the movement "the sect of the Nazarenes" (24:5). It is the name Peter used in his earliest preaching (2:22, 3:6, 4:10) and the name the risen Jesus used to identify himself to Paul on the road: "I am Jesus the Nazarene, whom you are persecuting" (22:8).

Paul's description of his persecution is the most detailed in any of the three accounts. He lists five specific actions:

First, "I locked up many of the saints in prisons" (pollous te ton hagion ego en phylakais katekleisa). The word hagioi ("saints, holy ones") is Paul's standard term for believers -- those set apart by God. The verb katekleisa (from katakleio, "to shut up, to lock in, to imprison") is emphatic: not merely arrested but locked away. Paul received "authority from the chief priests" (ten para ton archiereon exousian labon) -- his persecution was not freelance violence but institutionally authorized action, carrying the formal sanction of the Jerusalem religious establishment.

Second, "I also cast my vote against them when they were being put to death" (anairoumenon te auton katenegka psephon). The phrase katenegka psephon literally means "I cast down a pebble" -- the psephos was the voting stone used in judicial proceedings. A white stone meant acquittal; a black stone meant condemnation. The phrase "when they were being put to death" (anairoumenon auton) uses the present passive participle of anaireo ("to kill, to do away with, to put to death") -- Paul voted for their execution. The plural "them" indicates that Stephen was not the only believer killed during this period; there were others. Whether Paul's "vote" indicates formal membership in the Sanhedrin or participation in local judicial proceedings is debated, but the meaning is clear: Paul actively endorsed and facilitated the killing of believers.

Third, "I punished them often in all the synagogues" (kata pasas tas synagogas pollakis timoroon autous). The verb timoreo means "to punish, to avenge, to impose penalty." The synagogues served as local judicial courts for the Jewish community, where disciplinary measures -- including flogging (the thirty-nine lashes, cf. 2 Corinthians 11:24) -- were administered. Paul conducted a systematic, repeated (pollakis, "often, many times") campaign of punishment across "all the synagogues" (pasas tas synagogas).

Fourth, "I tried to force them to blaspheme" (enankazon blasphemein). The verb anankazo means "to compel, to force, to coerce." The word blasphemeo means "to speak evil of, to revile, to blaspheme." Paul attempted to force believers to blaspheme -- presumably to curse Jesus or to renounce their confession that Jesus is Lord. The imperfect tense (enankazon, "I was forcing, I kept trying to force") indicates a sustained effort, not a single incident. The word "tried" in many translations reflects the conative imperfect: Paul kept attempting to force them, though the text does not say he always succeeded. The detail is chilling: Paul did not merely imprison or punish; he sought to destroy faith itself, to force the believers to deny their Lord under coercion.

Fifth, "I kept pursuing them even to foreign cities" (diookon eos kai eis tas exo poleis). The verb dioko ("to pursue, to persecute, to chase") describes a hunting metaphor -- Paul was tracking believers beyond the boundaries of Judea into "foreign cities" (literally "cities on the outside"), which is what the Damascus journey represents. The persecution was not contained within Jerusalem or Judea; it was an expanding campaign.

The phrase "since I was extremely enraged at them" (perissoos te emmainomenos autois) uses two intensifying elements: perissoos ("exceedingly, extremely, beyond measure") and emmainomenos (from emmainomai, "to be enraged against, to be mad at" -- the same root as mania, "madness"). Paul was not merely opposed to the movement; he was consumed by rage against it. The irony deepens when Festus later accuses Paul of madness (mania, 26:24): Paul will be called insane for proclaiming what he once was insane in opposing. The same vocabulary of madness applies to both his former persecution and his present preaching -- but in opposite directions.

The rhetorical purpose of this detailed self-indictment is twofold. First, it establishes credibility: a man who persecuted the movement with this intensity would not have joined it without overwhelming cause. Paul was not a gullible convert; he was the most hostile possible witness. Whatever changed him must have been extraordinary. Second, it creates the dramatic contrast that makes the Damascus road encounter the turning point of the speech: from verses 9-11 (persecution) to verses 12-18 (encounter and commission), the reader moves from maximum hostility to total transformation. The sharper the before, the more powerful the after.

## Verses 12-15: The Encounter on the Damascus Road

"While so engaged, as I was journeying to Damascus with the authority and commission of the chief priests, at midday, O king, I saw on the way a light from heaven, brighter than the sun, shining around me and those who were journeying with me" (26:12-13).

The phrase "while so engaged" (en hois -- literally "in which things, in the midst of which activities") connects the encounter directly to the persecution. Paul was on an active mission of destruction when the encounter occurred. The "authority and commission" (exousia kai epitrope) from the chief priests identifies this as an official operation -- Paul was carrying institutional authorization. The word epitrope ("commission, permission, full power") is stronger than mere permission; it is a delegated mandate.

"At midday" (hemeras meses -- literally "the middle of the day") is a detail that appears in the Acts 22 account ("about noon," meson hemeras, 22:6) but not in Acts 9. The detail matters: at midday, the sun is at its brightest. For a light to be visible at noon and described as "brighter than the sun" (hyper ten lamproteta tou heliou) means the light exceeded the most intense natural illumination possible. The word lamprotes ("brightness, brilliance, radiance") describes the quality of the light. In the LXX, heavenly light and divine glory are associated with the theophanic tradition -- God's visible presence. Ezekiel describes the glory of God as "like the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud on the day of rain, so was the appearance of the brightness all around" (Ezekiel 1:28). Daniel describes a heavenly figure: "his face was like the appearance of lightning" (Daniel 10:6). The light Paul describes is not merely bright; it is divine radiance, exceeding the most powerful natural light source.

"Shining around me and those who were journeying with me" (perilampsan me kai tous syn emoi poreuomenous). The light was not a private, internal experience. It was visible to the entire traveling party. This detail is consistent across all three accounts (9:3, 22:6, 26:13) and serves as evidence: multiple witnesses perceived the phenomenon.

"And when we had all fallen to the ground, I heard a voice saying to me in the Hebrew dialect, 'Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting Me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads'" (26:14).

This verse contains two details not present in the earlier accounts.

First, "in the Hebrew dialect" (te Hebraidi dialekto). This phrase appears in Acts 21:40 and 22:2 for Paul's speech to the Jerusalem crowd. In first-century usage, "the Hebrew dialect" (he Hebrais dialektos) most likely refers to Aramaic, the spoken vernacular of Palestinian Jews, though it could designate Hebrew proper. The significance for the present audience is that the voice spoke in the language of Israel. For Agrippa, who is expert in Jewish customs, this authenticates the encounter as a theophany in the tradition of Israel's God -- a God who speaks in Israel's language. The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob addresses Paul in the tongue of the covenant people.

Second, "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (skleron soi pros kentra laktizein). This saying does not appear in either the Acts 9 or Acts 22 accounts. The kentron (plural kentra) is a cattle goad -- a sharpened stick used to drive oxen. The verb laktizo means "to kick." The image is of an ox kicking backward against the prodding goad, which only drives the sharp point deeper into its flesh. Resistance is not merely futile; it is self-destructive. The expression is well attested in Greek literature: Euripides uses it in the Bacchae (795), where Dionysus warns Pentheus that it is better to sacrifice to the god "than to kick against the goads in anger"; Aeschylus uses it in the Agamemnon (1624); Pindar employs a similar image in the Pythian Odes (2.94-95). The saying would be immediately recognizable to the educated Greco-Roman members of Paul's audience -- the military commanders and civic leaders present at the hearing. But the image is fundamentally agricultural, drawn from the universal experience of livestock management, and the concept would be accessible to anyone in the ancient world. Paul renders the voice's words in a form that communicates across both Jewish and Greco-Roman cultural contexts.

Applied to Paul's situation, the saying means: your persecution of my followers is futile resistance against my purpose, and it only increases your own pain. Every arrest, every imprisonment, every attempt to force believers to blaspheme was a kick against the goad -- an action that appeared forceful but was actually self-inflicted suffering. The voice is telling Paul that a superior power has been directing events, and Paul's opposition has been resistance against that power. The word skleron ("hard, painful, harsh") describes the consequence for the one who resists, not the strength of the resistance.

"And I said, 'Who are You, Lord?' And the Lord said, 'I am Jesus whom you are persecuting'" (26:15).

The question "Who are You, Lord?" (tis ei, kyrie) is identical across all three accounts (9:5, 22:8, 26:15). The word kyrios ("lord") at this stage of the conversation may be merely a respectful address ("sir") -- Paul does not yet know who speaks. But the answer transforms its meaning: "I am Jesus whom you are persecuting" (ego eimi Iesous hon sy diokeis). The divine self-identification formula ego eimi ("I am") echoes the theophanic tradition of the Hebrew scriptures, where God identifies himself to Moses at the burning bush: "I am who I am" (Exodus 3:14, LXX: ego eimi ho on). The one who speaks from the heavenly light and uses the language of divine self-revelation is Jesus -- the dead man whom Paul's accusers said Paul "asserted to be alive" (25:19). The encounter answers Festus's bewildered summary: Jesus is not merely a dead man Paul claims is alive; he is the one who speaks from heaven, surrounded by divine glory, using the language of theophany.

The phrase "whom you are persecuting" (hon sy diokeis) identifies Paul's persecution of the believers as persecution of Jesus himself. The pronoun sy ("you") is emphatic -- you, Saul, personally. The present tense diokeis ("you are persecuting") describes an ongoing action. The identification of the community with Christ is absolute: to persecute the followers is to persecute the Lord. This identification has been consistent since Jesus's teaching: "Whoever receives you receives me" (Matthew 10:40); "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40). The risen Jesus on the Damascus road affirms the same principle: the community of believers is so intimately connected to him that violence against them is violence against him.

## Verses 16-18: The Commission

"But get up and stand on your feet; for this purpose I have appeared to you, to appoint you as a servant and a witness not only to the things in which you have seen Me, but also to the things in which I will appear to you, rescuing you from the Jewish people and from the Gentiles, to whom I am sending you, to open their eyes so that they may turn from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins and an inheritance among those who have been sanctified by faith in Me" (26:16-18).

These verses constitute the most detailed commission Paul presents in any of the three Damascus road accounts, and they are saturated with the language of Israel's prophetic tradition. Before analyzing the content, a structural observation is necessary. In the Acts 9 account, the commission was given indirectly -- the Lord spoke to Ananias about Paul: "he is a chosen instrument of Mine, to bear My name before the Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel" (9:15). In the Acts 22 account, Ananias delivered the commission to Paul: "The God of our fathers has appointed you to know His will and to see the Righteous One" (22:14), and the Gentile mission was announced later in a temple vision (22:17-21). In Acts 26, Paul compresses the commission into a single, direct speech from the risen Jesus on the road -- omitting Ananias entirely. This is not a contradiction with the earlier accounts but a rhetorical compression suited to the audience and occasion. Before Agrippa and the assembled dignitaries, Paul emphasizes the directness and divine authority of his appointment. The essential content -- what Paul was called to do -- is consistent across all three versions. The form of presentation highlights the unmediated encounter between the risen Lord and his appointed servant.

"Get up and stand on your feet" (anastethi kai stethi epi tous podas sou). The command echoes Ezekiel's commission. When the glory of God appeared to Ezekiel, the prophet fell on his face, and the Lord said: "Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you" (Ezekiel 2:1, LXX: huie anthropou, stethi epi tous podas sou, kai laleeso pros se). The verbal parallel -- stethi epi tous podas sou in both texts -- places Paul's commission in the tradition of prophetic calling. As Ezekiel was called to stand and receive his commission from the God of Israel, so Paul is called to stand and receive his commission from the risen Jesus. The reader familiar with the prophetic literature would recognize the pattern: a divine appearance, the human prostrate in response, the command to stand, the delivery of the commission.

"To appoint you as a servant and a witness" (procheirisasthai se hypereten kai martyra). The verb procheirisasthai (from procheirizomai, "to appoint, to determine beforehand, to choose for a task") appears in Acts 3:20 for God's appointment of the Messiah and in Acts 22:14 for Paul's own appointment. The word hyperetes ("servant, attendant, minister") means literally "an under-rower" -- one who serves under another's direction. In Luke-Acts, the word describes subordinate functionaries: synagogue attendants (Luke 4:20), officers of the Sanhedrin (Acts 5:22, 26), John Mark as Paul and Barnabas's assistant (Acts 13:5). Applied to Paul, it identifies his role as a subordinate agent -- he serves under the authority of the one who commissions him. The word martys ("witness") is the defining term of Acts -- the apostles are witnesses (1:8, 1:22, 2:32, 3:15, 5:32, 10:39, 10:41, 13:31). Paul is now enrolled in the same category: a witness to what he has seen and will see of the risen Jesus.

"Not only to the things in which you have seen Me, but also to the things in which I will appear to you" (hon te eides me hon te ophthesomai soi). The commission extends into the future. Paul is a witness not only to the Damascus road encounter but also to subsequent appearances and revelations that the Lord will grant. The verb ophthesomai ("I will appear") is the same verb used throughout the LXX and New Testament for divine appearances (Genesis 12:7: ophthe kyrios to Abram, "the Lord appeared to Abram"; Exodus 3:2: ophthe de auto angelos kyriou, "the angel of the Lord appeared to him"). The Lord promises ongoing revelation -- Paul's witness will not be limited to this single encounter.

"Rescuing you from the Jewish people and from the Gentiles, to whom I am sending you" (exairoumenos se ek tou laou kai ek ton ethnon eis hous ego apostello se). The verb exairoumenos (from exaireo, "to take out, to rescue, to deliver") is the same verb used throughout Acts for God's rescuing action in Israel's history: God "rescued" Joseph from all his afflictions (7:10); God came down to "rescue" Israel from Egypt (7:34, quoting Exodus 3:8); the angel "rescued" Peter from prison (12:11). The word places Paul's experience in the same pattern of divine rescue that defines Israel's national story. As God rescued Israel from Pharaoh, as God rescued Joseph from his brothers, so God will rescue Paul from the dangers he will face among both Jews and Gentiles. The promise of rescue does not promise the absence of danger; it promises preservation through danger -- which is exactly what the narrative of Acts will demonstrate through Paul's arrests, beatings, shipwreck, and survival.

The phrase "to whom I am sending you" (eis hous ego apostello se) uses the verb apostello -- the verb from which "apostle" (apostolos, "one sent") derives. Paul is being sent. The commission is not a suggestion or an invitation; it is an authoritative sending, from the Lord who has authority to send, to the peoples -- both Jewish and Gentile -- who need the message.

Verse 18 unfolds the content of the commission in language drawn directly from the Servant Songs of Isaiah.

"To open their eyes" (anoixai ophthalmous auton). Isaiah 42:7 (LXX): anoixai ophthalmous typhlon -- "to open blind eyes." The Servant of the Lord in Isaiah is commissioned to open blind eyes; now Paul is commissioned for the same task. The blindness is not physical but spiritual -- the inability to perceive God's action, the failure to recognize the fulfillment of the promise.

"So that they may turn from darkness to light" (tou epistrepsai apo skotous eis phos). Isaiah 42:16 (LXX): "I will lead the blind by a way they do not know... I will turn the darkness before them into light" (theso to skotos... eis phos). Isaiah 9:2 (LXX 9:1): "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (ho laos ho poreuomenos en skotei idete phos mega). The contrast between darkness and light is one of the most fundamental images in the prophetic tradition, representing ignorance and knowledge, bondage and freedom, death and life. The Servant's role -- and now Paul's -- is to facilitate the transition from one to the other.

"And from the power of Satan to God" (kai tes exousias tou Satana epi ton theon). The word exousia ("power, authority, domain") describes Satan's sphere of influence. The movement described is from one dominion to another -- from the authority under which Satan operates to the authority of God. This is not merely a change of belief but a transfer of allegiance, a change of lordship. The language echoes the exodus pattern: Israel was transferred from the power of Pharaoh to the covenant of God. The commission describes a new exodus, available to both Jews and Gentiles.

"That they may receive forgiveness of sins" (tou labein auton aphesin hamartion). The word aphesis ("release, forgiveness, remission") is the same word used in Isaiah 61:1 (LXX) for the proclamation of release to captives: keryxai aichmalotois aphesin -- "to proclaim release to the captives." In Luke 4:18-19, Jesus read this Isaiah passage in the Nazareth synagogue and declared: "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). The same word now appears in Paul's commission: the forgiveness of sins is the release from captivity that the Servant was sent to proclaim.

"And an inheritance among those who have been sanctified by faith in Me" (kai kleron en tois hegiasmenois pistei te eis eme). The word kleros ("lot, portion, inheritance, allotted share") is the standard LXX term for the inheritance of Israel. Each tribe received its kleros -- its allotted portion of the promised land (Joshua 14-19). The Levites had no territorial kleros because "the Lord God of Israel is their inheritance" (Joshua 13:33, LXX: kyrios ho theos Israel, houtos kleros auton). In Deuteronomy 32:9 (LXX): "For the Lord's portion is his people; Jacob is the allotted share of his inheritance" (hoti meris kyriou laos autou, Iakob schoinisma kleronomias autou). Daniel 12:13 promises Daniel: "you will rise for your allotted portion at the end of the days" (anastese eis ton kleron sou). The word kleros carries the full weight of Israel's covenant theology -- the inheritance God gives to his people.

The phrase "among those who have been sanctified" (en tois hegiasmenois) uses the perfect passive participle of hagiazo ("to make holy, to set apart, to sanctify"). In the LXX, this is the language of Israel's consecration: "You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2); "I am the Lord who sanctifies you" (Exodus 31:13, LXX: ego kyrios ho hagiazoon hymas). The "sanctified ones" are those whom God has set apart as his own people. Paul's commission opens the inheritance of Israel's sanctified community to all who are joined by "faith in Me" (pistei te eis eme). The inheritance is not redefined or replaced; it is opened. Those who come to faith in Jesus receive a share in the same inheritance that belongs to God's holy people. Gentiles who trust in the risen Christ receive a kleros -- an allotted portion -- among those whom God has set apart. The commission does not create a new people alongside Israel; it extends Israel's inheritance to those who enter through faith in Israel's risen Messiah.

The cumulative effect of verse 18 is to present Paul's mission as the fulfillment of the Servant's role in Isaiah. The Servant opens blind eyes, brings light to darkness, proclaims release to captives, and extends God's salvation to the nations. Paul's commission repeats these elements almost verbatim. Before Agrippa -- the expert in Jewish customs and questions -- Paul presents his Gentile mission not as a departure from Judaism but as the enactment of Isaiah's vision. The prophets predicted exactly what Paul does.

## Verses 19-21: Paul's Obedience to the Heavenly Vision

"For that reason, King Agrippa, I did not prove disobedient to the heavenly vision, but continually proclaimed to those in Damascus first, and in Jerusalem, and then all the region of Judea, and even to the Gentiles, that they are to repent and turn to God, performing deeds consistent with repentance" (26:19-20).

The phrase "I did not prove disobedient to the heavenly vision" (ouk egenomen apeithes te ouranioo optasia) uses the word apeithes ("disobedient, unwilling to be persuaded") -- a word that will resonate when Agrippa shortly after speaks of being "persuaded" (peitheis, 26:28). Paul was not apeithes to the vision; the question is whether Agrippa will be peithes (persuaded) by Paul's testimony. The word optasia ("vision, appearance") is used in Luke 1:22 for Zechariah's vision in the temple and in Luke 24:23 for the women's report of the angelic vision at the tomb. The qualifier "heavenly" (ouranioo) identifies its source: this was not a dream or a hallucination but a communication from heaven itself.

Paul describes the content and geography of his proclamation. The geography follows a pattern: "those in Damascus first, and in Jerusalem, and then all the region of Judea, and even to the Gentiles" (tois en Damasko proton te kai Hierosolymois, pasan te ten choran tes Ioudaias kai tois ethnesin). This sequence echoes the programmatic statement of Acts 1:8: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." Paul's itinerary mirrors the apostolic mission's expanding geography, starting from Damascus (the site of his commission) and widening to Jerusalem, Judea, and finally the Gentiles. The word "even" (kai) before "to the Gentiles" marks the final and most expansive circle: the message that began in Israel's heartland reaches the nations.

The content of the proclamation is described with three verbs: "repent" (metanoein), "turn to God" (epistrephein epi ton theon), and "perform deeds consistent with repentance" (axia tes metanoias erga prassontas).

The verb metanoeo ("to repent, to change one's mind, to turn around") describes a fundamental reorientation of thought and allegiance. The verb epistrepho ("to turn, to turn around, to return") describes the movement that repentance produces -- a turning from one direction to another, specifically a turning toward God. Together, the two verbs describe a complete reversal: the mind changes (metanoeo), and the life follows (epistrepho).

The phrase "performing deeds consistent with repentance" (axia tes metanoias erga prassontas) echoes John the Baptist's preaching directly. In Luke 3:8, John declared: "Therefore bring forth fruits in keeping with repentance" (poiesate oun karpous axious tes metanoias). The word axia ("worthy, befitting, consistent with") demands that the external life match the internal transformation. Repentance that produces no corresponding action is not repentance. John required it; Paul preaches it. The continuity between John's proclamation and Paul's is explicit: both demand a repentance that is visible in deeds. The Paul of Acts does not preach a faith that is severed from conduct. He preaches repentance that turns toward God and produces action consistent with that turning.

"For these reasons some Jews seized me in the temple and tried to murder me" (26:21).

The phrase "for these reasons" (heneka touton) links the hostility directly to the content of Paul's proclamation. Paul was not seized for committing a crime but for preaching repentance, turning to God, and performing deeds consistent with repentance -- to both Jews and Gentiles. The word "seized" (syllabomenoi -- from syllambano, "to seize, to arrest, to lay hold of") refers to the events of Acts 21:27-30, where Jews from Asia grabbed Paul in the temple and dragged him out. The phrase "tried to murder me" (epeiroonto diacheirisasthai) uses the verb diacheirizomai, which means "to lay violent hands on, to kill" -- the same verb used in Acts 5:30 for the killing of Jesus: "whom you killed by hanging on a tree" (hon hymeis diecheirisasthe kremasantes epi xylou). The word carries the connotation of hands-on, physical violence. Paul's opponents did not merely accuse him; they attempted to kill him with their own hands.

## Verses 22-23: Paul's Message as the Fulfillment of Moses and the Prophets

"So, having obtained help from God, I stand to this day testifying both to small and great, stating nothing but what the Prophets and Moses said was going to take place, as to whether the Christ was to suffer, and whether, as first from the resurrection of the dead, He would proclaim light both to the Jewish people and to the Gentiles" (26:22-23).

The phrase "having obtained help from God" (epikourias oun tychon tes apo tou theou) uses the word epikourias ("help, aid, assistance"), which appears only here in the New Testament. The word comes from epikouros ("helper, ally, one who comes to aid"). Paul attributes his survival -- from the Damascus road to the present moment in Agrippa's hearing room -- to divine assistance. The God who commissioned him on the road has sustained him through every danger. The phrase "I stand to this day" (hesteka achri tes hemeras tautes) emphasizes continuity: despite imprisonment, beatings, assassination plots, and shipwreck (all narrated or anticipated in Acts), Paul continues to stand. The verb hesteka (perfect of histemi, "to stand") denotes a settled, ongoing posture -- Paul is still standing, still testifying.

"Both to small and great" (mikro te kai megalo) -- Paul's audience has included people of every social rank. The word "small" (mikros) encompasses the ordinary, the humble, the insignificant; "great" (megalos) encompasses the powerful, the prominent, the ruling class. Paul has preached to slaves and to governors, to merchants and to kings. The present audience -- Agrippa, Festus, Bernice, military commanders, civic leaders -- is the most concentrated gathering of "great" Paul has yet addressed.

"Stating nothing but what the Prophets and Moses said was going to take place" (ouden ektos legon hon te hoi prophetai elaleesan mellonton ginesthai kai Moouses). This is Paul's central forensic claim: everything he preaches was already predicted in the Jewish scriptures. The word ektos means "outside, beyond, apart from." Paul claims he has not gone beyond the prophetic and Mosaic writings. His message is not innovation; it is interpretation. He has not added to the scriptures; he has proclaimed their fulfillment. The order "the Prophets and Moses" (rather than the more conventional "Moses and the Prophets") may reflect the rhetorical emphasis of the speech: the prophetic vision of the Servant, the suffering Messiah, and the light to the nations has been the dominant scriptural theme of Paul's address. Moses is mentioned second not because he is less important but because the prophetic fulfillment is the climactic point.

Verse 23 compresses Paul's entire theological message into three claims, each introduced by the word "whether" (ei), framing them as questions that the scriptures address:

First: "whether the Christ was to suffer" (ei pathetos ho Christos). The adjective pathetos ("subject to suffering, capable of suffering, destined to suffer") appears only here in the entire New Testament. It raises a question that divided first-century Jewish interpretation: Is the Messiah a suffering figure? The dominant messianic expectation in Second Temple Judaism envisioned a royal, victorious descendant of David who would defeat Israel's enemies and restore the kingdom -- a conquering king, not a suffering servant. But certain scriptural texts pointed in a different direction. Isaiah 53 describes a servant who is "despised and rejected," who "was pierced for our transgressions" and "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:3-5). Psalm 22 describes one who cries "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" and whose hands and feet are pierced (Psalm 22:1, 16). Zechariah 12:10 speaks of the one "whom they have pierced." Daniel 9:26 speaks of an anointed one who "will be cut off." Paul's claim is that these texts describe the Messiah and that Jesus's suffering fulfills them. The question "whether the Christ was to suffer" is a hermeneutical question -- a question about how to read the scriptures. Paul answers it by pointing to Jesus.

Second: "whether, as first from the resurrection of the dead" (ei protos ex anastaseos nekron). The phrase protos ex anastaseos nekron does not mean "the first person ever brought back to life" -- Elijah raised the widow's son (1 Kings 17), Elisha raised the Shunammite's son (2 Kings 4), and Jesus himself raised Lazarus (John 11). It means "the first to rise in the eschatological resurrection" -- the first instance of the final, end-time resurrection that the Pharisees and the prophets anticipated. Paul makes the same argument in 1 Corinthians 15:20-23: "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep... Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ." Jesus's resurrection is not a one-off miracle; it is the inauguration of the general resurrection, the beginning of the new age. This is why Paul's preaching of Jesus's resurrection is the fulfillment of the Pharisaic hope, not its contradiction: the resurrection the Pharisees await has already begun.

Third: "He would proclaim light both to the Jewish people and to the Gentiles" (phos mellei katangelein to te lao kai tois ethnesin). The risen, exalted Christ "proclaims light" to both Israel and the nations. This connects directly to the Servant Songs of Isaiah. Isaiah 49:6 (LXX): "I will also make you a light for the nations, so that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth" (dedoka se eis phos ethnon, tou einai se eis soterian heos eschatou tes ges). Isaiah 42:6 (LXX): "I have given you as a covenant for the people, a light for the nations" (eis phos ethnon). The risen Christ fulfills the Servant's role: he is the light that illuminates both Israel (to lao -- "the people," the standard LXX designation for Israel as God's chosen people) and the Gentiles (tois ethnesin -- "the nations"). The phrase "both... and" (te... kai) insists on the universality: the light is not for one group at the expense of the other. It is for the people and for the nations. Paul's Gentile mission is not a betrayal of Israel but the fulfillment of Israel's own prophetic destiny -- to be a light to the nations through the Messiah.

The three claims of verse 23, taken together, present a complete Christological summary: the Messiah suffers (Isaiah 53, Psalm 22), rises as the firstfruits of the dead (Daniel 12:2, the Pharisaic hope), and through his risen life proclaims light to all peoples (Isaiah 42, 49). Paul insists that all of this was predicted by Moses and the Prophets. He is not introducing new theology; he is reading the scriptures and pointing to their fulfillment.

## Verses 24-26: Festus's Interruption and Paul's Response

"While Paul was stating these things in his defense, Festus said in a loud voice, 'Paul, you are out of your mind! Your great learning is driving you insane'" (26:24).

Festus's interruption comes at the precise moment Paul reaches the climax of his theological argument -- the claim that the Messiah suffered, rose from the dead, and proclaims light to all peoples. For Festus, this is the breaking point. A dead man who is alive, who is the first instance of a general resurrection, who proclaims light to both Jews and Gentiles from beyond the grave -- this is beyond anything the Roman governor's intellectual framework can accommodate.

The phrase "in a loud voice" (megale te phone) indicates that Festus did not whisper an aside. He shouted. The verb phesi ("he said, he declared") is in the present tense -- vivid, immediate narration. Festus's outburst is spontaneous and emotional, breaking the decorum of the formal hearing.

"You are out of your mind" (maine, Paule) -- the verb mainomai means "to be mad, to be insane, to rave." The word is related to mania ("madness, frenzy"). Festus is not making a clinical diagnosis; he is expressing incredulity. The claim about a dead man rising and proclaiming light sounds like the speech of a deranged person to a Roman administrator with no scriptural background.

"Your great learning is driving you insane" (ta polla se grammata eis manian peritrepei). The word grammata ("letters, writings, learning") refers to Paul's evident familiarity with Jewish scriptures and literary tradition. The word polla ("many, much, extensive") acknowledges the breadth of Paul's education. The verb peritrepo ("to turn around, to overturn, to drive to") describes learning as a force that has tipped Paul over the edge from rationality into madness. Festus's accusation is simultaneously a compliment and a dismissal: Paul is clearly learned, but his learning has led him to believe absurd things. The irony -- visible to the reader if not to Festus -- is that Paul described his own former state with the same vocabulary of madness: "I was extremely enraged" (perissoos emmainomenos, 26:11), using the same root (mainomai). Paul was mad when he persecuted; Festus says he is mad now that he proclaims. The vocabulary of madness applies to both states -- but in only one of them was Paul actually opposing God's purpose.

"But Paul said, 'I am not insane, most excellent Festus; on the contrary, I am speaking out with truthful and rational words'" (26:25).

Paul addresses Festus by his title -- "most excellent" (kratiste, the formal address for a governor of equestrian rank, the same title used for Theophilus in Luke 1:3 and for Felix in 24:3). The response is measured and courteous, not combative. Paul denies the charge of madness and counters with two adjectives that describe his speech.

"Truthful" (aletheias -- genitive of aletheia, "truth") -- Paul's words correspond to reality. They are not fictions or delusions.

"Rational" (sophrosynes -- genitive of sophrosyne, "soundness of mind, self-control, moderation, sanity"). The word sophrosyne is one of the cardinal Greek virtues -- the opposite of mania. In Greek philosophical tradition, sophrosyne was the quality of a well-ordered mind, the capacity for balanced judgment and self-governance. Plato placed it alongside wisdom, courage, and justice as the foundational virtues (Republic 4.427e-434d). Paul uses the language of Greek virtue to refute the charge of Greek-recognized madness: what he speaks is not mania but sophrosyne -- not raving but rational discourse. The choice of this word is audience-sensitive: Festus, as a Roman administrator educated in Greco-Roman culture, would recognize sophrosyne as the opposite of the condition he diagnosed.

"For the king knows about these matters, and I also speak to him with confidence, since I am persuaded that none of these things escape his notice; for this has not been done in a corner" (26:26).

Paul pivots from Festus to Agrippa. The phrase "the king knows about these matters" (epistatai gar peri touton ho basileus) uses the verb epistatai (from epistamai, "to know, to understand, to be acquainted with") -- a word of confident knowledge. Agrippa has the scriptural and cultural framework to evaluate Paul's claims. What sounds like madness to Festus is intelligible to Agrippa because Agrippa knows the prophets, the hope of Israel, and the tradition of resurrection.

"I am persuaded that none of these things escape his notice" (ou gar estin en gonia pepragmenon touto -- literally "for this has not been done in a corner"). The phrase en gonia ("in a corner") means "in secret, in obscurity, in a hidden place." The events Paul describes -- the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus, the spread of the movement throughout the empire -- have not occurred in a hidden corner of the world. They have been public events: Jesus was executed by the Roman governor Pontius Pilate in Jerusalem, the capital of Judea; the movement has spread through every province of the empire; Paul's own trial before multiple governors is itself a public event. Paul is arguing that the facts are available for examination. This is not a mystery cult whose rites are performed in secret; this is a movement whose foundational events are a matter of public record. Agrippa, with his knowledge of Jewish affairs and his connections to the temple establishment, would be aware of the events Paul references.

## Verses 27-29: The Exchange with Agrippa

"King Agrippa, do you believe the Prophets? I know that you believe" (26:27).

Paul's question is direct and rhetorically devastating. By asking Agrippa whether he believes the Prophets, Paul places the king in a dilemma. If Agrippa says yes -- which his public identity as a Jewish king with custodianship of the temple requires -- then Paul's next step is to demonstrate that the Prophets predicted exactly what Paul proclaims: a suffering Messiah, the resurrection of the dead, and light to the nations. If Agrippa says no, he undermines his own standing as an expert in Jewish affairs and as custodian of the temple. Paul does not wait for the answer; he supplies it: "I know that you believe" (oida hoti pisteueis). The verb oida ("I know") expresses certainty. Paul has closed the trap: Agrippa believes the Prophets, and the Prophets predicted what Paul preaches. The logical conclusion is available but unstated: Agrippa should therefore accept Paul's message.

"Agrippa replied to Paul, 'In a short time you are going to persuade me to make a Christian of myself'" (26:28).

The Greek of Agrippa's response -- en oligo me peitheis Christianon poiesai -- is among the most debated sentences in Acts. The phrase en oligo can mean "in a short time," "with little effort," "with so few words," or "in brief." The verb peitheis ("you are persuading, you are trying to persuade") can carry either a successful sense ("you are persuading me") or a conative sense ("you are trying to persuade me"). The word Christianon ("a Christian") appears only three times in the entire New Testament: Acts 11:26 (the disciples were first called Christians in Antioch), here, and 1 Peter 4:16. The name was coined by outsiders -- it is a Latin-form designation (the suffix -ianos indicates a partisan or adherent, as Herodianos means "a Herodian, a partisan of Herod") applied to the followers of Christos. Agrippa uses the outsider's label for the movement.

The overall force of Agrippa's statement is ambiguous. It could be read as genuine near-persuasion: "In short order you are going to make a Christian of me." It could be read as ironic deflection: "Do you think with so few words you can make me a Christian?" It could be read as amused acknowledgment of Paul's rhetorical skill: "You almost have me persuaded." The ambiguity may be deliberate on the author's part. Agrippa is caught between the force of Paul's argument (which is based on prophets he acknowledges) and the social and political impossibility of publicly aligning himself with the movement. His response occupies the space between intellectual recognition and personal commitment -- the space where Felix also resided, "alarmed" by Paul's reasoning about righteousness and judgment but unwilling to act on what he heard (24:25).

"And Paul said, 'I would wish to God that even in a short or long time not only you, but also all who hear me this day would become such as I myself am, except for these chains'" (26:29).

Paul takes Agrippa's statement at its highest possible value and raises it. Whether Agrippa meant it as near-persuasion or as irony, Paul responds with a genuine wish (euxaimen an -- the optative mood expressing a wish, the most courteous and literary form of expression in Greek) directed "to God" (to theo). Paul wishes that Agrippa and everyone in the room would become "such as I myself am" (toioutous hopoios kai ego eimi). The phrase is comprehensive: not merely believers, not merely sympathizers, but what Paul is -- a servant and witness of the risen Jesus, one who has received forgiveness of sins and an inheritance among the sanctified.

"Except for these chains" (parektos ton desmon touton). The exception is both literal and rhetorical. Paul is in chains -- the physical reality of his imprisonment. He does not wish imprisonment on anyone. But the exception is also a statement about Paul's condition: apart from the chains, Paul's situation is one he would wish on everyone. The man in chains, standing before the king in his pomp and the governor in his authority, claims to possess something more valuable than their freedom, their power, and their status. The prisoner presents himself as the one in the room whose condition is most desirable. The contrast with the "great pomp" (phantasia, 25:23) of Agrippa's entrance is total: the spectacle of royal power is set against the substance of a man in chains who has the inheritance of the sanctified and the commission of the risen Lord.

## Verses 30-32: The Verdict

"The king stood up and the governor and Bernice, and those who were sitting with them, and when they had gone out, they began talking to one another, saying, 'This man is not doing anything deserving death or imprisonment'" (26:30-31).

The hearing ends not with a verdict delivered from the bench but with a private conversation among the dignitaries after they withdraw. The verb aneste ("stood up") signals the formal conclusion of the hearing. The king stands first -- confirming that he has been presiding -- followed by the governor, Bernice, and the assembled company.

The verdict is stated as a consensus: "they began talking to one another" (elaloun pros allelous). The word allelous ("to one another, among themselves") indicates a group discussion, not a pronouncement. Everyone in the room reaches the same conclusion: "This man is not doing anything deserving death or imprisonment" (ouden thanatou e desmon axion ti prassei ho anthropos houtos).

The phrase "deserving death or imprisonment" (axion thanatou e desmon) echoes the assessments of every previous Roman official. Claudius Lysias wrote that Paul was "charged with nothing deserving death or imprisonment" (23:29). Festus declared that Paul had committed "nothing deserving death" (25:25). Now the entire assembled company -- king, governor, consort, military commanders, civic leaders -- reaches the same conclusion. The pattern is complete and cumulative. Not a single Roman official or Herodian ruler who examines Paul's case finds him guilty of anything warranting punishment. The author has built a wall of judicial findings, each confirming the last, each more emphatic than the one before. The unanimity is the author's demonstration that the Way is not a criminal enterprise, not a political threat, not a violation of any law -- Roman or Jewish. Paul is innocent. Everyone who examines the facts agrees.

The parallel with the verdict on Jesus in Luke's Gospel is unmistakable. Pilate declared three times: "I find no guilt in this man" (Luke 23:4, 14, 22). Herod Antipas found nothing deserving death (Luke 23:15). Now another Herodian -- Agrippa II, Antipas's grand-nephew -- reaches the same verdict about Paul. The Herodian dynasty encounters the message about Jesus across generations. Antipas found Jesus innocent but sent him back to Pilate; Agrippa finds Paul innocent but cannot release him because of the appeal. In both cases, the finding of innocence does not produce freedom. Innocence is established but does not govern the outcome. The machinery of political convenience overrides the verdict in both instances.

"And Agrippa said to Festus, 'This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar'" (26:32).

Agrippa's final statement -- apelolyto ho anthropos houtos ei me epekekeleto Kaisara -- is the chapter's closing irony and the narrative's definitive declaration. The verb apelolyto ("could have been released") is the imperfect passive of apolyo ("to release, to set free, to dismiss") -- the same verb used for Pilate's desire to release Jesus (Luke 23:20: theloon apolusai ton Iesoun) and for Barabbas's actual release (Luke 23:25: ton de Iesoun paredoken to thelemati auton). The word carries the weight of all the releases that did and did not happen in Luke-Acts.

Paul "could have been set free" -- but the appeal to Caesar has transferred jurisdiction. Once invoked, the provocatio ad Caesarem cannot be revoked by the provincial governor. The case now belongs to the Emperor. Paul's appeal, which was his only means of escaping Festus's willingness to trade justice for political favor (25:9), has now become the mechanism that prevents his release. The appeal both saved him (from the Jerusalem transfer and the ambush that awaited) and bound him (to the journey to Rome and the Emperor's tribunal).

But within the author's narrative framework, the irony reverses again. The Lord declared in 23:11: "As you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome." The dei -- the divine necessity -- requires that Paul reach Rome. Agrippa's statement of what "could have been" (release) is simultaneously the author's statement of what must be (Rome). Paul could have been freed from chains, but he could not have been freed from the divine commission. The appeal to Caesar, which prevents the release Agrippa's verdict would justify, is the instrument through which the Lord's declared purpose is accomplished. Agrippa sees a prisoner who could have been free; the author sees a witness being carried to his appointed destination.

## The Structure and Argument of the Chapter

Acts 26 is organized around Paul's defense speech (vv. 2-23), framed by the permission to speak (v. 1) and the responses to the speech (vv. 24-32). The speech itself follows a carefully constructed sequence that places the Damascus road encounter at its structural center:

A. Opening: address to Agrippa, appeal to his expertise (vv. 2-3)
B. Paul's Jewish identity and Pharisaic credentials (vv. 4-5)
C. The core issue: the hope of Israel, the promise to the fathers, resurrection (vv. 6-8)
D. Paul's former life as persecutor of the movement (vv. 9-11)
E. The Damascus road encounter and commission (vv. 12-18) -- the center
D'. Paul's new life as proclaimer (vv. 19-20)
C'. The consequence: seized for preaching this hope (v. 21)
B'. Paul's message is from Moses and the Prophets (vv. 22-23)
A'. Final exchange with Agrippa, appeal to his knowledge (vv. 27-29)

The persecution (D) is mirrored by the proclamation (D'). The hope of Israel (C) is mirrored by Paul's arrest for that hope (C'). Paul's Jewish identity (B) is mirrored by his insistence that his message comes from Moses and the Prophets (B'). The appeal to Agrippa's expertise at the opening (A) is mirrored by the direct appeal to Agrippa's belief in the prophets at the close (A'). At the center stands the encounter with the risen Jesus -- the turning point of Paul's life placed at the structural turning point of his speech.

Several themes converge in this chapter.

First, the continuity between Israel's hope and Paul's proclamation. Paul does not present himself as the founder of a new religion or the defector from an old one. He presents himself as the inheritor of Israel's deepest expectation. The hope of the promise to the fathers, the hope of the twelve tribes, the predictions of Moses and the Prophets -- Paul claims all of it. The resurrection of Jesus is not an innovation but the actualization of what Israel has always hoped for. The Gentile mission is not a departure from Judaism but the fulfillment of the Servant's commission in Isaiah. The charge against Paul is, by his argument, an accusation against the prophets themselves. If Moses and the Prophets predicted a suffering Messiah, a resurrection, and a light to the nations, then prosecuting Paul for proclaiming these things is prosecuting the scriptures.

Second, the three responses to Paul's message model three postures toward the gospel. Festus responds with incredulity: "You are out of your mind!" (v. 24). The Roman administrator lacks the scriptural framework to process the claims about a suffering, rising, light-proclaiming Messiah. What he hears is madness -- a learned man who has studied himself into delusion. Festus represents the response of those who have no categories for the message. Agrippa responds with recognition that falls short of commitment: "In a short time you are going to persuade me to make a Christian of myself" (v. 28). The Jewish king has the categories -- he knows the prophets, the customs, the questions -- but stands at the edge of personal response without stepping over. Felix exhibited the same pattern: "alarmed" by Paul's reasoning but ultimately unwilling to act (24:25). Agrippa represents the response of those who understand but do not yield. Paul, by contrast, embodies the response of full obedience: "I did not prove disobedient to the heavenly vision" (v. 19). He heard, he believed, he acted. The three responses -- rejection, hesitation, obedience -- are left standing without authorial commentary. The reader must decide which category they occupy.

Third, the pattern of divine purpose operating through human institutions. The hearing before Agrippa was convened for an administrative purpose: Festus needed help formulating charges for the Emperor (25:26). The hearing produces no charges. Instead, it produces a verdict of innocence (26:31) and a statement of what "could have been" (26:32). The administrative procedure that was supposed to help Festus write his report to Nero instead demonstrates that there is nothing to report -- Paul has committed no crime. The machinery of Roman and Herodian judicial investigation, operating according to its own logic and interests, confirms what the Lord already knew: Paul is not a criminal but a witness. The administrative hearing designed to serve the governor's paperwork needs becomes instead the platform for Paul's most comprehensive proclamation of the gospel before the most elevated audience he has yet addressed. Festus needed charges; Paul delivered theology. The institution's purpose was administrative; God's purpose was testimonial. Both purposes were served, though not in the way Festus intended.

Fourth, the three tellings of the Damascus road encounter. The author of Acts presents the conversion of Paul three times -- in the narrator's voice (chapter 9), in Paul's speech to the Jerusalem crowd (chapter 22), and in Paul's speech to Agrippa (chapter 26). Each version is tailored to its audience and rhetorical context. Before the Jerusalem crowd, Paul emphasized his Jewish credentials, described Ananias as "a devout man according to the Law, well spoken of by all the Jews" (22:12), and located the Gentile commission in a temple vision -- all details that grounded the story in Jewish legitimacy for a Jewish audience. Before Agrippa, Paul omits Ananias entirely, presents the commission as direct speech from the risen Jesus, includes the Greek proverb about kicking against goads, and saturates the commission with Isaianic servant language -- details suited to an audience that includes both a Jewish king who knows the prophets and Greco-Roman dignitaries who recognize literary allusion. The details differ because the audiences differ; the essential content -- Jesus appeared, commissioned Paul, sent him to Jews and Gentiles -- is consistent across all three. The triple telling also serves a narrative purpose: the repetition signals importance. What happens three times in a narrative demands attention. The author is telling the reader that this event -- the encounter between the risen Jesus and the former persecutor -- is not a sidebar in the story of Acts but its structural spine.

Finally, the chapter completes the arc of Paul's defense that began in Acts 22. Across four chapters of legal proceedings, Paul has stood before a Jewish mob (22:1-21), a Jewish council (23:1-9), a Roman governor (24:10-21), and now a Jewish king and Roman governor together (26:2-23). Each audience has heard a version of the same story, adapted to its capacity and concerns. The responses have ranged from murderous fury (22:22) through bureaucratic paralysis (24:22, 25:9-12) to the most comprehensive declaration of innocence in the entire narrative (26:31). The man whom the Jewish leadership wants dead is declared innocent by every Roman and Herodian authority who examines his case. Yet he remains in chains -- not because the system has found him guilty, but because the system cannot process what he is. He is not a criminal, but neither is he simply a citizen exercising his rights. He is a witness to the risen Messiah, carrying a commission from heaven, and no category in Roman law or Herodian diplomacy has a place for that. The system that finds him innocent also cannot release him, because the appeal that protects him from political manipulation is the same appeal that carries him to Rome. And Rome -- the seat of the power that governs the world -- is exactly where the Lord said he must go.
