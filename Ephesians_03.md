# Ephesians 3 -- Neutral Reading

## Context and Placement

**What the author was arguing immediately before (Ephesians 2):** The author opened chapter 2 by describing the shared pre-conversion condition of his audience: "you were dead in your trespasses and sins" (2:1), following the course of this world, under the ruler of the authority of the air, living in the desires of the flesh (2:1-3). The pronoun shifted at 2:3 to include the author and his Jewish compatriots: "among whom we also all formerly conducted ourselves in the desires of our flesh... and were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest." The "we" here is the Jewish "we" that has been operative since 1:3-12, now confessing the same condition as the gentile "you." Both groups were dead. Both were made alive together with Christ (2:5), raised together, and seated together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus (2:6). The purpose of this act was stated: "so that in the coming ages he might show the surpassing riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus" (2:7). Salvation was described as by grace through faith, not from works, "lest anyone should boast" (2:8-9), and the saved community was described as God's workmanship (poiema), created in Christ Jesus for good works (2:10).

At 2:11, the argument turned sharply to the specific situation of the gentile addressees. The author commanded them to remember their former status: "you, the gentiles in the flesh" (2:11), called "the uncircumcision" by those called "the circumcision." They were at that time "apart from Christ, alienated from the citizenship of Israel, strangers to the covenants of the promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (2:12). Five deprivations are listed, and all five are defined relative to Israel. The gentiles' problem was not merely moral corruption but exclusion from Israel's covenantal privileges.

The reversal came at 2:13: "But now in Christ Jesus you who were once far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ." The language of "far" and "near" echoes Isaiah 57:19 (LXX): "Peace, peace, to the far and to the near," which Paul will quote in 2:17. Christ "is our peace, who made both one and broke down the dividing wall of the partition" (2:14). The "both" (ta amphotera) are Jew and gentile. The "dividing wall" (to mesotoichon tou phragmou) likely alludes to the barrier in the Jerusalem temple that separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, beyond which no gentile could pass on pain of death. Josephus describes this barrier (War 5.193-194; Antiquities 15.417), and a Greek inscription from the temple warning gentiles against trespassing has been recovered archaeologically.

Christ abolished "the law of commandments in ordinances" (ton nomon ton entolon en dogmasin, 2:15) in order to create "in himself one new humanity" (hena kainon anthropon) out of the two, making peace, and to reconcile both to God "in one body through the cross" (2:16). He came and preached peace to "you who were far" and peace to "those who were near" (2:17, quoting Isaiah 57:19). Through him, "both have access in one Spirit to the Father" (2:18).

The chapter concluded with a series of architectural metaphors. The gentiles are "no longer strangers and aliens but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (2:19). The community is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (2:20). In him the whole structure grows into "a holy temple in the Lord" (naon hagion en kyrio, 2:21). And the addressees are being "built together into a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (2:22). The word synoikodomeisthe ("built together") is one of several syn- ("together with") compounds that define the chapter's argument: raised together (synegeiro, 2:6), made alive together (syzoopoieo, 2:5), and now built together. The image at the close of chapter 2 is of a temple under construction, a living structure where God dwells by the Spirit, composed of Jews and gentiles together.

**What the author argues immediately after (Ephesians 4):** Ephesians 4:1 opens with "I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, urge you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called." The word oun ("therefore") draws the ethical consequence from everything that has preceded. The self-identification as "the prisoner" (ho desmios) repeats the exact phrase from 3:1. The verb parakaleo ("I urge, I exhort") introduces the ethical half of the letter. Chapters 1-3 have laid the theological foundation; chapters 4-6 will draw the practical implications.

The first practical implication is unity. The qualities named -- humility, gentleness, patience, bearing with one another in love (4:2) -- serve the goal: "eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (4:3). The author then enumerates seven unities: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all (4:4-6). The "one body" is the body of 2:16 -- the single body in which Jew and gentile were reconciled. The "one Spirit" is the Spirit of 2:18 and 2:22 -- the Spirit through whom both have access to the Father and in whom the temple is being built. The "one hope" connects to the hope from which the gentiles were formerly excluded (2:12).

The author then described the gifts Christ gives to build up this one body (4:7-16): apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers, given "for the equipping of the saints for the work of ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ" (4:12). The temple-building imagery of 2:20-22 continues in the body-building language of 4:12-16. The goal is that the community would grow into maturity, "to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ" (4:13) -- a phrase that echoes the prayer in 3:19 ("that you may be filled to all the fullness of God").

**How chapter 3 connects these:** Ephesians 2 concluded with the image of a temple being built -- Jews and gentiles together as God's dwelling place in the Spirit. Ephesians 4 will begin drawing ethical implications and describing the gifts that build up this unified body. Chapter 3 stands between the theological exposition and the ethical exhortation, performing two functions. First, in 3:1-13, the author explains his own role in this drama: he is the steward of the mystery that the gentiles are co-heirs with Israel, and his imprisonment is itself part of that ministry. Second, in 3:14-21, the author prays that his audience would be empowered to comprehend and experience the full dimensions of this new reality. The chapter is therefore both autobiographical (explaining the apostle's commission) and intercessory (asking God to bring the audience into the experienced reality of what chapters 1-2 have declared). It provides the bridge from declaration to application: the mystery has been revealed, the steward has proclaimed it, now the prayer asks that the hearers would inhabit it -- which is exactly what chapters 4-6 will require of them.

## Verses 1-6: The Prisoner and the Mystery

"For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you, the gentiles--" (3:1).

The phrase toutou charin ("for this reason, on account of this") points backward to everything established in chapters 1-2, and specifically to the climactic image of 2:19-22: the gentiles being built together with Israel into a temple of God. The author is about to pray on the basis of this reality, but the mention of his situation -- as prisoner, as one who labors specifically for gentiles -- triggers a lengthy parenthetical explanation before the prayer resumes at 3:14.

The self-identification is striking: ego Paulos ("I, Paul"). The emphatic ego is unusual in Paul's letters; it signals that something about the apostle's personal situation is directly relevant to the theological argument. He calls himself ho desmios tou Christou Iesou ("the prisoner of Christ Jesus"). The genitive tou Christou Iesou is possessive: he is Christ Jesus' prisoner, not merely a prisoner who happens to follow Christ. His imprisonment belongs to Christ. The prepositional phrase hyper hymon ton ethnon ("on behalf of you, the gentiles") specifies the purpose and beneficiaries. The word hyper ("on behalf of, for the sake of") indicates that Paul's imprisonment is directly connected to his ministry to gentiles. The word ethnon ("gentiles, nations") is unambiguous: the audience is gentile, and the imprisonment is for their sake. This accords with the narrative of Acts, where Paul's arrest at the temple was precipitated by the accusation that he brought gentiles past the barrier into the inner courts (Acts 21:28-29) -- the very barrier that 2:14 described Christ as demolishing.

The sentence is grammatically incomplete. Paul begins "for this reason I, Paul..." and does not finish the sentence. Instead, the parenthetical explanation of verses 2-13 intervenes. The sentence is resumed at 3:14: "For this reason I bow my knees before the Father." The repetition of toutou charin at 3:14 signals the resumption.

"--if indeed you have heard of the stewardship of God's grace that was given to me for you" (3:2).

The conditional ei ge ("if indeed, assuming that") does not express doubt; like eiper in Romans 8:9, it assumes the truth of the condition for the sake of argument. The audience has heard of what Paul is about to describe. The word oikonomia ("stewardship, management of a household, administration, dispensation") is a key term in Ephesians. It appeared in 1:10 for God's plan: "a stewardship of the fullness of the times" (oikonomian tou pleromatos ton kairon) -- the arrangement by which God administers the culmination of his purposes. Here in 3:2, the oikonomia is "of God's grace" (tes charitos tou theou), and it was "given to me for you" (tes dotheises moi eis hymas). The stewardship is a trust: God entrusted Paul with an administration of grace directed toward the gentiles. The metaphor is household management: God is the householder (oikodespotes), Paul is the oikonomos (household steward, estate manager), and the gentiles are the ones toward whom this stewardship is directed.

The word charis ("grace") in Ephesians has appeared repeatedly: 1:2 (greeting), 1:6 ("the glory of his grace"), 1:7 ("the riches of his grace"), 2:5 ("by grace you have been saved"), 2:7 ("surpassing riches of his grace"), 2:8 ("by grace you have been saved through faith"). The grace is not an abstraction but the concrete action of God in including the gentiles in Israel's inheritance. Paul's stewardship is the management and distribution of this grace.

"--how that by revelation the mystery was made known to me, as I wrote briefly before" (3:3).

The word apokalypsin ("revelation, uncovering, disclosure") describes how Paul received the mystery: not by human instruction or rational deduction but by divine disclosure. The word mysterion ("mystery, secret, hidden purpose") is central to Ephesians. In the ancient world, a mysterion was not a puzzle to be solved by cleverness but a secret that could only be known if the one who held it chose to reveal it. In 1:9, God "made known to us the mystery of his will" (to mysterion tou thelematos autou). The mysterion in Ephesians is not the mere existence of God or the general idea of salvation; it is a specific content that has been hidden and is now disclosed. The content will be specified in verse 6.

The phrase kathos proegrapsa en oligo ("as I wrote briefly before") refers to what the author has already said in this same letter. The most likely reference is 1:9-10, where the mystery of God's will was mentioned as the plan to unite all things in Christ, and 2:11-22, where the practical content of that mystery -- the inclusion of gentiles as co-members with Israel -- was elaborated. The word oligo ("briefly, in a few words") indicates that the earlier treatment was compressed; the present passage will expand on it.

"--which, when you read, you are able to perceive my understanding in the mystery of Christ" (3:4).

The author directs attention to the text itself: when the audience reads (anaginoskontes) what he has written, they can perceive (noesai, aorist infinitive of noeo, "to perceive, to understand, to grasp with the mind") his synesis ("understanding, insight, comprehension") in the mystery. The phrase to mysterion tou Christou ("the mystery of Christ") names the mystery's central figure: it is Christ's mystery, the mystery that belongs to Christ and is disclosed in him. The genitive may be objective (the mystery about Christ), subjective (the mystery that Christ reveals), or possessive (the mystery that belongs to Christ's person and work). In context, all three dimensions converge: the mystery is about what God has done in Christ, it is revealed through Christ, and it belongs to the sphere of Christ's lordship.

"--which was not made known to the sons of men in other generations as it has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit" (3:5).

The relative clause ho ("which") refers to the mystery. It was not made known (ouk egnoristhe, aorist passive of gnorizo, "to make known, to reveal") to tois huiois ton anthropon ("the sons of men") -- a Hebraism for human beings generally (cf. Psalm 145:12 LXX [144:12]: "to make known to the sons of men his mighty acts"). The phrase en heterais geneais ("in other generations") names the period of concealment: the mystery was hidden throughout previous ages. The contrast is with nyn ("now"): it has now been revealed (apekalyphthe, aorist passive of apokalypto, "to unveil, to uncover, to reveal") to "his holy apostles and prophets" (tois hagiois apostolois autou kai prophetais).

The identity of "apostles and prophets" here matches their appearance in 2:20, where the community is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets." These are not the Old Testament prophets (who received partial and preparatory revelation) but the apostles and prophets of the new covenant community -- the first generation of Spirit-empowered witnesses and speakers. The order "apostles and prophets" (rather than the Old Testament sequence "prophets... apostles") confirms this reading. The agent of revelation is en pneumati ("by the Spirit," dative of instrument): the Spirit is the one who disclosed the mystery to the apostles and prophets. This is consistent with 1:17, where the author prayed that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ would give "a spirit of wisdom and revelation" (pneuma sophias kai apokalypseos).

The phrase "not made known... as it has now been revealed" (hos nyn apekalyphthe) uses the comparative hos ("as") to indicate degree rather than absolute absence. The mystery was not entirely unknown in previous generations -- the prophetic scriptures contained anticipations of gentile inclusion (cf. Isaiah 2:2-4, 49:6, 56:6-8; Amos 9:11-12 as cited in Acts 15:16-18; Genesis 12:3, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed"). But the full scope and nature of that inclusion was not disclosed to previous generations with the clarity and completeness with which it has now been revealed to the apostolic generation.

"--that the gentiles are co-heirs and co-members of the body and co-sharers of the promise in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (3:6).

This verse is the disclosure of the mystery's content. It is stated in a single sentence built around three syn- ("together with") compounds, all of which are rare or coined for this purpose:

(1) synkleronoma ("co-heirs, fellow heirs") -- the gentiles share the inheritance. The word kleronomos ("heir") appeared in the LXX for Israel's inheritance of the land (Deuteronomy 1:38 LXX: "he shall inherit it"; Joshua 14:1: "the inheritances which the sons of Israel inherited"). In Ephesians 1:11, the author said "we were made an inheritance" (eklerothemen) or "we received an inheritance" -- language rooted in Israel's identity as God's kleronomia (inheritance, allotted possession; Deuteronomy 4:20, 9:29). The gentiles are now syn-kleronoma: co-inheritors of what belonged to Israel.

(2) syssoma ("co-members of the body, belonging to the same body") -- the gentiles are incorporated into the same body. This word appears nowhere else in extant Greek literature before this text. It is apparently coined for this context. The "body" (soma) is the "one body" of 2:16 in which Christ reconciled both Jew and gentile to God. The gentiles are not a separate body alongside Israel's body; they are members of the same body.

(3) symmetocha ("co-sharers, fellow partakers") tes epangelias ("of the promise") -- the gentiles share in the promise. The word epangelia ("promise") echoes 2:12, where the gentiles were "strangers to the covenants of the promise" (xenoi ton diathekon tes epangelias). What they were formerly excluded from, they now share in as full partners.

The three syn- compounds are emphatic and deliberate. They do not describe a secondary or subordinate inclusion. The prefix syn- means "together with, jointly, in equal partnership." The gentiles are not guests in Israel's house; they are co-owners. They are not observers of Israel's inheritance; they are co-heirs. They are not attached to Israel's body as an appendage; they are co-members of the same body. The radicalism of this claim in a first-century Jewish context cannot be overstated. The gentiles, who in 2:12 had no Messiah, no citizenship, no covenants, no promise, no hope, and no God, are now full and equal partners in all of these.

The phrase en Christo Iesou ("in Christ Jesus") locates where this co-participation exists: in Christ. Outside of Christ, the old divisions remain operative. It is only "in Christ Jesus" that the co-heirship, co-membership, and co-sharing are real. The phrase dia tou euangeliou ("through the gospel") names the instrument: the gospel -- the proclamation of what God has done in Christ -- is the means by which this inclusion is effected and communicated.

## Verses 7-13: Paul's Commission and Its Cosmic Scope

"Of this gospel I was made a servant according to the gift of God's grace that was given to me by the working of his power" (3:7).

The word diakonos ("servant, minister, one who serves") describes Paul's relationship to the gospel. In the ancient world, a diakonos was not a menial slave but an authorized agent who carried out tasks on behalf of another. The word appears in 2 Corinthians 3:6 ("ministers of a new covenant") and 2 Corinthians 11:23 ("servants of Christ"). Paul is the authorized agent of the gospel.

This service came kata ten dorean tes charitos tou theou ("according to the gift of God's grace"). The word dorea ("gift, free gift") is the same word used in 4:7 ("to each one of us grace was given according to the measure of Christ's gift"). Paul's ministry is itself a gift -- not earned, not seized, but granted. The phrase tes dotheises moi ("which was given to me") repeats the language of verse 2 (tes dotheises moi, "given to me"). The repetition emphasizes that Paul's commission is entirely received, not self-assumed.

The instrument of this gift is kata ten energeian tes dynameos autou ("by the working of his power"). The word energeia ("working, operative power, effective energy") appears in Ephesians 1:19 for "the surpassing greatness of his power toward us who believe, according to the working (energeian) of the might of his strength." The same divine power that raised Christ from the dead (1:20) is the power that equipped Paul for his ministry. The word dynamis ("power, ability, capacity") is the raw capacity; energeia is that capacity in active operation. Paul's ministry is not the product of his own strength but of God's operative power working through him.

"To me, the very least of all the saints, this grace was given, to preach to the gentiles the unsearchable riches of Christ" (3:8).

The phrase emoi to elachistotero panton ton hagion ("to me, the least of all the saints") is a remarkable self-description. The word elachistoteros is a double comparative -- a comparative form (elachistoteros, "lesser") built on what is already a superlative (elachistos, "least"). Grammarians call this a "hyper-superlative": less than the least, the very smallest of the small. It is grammatically irregular, which suggests it is emphatic and intentional -- Paul strains language to express the depth of his self-assessment. The phrase echoes 1 Corinthians 15:9: "I am the least of the apostles, not fit to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God." But here the comparison is not with apostles only but with "all the saints" (panton ton hagion) -- all the holy ones, the entire community of believers. The man entrusted with the greatest revelation considers himself the lowest member of the community to whom it was revealed.

The grace given to him has a specific content: euangelisasthai tois ethnesin ("to preach the good news to the gentiles"). The verb euangelizomai ("to bring good news, to announce glad tidings") echoes Isaiah 52:7 (LXX): "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the one who brings good news" (euangelizomenou). The content of his preaching is to anexichniaston plouton tou Christou ("the unsearchable riches of Christ"). The word anexichniastos ("unsearchable, unfathomable, beyond tracing out") appeared in Romans 11:33: "How unsearchable (anexerauneta) are his judgments and how inscrutable (anexichniastoi) his ways!" The prefix an- is a negation; the root ichnos means "track, footprint, trace." The riches of Christ cannot be tracked to their source or traced to their boundary. They exceed investigation. The word ploutos ("riches, wealth") has appeared repeatedly in Ephesians: 1:7 ("riches of his grace"), 1:18 ("riches of the glory of his inheritance"), 2:7 ("surpassing riches of his grace"). The riches that were attributed to God's grace are now attributed to Christ. What God possesses in grace, Christ embodies.

"--and to bring to light for all what is the stewardship of the mystery hidden for ages in God, who created all things" (3:9).

The second purpose of Paul's commission: photisai pantas ("to bring to light for all, to illuminate everyone"). The verb photizo ("to illuminate, to enlighten, to make visible") is the same root as phos ("light"). Paul's task is to take what was hidden in darkness and bring it into the light of public knowledge. The word pantas ("all, everyone") is not restricted to gentiles but encompasses all people -- Jew and gentile alike need to see the mystery clearly.

The object of illumination is tis he oikonomia tou mysteriou ("what is the stewardship of the mystery"). The word oikonomia here may mean "stewardship" (how the mystery is administered) or "plan" (the arrangement by which the mystery is worked out). The mystery has been tou apokekrymmenou apo ton aionon ("hidden from the ages, concealed since the ages began"). The word apokekrymmenou is a perfect passive participle of apokrypto ("to hide, to conceal"): the mystery has been and remains in a state of having been hidden. The phrase apo ton aionon ("from the ages") indicates the duration of concealment: from the beginning of the ages until now.

The mystery was hidden en to theo ("in God") -- its hiding place was God himself. No human investigation could discover it because it was contained within God's own purpose. The appositional clause to ta panta ktisanti ("who created all things") identifies God as the creator. This is not a random theological assertion but a deliberate framing: the God who concealed the mystery is the God who made everything. The creator of all things has a plan for all things, and that plan has now been disclosed. The creation language echoes Genesis 1 and connects to 2:10, where believers are God's poiema ("workmanship, thing made") created (ktisthentes) in Christ Jesus. The same God who created the universe is now creating a new humanity.

"--so that through the church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places" (3:10).

The purpose clause hina ("so that, in order that") reveals the cosmic scope of the mystery's disclosure. The wisdom of God (he polypoikilos sophia tou theou) is to be made known (gnoristhe, aorist passive subjunctive of gnorizo) through the church (dia tes ekklesias) to the rulers and authorities (tais archais kai tais exousiais) in the heavenly places (en tois epouraniois).

The word polypoikilos ("manifold, many-colored, richly varied, much-variegated") appears only here in the New Testament. In classical Greek, poikilos described embroidered cloth, variegated stone, or complex musical compositions. The prefix poly- ("many, much") intensifies: not merely varied but richly, extravagantly varied. God's wisdom is not monochrome but kaleidoscopic -- it displays itself in a complexity of patterns and colors. The reconciliation of Jew and gentile into one body is itself a display of this variegated wisdom: the God who unites what was divided, who makes co-heirs out of strangers, who builds one temple from two peoples, is demonstrating the intricacy and range of his wisdom.

The audience of this demonstration is startling: the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places. The phrase tais archais kai tais exousiais ("the rulers and the authorities") appeared in 1:21, where Christ was seated "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (hyerano pases arches kai exousias kai dynameos kai kyriotetos). In the cosmology reflected in Ephesians and widely attested in Second Temple Judaism (cf. 1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Qumran War Scroll), the heavenly places are populated by spiritual powers that exercise authority over nations and cosmic domains. These powers are not necessarily malign (though 6:12 will identify them as enemies against whom the community struggles); they are cosmic intelligences who observe and learn.

The phrase en tois epouraniois ("in the heavenly places") is distinctive to Ephesians, appearing at 1:3, 1:20, 2:6, 3:10, and 6:12. At 1:3, believers have been blessed with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places. At 1:20, Christ is seated at God's right hand in the heavenly places. At 2:6, believers have been seated with Christ in the heavenly places. The heavenly places are not merely a location but a dimension of reality where God's purposes are being enacted. The church exists in this dimension -- seated with Christ (2:6) -- and it is in this dimension that the church becomes the demonstration of God's wisdom to the cosmic powers.

The implication is remarkable: the church -- the community of reconciled Jews and gentiles -- is the visual aid by which the cosmic powers learn what God is like. The existence of the church is itself a message. The multi-ethnic, formerly divided community, now united in one body, is the proof that God's wisdom is not abstract but operative, not simple but richly complex.

"--according to the eternal purpose which he carried out in Christ Jesus our Lord" (3:11).

The phrase kata prothesin ton aionon ("according to the purpose of the ages" or "according to the eternal purpose") connects to 1:9-11, where God's prothesis ("purpose, plan, resolve") was named as the basis of his action. The word prothesis appeared in Romans 8:28 ("those who are called according to his purpose") and Romans 9:11 ("so that God's purpose according to election might stand"). In Ephesians, the purpose is specifically the plan to unite all things in Christ (1:10). The genitive ton aionon ("of the ages") indicates that the purpose spans all ages -- it is not a recent adaptation but the plan God held from the beginning.

This purpose hen epoiesen en to Christo Iesou to kyrio hemon ("which he carried out in Christ Jesus our Lord"). The verb epoiesen (aorist active of poieo, "to do, to make, to accomplish") is in the past tense: God has already accomplished his eternal purpose in Christ. The plan is not still in draft form; it has been executed. The execution occurred en Christo Iesou -- in Christ Jesus, in his person, in his death and resurrection, in his exaltation. The title to kyrio hemon ("our Lord") brings the cosmic back to the personal: the Christ in whom cosmic purposes are accomplished is "our Lord" -- he belongs to the community and they to him.

"--in whom we have boldness and access with confidence through faith in him" (3:12).

The relative clause en ho ("in whom") continues from Christ Jesus. The word parresia ("boldness, frankness, freedom of speech, open confidence") was a political term in classical Athens: the citizen's right to speak freely in the assembly. It came to describe the confidence of one who stands in right relationship -- the ability to approach without fear or shame. The word prosagoge ("access, introduction, approach") appeared in 2:18: "through him we both have access (prosagogen) in one Spirit to the Father." The same word is now repeated, confirming continuity of argument. The access that 2:18 declared to be shared by both Jew and gentile is the access that 3:12 describes experientially as bold and confident.

The phrase dia tes pisteos autou ("through faith in him" or "through his faithfulness") names the means of access. The genitive autou may be objective ("faith directed toward him") or subjective ("his own faithfulness"). In context, both dimensions are present: Christ's own faithfulness in accomplishing God's purpose, and the community's faith in him as Lord.

The word pepoithesin ("confidence") is a noun derived from peitho ("to persuade, to trust, to have confidence"). The compound expression -- boldness and access with confidence -- is not redundant but layered: parresia is the freedom to speak; prosagoge is the right to approach; pepoithesis is the inner assurance that the approach and speech will be received. Together they describe the full experience of the former outsider who now stands before God with the status of a family member.

"So I ask you not to lose heart over what I am suffering for you, which is your glory" (3:13).

The word dio ("therefore, for this reason") draws a conclusion from the preceding argument. Because the mystery has been revealed, because Paul's commission serves the gentiles' inclusion, because the church is the display of God's wisdom to cosmic powers -- therefore, his sufferings should not cause the gentile audience to lose heart (enkakein, "to become discouraged, to grow weary, to lose courage"). The word thlipsesin ("sufferings, afflictions, pressures") mou ("my") hyper hymon ("on behalf of you") repeats the hyper hymon of verse 1: Paul's sufferings are for their sake.

The final clause hetis estin doxa hymon ("which is your glory") is striking. The relative pronoun hetis ("which") refers to Paul's sufferings (or his suffering on their behalf). His afflictions are their doxa -- their "glory, honor, distinction." The audience might have viewed Paul's imprisonment as evidence that his mission had failed or that association with him was shameful. He reverses the evaluation: the fact that he suffers for the gentiles' inclusion is itself evidence of their value, their importance in God's plan. An apostle's imprisonment on their behalf is not their shame but their distinction.

## Verses 14-19: The Prayer for Inner Strengthening and Comprehension

"For this reason I bow my knees before the Father" (3:14).

The phrase toutou charin ("for this reason") resumes the sentence broken off at 3:1. The author now completes what he began: "For this reason I, Paul..." (3:1) is completed with "For this reason I bow my knees before the Father" (3:14). The long parenthesis of verses 2-13 has explained why the prayer matters -- because of the mystery now revealed, because of Paul's commission to make it known, because of its cosmic significance. The prayer that follows is grounded in all of this.

The gesture kampto ta gonata mou ("I bow my knees") describes a posture of intense supplication. The normal Jewish posture for prayer was standing (cf. 1 Kings 8:22; Matthew 6:5; Mark 11:25; Luke 18:11, 13). Kneeling indicated extraordinary urgency or depth of feeling (cf. Daniel 6:10; Acts 7:60, Stephen kneeling at his stoning; Acts 20:36, Paul kneeling with the Ephesian elders; Acts 21:5). The phrase echoes Isaiah 45:23 (LXX): "to me every knee shall bow" (emoi kampsei pan gony) -- language Paul applied to Christ in Philippians 2:10. The author kneels before the one before whom every knee will ultimately bow.

The prayer is directed pros ton patera ("toward the Father"). The word pater ("Father") is the relational term that defines the community's access to God (2:18, "access... to the Father"). The prayer is to the Father from whom the community has its identity as sons and daughters.

"--from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named" (3:15).

The phrase ex hou pasa patria en ouranois kai epi ges onomazetai ("from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named") involves a wordplay: pater ("father") and patria ("family, lineage, clan, fatherhood"). Every patria derives its name -- its identity, its character as a family -- from the pater. The word patria appears in the LXX for the tribal and clan divisions of Israel (Numbers 1:2, "by their families [patriais]"; 1 Chronicles 4:38). The phrase pasa patria ("every family") -- in heaven and on earth -- extends the scope beyond Israel's clans to every kinship group that exists, including the heavenly beings (the "rulers and authorities" of 3:10) and the earthly peoples. All familial identity derives from this one Father. This grounds the prayer: the Father to whom Paul prays is the source of all family, all belonging, all kinship. The new family of Jews and gentiles together is rooted in the one from whom all family originates.

"--that according to the riches of his glory he would grant you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner person" (3:16).

The prayer's first petition: hina do hymin... krataiothenai ("that he would grant you to be strengthened"). The verb krataiotheai (aorist passive infinitive of krataioo, "to strengthen, to make strong, to empower") is relatively rare; in the LXX it appears in 1 Samuel 4:9 ("Be strong and be men") and frequently in the sense of being made powerful for a task. The power is described as dia tou pneumatos autou ("through his Spirit") -- the Spirit is the agent of strengthening, consistent with 2:22 (built together in the Spirit) and 3:5 (revealed by the Spirit). The location of the strengthening is eis ton eso anthropon ("in the inner person, into the inner human being"). The phrase ho eso anthropos ("the inner person") appeared in Romans 7:22: "I delight in the law of God in my inner being." In 2 Corinthians 4:16: "though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day." The inner person is the seat of understanding, will, and spiritual receptivity -- the dimension of the human being that is accessible to the Spirit's work. The strengthening is not physical but spiritual: it is empowerment in the deepest dimension of the person.

The standard for this empowerment is kata to ploutos tes doxes autou ("according to the riches of his glory"). The measure of the gift is not the recipient's capacity but the giver's resources. God gives out of the riches of his glory -- not sparingly, not according to desert, but according to the inexhaustible wealth of his own glorious being.

"--that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith" (3:17a).

The second petition (or the result of the first): katoikesai ton Christon dia tes pisteos en tais kardiais hymon ("that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts"). The verb katoikeo ("to dwell, to inhabit permanently, to take up residence") is stronger than the simple oikeo ("to dwell"). The prefix kata- intensifies: katoikeo means to settle down and make one's permanent home, not to visit or stay temporarily. In the LXX, this verb is used for God's dwelling in the temple: "the place where the LORD your God will choose to make his name dwell" (Deuteronomy 12:11, katoikesai to onoma autou ekei). In Colossians 1:19: "in him all the fullness was pleased to dwell" (katoikesai). And in Colossians 2:9: "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (katoikei). The verb describes permanent, settled residence -- Christ making his home in the community's hearts, not as a guest but as the permanent inhabitant.

The phrase en tais kardiais hymon ("in your hearts") locates the indwelling. The kardia ("heart") in Hebraic usage is not primarily the seat of emotion but the center of the whole person -- mind, will, affection, and intention (cf. Deuteronomy 6:5, "love the LORD your God with all your heart"; Proverbs 4:23, "Guard your heart, for from it flow the springs of life"). Christ's indwelling in the heart means Christ's presence at the governing center of the person. The means is dia tes pisteos ("through faith") -- faith is the channel through which the indwelling is realized.

The connection between Spirit-strengthening (v. 16) and Christ-indwelling (v. 17a) is not a sequence of two separate events but two descriptions of one reality: the Spirit strengthens the inner person so that Christ may dwell in the heart. The Spirit's work creates the conditions for Christ's residence. This is consistent with 2:22, where the community is "a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" -- God dwells by means of the Spirit. Here, Christ dwells through faith, enabled by the Spirit's empowering of the inner person.

"--that you, being rooted and grounded in love" (3:17b).

The participial clause errizomenoi kai tethemeliomenoi en agape ("having been rooted and grounded in love") uses two perfect passive participles that blend agricultural and architectural imagery. The verb rhizoo ("to root, to cause to take root") is from rhiza ("root"); in the LXX, Israel is described as a plant rooted by God: "You brought a vine out of Egypt... it took deep root and filled the land" (Psalm 80:8-9 [LXX 79:9-10]; cf. Isaiah 27:6, "Israel shall blossom and put forth shoots and fill the whole world with fruit"). The verb themelioo ("to lay a foundation, to found, to establish") is from themelios ("foundation"); it appeared in 2:20 for the community built "on the foundation of the apostles and prophets." The combination of images -- rooted like a plant and founded like a building -- is compressed: the community is simultaneously a garden and a temple, growing downward into soil and standing firm on bedrock.

The soil and bedrock are en agape ("in love"). Love is the medium in which the community is rooted and the foundation on which it is established. The word agape ("love") will appear again in verse 19 ("the love of Christ") and has already appeared in 1:4 ("in love he predestined us"), 1:15 ("your love toward all the saints"), 2:4 ("because of his great love with which he loved us"). Love is not a sentiment but the ground of the community's existence.

"--may have strength to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth" (3:18).

The third petition: hina exischysete katalabethai ("that you may have strength to comprehend"). The verb exischyo ("to be fully able, to have the strength, to be capable") is a strengthened form of ischyo ("to be strong"); the prefix ex- intensifies. Comprehension of what Paul is about to describe requires more than ordinary capacity -- it requires divinely granted strength. The verb katalambanomai ("to comprehend, to grasp, to seize, to apprehend") is the same verb used in Philippians 3:12: "I press on to make it my own (katalabo), because Christ Jesus has made me his own (katelemphthen)." It means to lay hold of something, to grasp it firmly, to take possession of it intellectually and experientially.

The comprehension is syn pasin tois hagiois ("with all the saints, together with all the holy ones"). The syn- prefix, which dominated verse 6 (co-heirs, co-members, co-sharers), now appears in the prayer: comprehension is not a solitary achievement but a communal one. The full dimensions of the mystery cannot be grasped by any individual alone. The phrase "all the saints" crosses ethnic boundaries: the gentile addressees comprehend together with all the saints -- Jewish and gentile believers together. This communal emphasis is consistent with the letter's entire argument: the mystery is precisely the unity of the community, and comprehending it requires the community.

The object of comprehension is ti to platos kai mekos kai hypsos kai bathos ("what is the breadth and length and height and depth"). Four spatial dimensions are named without an explicit noun -- the breadth, length, height, and depth of what? The text does not specify. In the immediate context, the most natural referent is love -- the love in which the community is rooted and grounded (v. 17b) and the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge (v. 19). The four dimensions describe the immeasurability of this love: it extends in every direction beyond what can be calculated. Some have connected these dimensions to the temple imagery of 2:20-22 -- the dimensions of the new dwelling of God -- or to the cosmic scope of the mystery itself. In the LXX, Job 11:7-9 uses similar dimensional language for the unsearchability of God: "Can you find out the deep things of God?... Its measure is longer than the earth and broader than the sea." The dimensions describe something that exceeds human capacity to measure.

"--and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled to all the fullness of God" (3:19).

The prayer reaches its climax in a deliberate paradox: gnonai te ten hyperballousan tes gnoseos agapen tou Christou ("to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge"). The verb ginosko ("to know") and the noun gnosis ("knowledge") are from the same root. The love of Christ is to be known, but it surpasses knowing. The verb hyperballo ("to surpass, to exceed, to go beyond") appeared in 1:19 for "the surpassing greatness of his power" and in 2:7 for "the surpassing riches of his grace." The love of Christ is in the same category as God's power and grace: it exceeds all measurement. The paradox is intentional: the prayer asks for experiential knowledge of something that transcends intellectual knowledge. The community is to know by participation what it cannot exhaust by comprehension.

The phrase ten agapen tou Christou ("the love of Christ") is the genitive: either the love that Christ has (subjective genitive -- Christ's love for them) or the love directed toward Christ (objective genitive -- their love for Christ). In context, the subjective genitive is primary: this is the love that Christ demonstrates, the love expressed in 5:2 ("Christ loved us and gave himself up for us") and 5:25 ("Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her"). It is the love of the one who, being in the form of God, emptied himself (Philippians 2:6-7). This love is the content of the "breadth and length and height and depth" of verse 18.

The purpose of knowing the unknowable love: hina plerothethe eis pan to pleroma tou theou ("that you may be filled to all the fullness of God"). The verb pleroo ("to fill, to make full") is passive: the community is to be filled. The agent is God. The measure is pan to pleroma tou theou ("all the fullness of God"). The word pleroma ("fullness, completeness, that which fills") appeared in 1:10 ("the fullness of the times"), 1:23 ("the fullness of him who fills all in all"), and will appear in 4:13 ("the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ"). In 1:23, the church was described as Christ's body, "the fullness of him who fills all in all." In Colossians 1:19, "all the fullness" was pleased to dwell in Christ. Here the prayer asks that the community be filled up to the measure of all the fullness of God -- that the divine fullness that dwells in Christ (Colossians 2:9) would fill the community that is Christ's body.

This is the most expansive petition in the prayer. It asks not for a partial measure but for the totality -- eis pan to pleroma ("to all the fullness"). The preposition eis indicates direction and goal: the community is to be filled up to the point of all God's fullness. This does not mean the community becomes God; it means the community is so permeated by God's presence, power, love, and character that it reflects the fullness of who God is. The temple imagery of 2:21-22 reaches its consummation: the temple filled with God's glory, as the tabernacle was filled with the cloud (Exodus 40:34-35, "the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle") and as Solomon's temple was filled with the cloud (1 Kings 8:10-11, "the glory of the LORD filled the house of the LORD"). The new temple, composed of Jews and gentiles together, is to be filled with God's fullness as the ancient temples were filled with God's glory.

## Verses 20-21: The Doxology

"Now to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think, according to the power at work within us" (3:20).

The prayer concludes with a doxology -- an ascription of glory to God. The doxology does not merely praise God in general terms; it completes the logic of the prayer by declaring that God is able to exceed everything that has just been asked.

The phrase to de dynamento ("to the one who is able") identifies God by his capacity. The verb dynamai ("to be able, to have the power") was used in Romans 8:7 for the flesh's inability to submit to God's law. Here it describes God's ability, which has no such limitation. The phrase hyper panta poiesai ("to do beyond all things") uses hyper ("beyond, above, more than") with the accusative panta ("all things"): God's capacity extends beyond the totality of what exists. The adverbs hyperekperissou ("far more abundantly, exceedingly beyond, superabundantly") are a triple compound: hyper ("beyond") + ek ("out of") + perissou ("abundance"). This is one of the most extreme expressions of excess in Greek: beyond-out-of-abundance. It strains language to convey that God's ability outstrips not only what has been requested but what can be imagined.

The phrase hon aitoumetha e nooumen ("than all that we ask or think") names two boundaries that God exceeds. The verb aiteo ("to ask, to request") covers the prayer just offered -- everything Paul has asked in verses 14-19. The verb noeo ("to think, to conceive, to imagine") covers what the mind can formulate beyond prayer -- the full range of human imagination. God's ability exceeds both the spoken prayer and the unspoken thought. The prayer for the fullness of God (v. 19) was already beyond human capacity to imagine fully; the doxology declares that God will exceed even that.

The standard: kata ten dynamin ten energoumenen en hemin ("according to the power working in us"). The word dynamis ("power") reappears from verse 7 and from 1:19. The participle energoumenen ("working, operating, being active") is from energeo, the verb form of energeia from verse 7. The power that works in the community is the same power by which Paul was made a minister (v. 7) and the same power that raised Christ from the dead (1:19-20). The doxology grounds its confidence not in God's transcendent power in the abstract but in the power already demonstrably at work within the community. God's ability to exceed all asking and thinking is certified by the power the community already experiences.

"--to him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, forever and ever. Amen" (3:21).

The doxology concludes: auto he doxa en te ekklesia kai en Christo Iesou ("to him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus"). The word doxa ("glory") carries its full LXX weight: the kavod of YHWH, the visible manifestation of God's presence and character (Exodus 33:18-22; Isaiah 6:3). God's glory is to be displayed en te ekklesia ("in the church") -- in the community of reconciled Jews and gentiles. This is the culmination of the temple imagery. As the kavod filled the tabernacle and the temple, so God's doxa is displayed in the ekklesia. The church is the location of God's glory-manifestation. The addition kai en Christo Iesou ("and in Christ Jesus") places the church's glory-display within Christ: the church manifests God's glory because it is in Christ, and Christ is the one in whom God's fullness dwells (Colossians 1:19).

The temporal scope is eis pasas tas geneas tou aionos ton aionon ("throughout all generations of the age of the ages, forever and ever"). The phrase is deliberately extravagant -- generations upon ages upon ages -- an expression of unending, unlimited duration. God's glory in the church and in Christ is not temporary or provisional. It extends through every generation and into the unending age.

The word amen ("amen, so be it, truly, let it be so") is the Hebrew particle of affirmation, transliterated into Greek. It seals the prayer and the doxology as the community's collective affirmation: what has been prayed and praised is true, and the community ratifies it.

## Summary of the Argument

Ephesians 3 occupies the structural center of the letter. The author was about to pray (3:1), but the mention of his situation as prisoner on behalf of the gentiles prompted an extended explanation of his commission (3:2-13). That commission is to make known the mystery: the gentiles are co-heirs, co-members of the body, and co-sharers of the promise in Christ Jesus (3:6). This mystery was hidden for ages but has now been revealed by the Spirit to the apostles and prophets (3:5). Its scope is cosmic: even the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places learn God's manifold wisdom by observing the church (3:10). Paul's sufferings in this service are not the gentiles' shame but their glory (3:13).

The prayer that follows (3:14-19) asks that the audience would be inwardly strengthened by the Spirit, that Christ would dwell permanently in their hearts, that they would be rooted and grounded in love, and that they would comprehend -- together with all the saints -- the immeasurable dimensions of Christ's love, so as to be filled to all the fullness of God. The doxology (3:20-21) declares that God is able to exceed even this extraordinary prayer, according to the power already at work in the community, and that God's glory will be displayed in the church and in Christ Jesus forever.

The chapter's internal logic moves from revelation (the mystery has been disclosed) through commission (Paul's role in making it known) to prayer (that the audience would experientially inhabit what has been revealed) to doxology (confidence that God's power will accomplish what exceeds all asking or thinking). The bridge from chapter 2 (the theological declaration of Jew-gentile unity) to chapter 4 (the practical exhortation to walk worthy of the calling) runs directly through chapter 3: the mystery has been declared and the steward has delivered it; now the prayer asks that the hearers be equipped to live it out; and the ethical instructions of chapters 4-6 will specify what that living looks like.

## Observations on Pronoun Usage

The pronoun pattern in Ephesians 3 is consistent with the pattern established in chapters 1-2. The second person "you" (hymeis, hymon, hymas) consistently addresses the gentile audience: "on behalf of you, the gentiles" (3:1), "given to me for you" (3:2), "to preach to the gentiles" (3:8), "what I am suffering for you" (3:13), "grant you to be strengthened" (3:16), "in your hearts" (3:17). The first person singular "I" (ego) refers to Paul throughout verses 1-14. The first person plural "we" (hemeis) appears in 3:12 ("we have boldness and access") and 3:20 ("all that we ask or think"), broadening to include both Paul and the audience -- both Jewish and gentile believers -- in the shared experience of access and prayer. The shift from "you" (gentile audience) to "we" (the united community) at 3:12 mirrors the transition in 2:14-18, where the separate "you" and "we" converged in the statement "he is our peace" (2:14) and "we both have access in one Spirit to the Father" (2:18). The pronouns track the argument: the mystery is about the gentiles' inclusion (you), but its realization is a shared reality (we).

## Old Testament and LXX Resonances

The following Old Testament texts are active in the background of Ephesians 3:

- **Isaiah 57:19** ("peace to the far and to the near"): activated in 2:13-17 and sustaining the framework of gentile inclusion that chapter 3 explains.
- **Isaiah 52:7** ("how beautiful the feet of the one who brings good news"): the verb euangelizomai in 3:8 echoes this Isaianic declaration.
- **Genesis 12:3** ("in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed"): the mystery that the gentiles are co-heirs fulfills the Abrahamic promise of blessing to all families/nations.
- **Psalm 80:8-9 [LXX 79:9-10]** (Israel as a vine planted and rooted by God): the verb rhizoo in 3:17 draws on this tradition of God's people as a rooted plant.
- **Exodus 40:34-35** and **1 Kings 8:10-11** (the glory filling the tabernacle and temple): the prayer that the community be "filled to all the fullness of God" (3:19) and the doxology placing God's glory "in the church" (3:21) evoke these temple-filling narratives.
- **Job 11:7-9** (the unsearchable dimensions of God's nature): the "breadth and length and height and depth" of 3:18 parallel Job's language about the immeasurability of divine wisdom.
- **Isaiah 45:23** ("to me every knee shall bow"): the kneeling posture of 3:14 evokes this text, which Paul applied to Christ in Philippians 2:10.
- **Daniel 6:10** (Daniel kneeling in prayer): the kneeling posture as a mark of urgent supplication.
- **Deuteronomy 12:11** (God choosing a place for his name to dwell): the verb katoikeo in 3:17 carries temple-dwelling connotations from the Deuteronomic tradition.
- **Isaiah 49:6** ("I will make you as a light for the nations"): the gentile-inclusion mission that Paul describes as his commission fulfills this Servant-of-the-LORD vocation.
